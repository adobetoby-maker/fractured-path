Cael arrived at the Cinder House yard two hours before the bout, which was one hour earlier than he had told anyone he would, and found that the yard had gotten there before him.

Not the crowd — the crowd was still arriving, would keep arriving until well past the point where arriving made sense — but the yard itself had already changed. Someone had cleared the training racks against the walls. Someone had chalked the circle wider than he'd ever seen it chalked, wide enough for a professional's range. The betting slates near the gate had been wiped and rewritten in a larger hand, as if the numbers themselves expected to be read from farther away. Cael stood in the entrance and did what he did with everything: he counted. Forty-one people already inside, at two hours out. The yard held perhaps three hundred standing, if nobody minded touching strangers. By the look of the queue forming along the outer wall, nobody was going to mind.

"Stop counting the house," Lira said, beside him. "The house isn't fighting you."

"The house is the point."

"I know the house is the point." She was carrying his wraps and her own nerves, and only one of those showed. "Count it after. When it's a number that means something."

He let it go, which she would know cost him nothing, because he already had the number he wanted. Three hundred witnesses, more outside. Two weeks ago, when he had asked Vell for this bout, that number had been the entire argument. A fourteen-year-old nobody could be made to disappear between one morning and the next, and the city would flow shut over the space he'd occupied. A fourteen-year-old that three hundred people had watched, wagered on, argued about — that was a different arithmetic. That was a boy whose absence would itch.

Whether the arithmetic actually worked — whether visibility could do the thing he had built this whole afternoon to make it do — was not something he could know yet. It was only the best answer he'd found to a problem that had no good ones, arrived at the night he'd understood that quiet wasn't protecting him. It had never protected anyone the Compact was interested in. It just made the paperwork easier.

The betting was running eleven to one against him when he passed the slates. By the time he'd finished wrapping his hands in the little roped-off corner that served the circuit as a fighters' preparation area, it had drifted to thirteen.

"Thirteen to one," Feryn reported, arriving the way Feryn arrived everywhere, as though the crowd had been assembled specifically to be moved through. He'd dressed for the occasion, which for Feryn meant dressing exactly as always and standing straighter. "I want you to understand that I put money on you at eleven and I resent every point of drift since. When you win, I am buying a coat."

"When," Lira said.

"When," Feryn said. "I've been hit by this boy. I'm allowed the word." But he crouched down to where Cael sat, and the showman drained out of his voice, and what was left was the Bronze-tier professional who had spent two weeks assembling everything the circuit knew about Darrow Innes. "Say it back to me. Last time."

"He doesn't defend early," Cael said. "He lets the first exchange land. All of it. He's not lazy and he's not slow — it's deliberate. He wants your best work on record so your body can start learning it doesn't matter. That's the actual weapon. The Iron Path is the floor. The futility is the fight."

"And so."

"And so the first exchange isn't wasted even though it accomplishes nothing." He flexed his wrapped hands, watching the tendons move. "Because he's going to stand there and let me take measurements."

"The man's been a professional for eight years," Feryn said. "Every fighter I found who'd shared a circle with him said a version of the same thing. The Copper who lasted longest told me it was like punching a harbor wall that was politely waiting for you to finish. The old circuit hand who'd watched him fight elsewhere said the worst part isn't the resilience. The worst part is his face. He watches you understand it isn't working, and he doesn't gloat, because he doesn't need to. It's not personal for him. That's what makes it terrible."

"It's not personal for me either," Cael said.

Feryn looked at him for a moment. "No," he said. "I genuinely believe it isn't. That may be the one advantage nobody's priced in."

Lira knelt and took over the last of the wrapping herself, not because Cael was doing it wrong but because her hands wanted a task. Two weeks of mornings were in her fingers — two weeks of the same drill run past the point of boredom into the place where boredom becomes precision. Not power. They had settled that the first night, the two of them and Feryn's notes spread across the boarding-house table: nobody hits through the Iron Path. You could break your hands proving it. That much was sworn testimony, the one point every account Feryn had gathered agreed on: power went in and Darrow's body drank it. Where the power went after it was drunk — that was where testimony stopped and Cael's work had started.

But shedding is work. That had been Cael's contribution, the wager two weeks of study had sharpened into a plan, and in his head it was written up the way he wrote up everything, in the notebook's three columns. Claim: absorption wasn't magic, it was structure, and structure meant the force went somewhere, along something. Evidence: listen to enough fighters describe the feeling of hitting him and a shape emerged — every description consistent with one road, across, down, out through the braced back leg, and any road that carried everything ought to narrow somewhere. Low on the left side, if the shape he'd drawn was honest, where the ribs would hand their load to the hip. Every account agreed without knowing it agreed, which was the best kind of evidence and still wasn't proof. Ruling: pending. Nobody had ever fought Darrow as if the road existed, which was half of why the odds were what they were, and all of why Cael thought the odds were wrong. Call it the crossing — provisionally, the way he labeled everything he hadn't yet watched be true. If it was there, you couldn't break it and didn't need to. You needed to keep it busy — the same place, again and again, each strike arriving while the road was still carrying the last one, until the structure that made him unhittable became the structure that was too loaded to answer. If it was there. The first exchange existed to find out.

"Same place," Lira said quietly, tying off the wrap. "Every time. No matter what the rest of you is doing. Say it."

"Same place. Every time."

"Even when it's boring. Even when it's costing you."

"Especially then."

"And you don't get greedy in the third." Her voice was level and her hands weren't, quite. "He's going to be tired of you by the third. Tired professionals end things. You survive the third, you—" She stopped herself. They had agreed not to plan past the third in words, because plans past the third depended on Darrow, and Darrow had not agreed to their plans. "You survive the third."

"I survive the third," Cael said.

Through the wall of sound behind them, the yard was filling the way a lock fills, pressure equalizing from every side. He could hear the errand boys who ran slate-changes for the bookmakers threading the crowd and calling numbers, their voices cracking with the importance of it. He could hear an argument about whether assessed-Copper meant anything against a real rating, and another argument about what the boy actually was, the word nobody said aloud sitting under both of them like a stone under cloth. The baker from the market lane had shut her stall for the afternoon — he'd seen her in the third row, arms folded, wearing the expression of a woman who had sold this boy bread at dawn for half a year and intended to see personally what the fuss was. In the far corner, on the bench that was his by decades of custom, the old man who kept the yard sat with his cane between his knees, still as furniture, missing nothing.

And then the crowd's sound changed pitch, and Cael knew without looking that Darrow Innes had come into the yard.

He looked anyway. That was the job.

Darrow crossed the open ground toward the circle the way other men crossed their own kitchens. He was not enormous. That surprised people, Feryn had warned — they expected the Iron Path to come wrapped in slabs, and instead it came in a broad, settled frame that had stopped needing to advertise years ago, a man with a fighter's economy and a face like the last page of a well-kept account book. He set his kit down at the far edge, nodded to Vell with genuine courtesy, and began his preparation without once glancing at the betting slates. He knew what they said. They had said some version of it for eight years.

He did glance, once, at Cael. It was not a hard look or a soft one. It was the look a professional gives a task: brief, complete, filed.

"He just measured you," Lira murmured.

"I know," Cael said. "I felt the tape."

Vell took the center of the circle without ceremony, and the yard, which had been shouting, discovered it could be quiet.

She stood the way she always stood, a woman in her fifties with her ledger under one arm and half a lifetime of the circuit's weight sitting on her patience. When she spoke she didn't raise her voice, and the yard bent close to meet it, which was the point.

"This is a recorded bout," Vell said. "Witnessed, wagered, and entered. Darrow Innes. Bronze, Rank One, Iron Path, eight years professional." A sound moved through the crowd, appetite and awe. "Cael Hesk-ward. Assessed-Copper, unclassified." A different sound. He was used to that sound. "The bout continues to submission, incapacitation, or forfeit, and nothing past my call. Nothing past my call," she said again, at the same volume, and somehow the second time was iron. "The circuit keeps this circle by keeping its rules. Anyone with an objection can carry it out through the gate."

Nobody carried anything anywhere.

"Fighters to the mark."

The chalked circle was wider than any circle Cael had fought in, and walking to the center of it took a long time and no time at all. Up close, Darrow smelled of cheap soap and old leather, and his breathing was the breathing of a man reading in a chair. He gave Cael a small nod — not warmth, not contempt, the plain acknowledgment of one tradesman arriving at a shared job.

"Whenever she says," Darrow said, mildly.

"Begin," said Vell.

For three full breaths, neither of them moved, and the crowd's silence took on weight. Cael spent the breaths the way he'd budgeted them: watching the stance settle. There it was, as advertised — the left side quiet, the right foot back and rooted, the whole architecture organized around receiving. Eight years of accounts had described it. None of them had conveyed the strange calm of standing in front of it, the sense of squaring off against a door in a hill.

Cael went in first, because someone had to and Darrow demonstrably wouldn't.

He opened with the best sequence he owned. There was no reason to hide it and every reason to spend it — a low feint flowing into a three-strike line he'd drilled until it lived below thought, elbow, ribs, jaw, each placement true, each landing exactly where he'd aimed it. He knew they were good strikes. He'd broken a Copper Rank 4's guard with the same line in the spring.

Hitting Darrow was like hitting the memory of a man. The strikes landed — he felt each one connect, clean — and the force went away. Not blocked. Not slipped. Received, the way the harbor wall received, and passed along somewhere with a courtesy that was worse than any counter. Darrow's head didn't move. His breathing didn't change. From half a pace away, Cael watched his own best work vanish into that broad frame like coins into deep water, and felt — measured, precise, unwelcome — the first cold touch of the thing Feryn had warned him was the actual weapon.

It doesn't matter. Nothing you do matters.

The crowd felt it too. Three hundred people made a sound like money changing its mind.

Darrow let him work for the length of the exchange, answering just enough to be honest — a push here, a short hooking counter that Cael gave ground before rather than test, one lazy-looking sweep that was nothing like lazy and that Cael escaped by a hand's width with his pulse suddenly loud. And all the while, under the futility, Cael did the thing he had actually come to do.

He took measurements.

Every strike that vanished told him something — or he made it tell him something, which from inside a fight came to the same thing. He landed on the ribs and read the give under his knuckles as load traveling left and down. He landed on the shoulder and read the same. Across, down, out through the rooted back leg — the road he'd drawn from two weeks of secondhand accounts kept fitting everything his hands brought back, and at the low left side, where ribs handed off to hip, he thought he could feel the narrowing. The crossing. Or something that behaved enough like a crossing to bet an afternoon on, which was the only kind of true the circle sold. His body was learning the route — if it was a route — the way Hesk's notebook had promised his body learned everything. Your body will learn before your mind does. His mind was taking notes anyway. That was the partnership.

They separated — Darrow stepped back first, unhurried, granting the exchange its end the way a man grants a break in a conversation — and the yard exhaled and began to roar, and the first exchange was on the books.

"Nothing," someone shouted from the slates, gleeful. "The boy landed six and nothing!"

Eleven to one drifted to fifteen.

At the circle's edge, Lira's face was carved calm. She held up one hand, fingers spread: the crossing. Confirmed?

Cael, circling, gave her the smallest nod. Confirmed.

The second exchange, Cael started boring.

He didn't look bored — he couldn't afford to look like anything but a boy fighting for his life, and mostly that required no acting. Darrow had decided the demonstration phase was over and had begun, without hurry, to fight, and the difference was immediate and educational. The counter that had looked lazy in the first exchange came again at real speed and Cael took it glancing across the forearm, a bright shock of pain that would be a bruise by evening, the yard's first blood-taste of what the odds were pricing. The sweep came again, paired with a short straight blow that Cael read half a beat late and escaped by giving up the whole center of the circle. Darrow walked him down calmly, cutting the space, thoroughly professional, entirely unbothered, and began teaching a second lesson with the same patient grammar as the first: you cannot hurt me, and I can hurt you whenever I decide the moment has arrived.

And Cael, paying for every opening in coin he could not afford many of, hit the crossing.

Not dramatically. That was the discipline Lira had run him through until he could have wept with the tedium of it — no wind-up, no announcement, nothing that looked like strategy. A short strike to the low left ribs in the middle of a scrambling retreat, thrown as if in desperation. A checked kick that skimmed the same handspan of side while his arms were busy surviving. An elbow, ugly and close, same place, as he was shoved out of a clinch he should never have been in. Same place. Every time. Even when it was boring. Even when it cost him — and it cost him: taking the angle that reached the crossing meant declining better angles, safer ones, twice meant eating a blow he'd seen coming, riding it with his frame the way he'd learned in a hundred circuit nights, jaw rattling, staying in.

Six strikes across the exchange, one address.

The seventh time, Darrow noticed.

It wasn't a stumble or a flinch — nothing so generous. It was a flicker of attention, an audit: the pale eyes dropped once to his own left side and came back up, and something behind them that had been running the bout on habit sat forward slightly. When the exchange broke — Cael backpedaling out of range, lungs burning, arm throbbing — Darrow didn't press. He stood in the middle of the circle he now owned, rolled his left shoulder a fraction as if consulting it, and looked at Cael with the first genuine expression of the afternoon. Mild. Interested. The way a man is interested in weather that has done something out of season.

"You've picked a spot," Darrow said. His voice didn't carry to the crowd. It was meant for two people. "They do that, now and again. The clever ones." He shook his head, not unkindly. "It's a wall, son. They keep thinking there's a door in it somewhere. There's no door."

Cael said nothing, because talking was spending, and spent nothing.

But he had felt it — or the model had, and lent him the feeling. On the sixth strike, or maybe the fifth: a half-beat longer before the low left side went quiet again. The road, if he was right about the road, still carrying the last load when the next arrived. Nothing a watcher would see. Nothing Darrow's face admitted. Nothing he could have proven to anyone. Evidence, his head filed it anyway, and nudged the ruling a notch. A half-beat, low on the left side, in a body that had spent eight years never needing to think about whatever its own architecture actually was.

He counted in half-beats. It was most of what he had.

Fifteen to one. The errand boys' voices had gone hoarse. Somewhere in the third row the baker was explaining loudly to a stranger that the boy bought day-old loaves and always counted his change twice, as though this were evidence of something, which, Cael supposed, it was.

The third exchange was the one that nearly ended it, and it nearly ended it because Darrow stopped being patient with the wall's tax.

He didn't rush — Cael doubted the man had rushed anything in a decade — but the quality of his forward pressure changed, and the yard felt it before Cael's mind named it: the shift from a professional conducting a bout to a professional concluding one. The space Cael needed for his angles began disappearing in wide, calm slices. Darrow guarded the low left now — not obviously, nothing so flattering, but the elbow rode a little closer to the hip, the stance quarter-turned to take the crossing off the easiest lines, and every road to it now ran through somewhere Cael would be punished for going.

What followed was the longest span of the afternoon, and Cael spent almost all of it losing.

He survived the first press by giving ground he'd planned to give and then ground he hadn't. He survived the second because two weeks of Lira's Wind Path coaching had put escapes into his legs that assessed-Copper had no business owning. The third press caught him. A short straight blow through a gap he'd thought he'd closed — his guard soaking some of it, not enough, the force taking him off his line and the follow-up already arriving, a hooking blow to end the afternoon, and there was no angle left to buy with footwork alone—

—and his body took the other angle. The one that wasn't taught. The Wind-adjacent thing rose through him the way it had against Feryn's pressure-wave in the spring, not a technique but a permission, and he was suddenly moving along a line that hadn't been available, a hand's width of impossible clearance, the hook parting air where his skull had been committed to be.

The yard made a sound like a held breath breaking.

He hadn't wanted to show it. He'd budgeted the whole fight around not showing it — win plainly, win explicably, be a boy who watched well and worked hard, nothing worth a second look from anyone whose second looks were dangerous. He felt the cost of it land somewhere in the crowd's roar, in whatever was now being said at the slates. Nothing to be done. The alternative had been showing them a boy unconscious on the chalk.

Darrow paused. One breath's worth. The audit behind his eyes ran again, longer this time, and filed something new.

"Huh," he said.

Then he came on, and Cael stopped thinking about anything but half-beats.

He paid for the crossing twice more in that exchange. That was all the traffic Darrow's new geometry would sell him, and the price was terrible: the first bought with a blow to the shoulder that made his whole left arm ring, the second with a body shot he half-rode that still folded him briefly around its memory. But he felt the eighth strike land while — by his count, on his model — the road was still loaded, and the ninth — and on the ninth, for the first time, something under his knuckles gave back a sensation other than deep water. Not weakness. Congestion, the model said, offering him the word, and he took it because it fit what his hand had felt: a structure still carrying its last three loads, clearing slower, the half-beat now approaching a beat, low on the left side of a man who had never in eight professional years needed to know whether his own body had a rush hour. That was the reading. The fourth exchange would deliver the ruling, one way or the other.

They separated. The chalk under Cael's feet tilted faintly and steadied. His breath was expensive now, every one of it, and blood from somewhere small was ticking down the line of his jaw, and the crowd was a single continuous weather system, and through all of it he watched Darrow across the circle and saw the thing Lira had told him to fear in the third.

The professional had finished his audit. The number at the bottom said: end this.

There was no malice in it. That was the last and truest thing all those secondhand accounts had agreed on — the man didn't hate, didn't showboat, didn't punish. He concluded. Somewhere in the last nine strikes and one impossible evasion, Darrow Innes had reclassified Cael from an afternoon's exhibition into a problem with his name on it, and eight years of professional judgment now proposed the standard solution to problems: stop metering the fight, commit the whole account, and close it with one strike the boy could not survive answering.

Cael watched him settle into it. The stance deepened. The quiet left side came around square. The economy that had spent all afternoon receiving reorganized itself, plainly and without hurry, around sending — and the crowd saw it too, and the yard's roar sharpened into something with an edge of fright in it, and at the rope Lira's voice cut through everything, one word, his name, nothing else, because there was nothing else to coach. This was past the third. They had agreed not to plan here.

Vell had not moved. Her eyes were the only fast thing about her.

The fourth exchange began with Darrow crossing the circle like a tide.

Cael had perfect clarity. That was the terrible gift of it — no confusion, no blur, his fight-brain running clean and cold and fully lit, reading everything and mattering to none of it. He saw the feint that wasn't quite a feint, herding him left. He saw the second step close off the deep retreat. He saw the strike itself being born, down in the rooted legs, up through the hip, all of it, the whole committed account, eight years and a Bronze frame and every quiet exchange of the afternoon gathered into one blow whose only mercy was that it wasn't personal. He saw exactly where it would arrive and exactly when, and his mind — his good mind, his counting mind, the mind that had gotten him out of Denvash and through a dozen circuit bouts and past two Compact procedures — completed its arithmetic and returned the answer: not enough.

The Wind-adjacent angle was already rising through him. He'd stopped being able to prevent that days ago in drills; under real threat it came like flinching. And with the same cold clarity he could see it wasn't sufficient — the strike was too wide, too committed, thrown by a professional who had watched the impossible evasion once and priced it in. The clearance the Wind could buy him was a hand's width. He needed three.

What happened next, he did not decide.

He would go over it that night, and for many nights, and the honest account never changed: there was no moment of choosing. His body, already reaching along the Wind-adjacent line, reached further — past it, or under it, or through it, words all failed at the same place — and found the other thing, the Pressure-adjacent thing, Feryn's gift he'd only ever used alone and in fragments, and took hold of both at once.

They arrived together.

Not one then the other. Together — a single motion with two natures, the angle and the absorption, the escape and the receiving, folded into each other in a way he had never drilled, never imagined, never once produced in a hundred mornings of practice, and his body executed it with the fluency of something it had always known and simply never been asked.

He was inside the strike. Not clear of it — inside it, along it, the Wind-adjacent line threading him through the blow's committed geometry while the Pressure-adjacent thing opened and drank. Force came into him off Darrow's strike like a river finding a new channel — too much, far too much, more than the fragment alone had ever held — and some of it, some fraction his body metered by a logic he couldn't witness, turned. Reversed. Went back the way it had come — that was how it read from inside, the only account of it he would ever be able to give — through wrist and forearm and elbow and shoulder, back into the architecture that had generated it: Darrow's own force, arriving home along the road Cael had spent two weeks betting all of Darrow's force traveled, absorbed and redistributed into the one frame in the yard built on the principle that force must go somewhere.

For eight professional years, no one had ever sent it back.

The rest of the strike hit Cael.

Whatever his body had taken and turned, it hadn't taken all — the balance came through and landed, bone-deep, along his right side, a detonation of white that erased the crowd, the chalk, the count. He felt something in his ribs sound like a struck beam. The world tilted a half-degree and did not entirely tilt back; a wobble entered his vision at the edges, faint and liquid and wrong, like looking through the surface of disturbed water. Pain arrived a half-beat later, enormous, patient, moving in for a long stay. He was still on his feet. He didn't understand why. He didn't understand anything that had happened in the last half-second, and understanding was his whole method, and there was no time to mourn it, because in front of him, wrapped around the middle of his own returned strike, Darrow Innes had stopped.

Stopped. The committed frame arrested mid-conclusion, the striking arm fallen half-dead at his side, and on the professional face, for the first time in the afternoon — for the first time, Cael would have wagered, in years — an expression with no file to go in. Not pain, or not only pain. Disorientation — so Cael read it, with the piece of his mind that was somehow still running the model even while the rest of him rang, and he staked what remained of the afternoon on the reading: the bewilderment of a man whose own force had just come home along the road Cael had wagered it traveled, crowding into a crossing already carrying — if the whole afternoon's arithmetic held — nine strikes' worth of patient, boring, accumulated load.


Half a second. That was the whole gift — Cael could see it already ending, the professional recovery routines engaging, eight years reaching for balance.

A body must recover its balance before it can generate meaningful force. Lira had said that in their first week of drills as though it were the most obvious sentence in the world. On such sentences, everything.

Cael was already moving. That part he did choose — with his ribs screaming and his vision swimming at its edges and something vast and unexplained still ringing through him, he chose: not to fight free of the strangeness, not to give the moment back, but to spend it. Two steps. Inside the dead arm. The crossing stood open — square, unguarded, quarter-turn abandoned by the committed strike, every road to it clear for the first time all afternoon — and Cael put the tenth strike into it with everything Lira's two weeks had built: no power a slate would respect, no wrath, just placement, timing, and the whole afternoon's compound interest, arriving — if every reading he'd taken since the first exchange was true — at a structure carrying more load in that moment than it had ever been asked to carry in its life.

The sound it made was small.

Darrow went down the way a ledger closes. No stagger for the crowd, no toppling drama for the slates — his legs simply concluded, the way a column of figures concludes, and the man who had spent eight years as the circuit's proof that force must go somewhere sat down on the chalk, and then lay down, with the unhurried finality of a body that has been keeping accounts all afternoon and has just been shown the total. He tried to rise. Everyone in the yard watched him try — you could hear three hundred people not breathing — and the frame that had never in his professional life failed to answer gathered itself, and asked, and got silence. Not injury — nothing Cael's eye could name as injury. Depletion, the model called it, delivering its ruling right on time, and for once he let a ruling stand unwitnessed: a structure with nothing left, just then, to redistribute with. He lay back down, carefully, like a man setting down something borrowed, and breathed.

Vell crossed the circle and stood over him for the span of a long look. Whatever passed between the retired Bronze on her feet and the current Bronze on the chalk was brief and sufficient.

She straightened.

"The bout is called," Vell said, and her voice, unraised, reached the last row of the yard and the street beyond it. "Cael Hesk-ward. By incapacitation. Fourth exchange." She opened the ledger against her forearm, and her stylus moved, and the yard learned all over again that it knew how to make noise. "It goes in the book," she said, beneath the roar, to nobody, to the circuit itself, "as it happened."

What happened after that, Cael experienced in pieces, because the wobble at the edge of his vision had opinions now, and his ribs had claimed a whole hemisphere of his attention, and three hundred people were producing a single continuous sound that pressed on the yard like a second sky. The chalk circle filled. Somebody's hands steadied him — Lira's; he knew before he turned, the way he knew her footwork blind. The errand boys were fighting through the crush toward the slates, howling numbers that had just become either fortunes or ruins. Thirteen to one, at the close. Somewhere in the noise, Feryn was standing on a bench with his arms spread wide, conducting the crowd's disbelief like weather he'd personally ordered, shouting to anyone stationary enough to be shouted at that he had put money down at eleven and no, he would not be modest about it.

The baker was crying and pretending she wasn't. In the far corner, the old man had not left his bench and did not now — but as Cael's eyes crossed his through a gap in the crowd, the old man tipped his head once, a nod of exactly one degree, the largest gesture Cael had ever seen him spend.

"Sit," Lira was saying, "sit, you're gray—"

"I'm standing." It seemed important. Some cold tactical remnant at the back of his skull was still running the visibility arithmetic: they came to see it, so let them see it standing. "I'll sit after."

"You'll sit after," she agreed, in the tone of a person filing charges for later, and kept her shoulder under his arm anyway, and did not say the other thing, though he could feel it in her, gathering.

Across the circle, Darrow Innes sat up.

He did it in stages, with the deliberateness of a professional inventorying himself, and waved off — courteously — the several hands offering help. For a while he simply sat on the chalk, forearms on his knees, watching Cael across the trampled circle with an expression that had gone all the way through disbelief and come out somewhere with better light. Then he got up. Alone, under his own power, in stages, a man climbing his own frame like scaffolding — and the yard, which had been howling, dropped its voice for it, because the circuit understood exactly what it was watching. He stood, found his balance, tested the half-dead arm with two slow rotations, and crossed to Cael, and the crowd made a lane without being asked.

Up close, he looked older than he had at the mark and younger than he had all afternoon.

"Eight years," Darrow said. His voice was even. The evenness was costing him; that was visible now, and he was letting it be visible, which Cael understood was its own kind of payment. "Eight years, and I have been hit by every kind of thing a circuit can raise." He glanced down at his own striking arm, flexed the fingers, watched them answer slowly. "Nobody ever gave it back. Do you understand me? Whatever else is true about you — the wall has been taking it in for eight years, and tonight the wall got its own principle handed back up the road it came down, and I felt my own strike arrive in my own side." He shook his head, slow. "I know every way that isn't possible. I built my life on the list."

The pale eyes came up, and there was no professional filing in them now. Just the question, naked.

"What are you?"

The yard had gone quiet enough that the question stood in the air with room around it. Three hundred people, Cael thought distantly, were owed a lie sturdy enough to carry. He was so tired. His ribs moved like a hinge full of glass, and the wobble hung at the edge of everything, and inside him, where his method lived, where the notebook lived, where every answer he'd ever needed had eventually reported for duty, there was a shape he could not name doing something he could not describe, and it wore his own body like a coat it had always owned.

He gave Darrow the only thing in stock. The truth.

"I don't fully know yet."

Darrow looked at him a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, unprofessional breath of it, wincing as his side objected — and extended his working hand, and said, "No. I don't imagine you do." They shook, carefully, invalid to invalid. "When you find out," he said, turning to gather his kit, "the circuit will hear it before you've finished saying it. Nothing travels like a thing three hundred people watched and can't explain."

He walked out of the Cinder House yard the way he'd walked in, unhurried, on his own legs, through a crowd that touched his shoulder as he passed the way people touch a bridge that held.

Lira got Cael to the bench before his legs could file their own conclusion, and knelt in front of him, and began checking his side with two fingers and a frown, and for a moment her hands were all business, and then they weren't. They stopped. They rested flat against the bad ribs, very lightly, as if listening through them.

"That wasn't the plan," she said.

"No."

"The fourth exchange." Her voice was level in the way of a rope at full load. "I've watched every fight you've ever had in this city. I've sparred you every morning for months. I have seen the Wind thing, and I have seen the Pressure thing, and until this afternoon I had never once seen them at the same time — because it's never happened. Has it. That was the first time. Both of them. Together. Under a strike that would have put you in the ground."

"Yes," Cael said. And then, because she of all people was owed precision: "I didn't do it. It happened in me. There's a difference and I don't know what it is yet."

Lira breathed in, and held it the length of a decision, and let it go — and with it, at last, now that the bout was called and the yard was roaring about payouts and the strike had already missed him, she let the fear arrive. It came up through her face openly, undefended, the whole afternoon's worth, two weeks' worth, and she let him see every degree of it, because it finally had nowhere left to go.

"You were a hand's width from gone," she said. "I ran the line from the rope. The Wind angle alone wasn't going to clear it. I saw that before you did. I was already through the rope, Cael, I was two steps in with no plan at all — and then you were somewhere a body can't get to, and he was falling, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'm allowed to be frightened after. That's the arrangement I've made with myself. During, I coach. After, I get to be a person."

"It's a good arrangement."

"It's a terrible arrangement," she said. "It's the only one that works." Her hands were steady again, already re-becoming a coach's hands, pressing gently along the rib line while he hissed. "A week of bruising. Maybe more. You don't train until I say, and when we do—" and here she looked up at him, and under the fear, implacable, was the other thing, the thing that made her Lira: the interest, the appetite, the mind already circling the impossible like a fighter looking for the road in — "when we do, we are going to find out what that was. Slowly. On purpose. Where nobody's swinging at you."

"We can try," Cael said, and didn't say what he suspected even then, sitting in the roar of his own engineered visibility with his side full of glass: that trying was going to be like drilling for lightning.

They walked back to Torvin's in the last of the afternoon light, the long way, because the short way was packed with people who wanted to touch his shoulder like bridge-stone. It happened anyway, over and over — his name, in strangers' mouths, easy, familiar, as if they'd been saying it for years. The baker pressed tomorrow's loaf on Lira and wouldn't take coin. The errand boys shadowed them for two streets at a respectful distance, arguing about the fourth exchange in fierce whispers, getting all of it wrong, keeping all of it alive. From doorways and windows and the mouths of lanes, the Unranked District looked at Cael Hesk-ward, and marked him, and did the thing he had wagered everything it would do.

It noticed him.

He counted, because he was himself. Forty-one greetings between the Cinder House and Torvin's door. Three hundred in the yard. More at the gate, more on the slates' losses and winnings, more tomorrow when the story had been retold enough times to be wrong in the good directions. Every one of them was a person who would ask a question if he vanished — a thread, thin as thread was, but three hundred threads woven made the only armor left to a boy the registry wanted forgettable. Being seen is not the same as being safe, Hesk had written him. But it's not nothing.

It was done, in any case. Quiet had stopped being available around the time three hundred people watched a Bronze professional sit down on the chalk. Whether it would hold against what was actually coming — whether anything held against people who could put senior seals on documents — was tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight he had bought the thing he'd priced, and paid what it cost, and there would be time later to find out what he'd actually purchased.

In the room at Torvin's, by lamplight, he sat on the edge of the bed for a long time before undressing, because undressing was going to be an expedition. The bruising along his right side had come up in earnest — a deep, spreading dark under the skin, rib to hip, tender to breath, a week's tenant at least, his first real injury in a life that had been, he understood now, absurdly lucky by the standards of the trade he'd chosen. The wobble at the edge of his vision had faded through the evening but not entirely gone; when he turned his head fast, the lamp-flame left a faint liquid ghost. He noted it the way he noted everything. Duration: undetermined.

The notice came while he was reaching for the notebook.

It arrived the way the others had arrived — out of nowhere and from inside, printed on the dark behind his eyes, in the same spare hand as always:

FRAGMENT NOTICE
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. [unnamed] — Pressure-adjacent.
Concurrent function: recorded. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.

He sat with it a long time.

It told him nothing. That was the thing he kept arriving at, turning the words over: the system, whatever the system was, had watched the same half-second the whole yard watched, and its entire contribution was recorded — a clerk's word, an entry, the fact of the thing noted in a ledger he couldn't read, by a keeper he couldn't question, with no more explanation than the sky owed the street. Even the thing inside him only kept accounts. He almost laughed, and his ribs advised against it.

He opened the notebook to a clean page and did the work in the order the work went.

The tactical entry came easily, because it was his and he understood it: the crossing — claim made two weeks ago over Feryn's notes, ruling delivered tonight: it held, low left, rib-to-hip, in the one body he'd tested it on, whatever the truth of the road he'd drawn to find it; the futility weaponized exactly as Feryn's scouting said, survivable if priced in advance; accumulation over power vindicated in full — write it plainly: Lira's method won this fight; the third-exchange squeeze; the fourth-exchange commitment, readable three heartbeats out for anyone taught what committed legs look like. Costs: ribs, a week down, vision wobble, fading. Result: called in the fourth, incapacitation, recorded. Every line of it settled the fight further into the past, where it could be studied, which was where he kept everything.

Then he sat with his stylus over the page and tried to write down what had actually happened in the half-second that won it, and found that he couldn't.

Not wouldn't. Couldn't. He tried three openings and scratched all three out. It wasn't the Wind and then the Pressure — that was false, and the notebook was no place for false things. It wasn't a technique; techniques could be described to be repeated, and he could no more describe it than describe how he grew. He had reached for one thing and arrived holding both, and the how of it sat exactly where the light of his attention didn't reach, the way it had sat all evening, the way he suspected it would go on sitting no matter how many mornings he and Lira spent drilling for lightning. His body had known something all the way down. His mind had been informed afterward, like next of kin.

He wrote, finally, the only sentence that survived scratching out. It wasn't an explanation. It was a boundary marker — a fence around the thing he didn't understand, so that at least he'd always know exactly where his understanding stopped.

They're not separate things I switch between. They're one thing with parts I haven't found all of yet.

The Cinder House yard held more people than Cael had ever seen in it. He counted anyway, out of habit — the benches along the east wall full, three deep standing behind them, a second cluster by the water barrels that kept growing as word moved through the market that the Bronze bout was actually happening. Forty-one people when he stopped counting. The circuit's regulars were easy to pick out because they had claimed the sightlines early and were not talking. The people who had come for a spectacle were the ones making noise.

Vell had set her table at the circle's edge, the ledger open, her pen lying across it at the angle that meant the page was ready and she was waiting on the last signature. She had presided over every bout Cael had fought in this yard and she looked exactly as she always looked: unhurried, unimpressed, and paying attention to everything.

The dirt of the circle had been raked that morning. Cael noted the rake lines, the slight softness near the south arc where water had been thrown down to keep the dust low, the firmer footing everywhere else. Three weeks of preparation, and the part of him that had done the preparing was calm. The rest of him was fourteen and standing in front of forty-one people, and he catalogued that too, because pretending it wasn't there had never once helped him.

Feryn came through the yard gate talking over his shoulder to someone, easy, loose, a man arriving at work he was good at. He was tall without being built for show, and he moved the way the best circuit fighters moved — nothing wasted, nothing advertised. Then he saw who was standing in the circle, and he stopped walking.

"No," he said.

He said it to the yard in general, then found Vell's table and said it to her. "He's a child."

"He's fourteen," Vell said. "The bout was posted for any opponent holding an assessed circuit rating. He holds one. Assessed-Copper, four wins over his last six weeks, entered under witness with the fee met." She turned the ledger slightly, an offer, not a defense. "The terms are the terms you posted."

"I posted for interesting opponents." Feryn crossed to the table and read the page anyway, which Cael marked in his favor — a man who checked. "I didn't post for someone whose bones haven't finished."

"Then the terms were poorly drafted," Vell said, without heat. "The consent question is his, not yours and not mine. Ask him."

Feryn turned and looked at Cael properly for the first time. Up close his face was younger than his reputation, twenty-three or twenty-four, with the flattened calm of someone who had been hit by professionals and had stopped finding it novel. He didn't ask how old are you, which Cael had expected. He asked the better question.

"Do you understand what a Pressure Path does to the person standing in front of it?"

"I've spent three weeks studying exactly that," Cael said. "I want this fight. Nobody talked me into it — I asked Vell to enter me the day the bout was posted. I want to find out what the three weeks bought, and there's no way to find out from the benches."

The yard had gone quieter around them. Feryn held his eyes for a long moment, and Cael understood that he was being weighed — not for size, for something else. Whether the answer had been rehearsed. Whether someone behind him was pushing. Cael let him look. There was nothing behind him except Lira, somewhere in the crowd near the water barrels, and she had already said everything she meant to say on the walk over.

"All right," Feryn said at last. He said it like closing a door, done and decided. He turned back to Vell. "Note that I objected."

"Noted," Vell said, writing. "So is his answer."

They took their marks. The circle at the Cinder House was eleven paces across; Cael had paced it a dozen times on empty mornings. Vell stated the rules for the witnesses — to concession, to incapacity, or to her call, her call final, no-kill under circuit law — and the crowd noise fell away into the particular silence that Cael had learned to love from the inside, the silence of people about to watch a question get answered.

He rolled his shoulders once and set his guard, and watched.

Corvane's framework lived in his head as a drawing scratched in dirt two districts away: a stick figure, a spiral wound tight against its chest, and a tally of heartbeats. *Pressure isn't thrown,* the old instructor had said, tapping the spiral. *It's built. The building takes time and the body has to hold it somewhere while it builds. He'll hide the holding under movement. Everything that moves is the lie. The thing that doesn't move is the truth.*

On dirt, as a diagram, it had seemed almost simple.

Feryn came off his mark at a walk. That was the first wrongness — no rush, no probing jab, just a man strolling the arc of the circle with his hands half-raised, shoulders rolling loose the way a laborer loosens them at a start of a shift. He took a half-step left that looked like repositioning. His weight never visibly shifted. Nothing wound up. Cael was watching for the stillness under the movement and he could not find it, because all of it looked like stillness and all of it looked like movement, and while he was still sorting one from the other the air came down on him like a roof.

There was no impact point. That was what the benches could never have taught him. It wasn't a blow, it was a *lean* — the whole width of the air in front of him turning solid and shoving, everywhere at once, his guard not so much broken as folded, his own forearms driven back into his chest hard enough to take his breath out through his teeth. The ground swung up. He hit flat on his back with the crowd noise arriving distantly, like sound through water, and lay there doing the accounting: breath gone, ribs bright with a deep, ringing soreness where his arms had been driven into them, nothing structural. His lungs re-inflated in stages.

The count was at six when he rolled to a knee, and at eight when he stood.

Feryn had not pressed. He stood at mid-circle waiting, weight easy, watching Cael get up with the mild professional interest of a man confirming the afternoon would be short. Tour-weight, Cael thought. He's carrying tour-weight. This is him being polite.

*Release came four heartbeats after the shoulder roll,* Cael filed, walking back to his mark. *The roll wasn't the tell. The roll was the cover. Find what it was covering.*

They reset. This time Cael gave up trying to hold the whole picture and narrowed the way Corvane's drawing had actually instructed — not the shoulders, not the feet, the frame beneath them, the one line of the body that had to stay committed while everything else spent motion like small change. Feryn strolled. Rolled his shoulders. Took the half-step that repositioned nothing.

And under it, this time, Cael caught it — a settling, low through the trunk, the truth holding still beneath four moving lies, there and building. A fraction of a second, that was all the reading bought him. Nowhere near enough to get out of the way. Enough to choose an angle.

He didn't meet the wave. He turned his guard oblique, left forearm leading, and let the pressure shed across him instead of into him, the way a steep roof sheds snow. The lean arrived — enormous, patient, wrong in the same total way as before — and it drove him skidding back two full paces, boots plowing rake-lines out of the dirt, and it did not put him down. The shed force ran down his leading forearm and buried itself there, a deep ache blooming from wrist to elbow, the kind that promised to spread and stiffen and stay for days. He came to a stop still standing, arm already throbbing, breath still his.

The crowd made a sound he hadn't heard from a crowd before — not a cheer, an intake.

Feryn had stopped moving. He looked at Cael, then at the two long skid marks, then at Cael again, and something in the easy professional surface of him tilted.

"You read that," he said. Not a question. He said it aloud, to the yard, to Vell's ledger, to himself. "Three weeks, and you're actually reading me." He rolled his neck slowly, and when he set his feet again there was a fraction more attention in it. "Nobody reads the build. People read my feet for a year and never find the build."

*He talks when he recalibrates,* Cael filed. *Noted.*

The third exchange began the same way and was not the same. Cael had made his choice between resets, flexing his aching forearm and doing arithmetic: reading the build at release-minus-a-fraction had kept him upright and cost him an arm. The margin was too thin to spend twice. He needed the build at its beginning, before the cover motion even started — the decision to build, if such a thing could be seen. Corvane's framework didn't extend that far. Corvane's framework was a drawing in dirt, and the man in front of him was not.

Feryn strolled the arc. Cael tracked the frame, the trunk, the line — and there, early, absurdly early, before the shoulder roll, before the half-step, before any cover at all: the settling. Small as a held breath. The truth arriving ahead of its lies, and Cael's body was already moving.

He would try, afterward, to write down the order of what happened, and fail. It was not that he decided to step and stepped. It was that the step was already underway with his weight fully committed to it before the part of him that decided things had been consulted at all — a long, low, diagonal glide that his legs executed with a sureness he had never trained into them, familiar and not his, quick the way wind through a doorway is quick, and it carried him not back from the Pressure wave but *around* it, outside its whole line of release. The lean crashed through the place he had been standing. He felt the edge of it pass — a hard tug of solid air dragging at his sleeve, close enough to count as a touch — and then he was standing at an angle no evasion should have reached, balanced, guard up, facing Feryn's exposed flank.

The yard went silent. Genuinely silent — forty-one people and the water barrels and nobody breathing. Cael heard the dirt settle where the wave had plowed it. He heard Vell's pen stop.

He didn't take the flank. By the time the deciding part of him caught up to where his body was standing, the opening had already half-closed, and the honest accounting was that he had no idea what he had just done or whether it could be done twice. The step had come when the pressure came. It had never once come when he called it, three weeks of trying, alone, on empty mornings. It was not a tool. It was a reflex wearing his legs.

Feryn turned to face him fully. The silence stretched one heartbeat past comfortable, and then the crowd made up for the intake all at once, a wall of noise, and Feryn ignored every bit of it. He was looking at Cael the way Cael looked at fighters — the way you look at a thing that has just rewritten your notes.

Then he settled. That was the only word for it. The looseness went out of him, the strolling ease, the tour-weight, all of it set aside like a coat, and what stood in its place was a Bronze Rank 2 professional at working weight. "Last one, then," he said, and his voice was different too — no announcement in it, just intent.

Cael had a choice, and he made it walking back to his mark, with his forearm aching and his ribs sore and the crowd noise washing off him like weather. The safe line was to fall back, circle, protect the margin, make Feryn spend the fight chasing — lose long and look good doing it. He rejected it without ceremony. He had not asked for this bout to look good. He had asked for it to find out, and there was one thing left to find out: what his reading was worth against the real weight, at full commitment, with nothing held back on either side of the circle. He set his feet and closed.

The fourth exchange lasted four seconds.

Feryn came in fast, and the speed changed everything the way the benches had warned him speed always did. The build was still there — Cael caught it, he *caught* it, even compressed, even with the cover motions layered tighter — but catching it bought him almost nothing now, the fraction shaved to a sliver, the sliver to nearly nothing, his margin collapsing not because the reading failed but because reading was no longer enough. He read a release coming and moved to slip it, his own step this time, deliberate, trained, honest — and the reflex that had saved him in the third exchange stayed wherever it lived, unsummoned, because he was summoning it — and Feryn's hand arrived instead of the wave.

It closed on Cael's shoulder mid-motion, and Feryn simply agreed with him. That was how it felt — no collision, no wrenching counter, just Cael's own evasive momentum accepted, joined, and steered, the direction of his slip turned four unsurvivable degrees so that his speed became the weapon and the ground became the target. The yard rotated. He had time to understand that he had thrown himself.

And in the middle of it — falling, past all deciding — he registered the grip. The hand on his shoulder stayed. The redirect was already finished, his weight already committed past recovery, the mechanics of the throw complete and paid for, and Feryn's hand stayed on him anyway, one held beat, a full pressed second of contact where a glancing touch would have served, riding him halfway down before it let go. It meant nothing. It was a detail. He filed it the way he filed everything, because it was there and it didn't fit, and then the dirt hit him along his whole right side at once and drove the filing and the breath and the fight out of him together.

He lay in the raked dirt of the circle with the sky above him and conducted the assessment from far away. Everything hurt in the general way — shoulder, hip, ribs re-announcing themselves, the forearm ache now fully wrapped around the bone — and nothing hurt in the specific way that meant broken. His body answered the stand-up order with a counteroffer: *presently.* Not yet. He got an elbow under himself. The yard tilted.

"The match is called," Vell said, from very close by. She had left her table. Her voice carried the whole yard without effort. "Feryn, Bronze Rank Two, by incapacity. So entered."

Some part of Cael assembled the protest — *I can stand, give me the count, I can stand* — and the rest of him, the part that did the accounting, reviewed it and threw it out. It took him six more seconds to make his feet, and he made them swaying. The margin was gone and it wasn't coming back; the fourth exchange had proven that at any speed Feryn cared to use, reading alone put Cael in the dirt four seconds after the reset. A fifth exchange existed to injure him, not to inform him. Vell's call was not early. Vell's call was exact — she had ended the fight at the precise moment it stopped producing anything but damage, which was, he understood now, the entire art of her job.

He stood there swaying and made himself say it, because it was true and because the ledger should hear it: "Good call."

"I know," Vell said, already walking back to her table.

The crowd was loud and Cael let it stay far away. Feryn crossed the circle to him — and this too Cael marked, that the man came to him, at once, ahead of the well-wishers and the bettors, ahead of everything. He put out his hand. Cael took it with the arm that ached less, and Feryn pulled him the rest of the way to steady, one smooth pull, careful of him without making a display of the care.

"What tier are you?" Feryn asked.

The question everyone eventually asked, and Cael answered it the way he had decided, months ago, to always answer it — plainly, first, before it could be discovered sideways.

"[SHATTERED]," he said.

He watched for the flinch. He had a full catalogue of the ways people received the word — the step backward, the careful blankness, the too-quick nod, the eyes going to the exits. He had learned the taxonomy at gates and in registry offices and across boarding-house tables, and he watched Feryn's face for which entry it would be.

It was none of them. Feryn's eyebrows went up, and what arrived in his face was not fear, not even a well-hidden version of it. It was appetite. The pure, unguarded curiosity of a professional who has just been handed a problem outside his entire training.

"Huh," Feryn said. He looked at Cael for a moment longer — at the whole standing, swaying, fourteen-year-old fact of him. "I fought in Bronze evaluations for a year. Panels, months of it. I have never once seen anything I couldn't file." He shook his head slowly, and there was respect in it, the quiet kind, the kind nobody performs. "Third exchange. I know what I saw. I don't know what I fought."

"That makes two of us," Cael said, and Feryn laughed — surprised into it, short and real.

"Have dinner with me," he said. "I'm buying."

At her table, Vell looked up from the ledger with both eyebrows raised, which from Vell was an editorial. Feryn caught it. "What?"

"In eleven years," Vell said, returning to her page, "I have watched you win forty-some bouts in three cities and never once feed anybody afterward."

"In eleven years," Feryn said, "nobody's been interesting." He looked back at Cael. "Well?"

Lira had worked her way to the circle's edge by then, and Cael found her over Feryn's shoulder. She had her arms crossed and her mouth pressed flat on something that wasn't disappointment and was trying hard not to be visible pride, and when she saw him looking she gave him a single nod that meant *go — and I want every word of it later.* Cael's ribs hurt, his forearm was stiffening by the minute, and he had lost, decisively, in front of forty-one people.

"All right," he said. "I could eat."

---

The food-hall two streets over was loud enough for privacy, the way the district's eating places generally were. Feryn ordered like a man who had fought at working weight that day, and then ordered again for Cael when he judged — correctly — that Cael was underestimating what the fight had spent. Cael's forearm had settled into a deep, steady throb that made lifting the cup a two-stage negotiation, and his ribs commented on every laugh, which was inconvenient, because Feryn turned out to be worth laughing at. He told circuit stories the way Cael kept notes — exact, unsparing, mostly at his own expense. The one about his first Bronze evaluation panel cost Cael's ribs considerably.

But that wasn't why they were there, and they both knew it, and eventually Feryn set down his cup and got to it.

"The third exchange," he said. "I want to walk through it."

"All right."

"I've been releasing on people for six years. I know what every kind of evasion looks like. Trained slips, panic dives, Wind Path work — I've fought Wind practitioners, real ones, ranked ones." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, precise, the first thing he'd done all dinner that read as careful. "What you did wasn't a slip and it wasn't panic, and you're not declared on any Path or you'd have led with it. You moved before I released. Around the release, not away from it — like you knew the whole shape of where it was going to be." He looked up. "How."

And here was the question, arrived at last, the one Cael had been asked before by Lira in almost the same words, and he gave Feryn what he had given her, because it was all he had and because the man had earned the real answer.

"I don't know yet."

Feryn studied him. Cael let him. There was no performance to hold up under; it was just the truth, sitting on the table between them, unfinished.

"Yet," Feryn said.

"I keep records." Cael turned his own cup, once, with the arm that worked. "Everything I can't explain goes in the record, and everything in the record eventually gets explained or gets ruled out. That one's been in the record for a while. It happens when it happens — under pressure, real pressure. It has never once happened because I asked it to. Today included. I tried to ask it to, in the fourth exchange." He half-smiled. "You saw how that went."

He watched Feryn weigh the non-answer — watched him note it, turn it over, and decide, visibly, not to press. That registered too. Most people pressed. Pressing was what people did with the unexplained; they pressed or they backed away from it, and Cael had a catalogue for that as well. Feryn just nodded slowly, like a man leaving a good bout unfinished on purpose because the rematch would be worth more.

"When you figure it out," he said, "I want to know."

He said it the way he'd said *all right* in the circle — done and decided. Not a courtesy. An open account.

"You'll be somewhere I can find?"

"I'm always somewhere," Feryn said, which was circuit for yes.

He paid, as promised. At the door of the food-hall, out in the first of the evening cool, he stopped and looked at Cael a last time — the whole standing catalogue of him, bruises and all — and shook his head once more, the quiet respect again, unperformed.

"Fourteen," he said, to himself, to the street, and went off laughing quietly into the dark.

---

Cael walked back to Torvin's slowly, because slowly was the available speed.

In his room he went through the ritual the way he had after every bout since his first: sat on the cot, opened the notebook, and paid the fight into it before sleep could blur the edges. It took longer than usual. His writing arm was the aching arm, and there was more than usual to pay in.

He wrote the four exchanges as he always wrote exchanges — what happened, what he read, when he read it, what the reading cost and what it bought. The first, unread, the roof of air, flat on his back at a count of eight. The second, read at release-minus-a-fraction, the shed angle, the forearm's price for staying upright. The third he wrote and rewrote, and finally settled for the truth: *read the build before the cover motion — earliest read yet — and the step came. Came on its own, same as always. Fourth: read fine, margin gone anyway. Reading is necessary. Reading is not sufficient. Speed defeats a sliver.*

He wrote: *He didn't beat the framework. He outran it. Corvane's drawing holds. The drawing needs a faster student.*

He was flexing his forearm, considering whether the account was fully paid, when the notice came.

It arrived the way the first one had arrived, six weeks ago now — no sound, no light, no direction it came from. Just there, hanging in his awareness the way a remembered voice hangs, through whatever channel it was that owed him nothing and explained less.

FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Pressure-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.

Cael sat very still on the edge of the cot for a long time.

Pressure-adjacent. He read it until the words stopped rearranging themselves, and then he did what he did with everything, which was to lay it against the record and see what fit.

The Wind fragment had taken months. Months of mornings, watching Lira move, tracking her patterns until his body had quietly filed the shape of them — sustained watching, hundreds of hours of it, and even then the notice had come only after the shape had already surfaced once under pressure. That was the precedent. That was the only precedent. One fight — four exchanges, four seconds of the last one — was not months of watching. He had studied Pressure for three weeks, but from Corvane, from a drawing in dirt, and no notice had come from that. He had watched Feryn fight for the length of exactly one bout: his own. If watching were the mechanism, the arithmetic refused outright.

So it wasn't the watching.

He went back through the four exchanges again, slower, the way he'd walk a fight looking for the moment it turned, and stopped where some filing clerk in the back of his mind had already left a marker: the fall. The hand on his shoulder. The grip that stayed — the redirect finished, his weight already gone past recovery, the throw's mechanics complete, and the hand pressed on him anyway, one full held beat longer than anything about the throw required, riding him halfway to the ground. He had filed it because it didn't fit. It fit now. It might fit now.

He picked up the pen, and his hand was not entirely steady.

*Second fragment. Pressure-adjacent. Source: Feryn — I think. Not from watching. The watching doesn't add up: one bout on my side against months for Lira's. What doesn't fit anywhere else: in the fourth exchange his hand stayed on my shoulder longer than the throw needed. Held contact. Skin-to-shirt, one second, maybe less. If the transfer can ride contact, one second of touch might do what months of watching did.*

*Might. One data point. I said this exact thing about the first notice — one might be coincidence — and I am saying it again now, in writing, so I can't quietly forget to doubt it: I do not know that the contact did this. I know the watching doesn't add up and the contact is the thing that doesn't fit anywhere else. That is not the same as knowing.*

*But if it's real — if there are two doors and not one — then the Kestrel experiment needs rereading. Two weeks of watching him and nothing came of it, and I filed it as inconclusive: maybe watching takes months, maybe Force doesn't take, maybe I watched wrong. Add a fourth maybe now: maybe watching was never going to be enough there, and the whole experiment was standing at the wrong door. I don't know. I don't know which variable matters — time, contact, the person, the Path, something I haven't got a name for. Questions for the record: does contact always do this? Does duration matter? Did he have to be trying? He wasn't trying. He was throwing me. Whatever passed, passed through a hand that was only there to put me in the dirt.*

He sat back, careful of the ribs, and read the pages over, and found the account still unbalanced — because the record now held a thing the record had no column for, which was how the day had actually felt. He had been beaten in four exchanges by a professional who never stopped being one. He had been read, outpaced, thrown with his own speed, and put down in front of forty-one people, and every specific hurt of it was still on him: the ribs, the forearm, the full-body ache of the fall, the six seconds he couldn't stand.

He turned to a fresh line and paid the last of it in.

*Loss. Feryn, Bronze Rank 2, Pressure Path, by incapacity, Vell's call. The call was correct. Read him in three exchanges out of four. The framework holds. The step came once, on its own terms, same as always. Earned: the read, the respect of a professional, a standing invitation, and — maybe — a second door I didn't know was there. Paid: bruises and a fall. Three weeks bought more than I thought I was buying.*

*I don't think I've ever felt less like I lost anything.*

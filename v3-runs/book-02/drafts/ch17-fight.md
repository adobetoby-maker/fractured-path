Dace cleared the main floor's schedule by mid-afternoon, which told the district everything it needed to know before a single word went up on the notice board. The Ironyard scheduled around its economy — side alcoves for training, the main floor turning over paying bouts at a pace Dace managed the way other men managed freight. The floor did not sit empty on a working evening. It sat empty now, roped and swept, lit from all four practitioner-installed fixtures instead of the usual two, and the emptiness said more than any announcement Dace could have paid for.

Cael counted the crowd from the preparation alcove, because counting was what his attention did with waiting. Three deep at the rails by the half-hour mark. Four deep at the corners, where the sightlines were worth standing on a stranger's feet for. The bench along the east wall held six more people than it had been built for, and nobody on it was complaining. Lira and Brom had a place at the near rail, close enough that he could have spoken to them without raising his voice, and neither of them tried to speak to him, which was its own kind of understanding. The room smelled the way it always smelled — impact dust, old iron fittings, the mineral bite of stone worn smooth by thirty years of feet — but the sound was wrong for the hour. Too many voices, pitched too low. A crowd that talks quietly before a bout has already decided the bout matters.

Vell sat at the ledger table at the floor's edge with the current volume open in front of her and her own pen in her hand. She had keepers under her who recorded most evenings. She was not using them tonight.

Cael filed all of it under the same heading: the circuit had decided this fight was significant before anyone had thrown anything. What he could not file yet, because the information did not exist yet, was what the room would decide it meant afterward.

Dace came to the alcove entrance at the quarter-hour. He didn't come in. He stood in the gap with his hands loose at his sides and looked at Cael the way he looked at a scheduling problem that had resolved itself in an interesting way.

"Floor's yours until it's done," he said. "However long that takes. I moved everything."

"I noticed."

"I thought you might." Dace glanced once at the crowd, then back. "Keth asked for you specifically, when we set terms. You knew that."

"Yes."

"He's watched you fight four times that I know of." Dace's voice carried no warning in it, only inventory. "I'd assume more that I don't know of."

"I would too," Cael said, and Dace almost smiled, and left.

He walked out to the floor at Vell's signal and the sound of the room changed the way water changes when a current moves through it — not louder, exactly. Redirected. Keth was already at his mark, and Cael took the walk as a last chance to look at him with ordinary eyes, before the work started. Twenty-eight years old and built the way the Blade Path built its serious practitioners, which was to say not dramatically: lean through the shoulders, weight carried low, a practice blade of guild pattern held in the relaxed grip of a man who had earned the relaxation. Everything about Keth's body was information Cael already had. Four months of it. The most detailed entries the observation notebook held, and every one of them ending in the same fraction-of-a-second seam.

Vell stood. She did not raise her voice much. She never needed to.

"Assessment bout," she said. "Keth. Iron-equivalent, Blade Path. Cael. Copper-equivalent, no Path designation." She let the asymmetry sit in the open, because pretending it wasn't there would have been a different kind of dishonesty than her ledger permitted. "Outcome enters the record as witnessed. The no-kill rule holds. I stop it, it's stopped. Confirm."

"Confirmed," Keth said.

"Confirmed," Cael said.

"Then begin when you like," Vell said, and sat down, and picked up her pen.

Neither of them began immediately. That, too, was information. A younger fighter, or an angrier one, would have spent the first three seconds proving something. Keth stepped to the center at a walking pace, set his line, and waited — offering the opening move the way an experienced merchant offers the first price, expecting it to be declined.

Cael declined it. He came in at an angle instead, unhurried, and the bout started the way he had known it would start, which was with Keth being patient at him.

The first exchange was a survey. Keth's opening declaration ran through the blade the way it ran in every recorded bout Cael had watched — a low hum of commitment along the edge, the Blade Path's first form, position-holding, defensive geometry made slightly truer than geometry had any right to be. It wasn't an attack. It was a question: *what do you do under pressure that arrives slowly?* Keth advanced behind it in measured steps, closing the floor down a strip at a time, cutting off the lazy exits first.

Cael gave him movement and nothing else. A step outside the line. A drop beneath the first real cut, the blade passing through the space his shoulder had been leasing a half-second prior. The evasion architecture was there before he was — and he registered that fact even as he used it, filed it mid-motion in the part of his attention that never fully closed. He had not reached for the Wind-adjacent framework. He had not done the small deliberate interior turn that deploying it had always required, the reach he'd practiced until the reach itself was fast. There had been no reach. The pattern had simply been in his legs already, the way his own name was in his mouth without being summoned, and the cost of it was nothing he could detect. His breath came back level. His balance returned before the next cut needed it.

*First time*, he noted, and put the note away, because the exchange was still live.

He spent the exchange's remainder on the compound gaze, full attention, the way he'd budgeted: watch the architecture during the work, rest during the resets. Keth's declarations moved from primed to committed with the clean edges of a man whose training had been formal and whose repetitions numbered in the tens of thousands. First form to second form — the second declaration was the cutting line, offense proper, the edge's hum sharpening a register — and there, exactly where four months of notes said it lived, the seam. The transition from second declaration to third, the follow-through form, the re-declaration that let a Blade Path fighter chain committed cuts. For a fraction of a second between the two, the commitment locked the blade's angle and the stance could not adjust. Cael watched it open and close twice from the wrong distance, small and bright and useless, like a door glimpsed across a market square.

He didn't move toward it. Wrong range, wrong footing, and a window seen was not a window verified. He let the exchange end in Keth's favor on floor position — Keth had taken the center and held it — and stepped back to his mark, and deliberately let the compound gaze go slack, dropping to ordinary watching while the room breathed. The strain sat behind his eyes like the beginning of a headache that hadn't decided to happen yet. Sustainable. He was counting that too.

Keth studied him across the reset with an expression Cael had seen in the recordings of his mind a hundred times: the look of a technician revising an estimate.

"You're not going to come to me," Keth said. Not taunting. Confirming a measurement.

"No," Cael said.

"Hm," said Keth, and came to him.

The second exchange was the argument. Keth's patience had a working range, and calculated aggression lived inside it comfortably — he attacked in structured series now, second-declaration cuts arriving in threes and fours, each series a sentence with grammar, testing whether Cael's movement was a system or a collection of tricks. The crowd's sound picked up an edge. They had come to watch the unclassified fighter do the impossible thing, and what he was doing instead was retreating in clean angles and declining to be hit, and a crowd can tell the difference between a fighter who can't engage and one who won't, but it takes them longer than it should.

Cael worked the pulse for the first time in the bout. Close range only — the Iron-adjacent fragment was young and he knew its temper — a single sharp interrogation of the surface pressure coming off Keth's committed edge, then nothing, then another pulse three beats later. Never continuous. Continuous wasn't a thing it could do yet, and pretending otherwise mid-bout was how a young fragment taught you humility. The reads came back the way they'd come back in training: a smear of static with usable signal buried in it. The first pulse told him nothing he could hold. The second caught the second declaration's commitment a breath before the cut arrived — pressure gathering along the edge like weight shifting onto a cart's front axle — and that breath was the difference between evading well and evading early.

The third pulse returned pure noise, and it cost him.

He'd leaned a half-thought too far on the read, waiting for confirmation instead of trusting his eyes, and Keth's series turned inside its own rhythm — a shortened cut, off-tempo, a professional's answer to an opponent who was timing him. Cael got most of the way out. The blade's last handspan crossed his upper arm on the way past, a shallow line of contact, controlled — Keth's precision holding the no-kill margin without apparent effort even mid-adaptation. Heat, then sting, then nothing that mattered. The crowd made the sound crowds make when the pattern breaks.

Cael stepped out of range and did the accounting while his feet did the work. The cut was nothing. The information was not nothing: he now had the exact interval of Keth's shortened tempo, the variation he used when a rhythm was being read — and Keth had shown it to him in the second exchange, which meant Keth considered it disposable. Which meant the deep habits, the ones four months of notes were built on, were still underneath, unexamined, trusted by their owner the way a man trusts a floor.

*More useful than it cost*, he filed, and felt the truth of it, and kept moving.

The exchange went to Keth on any card anyone was keeping. Cael reset, rested the gaze again, and watched Lira at the rail refuse to look worried on his behalf, which she accomplished by looking furious at the ceiling instead.

The third exchange was the temptation. Keth had revised his estimate again — Cael could all but read the marginal notes — and the new strategy was bait. Keth began leaving things open. Small things, plausible things: a line a hair slow to close, a recovery a half-step wide. Invitations engineered to look like fatigue. It was well done. It would have worked on a fighter who wanted the opening to be real, and most fighters, by the third exchange in front of a full room, badly want the opening to be real. The crowd's restlessness had teeth now. Somebody at the far rail was audibly explaining to his neighbors what Cael should be doing.

And inside one of Keth's engineered invitations, the true seam opened a second time.

It arrived where it always arrived, second declaration into third, the locked angle, the stance that couldn't adjust — but half-shuttered, at the edge of Cael's reach, requiring a longer entry than his position honestly offered. He could make the range. He knew exactly what making it would take: the Pressure-adjacent fragment, opened at full deployment to carry him through the gap fast enough to matter. He had carried the fragment whole through the entire bout, integrated, tested in training, untested in this ring. It sat in reserve the way a sealed jar sits in a pack: weight you're glad of precisely because you haven't needed it.

He declined the window. Not because he doubted the fragment — because he doubted the window, at that range, exposed for that long, against a man whose disposable tricks had already grazed him once. A half-verified opportunity was a wager. He had not spent four months building a wager. He'd built a certainty, and the certainty had a shape, and this wasn't quite its shape.

The gaze was costing him now. He could feel it as a specific weight, the strain of watching architecture instead of motion, a pressure at the temples that had graduated from theoretical to present. He spent it anyway, in the disciplined stretches he'd budgeted, and rested it in the resets, and knew roughly what tomorrow's accounting would look like, and considered it acceptable.

Keth broke off the exchange himself, stepping back to his mark with the first visible flicker of something that wasn't calm. Not frustration exactly. Recalculation with a deadline on it. He rolled his shoulder once and looked at Cael down the length of the cleared floor.

"You're waiting for something specific," Keth said. The room was quiet enough to carry it.

"Yes," Cael said.

Keth nodded slowly, as though this confirmed a private hypothesis, and did not ask what. Asking would have been free, and both of them knew the answer would have cost too much to give.

The fourth exchange, Keth came to end it. Whatever he had recalculated, its conclusion was that a long fight favored the man reading him — so the aggression came real this time, unbaited, the full grammar of the Blade Path deployed by a practitioner at the peak of a decade's discipline. First form flowing into second, cutting lines arriving from angles Cael's notes called rare, series chained through the third declaration faster than at any point in four months of observation. It was, some cataloguing sliver of Cael observed, the best Keth had ever fought in front of him. The observation notebook would want that recorded. Later.

He survived it on the Wind-adjacent framework and nothing else — evasion after evasion arriving unsummoned, costing him breath he immediately got back, the fragment carrying his body through geometry his conscious planning had no time to authorize. He pulsed the read twice at the tightest ranges, quick interrogations, one pure static, one signal: pressure massing on the edge, second declaration, committed. He gave ground in a slow spiral and let Keth spend speed on him, and watched, gaze at full cost, the architecture underneath the assault — because the deep habits were still there. Faster now, compressed by urgency, but Keth's transitions were Keth's transitions, worn into him the way a tool wears a groove into the hand, and no fighter escapes his own grooves by hurrying along them.

Second declaration. Cut. Third. Chain. Second. Cut —

The seam opened.

His body was already inside it. That was the part he would take apart in the log that night and never fully reassemble: the commitment had happened a half-beat ahead of his conscious confirmation, his weight already through the entry step while the watching part of his mind was still stamping the verification — *locked angle, stance fixed, window true* — like a clerk certifying a shipment that had already left the yard. Four months of study had not given him a decision. It had given him something faster than a decision, a groove of his own, worn by watching.

He spent everything on it. The full remaining budget of the gaze, burned in one sustained close-range read of Keth's locked geometry. A last pulse of the Iron-adjacent fragment at a range where it could not miss — signal, clean for once, the edge's pressure pinned mid-transition, angle immovable for one more fraction of a second. The Wind-adjacent framework carried him through the gap where the Blade Path's third declaration needed the stance to be and the stance could not be. No reserve held back for a safer, smaller window later. There was no later in the plan. There had never been a later in the plan.

His strike took Keth at the shoulder joint of his blade arm, inside the locked angle, with his whole moving weight behind it — clean, blunt, decisive, precisely as hard as it needed to be and no harder. The blade's hum cut out. Keth went sideways and down, balance gone before recovery was possible, and Cael was standing over the seam he had waited four months and four exchanges to be standing in, not breathing hard, the sting in his arm the sum total of the evening's damage.

Keth hit the stone, rolled with it like the professional he was, and did not try to rise into a position the bout no longer contained. He lay flat a moment, looking at the ceiling. Then he raised one hand, palm open.

"Done," Keth said, to Vell.

"Match," Vell said, and the room came apart.

Cael heard the noise the way he heard weather — real, large, happening to the building rather than to him. Lira's voice was in it somewhere, and Brom's was not, because Brom would be watching still, taking the fight apart exchange by exchange, and there would be questions at dinner. Dace was already moving along the rails, managing the crowd's dispersal the way he managed everything, before it decided to become an event.

Keth got up without help, which mattered to him, and Cael let it matter. He collected his blade, checked its edge out of habit, and crossed to Cael through the noise as though the noise were furniture.

"Four months," Keth said. "Dace says four that he knows of."

"About that."

"The pause between my second and third declarations." He said it the way a man reads his own ledger and finds the arithmetic error that has been there for years. "That's what you were waiting on. I felt where the hit came through. That angle only exists for — " he moved his free hand slightly, feeling for the number — "almost nothing. A blink."

"A fraction of a second," Cael said. "It's in the transition. The commitment locks your angle before the third declaration frees your stance."

Keth stood with that. He did not ask *how do you know*, because the answer was standing in front of him with an observation notebook's worth of patience behind its eyes.

"You won because you knew something about me that I didn't know about myself," he said finally.

"Yes."

"Does that bother you?"

"No."

"It should," Keth said. There was no bitterness in it. There was, if anything, the particular generosity of a technician handing over the one tool he wished someone had handed him. "Because it works in both directions. Tonight there are more people in this room than have ever watched you fight at once, and every one of them saw you do it. You've just become worth studying, the way I was worth studying. Anyone with your kind of patience can build the same thing against you that you built against me." He glanced at the crowd, then back. "Most of them don't have your kind of patience. Someone will."

Cael took that in without answering it, because it did not need an answer. It needed a page in the log, and it would get one.

"Come find me sometime," Keth said. "Not for a rematch. I want to know what else is in that notebook about me." A pause, and then, dry as Vell at her driest: "I'd rather buy it than fight it again."

He inclined his head — to Cael, and then, precisely, to the ledger table — and walked off into the part of the crowd that wanted to console him and would find him entirely unconsoled, because he had lost the way he did everything else: correctly, and with complete information about why.

Vell was writing when Cael came to the table. He waited. One did not read over Vell's shoulder; one was shown, or one was not. She finished the line, considered it, and turned the volume around on the table so that it faced him.

*Iron-equivalent. Cael. No Path designation.*

The words sat on the page in her precise, unhurried hand, between an entry about a Copper-tier scheduling dispute and the blank space where tomorrow would go. Rating, name, and a designation that was not a designation — a categorical shrug, entered as fact, in the one book in Ardenmere where entered facts stayed facts.

"Iron-equivalent, witnessed, clean," Vell said. "The chain holds — Keth's rating traces to formal benchmarks, and you beat Keth with the whole district watching. Nobody disputes this entry. Nobody would know how." She looked at the line she had written, and Cael watched something rare cross her face: the expression of a keeper encountering a record with no precedent behind it. Forty years of ledgers, her own and her predecessor's and the older ones besides, and she was looking at her own handwriting the way she looked at the archive's strangest pages.

"First time I've written that," she said. "That exact combination. Iron-equivalent, no Path designation. Forty years of records in this building and it's never gone in the book before tonight."

"Does it matter?" Cael asked.

Vell capped her pen with the small, final click that ended her evenings, and considered the question with the seriousness she considered everything that touched her ledger.

"In this room? No," she said. "The record is the record. You fought, you won, it's witnessed, it's true. That's the whole of my law." She turned the volume back around to face her, squaring it against the table's edge. "Outside this room? Different question."

Cael had known it would be fast. Nothing. Most fighters carried a signature in their idle weight — a favored foot, a habit of the hands, some small preparatory ritual their body performed before committing that a patient observer could learn to bill them for. Cael had built two years of wins out of exactly those habits. Reydan stood the way a tool lay on a bench. Cael's observation notebook had a hundred entries on a hundred fighters and not one page that would have helped him here. The pressure-read was the page. Brom's nine days were the page. Reading me already, Cael thought. Good. Read this.

He gave Reydan the evasion framework, whole and clean. That's when I move. The third came high and outside, a line that only paid if Cael ducked rather than folded — he folded — and the fourth came a half-beat early, clipping the interval short to see whether Cael's timing was rhythm or perception. It was perception. The read tightened, the framework moved him, and the early burst found nothing but the torchlight where he'd been. Each miss was a question answered, and Cael was uncomfortably aware that he was answering them — that a fighter this deliberate wasted no ammunition, that every burst thrown was a datum bought, and that the price of surviving Reydan's questions was teaching him the shape of the answers. Standing inside it, the truth was uglier: there was no wind-up. He evaded all four. He threw nothing back.

"First exchange," Vell said, level, to the ledger. Her pen scratched once.

Auditing her own work under load, the way he would have.

He had read every burst in time, and Reydan knew it now — knew the timing was being read, even if he couldn't yet know how. That was a purchase, not a loss. But the exchange had gone to Reydan on the only ledger that mattered inside the circle: Cael had spent the whole of it being studied, and the man across the floor did not look like someone holding a puzzle. He looked like someone finishing one.

One read bought, Cael thought. Now make him throw it away.

Reydan came in again without any signal Cael could name — one moment settled, the next in motion, and the pressure-read tightened around Cael's awareness like a hand closing. Inward compression, right side, the same drain-pull signature as the second burst of the first exchange, and Lira's framework was already folding him leftward along the drilled line, four inches from exactly where he was —

The burst came from the same signature and a different line.

He understood it in the only way there was time to understand anything, which was afterward, in the quarter second while the world reorganized: the read had said again and the burst had said new. Same inward tightening, same weight, same timing — and released across an angle the first-exchange version had never used, cutting into the fold instead of behind it, into the seam. The seam Lira had found on day two of drilling and they had narrowed and narrowed and never fully closed, the coverage gap in the lateral fold's third beat, and Reydan had found it in one exchange and put a burst through it.

Cael got his forearm up.

That was the whole of what the four-tenths bought him — not evasion, just conversion. The burst that had been aimed to fold him up rib-first took his left forearm instead, and the impact went through the bone like a bell being struck, a flat white detonation of sensation that his mind refused to call pain for one merciful instant and then called pain. He was moved. Bodily — two steps, balance surrendered and clawed back, the crowd's sound spiking somewhere far away on the other side of the ringing.

His arm. He made the hand close. It closed — slow, half-numb from elbow to fingers, a deep wrongness in the bone that would have a color and a shape tomorrow. Not broken. He made the judgment with an observer's coldness even as he gave ground: functional, degraded, guard-capable, do not block with it again.

Reydan followed — of course he followed, a professional didn't leave a purchase like that on the table — and the next span of time was the worst of the fight so far, a compressed, ugly interval of survival where the framework did its work with a hole in it and Cael's read gave him timing and nothing else. He knew when. He no longer trusted where. The bursts came in twos now, paired releases with the second packing before the first had landed, and each pair asked the same question from a different direction: is the seam still open. Cael answered by refusing to use the fold that owned the seam — improvising around his own best technique, spending doubled steps where single folds should have lived, feeling the extra cost go on the ledger of his legs with every evasion that ran long. Twice he moved on the tell and guessed the geometry and guessed right. The third time he guessed wrong and only Reydan's own economy saved him: the man had thrown the burst to herd, not to finish, spending small because he was sure now, and the pressure grazed across Cael's shoulder blade like a hand shoving him toward the floor's edge.

Herding me. The circle's boundary rope swam at the edge of his vision. Forfeit by exit was still forfeit.

He planted, took the shortest line that wasn't the line Reydan was pricing, and got out of the funnel — not clean, not pretty, a scrambling diagonal burst of footwork with none of Lira's economy in it, and the crowd made the sound crowds make when a fight almost ends. His forearm throbbed against his ribs where he held it. Reydan let him go, disengaging with the same unhurried punctuation as before, and Cael read on his face the arithmetic reaching its sum.

"Second exchange," Vell said. The pen scratched.

Breathe. Count. Inventory.

The read fails on repeats, he told himself, forcing the finding into words while his lungs worked, because words were the only place he could keep it where panic wouldn't spend it. The signature carries timing and weight. It does not carry direction. He rehearses a tell and changes the angle behind it. That was the entry, filed. Brom's nine days had bought him warning that no one else in this room could have had, and Reydan had already found the boundary of it — had found it faster than Brom had found the gap in anything, faster than anyone had ever found a hole in anything of Cael's. Iron-tier, rank eight, said some distant clerical part of him. This is what the number means.

The crowd had gone loud and stayed loud. The betting murmur wasn't murmuring anymore; odds were being restated at conversational volume, and the conversational volume of a packed Ironyard was a roar. Two exchanges, both to Reydan — the first on study, the second on blood. Somewhere in the front tier a payout was already changing hands, some bettor cashing out of Cael's side of the book while the price was still worth taking.

Across the floor, Reydan rolled his right shoulder once, loose and easy. He was not gloating. He looked, if anything, mildly and professionally satisfied, a man whose evening was going according to a schedule he had drawn up himself. Two reads he had built and neither abandoned — no. Cael stopped the thought and rebuilt it, because precision here was worth more than comfort. The first read Reydan built, I broke by surviving it: the evasion pattern he mapped in exchange one had a seam, he priced the seam, and the seam only paid him once. A forearm was not the fold of ribs the burst had been priced for. Read one, spent. Read two — the herding funnel — I broke by leaving it. Also spent.

Two reads abandoned. His plan, written in the closed log in the other room, had three as the threshold. Force the third abandonment and the fourth response comes pattern-broken.

Which meant the third exchange had exactly one job, and the job was not surviving.

There was a cost column to fill in first, and he filled it honestly, because the plan deserved honest numbers. Going forward meant showing the amplification. It meant the whole room — the bettors, the regulars, the woman with the notebook, whoever else was recording tonight for whatever ledgers he couldn't see — watching an unclassified Iron-equivalent drive strikes that no unclassified anything should be driving. Every ability shown was information spent, and information, he had learned from Keth's mouth after the certification bout, worked both ways: anyone who studies you long enough can do the same thing. Tonight would give the studiers more than his last ten fights combined. He weighed it for exactly as long as the weighing deserved. The alternative was losing with his secrets intact, and a secret was not a thing he could stand on. The rating was the thing he could stand on, and the rating was across the circle, wearing the body of a man who had priced him three ways and been wrong three times and would not be wrong a fourth time for free.

Spend it, he decided. Spend all of it if the fight asks. Just don't spend it early.

Cael straightened out of the guard he'd been resting in, and let the held breath under his sternum go to work.

He crossed the circle first this time.

He watched it land on Reydan — the single blink of recalibration as the fighter who had spent two exchanges retreating came forward instead, and came forward wrong, no orthodox entry, the Wind-adjacent framework repurposed from escape into approach. Lira's angles ran in both directions; that had been her first lesson and her favorite, evasion is footwork, footwork doesn't care which way you're going. Cael folded through the space where a counter-burst pulled inward and fired — read, four-tenths, gone before it fired — and was suddenly inside the range where burst compression stopped being artillery and became a knife fight.

Everything happened close after that.

The pressure-read changed character at this range. It stopped being weather and became texture — Reydan's containment signature no longer a distant drain-pull but a graining of the air against Cael's skin, sharp-edged where the next burst was being packed tight, and Cael took the four-tenths and did something he had never once done in a real bout: he positioned into it. Not away. Into the space the burst would vacate, the dead angle behind its own release, the way you stepped inside a swinging door. The burst fired past his ear, deafening, harmless.

And his right hand was already moving with the Pressure-adjacent fragment run through it at full amplification.

He hit Reydan in the ribs, and it was like hitting a wall that grunted — the man's frame was harder under impact than any circuit fighter Cael had ever touched, conditioning stacked on conditioning, but the fragment drove the strike deeper than muscle had any right to, and Cael felt the impact go in. Felt it land as structure, not surface. The shock came back up his own knuckles and wrist in the same instant, a bright jarring cost, the fragment amplifying both directions of the contact the way it always did in the drills he'd logged and never yet paid for at full price.

Reydan turned with the hit — turned into it, riding the impact instead of absorbing it, converting Cael's own strike into rotation the way only a fighter with real conditioning could afford to — and fired a burst point-blank out of the turn. At this range the four-tenths was barely a warning at all; it was the difference between dead and moved, and Cael spent all of it being moved. The release scorched past close enough that the pressure of it dragged at his shirt. Then the framework again, four inches, exactly four inches, a fold inside the man's reach where bursts were awkward and elbows were not, and Reydan's elbow came and found Cael already under it, and the second amplified strike landed where the read said the man's balance had gone to, high on the shoulder joint, knuckles screaming now, and this time the crowd's roar arrived inside the same second as the impact instead of after it.

They were close enough that Cael could hear him breathe. That was its own information — the rhythm of it still disciplined, still counted, but with an edge of effort the first two exchanges hadn't carried. Reydan reset his feet in a quarter of the space a reset should have needed, no retreat in him at all, and for two long beats they traded position in the tight interior geometry where neither of them could fully commit — Cael denied the range to load a burst, Reydan denying the angles the framework wanted, both of them fencing with footwork alone while the crowd's noise stood at its peak like held weather.

Three hits. Landed clean, taken standing — the man was still up, still square, breathing harder now with a hitch on the right side where the ribs had taken structure. "Third exchange," said Vell, and even her cadence — measured, level, the same for a first-bout novice as for this — could not quite disguise that the pen scratched longer than usual.

Cael heard his own name in it, in more than one mouth, in that strange plural way a crowd said a name — not calling him, announcing him to itself. At the back, in the thinned light, the woman with the notebook had not looked up in the pause. Her head was bent. Her hand was moving fast, faster than in the earlier pauses, the pen making short decisive runs across the page like a fighter's footwork.

He filed it. Later.

Three reads spent, he counted, and made himself verify instead of hope. The evasion map: spent in exchange one. The herding funnel: spent in exchange two. And now the whole architecture of fighting a defensive opponent — every price Reydan had computed across three exchanges, every model with Cael retreating in it — spent, worthless, abandoned at the exact moment Cael came forward with amplification the man had never been shown. Three consecutive reads built, priced, and thrown away.

His plan said: the fourth response comes pattern-broken. That's when I move.

Pattern-broken did not tell him what it would look like. He had three candidate shapes for it, worked out in the preparation room and worth exactly what guesses were worth: a single maximum-commitment burst, everything spent on one unreadable line. A grapple-range conversion, abandoning compression entirely for whatever the man's base training was. Or volume — trading precision for rate, betting reserves against reserves. He didn't know which. That was the point of the plan and also its terror: he had spent three exchanges buying a fourth exchange he could not predict, on the theory that Reydan unpredicted was better than Reydan prepared. It had seemed sound in a quiet room. It seemed less sound with his hands wrecked and the framework fraying.

Legs — honest, still honest, Lira's drilling had gone into legs that were fifteen years old and had walked out of one life already, and they were the best thing he had left. The framework itself: fraying. He could feel the lag creeping into the folds, the four inches becoming five, the drilled economy leaking away one exchange at a time. Everything Lira had built him was holding, and holding was a bucket with a hole in it, and everyone in the room who knew fighting could see the water going down.

Reydan could see it too. That was fine. That was the plan working, if it was working, and pattern-broken did not mean weak, and whatever came next would be the strongest thing the man had, thrown without a read behind it.

Cael watched Reydan stop calculating. Watched him — there was no other word for it — decide.

The fourth exchange opened with a burst thrown at half power, and Cael understood the shape of the ending while he was still folding away from it, because the second burst was already packing before the first had finished spending itself against the floor.

Volume.

No read, no seam-hunting, no priced angles. Reydan had stopped trying to solve him and had elected instead to spend him — a sustained sequence, burst after burst after burst on a metronome beat, each one under full power and none of them needing to be otherwise, because the strategy wasn't any single burst. The strategy was the framework's hole in the bucket. Continuous evasion against continuous fire, and one of them was drawing on a rated Iron-tier practitioner's trained reserves and the other was drawing on two weeks of drilling and a fifteen-year-old's legs, and both of them knew the arithmetic, and the crowd knew it half a beat later, and the sound of the room changed into something with a countdown inside it.

Cael moved.

The first three bursts he evaded clean, the framework running on its residue, fold and fold and fold. The fourth he took the outer pressure of across his back like weather. The fifth he was late on — five inches, six — and the edge of it clipped his hip and turned him and the only reason the sixth didn't end the bout was that Lira's voice lived in his hamstrings: recover before you respond, balance before force, you are no use to anyone mid-stumble. He got his feet back under his weight. Fold. Burst. Fold.

The sequence did not pause between its runs. That was the cruelty of the design: Reydan wasn't throwing flurries with rests in them, he was throwing a climate, seven bursts and a breath and seven more, each individual release cheap enough that he could sustain the rate for longer than Cael wanted to compute, and the sum of them redrawing the circle's geography burst by burst. There was nowhere to reset. The floor Cael had used all night — the full width of it, the angles off the boundary rope, the deep lateral space the framework fed on — contracted around him as the metronome walked him backward and sideways and backward again, herding without needing to aim, the way weather didn't need to aim. He folded through the eighth and ninth and took the wash of the tenth across his ribs. His breath was going ragged in a way he could hear. Somewhere very far away the crowd had found the rhythm too and was riding it, a rising collective sound on every beat, five hundred people counting his bucket down.

And there — inside the exhaustion, inside the narrowing — Cael's observer, the part of him that took notes while the rest of him bled, saw the thing it had been waiting for.

The beat was a beat. Rhythm was pattern. Pattern was readable.

Reydan without his reads had defaulted to the most trained thing in him, and the most trained thing in him was regular. Every burst arrived on the same interval, the compression packing on the half-count like a smith's hammer coming down, and a man who kept time that honestly told you where the next blow lived. Cael could not outlast the sequence. But he did not need to outlast it. He needed one burst he knew everything about — timing from the read, direction from the rhythm's geometry, weight from the metronome's own discipline — and he needed to stop fleeing it.

The redirect. Drilled forty times in nine days: meet the burst on the oblique, take the compression through the frame on an angle that dispersed it — shed it through braced legs into the stone, let the floor eat what flesh couldn't. He and Brom had built it as a last-resort answer to exactly this, a volume sequence, a fight where evasion was a bucket running dry. It cost pain every time. It had worked, in drills, at drill intensities, against Brom's simulated bursts. He had never once fed it a real one.

There was no version of waiting that improved the odds. Every burst he spent evading was water out of the bucket, and the redirect needed legs with brace left in them, and the arithmetic on his legs was public knowledge by now. Do it while you can still stand into it, or don't do it at all.

Third burst of the next sequence, he decided, and the decision made everything quiet.

Fold — the first burst passed. Fold — the second, wider, his legs shouting, six inches now and the crowd seeing it, seven. The compression packed for the third burst, inward, tight, on the half-count exactly where the beat said it lived, and Cael did not fold.

He stepped into the oblique and met it.

The burst hit his raised frame like the world clapping shut. For the first instant the drill held — the angle true, the compression sliding into his structure and turning, the awful load of it routing down through hip and braced knee toward the stone the way forty repetitions had promised, disperse, shed, survive —

He would try, afterward — that night, and for many nights — to find the seam where it began, and fail. There was no seam. There was the drilled redirect running true, force pouring through him toward the floor, and then without any decision he could locate, the pouring gathered. The motion he had started completed itself along a line he had never drilled, never seen, never imagined as a line at all: the compression did not disperse into the stone. It collected — in his shoulder, through his shoulder, the joint suddenly a fulcrum for something no drill had ever asked of it — and turned, the whole borrowed weight of Reydan's own generated force wheeling around inside Cael's frame like water reversing in a pipe, and pointed itself back along the exact channel it had arrived by.

Back through the burst's origin.

Cael had a choice. That was the strange mercy of it — the completion offered, it did not compel, and inside a stretched instant he stood at the fork with the gathered force straining in his shoulder and every instinct he owned screaming to fight free of the thing he could not name, shed it, drop it, get away from what he did not understand. He had built his whole fighting life on the opposite of this moment. Nothing untested in a real bout. Nothing deployed without a logged range, a drilled response, a known cost. Every win he had ever taken had been taken on things he understood down to the grain — and the thing in his shoulder was not understood, was not his in any way he could account for, had no entry anywhere, and was asking him to bet the fight on it while it strained against the joint like something that would not offer twice. Pulling free was there. Pulling free would spill the gathered compression harmlessly and leave him standing in range of a metronome that had two more bursts in this sequence alone — standing on legs that had already told him their number, in a bout he would then lose slowly, correctly, with all his principles intact.

He drove forward instead.

Into range — not away, into, his knees screaming as he closed the distance the burst had crossed in the other direction, shoulder leading, and the contact when it came was not a strike in any sense his log had a column for. It was a delivery. Everything Reydan had packed into the third burst came back through the point of contact with Cael's shoulder behind it and Reydan's own frame in front of it, the force arriving home along the channel that had made it, and Cael felt the doubled impact go through both of them — through Reydan as concentration, through his own shoulder as cost.

The cost was enormous. The joint took what the redirect drill had promised the floor would take, and his arm went dead to the fingers, and the compounded recoil of two fighters' force meeting in one contact point tried very seriously to fold his legs.

He locked his knees.

It was the last decision of the fight and he made it on purpose, with the full knowledge of what it spent: no give, no roll, no graceful yielding to save the joints for tomorrow. He stood into the recoil like a post being driven, because the alternative was going down with Reydan, and a bout that ended with both fighters on the stone was not a win, and he had not paid the fight's whole price to split the ruling. His vision whited at the edges. His knees held.

Reydan went down.

Not thrown — nothing so clean. He went down the way a structure goes down when its load path fails, the burst's returned force having gone in through the same right side the ribs had already paid on, and Cael watched him hit the stone and watched him try, immediately, to answer it. He probed one careful step toward what had happened — the gathering, the turn, the pipe reversing — and stopped, deliberately, at the edge of it. There was no entry for it. Not in Lira's drilling, not in Brom's nine days, not in any column of any log he kept. Something had finished his motion for him, and he did not know what, and he did not know where it had come from, and he was not going to stand in the middle of the Ironyard and guess.

It exceeded everything I have, he thought, and made himself leave it at that, because that was the only sentence in reach that was entirely true.

He unlocked his knees, one at a time, carefully. They held. The crowd was still climbing, tier on tier of sound, no ceiling to it yet; Vell's ledger was open under her flat palms as if she were keeping the book itself from joining the noise; and at the very back of the room, in the thinned torchlight where the roar was presumably loudest of all, the well-dressed woman had not joined it either. She was watching him over her notebook with her pen still moving — still moving, writing without looking down, the way people wrote when they could not afford to take their eyes off the thing they were recording.

He didn't know what she was recording. He didn't know most of what had just happened. He stood spent in the center of the circle he had won, holding a dead arm and a nameless ache, and became aware that on the floor in front of him, Reydan had gone still in a way that wasn't defeat — a gathering stillness, deliberate, the stillness of a man assembling himself before doing something as difficult as looking up.

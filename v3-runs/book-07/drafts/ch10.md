# Chapter 10 — Two Hundred Meters

Karis had the cord out before the light was on the road.

He catalogued the camp as he sat up, because it was the first morning he had ever woken beside a line. The stone in its rut, two fists of it, dew on the top and none underneath. The three boxes on the Thornwater side of the stone, one, two, three, as Brom had set them down. The mule, hipshot, cropping the verge with its rope round a scrub root. Seln, sitting where he had sat down the evening before, facing the way they had come, who might have slept and might not. Lira with the ankle's tape already done. Oryn on her back on the road with her coat over her and her hands folded on the coat, awake, looking at the sky. And Karis at the stone with the cord in both hands, running it through her fingers knot by knot, as a woman counts money she has counted already.

"Twenty meters," she said, to him, because he was the one looking. "Knotted at the fives. I checked it against the rule at Lowmarch." She looked past the stone at the ridge and the bend, and at the bowl beyond, where the ring stood on its floor with no sun on it yet. In the morning the floor was only grey. "Pike said we'd know. We know. Now I want to know where."

That was what she was for. He had watched her be it on a bench above a gorge, and at a barn table with three boxes open, and he had never seen it aimed at anything this size.

The procedure, said once at the stone, in her method register, while Brom ate.

Lira inside. One stride inside and no more, walking the line's inner edge with her Arbiter gone; if it came back she had strayed out, and she would say *back*, and step in, and say *gone*. Karis on the line, a stride outside Lira, with her own sigil present, laying the cord end over end along the ground and counting cords, and strides within the cords, because she had a number for her stride she trusted. Brom outside Karis, marking: a stone set down at the end of every cord, or a scrub stem broken and turned, whatever the ground gave. Seln ahead, because the ground had not been walked. Oryn at the stone with the mule, because a mule could not be told to keep to a stride. Cael at Karis's shoulder with the book open, writing the counts as she said them, because his hand was the faster and hers would be full.

"And what am I in this," Lira said. "Exactly."

"The instrument."

Lira looked at the ridge, and at the ring under it, and at her own two boots, and nodded once, and went to the stone.

---

She stepped over it at the rut, one stride, and said, "Gone," in the voice she used for a distance.

They went left off the road, along the shoulder's foot, into scrub that came to the knee. Karis laid the first cord from the stone with its knots along the ground and said "One," and Brom set a stone at its end, and Cael wrote *one*, and she took up the cord and laid it again. It was slow. It was meant to be. A cord, a count, a stone; a cord, a count, a stone; and a stride inside them Lira walking with her hand a little out from her side, not raised, as a woman walks a plank she can see.

"Back," Lira said, at the fourth cord, and stopped, and stepped in, and said, "Gone."

Karis did not stop. She had her own sigil for the other edge, and Cael watched it work on her face: a small check every few strides, the eyes going to the place and coming away, the way a man crossing a river checks the far bank. Twice in the first ten cords she said "Gone," quietly, of herself, and moved a half-stride out, and said "Back," and went on. Between Lira's stride and hers the line lay somewhere, and it never once lay wider than the two of them.

The scrub at the shoulder's foot gave way to grass, and the grass to a stony run where the ridge's rubble had come down, and then to scrub again, and through all of it the line bent left. Not sharply. The same small amount every cord, so that Karis, laying the cord straight, found its far end a hand outside Lira's stride every time and brought it in, a hand, every time, and by the twentieth cord had stopped saying anything about it and was simply laying the cord with the bend already in her wrist.

He wrote the counts and catalogued the country.

Inside Lira's stride: scrub, grass, stone, no different from the scrub and grass and stone outside it. No wall. No ditch. No change in the green. The ring stood on its floor in the bowl's middle, further off on this side than it had been from the road, and then nearer as they came round the bowl's east side. Nothing on the ground said *here*. If Lira had not been walking it with her hand out he could not have found the line with both eyes and a day.

At the third cord past the road — sixty meters, Brom bending with a stone in his hand — Brom straightened without setting it down.

"There's iron here."

Everybody stopped. He had his boot on it: a bar the thickness of a thumb standing a hand's height out of the scrub's root-tangle, brown, with the bush grown round it so that a man would have had to be putting a stone down on that exact place to feel it.

"Mark it," Karis said. "Keep the count."

Brom set his stone beside it, and they went on, and Cael wrote *three; iron*, and the line bent left.

At the far side of the bowl, where the ring's floor was nearest to the line, Seln was waiting on a rise of ground with his back to them and his face to the ring, and he did not turn as they passed him, and fell in behind. Cael counted the uprights from there because it was the closest he had been. Eleven. He counted them again at the next cord from a different angle, because one count is a count. Eleven.

At the sixty-second cord Lira said, "Road," and there was the road under her boots, and the stone in its rut, and Oryn at it with the mule, sitting on the middle box, and the sun well past noon. Karis laid the sixty-second cord and the stone was not at its end. She laid the cord's remainder along the rut and walked it in strides, counting aloud, and stopped with her heel at the stone.

"Sixty-two cords and twenty strides," she said. "Twelve hundred and fifty-six meters, and a piece."

She stood a moment, looking at the stone, and then took the book from him, and wrote, and while she wrote she talked, not to them.

"It bent left the same amount every cord. Every one. I never had to take the bend out of my wrist and I never had to put more in. A shape that does that is a circle." The pen went on. "A circle whose edge is two hundred meters from its middle goes round in twelve hundred and fifty-seven, near enough. I make it twelve hundred and fifty-six and a piece, with a stride's slop in the piece." She looked up, once, at the ring in the bowl's middle. "So from this stone to the middle of that ring is ten cords. Not ten and a piece. Ten. I'll walk it going in." Then, as a fact about a shape and nothing else: "A circle is a made shape."

She did not follow it. She wrote the count, and closed the book, and gave it back.

---

The iron first, because it was sixty meters off and Seln had already gone to it.

He was on one knee beside it when they came, with Brom's stone moved aside and the scrub's roots broken back from the bar with his knife, and he did not look up. What stood out of the ground was a forearm's length of square iron, driven, the top struck flat by whatever had driven it. Below the scale of five winters the metal was sound; where his thumb had rubbed the scale off the top there were marks struck into it with punches, and Cael, who had been reading punched marks off the corners of registry paper since he was fourteen, read them upside down before Seln said them.

"Registry stamp," Seln said. "And a number. Thirty-one."

The stamp was the one on the corner of every printed sheet in Karis's middle box. He did not need to look. He had stood under it on the wall of every office he had ever stood in.

"Survey stake." Seln turned it with his thumb, as far as a driven stake turns, which is not at all. "You drive them where you've measured to. Not where you've measured from." He put the thumb against the scale and pressed and looked at what came off. "Five years in the weather. A winter either side."

Karis ran the cord from the stake's foot to the place Lira's stride had passed it. It did not take a knot. It did not take a stride. The iron stood on the line to the width of a boot.

Nobody said who. Karis wrote the number down and the word on the stamp and the estimate and the width of Lira's boot, in that order, and Cael wrote them in his own book after her, and put a line under *thirty-one*, because a thing with a number on it has been entered somewhere, and a stake has a list.

The contract in his satchel said *surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site*. This was not an instrument. It was the thing instruments left.

---

The trials at the stone, after they had eaten, with the sun going over toward the ridge and the road.

Seln's rule first, said once: inside only. If a declaration rendered on the far side of the stone they would know something, and if it did not they would know something else, and only the first would call the dogs. Nobody declared on the near side. Nobody had needed telling, but he told them.

Lira first. She stood at the rut with her toes against the stone, on the near side, and set as she set for a burst: the knees, the breath taken and held, the whole of her put into the stride before the stride. Then she stepped over.

Cael watched the air. There was nothing to watch. He watched her instead, and saw her go through the burst from the inside — the shape of it in the legs and the back and the shoulders, the release, all of it, correct, the thing he had studied on a training floor at fourteen until he could see it with his eyes shut — and at the end of it she had taken one ordinary step and was standing one stride inside the line with the breath she had held coming out of her.

"That's a step," she said. "That was the whole of it. I did everything. It's in me. It's not in the air." She tried it again, small, a foot's worth. A foot's worth of step. "Nothing."

"Out," said Karis.

She stepped out backward, and Cael saw the thing arrive in her, at the stride, as a thing arrives: the eyes going to the place and finding it there.

"Back." She stood a moment with her hand half-lifted, as if to check the sigil against a light. "It doesn't come on like a lamp. It's just there, and it's where it was, like a clock that stopped and started again without losing the time."

Karis wrote that down verbatim, and asked her to say it again, and she said it again, and it went down the same.

Brom, then. He stepped over. He declared. Cael, who had watched Brom's Iron Skin come up in a hundred yards and rooms and once across a river, watched for the stillness that came into him when it came up, and it did not come. Brom looked at his own forearm and turned it, the raised dark line from wrist to elbow that Oryn's hands had left, and held it out, and Lira, inside with him, rapped it with her knuckles as she would have rapped a table.

"Skin," Brom said.

"Skin," Lira said.

He stepped out and stood with his shoulders doing the thing they had done on the road, once, the door closing the other way, and said nothing else, because Brom did not describe a door twice.

Karis went in without being asked, and stood a stride inside with the book under her arm and her scarred right hand out in front of her, and Cael saw her see it: the whole lattice, the way she saw it before she lit one, the points laid out on the air in the order her hand would take them.

"Here," she said, and moved the hand a foot. "Here. And there, on that stone, and the fourth above it." She held the hand where the first point went. Nothing went into it. "I can see every point. I know the order. I can't put anything in them." She lowered the hand, and looked at it, and then said the thing he had not thought of and she had. "It costs nothing. There's nothing to spend." She stepped out. "Back."

Seln stepped over the stone as he stepped over anything, and stopped, and stood, and for the space of three breaths looked at nothing in particular. Then he walked in. Twenty paces, through the scrub at the shoulder's foot, and Cael, who was watching for it, who knew what the man's Path did and had a piece of it, lost him anyway for the length of a breath between two bushes that would not have hidden a dog, and found him again coming back, and could not have said how either had happened.

"Gone at the stride," Seln said, at the stone. "Back at the stride." He looked at Karis's pen. "I've a knife and fifteen years. It was never most of what I do."

He said it as a man says his own weight. Karis wrote it down as one.

---

Oryn had not moved from the middle box through any of it. When Karis looked at her she stood up.

"Brom," she said. "The arm."

He gave it to her. She took it in both hands, outside the line, on the road, the forearm between her palms with the dark line under her thumbs, and her eyes went to nothing, and Cael counted with her, because he knew the count and it went to ten.

"Iron Skin," she said at ten. "Dense. The mend's holding. Same arm it was." She had had her hands on that arm on a mountain and at a table, and the count had come up the same each time. She did not let go. "Walk," she said to Brom. "One stride. I'll come with you."

They stepped over the stone together, her hands on his arm, and Cael watched her face because there was nothing else to watch, and saw the count stop in it. Not end. Stop, the way a man's breath stops at a tape.

"Nothing. Not nothing in him. Nothing in my hands." Oryn had not moved her hands.

She stood one stride inside a line in a rut on a road with a man's arm between her palms, and her face did what he had seen it do over his own ribs on a mountain, once, and never over anyone else's: the instrument returning nothing. Then she said, "Back," and Brom stepped back with her, and at the stride her eyes did the thing Lira's had done, and Karis's, and Brom's — went to a place and found it.

"There," she said. "What she said. The clock."

She did it three times.

Nobody told her to. She walked Brom over the stone and back, hands on the arm through all of it, the count running outside and stopping inside at the same stride, three times, because she was a professional and a thing that had happened once was a story, and at the third *back* she let go of his arm and stood at the stone on the near side with her hands at her sides, which Cael had not seen her do at this line, and said the sentence the carters had been saying to her for two years.

"A healer's hands stop working there."

She said it as a finding, to nobody, at the size Pike said things. Karis wrote it. Cael put it on the shelf beside Pike's *it stops*, because it was the same sentence with the hands in it.

---

"Cael," Karis said.

He had been at her shoulder with the book since the light came, and outside the stone all day, and she had noticed, because Karis noticed the one who had not been tried. "You've not been over."

"No."

"Twenty in. Tell me at the stride."

He put the book in the satchel and went to the stone.

The nine were held to nothing. They had been held to nothing since the east gate, and on the road last night, and he had counted them held while he counted cords, in the part of him that did that without being asked. He stood on the near side of the stone and, before the stride and not after it, took the hold off one thing.

The Iron-adjacent read. The cheapest thing he owned, and the quietest; a read of the ground through the soles, weight and give and what lay under the surface, taken from a man who always knew what a floor was doing under him. It wanted the road as it wanted every road. He gave it the road. It ran: the rut's packed edge under his left heel, the loose gravel at the rut's middle under the ball of the right, the stone's own weight where it sat, two fists, bedded a finger deep. Nothing anyone could see. A man standing on a road.

He stepped over.

It ran. The rut's far edge, packed like the near one; the grass past the rut, and the give of the root-mat under it; a pebble under his right sole the size of a thumbnail. He looked at the place, because Karis had said *tell me*, and the place was dark, as it had been dark on the road going in, as it had been dark for four years. The read went on running underneath the dark as if there were no dark. There was no dark. There was a read, and ground.

He walked in.

He counted the twenty in strides because he counted strides, through scrub to the knee along the line Seln had walked, with the read running under him the whole way — grass, stone, root, a soft place where the rubble had come down and settled — and at twenty he stopped, with his back to the stone and five people at it, and there was a fist of ridge-stone in the grass in front of his right boot.

He took the hold off a second thing, small.

The Wind-adjacent framework, a foot's worth. Not a burst. The other end of it: the evasion, the sidestep the framework took before the foot decided to, the thing he had learned on a training floor by watching a girl who did not know she was teaching it. A foot's worth of it, silent, round a stone in the grass.

It went. His right foot was a foot to the left of where it had been going, and the stone was beside it and not under it, and the framework had done it, not the foot; he knew the difference in his own legs as he knew his own name. From behind, from twenty strides, it was a man stepping round a stone.

He stood there for the length of a breath with his back to them. Nine fragments, held to nothing again, and two of them tried, and both of them ordinary, on ground where every Path at the stone behind him was dead at the stride.

Then he turned round and walked out, twenty strides, and stepped over the stone, and Karis had the pen ready.

"Well."

"It was dark before," he said. "It's dark now."

Both halves were true. Put together they were not the truth, and he knew the difference, and let them stand. Karis wrote them. Lira was looking at her own hands. Brom was looking at his arm. Oryn was looking at the stone. Nobody looked at him longer than it took to write a sentence, because everybody at the stone had a thing of their own that had just stopped, and he was the one who had said nothing had.

He catalogued that, and found he had a reason not to say more, and that it was the same two reasons as last night, and did not look for a third.

---

Six over the stone, then, with the sun in the west and the ring's floor beginning to take it, and the mule left on the road with two boxes and its rope on the root, because Karis had asked for the middle box and Brom had it by strap.

Karis walked it in cords. Ten cords from the stone across the scrub and the grass and over the lip, and the tenth cord's last knot lay on the floor at its middle, with the eleven standing round it. She did not say anything. She looked at the knot, and at the stone on the road behind them, and wrote *ten* in the book, and underlined it, and that was the finding said.

He catalogued the site, because he loved it and that was how.

Eleven uprights. He counted them a third time from inside, walking the ring, with a hand on each: eleven, standing at their own heights, the tallest to his shoulder and the shortest to his knee, none whole, each broken clean across at a different place as if a different thing had broken each. Grey. The same grey as the floor they stood on, and not the grey of the ridge's stone, and not the grey of anything Karis had a chip of.

The floor. He walked it edge to edge, twenty-six strides along the road's line, and twenty-six across it, and it was round to the eye. Level to the eye; and Karis ran her cord taut from one upright's foot to another's across the middle and lay down on the stone with her cheek on it and sighted along the cord, and moved the cord, and sighted again, and there was no daylight under it anywhere. Seamless. He went down on his knees at the middle, where the breath stopped at the tape, and put both palms flat, and let the read run on the stone under them, privately, a man touching a floor: it gave him one piece of stone from his hands to the lip, no join, no seam, no laid edge, nothing under the surface but more of the surface. Fused. Not laid. He did not write that word. He wrote *no seam* and let the read say the rest to him alone. At the edge, all the way round, the floor rose the height of a hand and turned over, as the rim of a shallow basin turns, so that a man standing on the grass outside it stood at a lip.

Karis had the middle box open on the floor by then, with the chips and the rubbings laid out in a row: Thornwater's mortar, the ridge's stone, and the masonries she had chips of, carried out of two academies and a records hall. She laid each against the floor's grey in turn, and looked, and put it back in the box. Then she said, "Not local. Not any masonry I've got a piece of," and shut the lid.

The lines. He had not seen them from the road and could not have; they were shallow, the depth of a fingernail, the width of a knife's back, cut round each upright in bands, regular as a rule, and they were in every band the same kind of line and never the same line. Karis had a sheet on the tallest upright with charcoal going across it before Cael had finished counting the bands on the second, and she copied through the afternoon without speaking, sheet after sheet, and when he asked her what alphabet, she said, "None I own. And I own more than the registry prints," and went on rubbing.

He walked the grass inside the lip, and the scrub out from it toward the road, and Seln walked the rest of the bowl, for the contract's instruments; and what there was on this ground that a person had put down and left was a bar of iron at the line with a number on it. Nothing on the floor. Nothing in the grass. Nothing in the scrub between the floor and the stone, or between the floor and any other part of the circle, and Seln had walked every part of it. A hundred marks on a slip in his satchel, and nothing to carry back to the plank for it but the number thirty-one.

"They're not here," Karis said, from the upright, without turning.

"No."

"Then the number's what we've got." She meant the stake's. She went on rubbing.

Oryn sat on the lip with her hands in her lap and her eyes on them, and did not raise them, and did not read anyone, because there was nothing here to read with. Lira, who had watched Karis work for a while, went and stood in the middle of the floor and set for a burst, and did the step, and stood on the spot with the breath coming out of her, and did it again, and then walked off the floor and sat down by Oryn and said nothing. Brom moved the box where Karis pointed. The floor took the sun. It went from grey to white along its whole width at once, as it had the evening before, and the eleven uprights stood on it with their bands of lines catching the light, and the light began to go long.

---

Seln, at the far lip, on one knee.

He had walked round the floor's edge on the outside of the lip while the rest were on the floor, slowly, looking at the ground the way he looked at a road, and at the far side, where the grass came up to the lip and the lip stood a forearm proud of it, he went down and stayed down, and did not call. Cael saw him stay down and went; and then Brom; and then the rest.

Tracks, first. Many. The round grey-dust print he had seen in the ford's mud with his own blood on it, here in a bare patch of earth at the lip's foot, over and over, pressed into each other so that the earth was print and nothing else. Bones, past them, in the grass: long ones, cracked for the marrow, and a jaw, and something with the hair still on it. Scat, heavy, the dark of a day old and no more. And under the lip, where the fused stone stood out from the earth with nothing beneath it but earth, the earth had been dug back, and back, a hollow going in under the floor further than the light reached, its mouth worn as smooth as a doorstep by things going in and out of it low.

Seln had a thumb in one of the prints, testing the dust's edge.

"Fresh," he said. "Today's. A lot of them." He looked into the hollow, at the dark under the stone they had spent the afternoon standing on. "They live in here because nothing that walks in can fight."

Nobody answered him.

Cael watched them look, because he was the one who watched. Lira looked at the line: two hundred meters back, west, through scrub, where a two-fist stone sat in a rut with a mule beside it. Brom looked at the line. Karis, with charcoal on both hands, looked at the line. Seln, from his knee, looked at the line, and then at the sun, which was on the ridge above the road and going, and then at the line again, measuring it. Oryn looked at the line with her hands at her sides. Then every one of them, one after another, looked at Oryn, whose hands did not work in here, and Oryn looked at the sun.

And then, one after another, they looked at him.

He was not looking at the line. He had looked at it once, when Seln said *today's*, and it had been a stone in a rut two hundred meters away, and he had known what it was to every one of them and known that it was nothing to him, and had not needed to look at it again. He was looking at the mouth of the den, and at Seln's thumb in the print, and at the light going off the floor behind him, and when he felt the five of them look at him he took the book out and wrote, standing, in the plain hand, while Brom got the box onto his back and Seln stood up off his knee.

*Ninety-sixth day. The Fallow Ring, inside the line, at the far lip. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed. Eleven uprights. One stake. Eighteen.*

*The circle is perfect. The stakes are the registry's. The dogs are inside. Everyone's Path dies at the line and mine didn't, and I've told nobody, and I'm going to need to think about why I haven't. Not tonight. Tonight we're inside the one place on the continent where a stillhound can't lose.*

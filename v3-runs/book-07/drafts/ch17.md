# Chapter 17 — Priced Too Well

Lowmarch had gone to the first cold while they were east of it, and he catalogued the difference from the road's last rise, because he had catalogued the town from that rise three times now and wanted the fourth to be honest.

The river lower and darker. The ferry landing with half the bales it had carried in the autumn, and those under canvas. The meadow where the five of them had slept in the first weeks, empty, the grass gone to straw, two tent-rings in it where crews had been and were not. Smoke from every roof that had a chimney and from three that had only a hole. The board's room at the lower inn with its landing door shut against the wind and its road door open a hand's width for the smoke, and through the hand's width, a plank on two barrels, and a man at it with one arm, writing.

Five of them, on the hundred and twelfth day past the Line, with a mule and three boxes and a girl with her arm across her chest and a taped right foot she put down flat at every stride, coming down into a town that had priced them at the bottom of a wall a season ago and had been moving them up it a line at a time for their outcomes, which was the only honest way to move.

Oryn was on her route. She had gone out of Thornwater's gate at first light on the hundred and eleventh with the leather roll on her back and the mule's spare rope in her hand for the next hold's beast, north, on a road that bent as every road out here bent, and had not looked back, because a healer walking her circuit does not look back at a crew walking off it, and the crew had not needed her to. He had the seven stops in the order she had said them at a table, and the one after Thornwater was a ford called Millrace, on the Stair road, a day south of Lowmarch; by her count she would be at its table by tomorrow's dusk, and he had not said so to anyone, because nobody had asked and the count was the same for everyone.

---

Pike said the Fallow Ring's line before they had the door shut.

He said it to the book, in the voice he used for a price, without looking up, because the procedure was that the line was read aloud before and after entry, and he had entered it eleven days ago off a carter's word from Thornwater, and this was the after. "*Recovery, Fallow road, broker's posting. Taken. Witnessed. Completed: incomplete — instruments not recovered — crew returned. Six went in. Six came out. No price paid.*" The pen stopped. "That's said."

"That's said," Cael said, because somebody from the crew had to, and it had always been him.

"Costs you a line." Pike put two fingers flat on the ledger and turned it, with one push, so that it faced across the plank, which he had done once before and Cael had never forgotten the feel of a page turned toward him. Eight completed lines under the five names. The eighth said what he had just heard it say. "Incomplete's incomplete. Instruments not recovered's not recovered. The wall goes by outcome, and the outcome was you went and came back with nothing to show for a hundred marks, and I've moved you down for it, because if I don't move a crew down for that I've no business moving one up for the other thing." He took the fingers off the ledger. "Eleventh. Teague's ten up."

Nobody argued. It was arithmetic, and it was correct, and Cael had spent four years learning that a ledger that could not move you down was not a ledger.

"And," Pike said.

He did not say it to the book. He said it across the plank, to the five of them, in the same voice, and the same voice made it a different thing.

"I've kept this wall thirty years. Thirteen crews on it now, and I've had forty-odd across the years, and I've walked past the Fallow Ring two hundred times and never once stepped over its line, and I've never once had a crew on my sheet that did. Not once. Not twice, which is the number the road counts." He looked at the eighth line where it lay. "You went in. You came out. Six in and six out, and the sixth a healer, and the only thing in my book that's got room for that is the outcome column, and the outcome column says *incomplete*." He picked the pen up. "The wall's the wall. I've moved you down. I'm telling you what the wall can't say, because I keep it and I'd sooner you heard it from the man who keeps it than worked it out from the room."

The room had worked it out.

He catalogued it, because it was the first room in a season that had had anything to work out about them. The board's room at the lower inn at the end of the day, a dozen people in it under a low roof, crews and carters and the hide-woman with her ledger, and every one of them had gone quiet at Pike's *six went in* and stayed quiet through *six came out* and was now not looking at the five of them in the way a room does not look at a thing it has decided about. Not fear. He had had fear from rooms for four years and knew its weight. This was the thing the Concourse had done on the bluff when a name went up a board it should not have been on: the room updating, all at once, without a word, on an outcome it had watched happen and could not price.

He had the room's pieces, because he had catalogued them coming in: the hide-woman at the middle table with her ledger shut on her thumb, who had looked at the five of them in the first week as at a thing that would be gone by the snow; the crew of three from the autumn, new canvas gone grey, at the table by the road door; two carters at the fire with their backs to it, one of whom had walked the Thornwater road behind them in the first week and said *first crew I've hired that knew to shut up*; the boy who carried the ledger up from the landing when the light went off the water. Every one of them had heard *six went in*. Not one of them said anything, and the not-saying was the room's whole verdict, and it was a better one than the wall could give, because the wall had a column for it and the room did not.

Teague stood up.

He was at the best table with his three, and he stood up when the five of them came in off the road and did not sit down again until they had crossed to the plank, and it was not a greeting. He did not nod. He did not speak. He stood as a captain stands when the ledger is about to change and he means the room to see that he saw it first — the weight down through the heels, the shoulders low and square, the hands loose — and when Pike had said *eleventh* he sat down, and turned his shoulder to the fire, and that was the whole of it.

Cael filed it beside the eighth line, and found that the two of them together said more than either.

Pike rewrote the wall that evening, in full, in the one-armed hand, as he rewrote it every time a line moved: thirteen crews on one sheet, top to bottom, and the five names eleventh, one below where they had been, with the mule-named crew still thirteenth and a crew of three from the autumn twelfth, and Teague's four at the top where they had been for three years. He pinned it where it went, by the road door, and stood back from it the way he stood back from a price he had just said, and did not look at the five of them, because the sheet was the saying and the saying was for the room.

---

The posting was on the wall between the door and the plank, where Pike put the ones that had been read and returned by everyone.

He saw the seal before he saw the paper. Dark wax, a hook and a bar, in the corner — the landing broker's, the one on a third of the bales — and thin broker's stock under it, and a clerk's hand, even, schooled, the letters sitting on a line that had not been drawn. He read it from the doorway, upside down, and then took it off its nail and read it the right way up at the plank, and Karis read it over his shoulder, and Seln did not read it at all, because Seln had read it from the doorway with the rest of the wall and had already gone still in the way he went still at a shelf.

*Recovery. Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Stair road. South of Lowmarch; the first day; where the road drops off the ridge and the ground opens. Three hundred marks (three hundred).*

Then the seal, and nothing else. No crew's mark. No HOLD. No name beside a word.

"Same broker," Pike said, to the book. He had not been asked. "Same hand on it. Same kind of paper. Posted the day after the ferry came in — the day after your report went west, if you're counting, and I've watched that boy count since the first week, so I'd say he is." The pen went on. "That broker's had bales on my landing five years and never once posted a slip until the Fallow one. Now two. Nothing else in a year. Nothing else ever." He looked up, for the first time since they had come in. "I'd not take it. I said so to Teague when he read it, and he said he'd worked that out for himself, and put it back, and it's been on that nail nine days."

Three hundred. The Fallow one had been a hundred, dry, and had been the best number on the board by forty. This was three times that, for a site one day south instead of four days east, and it hung on a nail where every crew on the wall had read it and put it back.

Karis had her finger on *the Stair road*.

"That's the carter's second circle," she said. Her voice was nearly whole; it caught on *circle* and came back. "South, a day, where the road drops. It's on the map with a blank round it. It's a site."

"It's a site," Seln said.

He said it to the plank, and then he took the slip out of Cael's hand, gently, as he had once taken a case off a shelf, and laid it flat on the wood under the river stone, and put one finger on it, and gave them the chapter he had been carrying since the doorway.

---

"Registry prose." The finger on *abandoned at a site*. "Same as the first. *Site*, not *the Stair*; *abandoned*, not *left*; the price twice, figures and words. A clerk inside wrote this. The same clerk. I could put the two slips against each other and show you the hand, and Pike's already done it, because Pike reads paper."

"Pike reads paper," said Pike, to the book.

"Nothing else posted in a year. A broker doesn't post recovery contracts; he moves bales. He's posted two, both for instruments that were never at the site, both in prose he didn't write, both under his own seal, and the second the day after the first's report went west." The finger moved to the price. "Three hundred. Read it as a number and it's an incentive. Read it as a size and it's something else. A hundred was priced so that no crew who knew the road would take it, and one crew who didn't would. Three hundred is priced for *one* crew. The crew that went in once and came out. Nobody else on this wall will touch it at any price, and he knows that, because the report told him, and he's put a number on it that a crew of six with a mule and a hundred and eight in hand can't walk past. That's not a wage. That's a lure, cut to fit."

Lira said, "And the site."

"The site's the thing." Seln did not look at her. He looked at the slip. "He didn't post the Fallow Ring again. He could have; the instruments are still 'abandoned' there, by his own paper. He posted a different circle. One day south, close enough to Lowmarch that a crew walks in the morning and is inside the line by the afternoon, on a floor he *knows*." The finger came off the price. "He knows the floor. He stamped the stakes. He drew the holes in the maps. He knows that at that line, six practitioners walk in and five bodies and one anomaly walk on — five people with their Paths gone and one that the registry has never been able to read and that his paper calls *shattered*. He's read the report. He knows we walked into one of these and walked out. What he wants to know now is what we did inside, and he's built a place to find out, where the finding-out costs him nothing and us everything."

Nobody said anything. The room, which had been not-looking, had stopped pretending.

Seln said: "This isn't a contract. It's a pre-restriction inventory. They've mapped what we do inside, and they've priced a floor where we can't do it."

Karis said, "They don't know about Cael."

"They don't need to." Seln's voice did not change at all, which was how they knew he meant it. "They want the case. The boy's the price of the case."

He took his finger off the slip. The river stone sat on its corner. He had said the whole of what he had, in the order he had it, and it was a report, and nobody had asked for it, and he had given it anyway to five people at a plank, which was the second time in his life.

Pike wrote something in the book that was not a line, and put the pen down.

---

The council was at the corner table with the road door open its hand's width and the smoke going out through it, and it was the second honest collision of the year, and the leavers lost it, and lost it for the right reason.

Lira first, because Lira asked the honest question first.

"Walk away." She said it to the table, flat, the arm across her chest and the taped foot up on the bench where the two days had put it. "That's the honest one. It's a trap. Seln's just told us it's a trap, and he's told us what it's for, and it's for him and it's for you, and I've got one arm. We don't take it. We take the axe-head fence at forty and the autumn walk at fifty and we pace the carter's third circle on our own time when nobody's paid us to walk into it. There's no rule that says the site has to be on somebody else's slip."

"Yes," Brom said. It was the weight being named. "What she said. We've a hundred and eight and a mule. We don't need three hundred. We need the winter, and the winter's on the wall at forty and fifty and fed." He looked at the slip, under its stone, across the room. "Three hundred's a number a man doesn't reach for. My father said that about a horse once. He was right about the horse."

Karis, then, and she said it as she had said everything that week, as a finding.

"I don't care who posted it. I care that it's a site." She had the carter's map out already, the second blank under her thumb. "It's on the bearing from the Ring — near enough; I'll know when I've paced it. It's a day off. It's got stakes in it, because he's stamped them, which means it's been *measured*, which means when I measure it I'll have a second number to put against my first, and one number is a count and two is a fact. I would go alone. I'm saying so plainly so nobody has to guess. If the five of you take the fence, I take the map and a cord and a crossbow I don't know how to wind and go and pace the Long Stair by myself, and you can enter that in the ledger under whatever column you like."

"We know," Lira said. "That's why we're at this table and not already at the fence."

Seln had not sat down. He stood at the table's end with the case on his back and his hand on the chair he was not using, and said the thing none of them had expected, in the voice for an inventory.

"Take it."

Brom looked up.

"Take the contract." Seln did not raise his voice; he never had. "A trap you've read is the only instrument you'll ever have for reading the hand behind it. I've read this one. I know what it's for and how it's built and what it assumes. That's more than I've ever known about them. Six years I've been carrying a case for people I can't see, and the one time they've reached for it in the open, it's a slip on a nail with a price on it." He looked at the slip, across the room. "They'll send people. A lure's no use if nobody's waiting at the end of it. People have faces. Six years I've wanted a face."

"And if the face is armed," Lira said.

"Then I'll have seen it." Seln sat down. "That's the whole of my argument. I'm not going to make it twice."

Cael had listened to the four of them, because that was what the table was for and he had learned it at another one, and he had the count: two to walk, one to go regardless, one to go *because*. He had the decision too. He had had it since the plank, since Seln's finger came off the price, and he had let the table make its case first because a decision that arrives before the argument is a decision that has not been priced.

He said it to the whole table, and it was the sentence he had never said aloud to more than one person at a time.

"Inside the circle, I'm the only one who works. They chose a floor that makes the rest of you helpless. They don't know it doesn't do anything to me."

The table was quiet.

"We take it," he said. "Not for three hundred. Karis is right that it's a site and the pattern needs it, and Seln's right that a trap you've read is an instrument, and Lira and Brom are right that it's a trap. All of that's true and all of it's on the table. Here's the thing that isn't on theirs." He put his hand flat on the map, over the second blank. "Everything they've built, they've built on one assumption. That inside that line, six practitioners are five bodies and one anomaly, and that the anomaly's a boy the registry couldn't read and can't fight either. They stamped those stakes five years before they'd ever heard my name. They don't know what the floor does to me because *nothing* does anything to me on that floor, and there's no report on any desk in Valdris that says so, because the only six people who've seen it are five at this table and one on a road north. The trap's real. The floor's real. The assumption's wrong, and they can't know it's wrong, and I can." He took his hand off the map. "That's why."

Lira looked at him for a long time, and then at the arm across her chest, and then at Brom.

"Doors open both ways," Brom said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Rooke said it. On a stair, once, to a cohort, about a hall that had a door at each end and a habit of using one." Brom looked at the slip under its stone. "They've built a door we walk in by. It opens the other way too. He's saying he's the one who can open it." He put his big hands on the table. "I don't like it. I'm in."

"I'm in," Lira said. "I want it entered that I said walk away. I was right. We're going anyway."

"Entered," said Karis, writing.

He wrote it himself, at the table, in the plain hand, before the argument had finished being true, because a decision that was not written down at the table became, by morning, a thing that had always been obvious.

*Hundred and twelfth day. Lowmarch, the board's room, the corner table. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Nineteen. Ten. Eleventh on the wall; Teague's ten up. Oryn on her route; Millrace tomorrow, by her count.*

*Priced too well. Seln says it's for the case. I say the floor's for me and they don't know it. South, tomorrow. Lira's arm is two weeks old. Brom says doors open both ways. He's quoting Rooke. He's never quoted anyone.*

---

Pike marked the slip at the plank without being asked to, which he had never done.

He took it out from under the river stone and wrote HOLD across the top of it and the five names under, in the one-armed hand, and put it back on its nail, and said, to the book, "Taken," and then, to the room, in the voice that carried under the roof, "Stair road. Recovery. Three hundred. Taken by the five." He did not say what he thought of it. He had said that already, and once was the number.

Then, still to the room: "Teague."

Teague was already up.

He had watched the council from the best table without hearing a word of it — the corner table was across the room and the road door's draught took the sound out — and Cael had watched him watch, because it was the thing he did, and had seen the man read every face at the corner table in order, the way he read a job: Lira's, Brom's, Karis's bent over a map, Seln's when Seln sat down, and his own, when he put his hand on the blank. Teague had read the decision off the table before Karis wrote it. That was what a captain who had been top of a wall for three years could do, and Cael had priced it, and it was worth what it cost.

He came across the room, and stopped a stride and a half off, which was a distance two people out here used and neither of them had learned from the other, and did not look at the slip.

"You're taking the Stair contract."

"Yes," Cael said.

"It's a bad contract."

"Yes."

Teague looked at him a moment longer. Behind him the tall woman with the spear had not moved, and the broad man with the crossbow had shifted his weight off the limp, and the young one at the table's end had stopped eating, and none of that was for the room. It was for their captain, who was about to say a thing he had not said all year to a crew ten lines under him.

"My crew's at Millrace in two days," Teague said. "If you're late back, we'll come look."

It was not an offer. It was not a kindness. It was a term, stated by a man who kept his own ledger and had just put a line in it, and Cael recognized the register from the other side, because he had used it himself at a plank and at a table and in a hearing room, and knew what it cost to say a thing that way to somebody you had been pricing for a season. He filed it beside Pike's *six went in*, and beside a line in a book on a bluff that said *the instruments will not see its like*, and beside a one-armed man's *he'll speak to you when you've cost him something*, and found that the shelf was full and that the last thing on it was a captain at the top of a wall telling the crew at the bottom of it where he would be.

He had never once said the word to anyone eleven lines up, or ten. He said it now.

"Noted."

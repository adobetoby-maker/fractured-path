# Chapter 8 — Quiet Ground

Lowmarch from the shelf above the ford, on the ninety-first day past the Line, a week south of Oxhollow by the void road, catalogued because it was the third time and he wanted the third count.

The river lower than he had seen it, the brown gone to the colour of tea, the ford's poles standing clear to their second mark. The ferry on the near bank for once. Both inns with smoke. The smith working. And the meadow, which had turned over again and turned over further: forty tents and carts where there had been thirty-one, and the grass between them gone from green to the flat yellow it went at the end of summer, cut to nothing round the tent-pegs and standing to seed in the lanes; the ash by the road with a different carter under it now, one mule; and the look, from forty places at once, of people turning toward the road to see who was coming down it. Five, on foot, no cart. He watched the look go along the meadow and come back. It was not the look it had been on the sixtieth day. He filed that it was different, and that the difference had gone along a meadow faster than five people walked.

The calf said what the calf said on the shelf's last slope — nine, and then it was a leg — and the breath stopped at the tape a finger short of where it wanted to stop, as it had stopped for ten days. Lira came down the slope beside him in a carter's boot bought off a cart at Oxhollow for two marks, a size too big and laced hard over the tape, with the sock of the healer's linen inside it; she had walked a week in it and said nothing about it, which meant it would do. Brom had a box on his back by the strap and had had the same box for seven days, because the week the healer had given the arm was up. Seln had the second box. The third was on Karis's back, because she had said so.

---

The smith first.

He counted six marks onto the anvil's foot from the hundred and fourteen in the satchel, and the smith looked at the six, and at him, and at the five of them without a cart, and said, "Gorge?" and Cael said, "Gorge," and the smith took the marks and said, "It was the second-best," and went back to the fire. That was the price. It had been six marks against the cart coming back; the cart had not come back; here were the six. A hundred and eight in hand. He wrote the number in his head and did not take the log out for it.

Then the board.

Under its roof by the landing, the planks at head height, and Pike under the roof with it at the plank's landward end, on the two barrels, the stone on the page's edge, the sleeve pinned; and Pike looked at the five of them for the length of time it took to count five and to see that there was no cart, and went back to the line he was writing. That was Lowmarch's greeting, and Cael took it as one.

He read the board as he read a board. All of it, twice, then sorted.

Nineteen slips where there had been thirteen. The season was turning and the holds were turning with it; he could read the month off the paper before he read a word. Eight escorts, the ferry busy, grain going south ahead of the frost. Three fence-lines. A roof. A well on the north road, four marks, in Pike's hand for a carter who could not write. Three recoveries: the load off a bank, which had been there since the sixtieth day and would be there at the frost; a mule in a bog; and one he had never seen the like of on this board, on a broker's thin paper, which he read once and put back in its place in his head, for later, because the sorting was not done. Four hold contracts, priced high, good paper, brands burned in the corner. HOLD in Pike's hand across the top of every one. Beside the word, on two of them, the name he expected: Teague. The north road walked ahead of the autumn carts, sixty and fed, again. A hold's granary wanted watched through the threshing, forty.

Beside the word on the other two — five names, in Pike's hand, spelled the way they had said them on the ninth day.

He read them twice. Thornwater's brand in the corner of the first, an ear of grain with a line through it, and the job under it: the road from the steading down to the ford walked at dusk for a week before the carts, because two dogs had gone downstream in the summer and had not been accounted for, and the steading wanted the crew that had done the ford. Twenty-five marks and fed. The second was Oxhollow's, on a slip that had come south a day ahead of them, in a factor's hand with rope in it: the void road north walked ahead of the ford's carts before the frost, forty. HOLD, in Pike's pencil, and five names beside it, because a hold had come to the ferry and asked who, and Pike had said the top of the sheet, and the hold had said no — the crew that did the traverse.

He stood and looked at the two slips for longer than he needed to read them, because he had catalogued this board since the ninth day past the Line and it had never once had his name on the hirer's side of a HOLD. It had it twice. Nobody had asked what they were. A ford and a steading had asked what they had done, and had written the answer on good paper and burned a brand in the corner of it and sent it to a plank to wait for them. He let himself stand there with it. Then he filed it, and moved his eyes to the thin paper, which was the slip that had sorted itself.

A broker's slip. The thin paper brokers used everywhere, the clerk's hand, the price written twice, and in the corner, instead of a brand, the mark he had seen on the ferry's bales — a Lowmarch broker's mark, the landing's, the kind a man put on a bale he was paid to move and did not own. Ninety marks. Ninety, in words, underneath, in case the figures were doubted. For a recovery.

He read the words for what they were. *Recovery. Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Fallow road. Cased. Whole or in part. Site as follows.* And then the reference: the Fallow road taken east from the Thornwater fork; a distance in miles; the bend where the road turned north; the site a mile south of the bend, off the road. *Delivered to the landing. Ninety marks on delivery.*

Nobody's finger had moved it. He could see that from the paper; a slip that had been marked sat crooked to its pin, and this one sat square, and had sat square long enough for the sun under the roof's edge to find it and take the ink toward brown along one side. A week and more on this board, at ninety marks, in a meadow with forty crews in it, and not one finger. He knew what that was worth to know. He had learned it at fourteen from a board where the fights nobody would take were the fights with the arithmetic missing.

"That one," Karis said, behind him. She had read it over his shoulder; she had read it faster than he had, because she read for the one word she wanted and he read all of them. "Instruments. Surveyor's. Somebody surveyed a site."

"Somebody says so." He did not touch it. Seln, at his other side, had not touched it either, and Seln's finger had been on Oxhollow's slip before Cael had finished reading it; the finger stayed where it was now, on the plank's edge, and that was a finding too.

He took the slip off its pin and carried it to the plank's landward end and put it under the stone's edge in front of Pike, where a captain put a slip, and waited for the pen to reach the end of its line.

---

Pike read it silently first, as he read every slip, and then turned it to the light to look at the corner, and the mark in the corner was the landing's and told him nothing he had not known; and then he read it again, the second time, and Cael was watching his face because he watched faces at planks, and somewhere in the reference — not the road, not the fork; at the bend, Cael thought, or a mile south of it — the face stopped.

He had seen it do that once, on the forty-ninth day, over a slip that said eleven lost to the water. It had not changed then either. It had stopped moving, the way a man on a road stops moving when he has heard a thing in the scrub, and it stopped now, over ninety marks and a bend in a road, and held; and Cael counted it, because he counted the things he could not price, and got past ten before Pike set the slip down with the two fingers he used for slips and did not take the fingers off it.

"You'll not have read that one," Pike said. To the slip. "Not properly."

"Twice."

"The road, I mean." He turned the slip a quarter turn with the two fingers, so that the reference ran toward Cael, and put a fingertip on the bend. "Fallow road comes off the Thornwater road below the steading and goes east. It's a void road; there's four carts a year on it and they all go through. A day past the fork it bends north. Bends round. Every carter that ever drove it bends with it, and nobody's ever had to be told." The fingertip moved a nail's width south of the bend, onto the paper's white. "That's the Fallow Ring. That's your site."

Karis, at the plank's end, had the notebook open and the pen up and was not writing, which was the rarer thing.

"Quiet ground," Pike said.

He said it as he said a price. Not lower. Cael marked that he had expected it lower, and that it had come out at the plank's ordinary level, because Pike was not a man who kept two levels, and because the ledger was the only honest record out here and he kept it, and what he knew, he said.

"You've heard the words." It was not a question. He looked at Karis, once, and the look said he knew who had been asking carters' wives about roads at Thornwater in the summer, because the ford's carters came to this plank. "Everybody's heard the words. Here's what's under them, and it's all there is, so listen once." He set the pen down, which he did not do for a slip, and wiped it, and laid it beside the stone.

"There's ground out here where the sigil goes to sleep. Yours. Mine. Anybody's. You walk onto it and it stops, and you walk off it and it starts, and that's the whole of what it does, and nobody's ever got it to do anything else. There's a handful of places along the void roads where it happens. Not many. Enough." He turned the fingers on the slip. "Carters walk round them. The roads bend where they bend because a carter bent them, a long time back, and nobody's straightened one since. Stillhounds live in them. The dogs den in there, and the carters know it, and that's the other reason the road bends; and if you ask a carter why a dog would den in a place like that, he'll tell you it's because a dog's not a fool." A pause, not for effect; a man reaching the end of what he had. "Nobody knows why. Nobody's ever needed to. The rule is don't go in, and the rule works, and out here a rule that works is worth more than the reason for it."

He looked at the slip. He did not look at any of the five of them, and it was, Cael thought, so that he would not have to look at one of them in particular, and he could not have said which.

"I've walked past the Fallow Ring two hundred times. Never once stepped over the line. Thirty years on the roads and I've never met a practitioner who did it twice."

The landing's noise went on behind them: the ferry's rope, a mule, the meadow. Nobody at the plank said anything into it. Cael catalogued the five of them not saying it — Lira, with her weight gone still on the big boot; Brom, who had set the box down; Seln, whose finger had not moved; himself; and Karis, whose pen had — and then Karis said the thing, in the research voice, the question meant exactly and no wider:

"What's the line?"

Pike picked the pen up again. "You'll know. Your Arbiter tells you. It stops."

Then, because a slip was a slip and he was the board: "Teague had his fingers on that one two days back. Long enough to read it twice. Took them off and took the north road instead, and he's on it." He drew the slip out from under the stone's edge and held it across the plank without looking at who took it. "Nobody asked me that. I'm telling you because it's the ledger."

Cael took the slip. Nobody marked it. He put it in the satchel against the log, and the five of them went up out of the roof's shade into the meadow, and behind them the pen started again on the line it had been on.

---

The wall was the evening's business, but the map was the afternoon's, and Karis would not wait for the afternoon.

Pike's sheet lay on the plank's landward end under its second stone as it had lain on the forty-ninth day — the carter's map, the stroke down the left edge that nobody needed told the name of, the roads going out from the ford's dots in a plain firm line — and Karis went to it with the notebook and the pen as she went to a fire, and Pike, writing, let her. She found the Thornwater road with a finger and went east along it, past the square that was the steading, to where a second line came off it and ran on east by itself, and stopped, and copied.

Cael watched her copy it from the near side of the plank, because this was the first thing she had copied with the pen held as she held it now, hard, as she had held it the week the palms were cut. The Fallow road. The fork, drawn as a fork. The road running east from it in the carter's line, a day of it, and then the bend Pike had put his fingertip on — a road turning north for no reason the map gave, and turning back, and going on; and inside the bend, on the south side of it, where the road had gone round, a small closed shape that the carter had drawn and written nothing in. She copied that too, the size it was, and drew nothing inside it, because there was nothing inside it to draw, and put no word beside it, because the carter had not. He knew her well enough to know what that cost. She had a word for it in the middle box, underlined twice, on a page from a cart-yard. She left the page in the box.

"A mile south of the bend," she said, to the copy. "He put his finger a nail's width off the road. That's a mile on this sheet." She measured the nail's width with the pen's end against the days written along the Thornwater road, and wrote a number. "Two days from the landing to the fork. Thornwater's on the road, the second day." The pen stopped. He watched it stop, and watched her not look up. "The ninety-third."

Nobody said the rest of the sentence. It did not need saying; the healer had said it at a gate a week ago, and every one of them had counted. Karis wrote the number down and closed the book on it.

---

He catalogued the meadow at the day's end, because it was the last day of a thing and he wanted its count.

Late summer, turning. The grass in the lanes gone to seed and the seed gone; the light coming in low and yellow across the tents from over the river, so that every rope threw a line of shadow twice its length. Carters mending. A mule being shod at the smith's, the ring of it coming across the meadow late, twice, once off the inn. Forty crews and carts, and the five of them among them with three boxes and no cart, and the look that had gone along the meadow that morning had settled into what it was, which was people not looking, in the particular way of people who had already looked and had what they wanted. Priced. It was on a plank by the landing in two hands that were not Pike's, and the meadow read the plank.

He let it be what it was. Since the ninth day past the Line he had been a heading with no column in it for what he was, and for the length of a summer that had been enough; he had written *free* in the log and meant it. Today a ford and a steading had written his name on the hirer's side of a slip, and nobody in the meadow had asked what any of them were; and he stood in the yellow light with a taped side and let himself have the whole of it, once, as a count: two HOLDs with five names beside them, and a broker's slip in the satchel that nobody would touch. That was the day. He did not put a verb between the two halves of it yet.

---

The board's room, with the light gone off the water.

Pike carried the book up from the landing under his arm and set it on the table by the fire and put the stone on it, and the room did what it did. Seven tables now; a trestle had come in from somewhere for the season. The carters at the two by the road door, and a third table of them. The best table by the fire with its four chairs pushed in and the jug on it full, because the north road was sixty and fed and Teague's crew was on it. Two crews he did not know. And the five of them at the table by the road door, because Brom could see both doors from it.

Pike read the traverse in before anything else. He turned the book back a page to the heading with the five names, and put two fingers on the slip that had come south from Oxhollow with a factor's mark on it, and read it aloud, to the room, once, so that the crew could say it was wrong:

"High traverse, Oxhollow to Thornwater. Mapped. Nine narrowings and the ledge, widths given. Does not hold a cart. Delivered at Oxhollow. Crew of five, none lost. Seventy, paid."

Nobody said it was wrong. It was not wrong; every word of it was a thing that had happened on a mountain, and the fourth column took the whole of it in the same ink as the ford. Then Pike wrote, in the margin, smaller, as he had written *crew says the bank went* on the forty-ninth day, and read that aloud too, at the same level: "Cart lost on the bench. Smith's. Six, paid." And set the pen down, which was the end of it, and looked across the room at the five of them for the first time since the plank.

"Map's a line," he said. "Nobody lost is the line I count. Cart's a line back. You'd have gone up three tonight. You go up two."

"Yes," Cael said.

It was fair. He turned it over once to be certain, and it was fair on every side he turned it to: a hold had paid seventy for a map and got one; a smith had been owed six for a cart and got them; five had gone up and five had come down, and the cart had not, and the ledger said all four of those things in the order they weighed. Teague had ruled the same at Oxhollow's ford, and the two men had never needed to speak to each other to agree. That was what a ledger was. Vell had shown him so at fourteen, and here it was for the third time in his life, and the third keeper had one arm and no institution above him at all.

Pike took the sheet down off its pegs one-handed and rewrote it, and pinned it back, and the five names were tenth of fourteen, and Teague's crew was first, and there were eight lines between; and Pike said, "That's said," to the book, and did not look up again.

---

The council was five, because it was always five, and the slip lay on the table under the jug's ring with the broker's mark up.

"It pays because nobody'll take it," Lira said. She said it first because it was the first thing, as she said the first thing at any fire: a fact for checking. "Ninety, for a walk and a case. That's not what the job's worth. That's what a week on the board with no finger on it is worth."

"Teague read it," Brom said.

"Teague read it and took forty and a granary." Lira did not look at the best table. "That's the second thing. He'd have taken this if it was a thing to take. It's ninety."

"It's ninety," Cael said, "because it's a mile south of a bend." He had the reference by heart now. "The instruments are worth the contract on their own. Surveyor's kit, cased — Karis has a folding rule in a box that cost more than a fence-line. A case of them is ninety marks before the walk. The walk's two days east and two back, on a road with a hold on it that's fed us twice." He laid it out as he had laid out a bout on the Cinder House board, the arithmetic first, because the arithmetic was true and was not the reason. "It pays."

Seln had the slip. He had picked it up while Lira was talking and had turned it over and back, and was reading it as he read a road — not the words; he had the words; the shape the words made on the paper, and the hand, and the mark — and he set it down square to the table's edge and put one finger on the third line.

"Abandoned at a site," he said.

They waited. He was looking at the line, and Cael had learned in a records room four years ago that a man who looked at a line that long had found something in it he had not been sent for.

"A carter says *left*. Or *lost*. A hold says *where* — it'd say at the bend, by the road, under the bank; a hold knows the place and says it. *At a site* is what a form says when the form's not going to tell you the place, because the place is a number somewhere else." He took the finger off it. "That's the registry. Fifteen years of it. Every recovery I ever read was abandoned at a site." He looked at the mark in the corner. "The mark's Lowmarch. The hand's a clerk's, and clerks are anywhere there's a landing. The words aren't from here. Somebody wrote this who learned to write inside."

Karis had the pen up. "Who?"

"I don't know." Flat; a man reporting the edge of what he had. "I know how the sentence was built. I don't know whose. That's all it is."

"It goes in," Karis said, and wrote it, and it went in, under the reference and above the ninety, in her hand, a finding with no verb after it. Cael watched her leave the verb off. He would leave it off in the log as well, and knew it while he watched.

"So," Lira said. "Somebody inside wants a case of instruments off a piece of ground Pike won't stand on, and they'll pay ninety to a crew that will, and the best crew on the board read it twice and walked away." She counted it on the table with two fingers, as she counted an opponent's weight. "That's three reasons not to. I'm saying them so they're said."

"Two days east," Brom said. "No cart. A case carries." He had been to the plank and back while they read the board. "Pike says a case is a case. Strap and a back. That's me." He looked at Karis, once, without any weight in it at all, and Cael saw the sentence Brom had not said and that everyone at the table had heard; it was in the boxes he had carried up a mountain and off a cart and down again.

Karis did not say anything. She did not have to. She had stood at a plank with the pen up and not writing while a one-armed man told her, at the level of a price, that there was ground two days east where the thing every practitioner on the continent carried went to sleep, and that nobody knew why. Cael had watched it reach her. He had watched a rumor from a cart-yard, underlined twice and left in a box for a summer because there was nothing under it a question could reach, turn into a road and a bend and a nail's width of white on a carter's map with a name on it, and he knew the look on her from three academies: a woman who has found the primary source and is deciding, already, that she will go and stand in it. If they did not take the slip she would take it. She would take it alone, with a folding rule and a cord, and walk two days east and stand at whatever a line was. Every one of the four of them knew it, and the fifth knew they knew, and nobody said it.

"There's a healer on that road," Lira said.

Cael had been waiting for it, and it was Lira who said it. "Two days east is Thornwater on the second day. She said Thornwater in nine days, at Oxhollow. Karis counted it at the plank. The ninety-third." She let it lie. "She's walked that road two years. Her route's the void road; she's walked past that bend. She's never been over any line, or Pike would have met one who did it once."

"Inference," Seln said.

"Inference. Wants checking." Lira looked at Cael then. "She wants to read you whole. She said so at a gate."

Nobody said the next thing. He catalogued them not saying it, all four, and himself fifth, and it was not that anyone had decided against it; it was that the sentence was not theirs. She had seven holds and a child three stops on. She had told them at a fire what she owned. A crew did not send for a woman who had added them to a route for four days and walked north at the end of it without looking back. They would be on the road she walked, on the day she had said. That was as far as anyone at this table had a right to go, and they went that far, and stopped.

Karis wrote it down. *Thornwater, the ninety-third; the healer's day.* He read it upside down, and she let him.

"Then it's this," he said. "It pays. That's the first thing, and I'm not pretending it isn't." He put a finger on the slip, not marking it, on the ninety. "The second thing is that we can. We walked a bench nobody's walked in three seasons and came down with the boxes and the map and nobody lost, and there are two slips on that board tonight with our names on the hirer's side. Eight weeks ago we were pulling a cart out of a wash for whatever a wash pays. We can go two days east and pick up a case." He did not say the third thing, because Brom had looked at Karis and that had said it, and because she would have minded hearing it said and been right to.

"And the fourth," Seln said.

"There isn't a fourth."

"There's a sentence built inside, on a landing's paper, for ground the best crew here won't stand on." Flat. "It's not a reason not to. It's a reason to know it's there." He turned the slip so the reference faced Cael. "Log it as what it is. Somebody wanted this posted here, and priced it so that whoever took it would be a crew that had never seen the rule work. That's us. I'd take it anyway. I'd like a face."

"It's logged," Cael said.

Lira looked round the table once, at each of them, as she looked round a ring before a bout. "Then we're going," she said, and it was not a question, and nobody made it one.

He took the pencil out of the satchel and marked the slip on the corner, where a crew marked it, and got up with it, and crossed the room to the table by the fire where the book was, with the calf saying nine on the boards and the breath stopping at the tape, and put the slip under the stone's edge in front of Pike, marked.

Pike drew it out and read it, silently, once, and did not go still this time; a man did that once. He turned to the heading with the five names and wrote the line — *Recovery. Fallow road. Broker's. Ninety on delivery* — and read it aloud, to the room, so that the crew could say it was wrong; and nobody said it was wrong, and the room, which had heard the words *Fallow road* said at a ledger, went on drinking with rather less than half its attention on the book.

"That's marked," Pike said, to the page. And then, still to the page, asked by nobody, at the plank's ordinary level, one thing more, which Cael kept for the shelf: "Come back and tell me what the line is. Thirty years, and I've never had it on a page."

---

He wrote it at the table by the road door, with the jug empty and Karis's book closed and Lira's boot up on the bench, and Brom and Seln already gone to the meadow to see to the boxes, because the summer was over and the log wanted its count.

*Eight weeks. Seven contracts. Pike says there's ground out here where the Arbiter goes quiet, and nobody goes in twice. Karis has been waiting her whole life for a sentence like that. Seln says the contract was written by someone from inside. I say it pays. Two days east, through Thornwater, on the day the healer said. For eight weeks nothing out here has read me. I'd like to see what a place that reads nothing does with that.*

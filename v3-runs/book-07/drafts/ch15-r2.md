# Chapter 15 — Strata

Karis took the common room on the hundred and third day and did not give it back for a week.

He catalogued the taking, because he had watched her take rooms before — a locked annex on a bluff, a long table by founding-era shelves, a bench above a gorge — and had never once seen it done to a room that had a hold's business in it. The steading's woman gave her the long table under the window. Karis gave the woman, in exchange, three days of the middle box's paper for the hold's own accounts, which the woman had been keeping on a board, and the woman looked at the paper and at Karis and said, "Well," and after that the common room was Karis's from first light until the fire was lit for supper, and the hold ate in the yard when the weather let it.

The table, by the third morning. The rubbings first, because they were the largest: eleven sheets, one per upright, charcoal on the registry's own paper turned to its blank side, each sheet the height of the stone it had been taken from and cut to that height, so that the eleven lay on the table in the order the uprights stood and at their own sizes, tallest to shortest, a ring laid flat. The cord, coiled, the knots at the fives still in it. The notebook, open to the perimeter's page: *sixty-two cords and twenty strides; twelve hundred and fifty-six and a piece; ten cords in from the stone to the middle; a circle*. The stake's number, thirty-one, copied under the registry's mark drawn from memory. Seln's charcoal circle, copied off the carter's map onto its own sheet, with the three words under it in her small hand. The chips, in their row, each on its own square of cloth. And the three boxes, open, standing on the floor at her left hand in the order she reached for them, which he had learned in three days and could have reached for himself.

The carvings she kept to herself for the first four days, and he let her. He had counted the bands on the second upright at the ring and stopped; she had copied all eleven and had been going over the sheets since with a pencil and a second sheet beside each, and the second sheets were filling with marks in rows, and the rows were a tally. He knew a tally when he saw one. He did not ask what she was counting until the fourth evening, and then she said, without looking up, "The kinds. They're in no alphabet I own, and I own more than the registry prints; I said so at the ring and I've had four days to be wrong about it and I'm not. Every band is the same kind of line and never the same line, and I want to know how many kinds there are before I say anything about any of them." He asked how many. "I have a number. I'm not saying it until I've a second site to check it against. A count from one place is a count. From two it's a fact." She went back to the pencil. It was the first time in four years he had heard her refuse to say a number, and he filed that as a measurement of the number.

She worked with the voice she had. It had come back, as Oryn said it would, in pieces, over days — hoarse and usable by the birthday, spent on a quarter of an hour's argument about apples that night — and she budgeted it now the way she budgeted paper: a low thing that went on her when she pushed it and came back when she rested it, and she rested it by writing. She said the findings aloud, one at a time, as they came, to whoever was at the table, and wrote each one beneath the saying, and the writing was the entry and the saying was for them, which was a rule he had learned from a one-armed man at a plank and which she had arrived at on her own.

He sat at the table's end with the log, most days, and did what he was for.

---

The rest of the unit found the week's shape and kept to it, and Thornwater went on round them, because a hold does not stop for a table.

It was the back of the autumn. The carts came in from the lower fields with the last of the grain and went up the ladder to the loft in sacks, past the place where six people slept, so that by the week's end they slept on the hold's winter and not on its floor; the mornings had a skin of ice on the trough that the boy broke with a stick before the mule could drink; the woman's second chimney smoked from first light now and not from supper, and the common room, which was Karis's, was the warm room, and the hold's people found reasons to come through it and look at the ring laid flat on the table on their way to somewhere else. Nobody touched a sheet. The woman had said something at the house door on the first morning, and after that nobody touched a sheet.

Brom moved boxes. He did it as he did everything that weighed something: without being asked, in the order Karis would want them, the mended hand doing its share and the string restriction three days expired, so that the crossbow went back on his belt where the hook was. Lira, who could not hold paper down with her left hand and would not be told she could not, learned to hold it with the right and a stone from the yard, and sat across from Karis for hours at a time with her arm across her chest reading the rubbings upside down, because she had never once in her life been near a thing she could not read without trying. Seln kept the door end, with the yard behind him and the case on his back, and said nothing for whole mornings, and once, when Karis reached for a sheet that was under his elbow, moved the elbow before she had finished reaching.

Oryn worked.

Thornwater was on her route. She had said so at the bend, and she said it again on the hundred and fourth day, at the house door, to the woman, in the voice for a distance: "I'm nine days late here and eleven at the next. Send me who's hurt." They came. He watched from the table, the way he watched everything he could not help with: a man from the loft with a rope-burn to the bone; a girl with a wrist that had been set wrong by somebody in the spring and had to be set again, which cost the girl a sound and Oryn a mending; the bowman off the gate-walk with a shoulder he had not mentioned to anyone in a year. She did them in the yard, in the cold, at the rate she had, and sat on the well's rim between them with her hands turned over on her knees, and did not come to the table. Once, from the window, he saw her look at it — at Karis, at the eleven sheets, at the ring laid flat — for the length of a count, and go back to the wrist.

He had told her what he was. He had not told her what he had done inside the circle, and neither had anyone, and the week went by with the fact sitting at the door end beside Seln, unfiled, where it would keep.

---

The first finding was the name, and she gave it on the hundred and fifth day, in the morning, before she had touched a sheet.

"Quiet ground," she said. Her voice was the dry hinge still, and she used it sparingly. "That's what Pike says. That's what the carters say, and the woman, and the girl with the wrist. Quiet ground. A place. You walk round it the way you walk round a bog."

She had the perimeter's page under her hand.

"It isn't a place. A place has edges that follow the ground — a bog's edge goes where the water goes. This edge doesn't follow anything. It's a circle, two hundred meters from a floor's middle, to a stride, all the way round, over scrub and grass and stone alike, and the stone's no different inside than out, and neither's the grass. The ground isn't quiet. Something is *happening* to the ground, in a circle, at a fixed distance from a made floor, and it's the happening I need a word for, not the place. I can't write *quiet ground* at the top of a page about a process. It's the wrong kind of noun."

She wrote at the top of the page, and turned it so he could read it, and it said *the Quieting*.

"A process," she said. "Something that quiets. I don't know what it is. I know it's a thing that *happens*, not a thing that *is*, and I need the grammar to say so, and that's the whole of the finding. Entered."

"And the circle," Lira said. "You said it at the stone. A circle's a made shape."

"I said it as geometry." Karis did not pick the pen back up. "A circle is the shape you get when one thing is a fixed distance from another thing all the way round. Nothing in the ground does that. Water doesn't. Frost doesn't. A bog doesn't. A cord with a stake at one end does. That's still geometry. It's a fact about the shape, and I've entered it as a fact about the shape, and I'm not going to walk one step past *shape* in the small hand, because the small hand is where the book keeps what it knows." She looked at the word at the top of the page. "The process has a shape. The shape is a circle. Two facts. Leave them where they are."

Lira, across from her, read it upside down, and said it once, aloud, to hear the shape of it — "The Quieting" — and Brom, from the boxes, said nothing and did not need to; and after that it was the word, at that table and on the road after it, and nobody at Thornwater ever said *quiet ground* to any of them again without one of them hearing the correction and not making it.

He wrote it into the log that night as a fact about the language: *Karis has named it. Not the place. The thing that happens. Adopted.*

---

The second finding was the failure, and it took her two days to say, because she did not know how to file it and would not say a thing she could not file.

She laid it out as she laid everything out, in an order that was already an argument.

"Five sigils." Her hand went to five points on the table, one for each. "Wind. Iron Skin. Ember. Shadow. Tide. Iron rank one, Copper, no ranking, Bronze, Iron rank four. Five people, five Paths, five standings, five histories of a thing that showed up at fourteen and has been each of ours since." She looked at the five points. "Every one of them went out at the same stride. Not the same stretch of road — the same *stride*. Lira called it and I called it and I never once had to correct her by a foot. Every one of them went out the same way: not dimming, not failing, not going wrong first — there, and then not there. And every one came back the same: at the stride, in place, the clock started again without having lost the time. Lira's words. Oryn's. Brom's, if Brom used words."

"Door," Brom said, from the boxes.

"Door. Thank you." She did not smile. "And Lira's clock. I want that in the record in her words and not mine, because it's the best description anybody's given of the coming back, and I couldn't have given it, because mine came back while I was laying a cord and I was counting knots."

"It's just there," Lira said, to the sheet in front of her, in the voice she used for a measurement. "Where it was. Like a clock that stopped and started again without losing the time. I said it at the stone."

"You said it at the stone. It's entered as said, and Oryn said it after you, in her own words, with her hands on Brom's arm, and I've hers too." Karis's hand went back to the five points. "Five of them, and no difference among them at all. That's the finding, and here's why I've sat on it two days." She put her pen down, which she did when she wanted a sentence heard as a sentence. "An Arbiter is a personal spiritual entity. That's the registry's phrase, and it's the only phrase anyone has, and every practitioner in Valdris has been raised on it: the thing is *yours*, it came to you at your Kindling, it's as much a part of you as your own hand, and no two are alike. That's what we're taught. And five of them, no two alike, belonging to five people no two alike, went out at one stride, in one instant, in one way, and came back the same." She stopped, and got her breath past the throat, and said the rest in the torn voice as if the tearing were part of the point. "A personal spiritual entity should not fail the way five looms fail when somebody shuts the sluice on the wheel that drives them."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know how to file that," Karis said. "I've a shelf for things about Paths and a shelf for things about the registry and a shelf, since Ostrand, for things about the layer under the registry, and this doesn't go on any of them, and I'm not building a fourth shelf out of one observation at one line. So it goes in as an observation. The five went out identically. Identically is the word. I've underlined it and I'm not going to explain it, because I can't."

She wrote it, and drew a line under *identically*, and turned the page.

Seln, at the door, had not moved. He said, to the yard, "Six," and Karis looked up.

"Six sigils on the road that day," Seln said. "Five went out." He looked at nobody. "I'm not filing that either. I'm counting it."

Karis held his look for the length of a breath, and then wrote a six beside her five, and put a line through the six, and wrote *one dark since fourteen; no change either side of the line; stated by him, not tested by me*, and that was all anyone said about it that day.

---

The third finding was the strata, and she did it physically, in front of them, because a dating was only as good as the room's ability to check it.

She cleared the table of everything but the chips, and laid them out in a row on their cloths, oldest to the right, and named each one as she set it down, the way she had named the stakes on the mountain and the documents in a sealed room, so that the room would have the ruler before it had the measurement.

"Registry-era." A grey chip with a dressed face. "The academy above Ostrand. Built under the registry's own standard, and the newest thing on this cloth." She set it down. "Standardization-era." Darker, with a lip of mortar on it. "A records hall's back wall, in Ardenmere. Two hundred years, near enough — the same era as the papers in a case Seln carries and hasn't opened; that's Ostrand's finding, not mine, and I'm using it, not adding to it." Down. "Pre-standardization." Paler, coarser, the dressing marks broader. "Greyvane's old course, from the converted waystation, older than the registry's standard by the dressing on it." Down. "And the founding era." The last chip, and the one she touched twice. "Waystation stone, from the wall Prynn's archive sat inside. Four hundred years, by the archive's own ledger, which I read the entry in. It's the oldest dressed stone I have ever had in my hand and it is the floor of my ruler. Below it I have nothing. I've never needed anything below it, because nothing I've ever measured was older."

Then the fifth square of cloth, at the row's far right, past the founding stone, and the thing on it was not a chip. It was a rubbing — the lip of the floor, where the fused stone turned over like a basin's rim, taken with charcoal on the day they went in — and beside it a second, smaller, from one of the carved bands. She had no chip. She had said so at the ring, with her hand flat on the stone, and said it again now: "You can't take a chip off it. I tried, with the knife, on the lip where nobody would see the mark. The knife marked. The floor didn't."

She laid the two rubbings against the founding stone.

"Dressing, first. Every chip on this table has a face with a tool's stroke in it — the width of the stroke, the angle, the chatter where the edge caught. The floor has no face. There isn't a stroke on it. There isn't a *join* on it — Cael put his hands on the middle and the read went to the lip without finding one, and I've his word and the cord's, and I lay my cheek on it and sighted the whole way across and there was no light under the string. No stroke, no join, no laid edge. I've no word for a stone with none of the three, and I'm not going to invent one." She moved her hand to the carved band. "Weathering, second. The founding stone's dressed face has four hundred years of edge on it — I know what four hundred years does to a dressed edge, because I've had that stone in my hand for two years and measured it against everything else. The carved lines on the uprights are cut a fingernail deep, and their edges are rounder than the founding stone's. Not a little. A great deal. Whatever cut them was cut long enough ago that the weather's had more time on it than it's had on the oldest dressed stone in Valdris, and I can't tell you how much more, because my ruler stops at the founding stone and the lines are past the end of it."

She set both rubbings down past the founding chip, on the far right, where there was nothing.

"So. Here is the limit, and I'm entering it as a limit and not a finding, because the difference is the whole of my discipline." She squared her hands on the table. "The floor is older than the registry. It's older than the standardization directive. It's older than the two-hundred-year modification we read in Ostrand, and it's older than the sub-layer that modification was written into — because that sub-layer is under every station on the continent, and the founding stone was a station, and the floor is older than the founding stone." A breath, past the throat. "It's older, I'll say once and not again, than [UNBOUND] — older than the word they retired, and I can't say by how much. Entered as a limit, not a finding. We explained the middle of the story in Ostrand. This is before the beginning, and I don't have a ruler for it."

She wrote it. She did not say the word again, that day or after, and he watched her not say it, and knew what it cost her, because she had had that word in her hand for four years and had just put it down on a table as a *thing the floor was older than*, and walked away from it.

"You could have said that with less," Brom said, from the boxes.

"I could."

"Say it with less."

Karis gave him the courtesy of thinking about it. "Everything I can date, I've dated. The floor's under all of it. I don't know how far."

Brom nodded once, and lifted the box.

---

The fourth finding was the stakes, and she entered it once, in four sentences, and did not pursue it.

The stake's number, on its sheet. Seln's circle, on its. The broker's slip, which Cael took out of the satchel and laid beside them without being asked, because he had learned the order she reached in.

"Iron, driven where somebody had measured to, stamped with the registry's mark, numbered thirty-one, on the line to the width of Lira's boot, five years in the weather by Seln's thumb. A map, carried on three postings, with holes drawn in it where the circles are. A contract, in registry prose, for a kit that was never at the site." She did not look at Seln, and he did not look at her. "The Compact measured this. They paced the circle before I did, with instruments, five years ago or six; they stamped their iron; they drew their maps with the holes left in; and they filed it, somewhere none of us can reach. Entered once. As said." She wrote the four sentences, and drew the line, and said, without looking up, "Anything else on this is a question and not a finding, and I'm not entering questions today."

Seln said, "Good," in the voice for an inventory, and that was the whole of the fourth.

Cael put the slip back in the satchel, flat, against the leather book, where it had ridden since the ninetieth day. It was four lines of thin paper under a hook-and-bar seal, and it had walked six people two days east and one of them over a line eight times alone, and it had cost an arm a season and a throat a week, and it had been written by somebody who had known there was nothing at the site to recover and had wanted to know who would go and look. He did not write that. Karis had entered the stakes once, and once was the number, and he had learned from a man at a stone that a thing said once had a number and that the number was its weight.

---

The fifth finding she wrote, and stopped, and he watched her stop.

She had cleared the table again by then. The chips were back in the middle box in their cloths, the rubbings rolled, the cord coiled; what was on the table was the notebook, open, and the lantern, because the light in the window had gone and the fire was not yet lit, and Lira, across from her, with the arm across her chest, and himself, at the end, with the log.

"Five," Karis said. "I've been putting it last for a week because I knew what it was going to be."

She wrote as she talked, and he could see the hand from where he sat — the small hand, the method hand, the one she used when a thing was going into the record and not the margin.

"Observation. Inside the perimeter, at every trial and in the fight, five practitioners' Paths did not render: Wind, Iron Skin, Ember, Shadow, Tide. Inside the perimeter, at two trials and in the fight, the sixth practitioner's — " she stopped, and started the word again, and it came out level — "the sixth practitioner's *fragments* rendered. All of them. Iron read, the whole day. Wind evasion, a foot's worth, silent, at twenty meters in. In the fight: Shadow, Wind full, Pressure, Ember, Blade, Storm, Compression at contact, Anchor perceiving. Nine of nine. Witnessed by five. His Arbiter unchanged either side of the line, stated by him." She wrote the last word and lifted the pen. "That's the observation, and it's the largest thing on this table, and I have it, and I'm going to enter it exactly like that and not one word further."

She did not put the pen down. She held it over the page, the nib a finger above the paper, and he watched her hold it there for longer than he had ever seen Karis hold a pen over anything.

"I have a hypothesis," she said. "You know I have. Everyone at this table knows I have; Lira's been watching me not write it for three days. I've had it since the stone. I've had it since Oryn took her hands off Brom's arm at the line and said *nothing in my hands*, and you walked twenty meters in with the read running under your boots and told me it was dark before and dark now." The nib did not move. "And I'm not entering it, because entering it would make it a finding, and it isn't one. It's a fear."

She put the pen down.

"A finding is a thing I can put a ruler against. I can put a ruler against the perimeter and the stakes and the founding stone and the five sigils going out at one stride. I can't put a ruler against *why*, and if I write why in the small hand, in this book, somebody opens this book in ten years and reads why as a thing Karis Dellenmoor established at Thornwater in the autumn of the ninety-something day, and it isn't. It's a thing she was afraid of at a table. Those go in different books." She closed the notebook on the pen. "The observation's entered. The interpretation's stopped. That's five."

Lira looked at the closed book, and at Karis, and said the only thing anyone said. "Does it help? Not writing it."

"No," Karis said. "It's just correct."

He sat at the table's end with his own book shut under his hand and catalogued the two of them, because it was the thing he could do: a woman who had a hypothesis and would not write it, because writing it would make it a finding; and a man who had a fact and would not say it, because saying it would need a sentence, and he did not have one, and had said so to Lira on a road and been told *later*. They were not the same discipline. Hers was a rule about what went in a book. His was a debt to five people at a stone. But they had arrived at the same table at the same hour with the same thing unwritten, and he saw her see it, across the lantern, and saw her decide not to say so, which was the third thing at the table nobody wrote down.

---

He wrote the log at the table while she cleared it, because she had said she wanted the room empty by the time the fire was lit and he had learned not to be one of the things on the table.

*Hundred and ninth day. Thornwater, the common room, the tables cleared. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Nineteen.*

*Karis dated the floor. Older than everything. She has a hypothesis she won't write down. I have four people who saw what I did inside, and one healer who felt what I'm made of, and I've told none of them why I think it worked. Lira first. Tomorrow. It's always been Lira first.*

He closed the book on the pencil.

Karis had the last box shut and the lantern moved to the table's end, and the room was the hold's again, or would be when the woman came in with the fire. She stood a moment with her hands flat on the empty table, the charcoal gone from her fingers for the first time in a week, the yellow gone from her throat, and looked at the place where the eleven sheets had lain, and then at him.

"The circle's perfect," she said. Her voice was nearly back; it went on the third word and came back for the rest. "Perfect isn't natural. Somebody made a floor that turns Paths off. It's older than anything I can date. And it doesn't turn you off. Three facts. I'm not putting a verb between them."

He had the log under his hand. He did not open it.

"You said you weren't entering the hypothesis."

"I'm not," Karis said. "I'm saying it to you. That's different."

# Chapter 21 — The Drowned Hall

The bearing was a line on a map for three days and then it was a place, and the place was where the line said it would be, to the stride, and he catalogued that from the last rise before he catalogued anything it contained, because Karis had walked three days with a straightedge in her coat and had not once said *if*.

Three days south and east of the Stair, along a road that was a road for a day, a track for a day, and a carter's memory for the third. The kit's bearing, set on the carter's map with the brass straightedge at the Stair's rim and run through the Ring's circle and on, had crossed the carter's third blank at its middle, and Karis had said so at the rim and had said nothing else about it for three days, and on the third afternoon the track came over a low rise into a marsh, and in the marsh's middle stood a hall.

He catalogued it from the rise. Walls of the grey stone to a man's shoulder and no higher, roofless, a square or near it, forty strides on a side. Water inside them — the light off it flat and brown, the surface reaching the walls' feet and standing in the doorway where a door had been — and water outside them, in a ring: a marsh, reed and sedge and standing pools, spreading out from the hall's walls in every direction to a line he could not see and did not need to, because he knew its radius before Karis laid a cord on it. A spring had come up through the floor. The hall had drowned in it, from the inside, and the drowning had gone out through the doorway and the walls' feet and made a country of wet ground round the thing, exactly as wide as the Quiet was.

"Third," Karis said, beside him, and took the straightedge out of her coat and did not open it.

---

The line was where Lira said it was, at the marsh's edge where the track left the last dry ground: "Gone," and a stride back, "Back," and Karis's stone in the track's rut, and the six of them stood at it with the mules behind and looked at two hundred meters of marsh with a drowned hall in the middle of it.

One stake. Seln found it in the reeds ten paces along the line, old, five winters' scale, the registry's mark and a number — thirty-three — and no second stake, and no second mark. He read the ground round it as he read all ground, and went still at the shelf, and said, "Churned."

It was. The mud round the stake's foot, and along the line for twenty paces either side of it, was not marsh mud. It was mud that had been *worked* — furrowed, heaved, turned over in ridges the length of a man and the width of a cart's bed, dried at the tops and wet in the troughs, and it ran from the line inward, toward the hall, and stopped at the water, and did not come out again.

"Something's been up the bank," Seln said. "Something big enough to leave that, and it went back into the water, and it lives in the water, and the water's inside the line." He did not stand up. "I don't like the stake."

Nobody asked why. Ten paces past the stake, at the hall's own lip where the wall's foot met the water, a second bar of iron stood out of the mud, and it was not straight. It was bent — the top of it a hand's height off true, and bent the way a nail bends when a thing under it pushes up, not the way a nail bends when a thing above it pushes down. Seln had seen it from the line. Seln had not gone to it.

"From below," Karis said, with the cord in her hand and not laid.

"From below," Seln said.

---

They spent the afternoon outside the line on trials, and he wrote it as one, because Karis would, and because the six of them had walked into two floors on folklore and a stake and had learned twice what that cost.

Oryn first, because Oryn had the word.

"Carters won't say it near water," she said. She was on the track's dry end with her hands turned over on her knees and the roll on her back, looking at the hall the way she looked at a thing with a rule in it. "There's a hall on a bearing south of the Stair road with a spring in it, and there's a thing under the spring, and the carters who go that way — there aren't many — call it the wold-wyrm, and they say it the way Pike says *the dogs*: as a fact about a road. I've never seen it. Nobody I've had my hands on has seen it whole. I've had a man's leg that had been in its mouth, and he said it came up under him out of a foot of water like the floor had opened, and he never saw a head." She looked at the churned mud. "That's the whole of what the roads know. Big. Under. Comes up. Nobody's seen it whole."

Karis wrote it in the notebook as a source, and underlined *comes up*, and said, "Then we make it come up where we can see it."

The trials, in order, on her page, in her method register, as she ran them.

The surface first. She had them watch the water inside the walls for the length of a quarter-hour from the rise, six people and no sound, and the surface did what still water does in a still afternoon, which is nothing — and then, once, at the hall's middle, where the doorway's line met the wall's, it did something else. Not a ripple. A *bulge*, a hand's height and a cart's width, rising and falling without a sound and without a wake, as if the water had taken a breath. "Moving wrong," Karis wrote. "Once in a quarter-hour. Middle of the floor."

The stone, second. A stone the size of a head, from the rise's rubble, carried by Brom to the wall's top — the walls stood to the shoulder outside and Brom could reach their top from the marsh's edge with his good arm — and set on the wall's coping, and pushed off, into the shallows a stride inside the wall, where the water was the depth of a boot. It went in, and settled, and lay there. Nothing. Karis counted. At eleven, the water answered — not at the wall, not at the doorway, but *at the stone*: the surface over it bulged and broke and something the colour of the mud went across the place where the stone had been, and the stone was not there, and the water closed, and the reeds on the far side of the wall rocked once.

"At the stone," Karis said. "Not at the wall. Not at the sound. At the place the weight is."

The second stone, further. From the same coping, thrown, by Lira, one-armed, side-arm, so that it went four strides out and came down flat and skipped — once, twice, a third time, a ring at each skip — and went under on the fourth, twenty paces out, and settled. The water did not answer the skips. Karis counted them and it did not answer. It answered at fourteen, at the place the stone had stopped, and only there.

"Skips are nothing to it," Seln said. He had the page. He had taken it from Karis, which nobody did, and he was writing on it in his own hand, which he had never once done in the notebook, and Karis let him. "It's blind. It reads weight on the floor. It strikes where the weight is. And it strikes what *stays*. A stone that lands and settles is hit. A stone that skips is nothing to it until it stops." He gave the page back. "That's the rule. Enter it as the rule and we'll see if it's the rule."

Karis entered it as the rule.

Then Cael's, because Karis looked at him, and because the rule had a piece in it that was his and nobody had said so yet.

---

He did it from the wall's top, with Karis on the coping beside him timing it by her own count, because she did not trust a count she had not made.

He had never once used it as a trial. He had used it on a road below Ostrand to open a cage, and on a rim two floors back to open a door, and every other time it had been an idle want — a doorway raising its hand, a survey stake asking, a well's rim — and he had told it no. Now he put the Anchor-adjacent on the flooded floor twenty paces out, where the second stone lay, and laid one fixed point there. Not a cage. A single binding, the thing the woman on the Ostrand road had laid four at a time, taken from her and made his: a point fixed *against* the surface it was laid on, so that the surface held it as a nail holds a board — held pressure, a weight that did not sit on the floor but pressed on it and kept pressing, and would keep pressing until he let it go.

The water answered at once.

Not at fourteen. At once, at the point, the bulge rising and breaking and the mud-coloured length going across it — and not going *on*. Going across, and turning, and coming across again, and again, the water over the fixed point breaking and closing and breaking as if a stone were being dropped there every count and never once settling, because to the thing under the floor that was what a fixed point was: a weight that never stopped arriving.

Karis counted. He held it. Forty. Sixty. The water over the point had not been still for a breath. Every stone on the floor had gone quiet in fourteen; this had not gone quiet at all, and would not, and the thing under the spring was going back and forth across a single fixed place on a flooded floor as a dog goes back and forth under a bone it cannot reach.

"It reads that as the heaviest thing on the floor," Karis said.

He let it go. The water went still in three counts.

"It reads that as the thing that *stays*," Cael said.

She wrote both. She wrote his under hers and underlined neither, because they were the same finding said by two people who used different words for weight, and the rule was now the rule, and the piece of it that was his was on the page where the rest of the unit could read it before anybody stood on that floor.

---

The approach was the most careful of the year, and the decision to make it was made honestly, at the stone, with the light going.

"The walls are carved," Karis said. It was not an argument; it was the fact the argument stood on. "I can see the courses from here. They're the same lines. It's the third set and I need it, because two's a count and three's a fact, and I've the Ring and the Stair and I've a straightedge that says this is on the bearing, and I want the walls." She did not look at anyone. "I'd go alone. I've said that before and it was true then."

"The kit's ledger says third site on the bearing," Seln said. "This is it. He didn't come in. There's no stake in the water. He stood where we're standing and wrote *confirmed* and went home, and I'd have done the same, and we're not going to." It was not an argument either. It was Seln saying the count.

Brom said, "Walls," which meant he had already priced them and they were shoulder-high and stone and would take his weight.

Oryn said nothing. She was looking at the water, and at her hands, and at the water, and she did not say *I can't help you in there* because she had said it at three lines and it was entered.

Lira, one-armed, with the taped foot flat on the track, said the thing that was the whole of the council.

"We're going in without Paths to look at walls with a thing under the floor."

"Yes," Karis said.

"Just checking that's the plan."

They went in at first light, because Seln said a thing that hunted weight in water would hunt it in the dark as well as the day and he would rather see the walls.

---

Six over the stone, Oryn's hands checked at the stride, and across two hundred meters of marsh on the track's line, which was the one dry way in — a causeway the drowning had left, a stride wide, sedge either side and standing water either side of the sedge — with the rule walking with them: quiet, and *moving*, and nobody stopping on anything that had water under it. The doorway. The water inside standing in it to the boot. The walls' inner faces going up from it a shoulder's height, carved floor to coping in the courses, and the hall's floor under a boot of water the depth of a boot everywhere but the middle, where the floor had broken and the spring came up and the water was the colour of nothing and had no bottom he could see.

Brom went to the near wall first, because Brom had priced the walls and the plan was the walls: get off the floor, get on the coping, let Karis copy from the top of the thing she wanted, with six people standing on stone the thing under the floor could not reach. He put his back to the inner face and his good hand down for Karis's boot, and Karis put her boot in it, and Brom *stopped*.

He had to. A man giving a shoulder to a woman climbing a wall is a man standing still on a floor with his whole weight on both feet and hers on top of it. He was the heaviest thing in the hall, and he had settled, and the rule said what the rule said.

It came up under him.

---

First. The floor opened.

That was the shape of it and the only shape he had: a boot of water and the floor under it, and then no floor — the water going up in a sheet the width of a cart, and in the sheet a length of something the colour of the mud and the size of nothing he had a word for, and a jaw. Brom went sideways. Not thrown; he had felt the water go before it went, because Brom felt a floor with his whole body, and he had put Karis *up* — both hands, the bad shoulder and the good, the woman going onto the coping on her hands and knees with the notebook under her — and had gone sideways off the place where he had been standing with the jaw closing on the water where his hip had been and not on his hip. He came up against the wall's foot in the shallows on his back with the water to his chest and the thing going past him and *under*, and the floor closing over it, and the water going flat.

Seln said: "It's blind. It reads the floor. Get *off* the floor." He was on the coping already, ten strides along, with the bow in his hand and his eyes on the water and not on Brom.

Cael had Brom's coat. He had crossed the shallows to him at the run because running was skipping and a man who stops is a stone, and got a hand in the coat's collar and the Iron read under his soles telling him where the floor was and where it was not, and Lira was on the wall above with her good arm down, and between the two of them Brom went up the inner face as a sack goes up a loft — the good shoulder taking the wall, the bad one taking nothing, and his own arm on the coping, and over. Cael went up after him. The water where he had been standing bulged, once, a count after his boots left it, and went flat.

Second. The walls.

Six people on a coping a boot wide, a shoulder's height above a flooded floor, in a square forty strides on a side, and the thing under the floor going round the inside of it under the water. He catalogued the terrain because the terrain was the fight: the coping was cover, because nothing on the floor could reach a shoulder's height; the coping was a trap, because it was a boot wide and stone and wet, and a foot that went off it went into a boot of water with the rule waiting; the water was a boot deep everywhere and then, at the middle, where the floor had broken and the spring came up, deep — deep enough that the length of the thing could turn in it, which it did, and which was where it went to turn. And it could not leave the water. He had watched the churned mud at the line and the bent stake at the lip and had the whole of what the roads knew, and the whole of it was *comes up*; nothing said *comes out*. It was a thing of the floor and the water on the floor, and the walls were not floor.

"It can't see," Seln said, along the coping. "It can't leave the water. It can't reach a coping. As long as nobody's on the floor, it's a thing in a pond."

"Then I'm copying," Karis said.

She was. She was on her knees on the coping with a sheet against the inner face of the wall she was kneeling on top of, and the charcoal going, copying the carvings off the wall under her own boots with a thing the size of a cart going round the water a shoulder's height below her, and Lira, three strides along with her back to the marsh and her arm across her chest, said, "You're copying," and Karis said, "I'm not getting another chance," and Lira said, "No, I can see that you're not," and for exactly the length of that exchange it was the funniest thing that had happened on a floor since Ardenmere, and then it was not, because Brom's shoulder had opened.

Third. The cost.

He was on the wrong wall. He had gone up the near wall with the bad shoulder and the bad shoulder had done what Oryn had said at a stone a mend would do if it were asked to hold a wall: the mark on the front of it had gone dark through his coat, and the coat was wet, and he was on a coping a boot wide with one arm that worked and one that was pouring, and he was slipping. Not falling. Slipping — the good hand flat on the wet stone, the bad arm not lifting, the weight going the way weight goes on a wet ledge, slowly, toward the water side.

Oryn was the nearest.

She had come up the wall behind Cael and had been on the coping ten strides from Brom with her hands at her sides, watching the water because there was nothing else for her to do with her eyes, and she saw him slip before anyone, because she watched patients the way Seln watched roads, and she went to him along the coping, and she was not going to reach him, because a coping is a boot wide and a slipping man is a thing that arrives at the water before a walking woman arrives at him.

She went into the water.

Not off the coping. Off the coping *forward*, into the shallows at the wall's foot, on purpose, because the shallows were the shortest line to where Brom would be when he came off, and she was a healer who could not heal on this floor reaching a patient who was falling, and he catalogued, with the whole of his attention already going somewhere else, that she did not once do it as a thing worth doing. She did it as the thing that was nearest.

And she kept moving.

That was the afternoon, on the page, in her body. She did not stop in the water. She did not stand. She went across the shallows at the wall's foot the way the second stone had gone — skipping, stride to stride, never the weight down long enough to be a weight — with the water to the knee and the bad footing and the coat heavy, toward Brom, who had come off the coping into the shallows on his back with the shoulder under him and was, for the length of a breath, the stillest thing in the hall.

The floor bulged under her first stride, a count after it, and she was not there. The bulge broke where her boot had been and went across it and *on*, because a skip was nothing to it, and she was three strides further, and the water behind her was flat.

The second pass turned.

He saw it turn because he had the Iron read on the floor under the water and the floor told him where a thing the weight of a cart was pushing on it: the length going past her under the water and coming round, not to where she had been, but to where she was *going*, because she was going to Brom, and Brom had stopped, and Brom was the heaviest thing on the floor, and a healer who reached a stopped man was going to stop.

Fourth. He went in.

Off the coping, into the water, declaring — and the decision was the afternoon's rule made into a weapon, and it had two pieces, and the reader of his log would have known both of them for two books and five: Compression, first, because the first strike was coming and it was coming at the place where a woman and a man were about to be one weight, and he could take a strike into a frame that had been built on a circuit floor to take a Bronze's whole force through it and out into the ground. He got between them. He got between the length under the water and the two of them, with his boots on the broken floor's edge where it went from a boot deep to nothing, and he *stopped*, on purpose, and let the read tell him the floor was pushing up.

It came up under him.

He took it. Reydan's, at contact — the jaw's strike arriving through the water into a frame that did not give the way a frame gives, the force going through him and out along his own bones into a floor that was fused stone and gave nothing back, and the water going up round him in a sheet, and the length of the thing against his legs and his chest for the length of a breath, armored, cold, the size of a cart and turning — and he was still standing, in water to the waist, at the broken edge, and it had struck a thing that stayed and had not moved it.

Then Anchor.

He laid it on the floor. Not on the water; on the *floor*, at the broken centre, a stride from his own boots, where the fused stone ended and the spring came up: one fixed point of held pressure, the binding from the Ostrand road, the thing that had opened a cage and a door and had never once been asked to *stay* — laid now to stay, against the stone, as a nail in a board, pressing and pressing and not going anywhere, the heaviest thing on the floor by the only arithmetic the floor knew.

A thing that hunts what stays commits to the thing that stays hardest.

It surfaced at the point. All of it — for the first time, the roads said, in anyone's memory: a length of armored mud-colour the size of a cart and a half, jawed, blind, its head coming up out of the broken centre with its mouth on a point of fixed pressure a stride wide that did not give, and its jaws closing on stone that was not stone and did not move, and closing again, and the whole of the thing's underside coming up out of the spring with the effort of it, pale, the colour of a thing that had never once been in the light, and *exposed*, at contact, for the length of the breath it took to bite a thing that would not be bitten.

"Brom," Cael said. "Wall. Your weight. Now."

Brom put his weight where he was told. He was on his feet in the shallows with Oryn's hands in his coat and the shoulder pouring, and he went to the wall's foot beside the broken centre, where the wall's inner face came down into the water two strides from the thing's pale side, and put his back to the stone and his good shoulder into the wall and his whole weight *down*, so that for the length of the strike there were two things on the floor that stayed — a fixed point and a big man — and the thing under the spring had its jaws on one and its flank to the other and could not turn.

Seln and Lira put two bolts each into the underside.

From the coping. Seln with his own bow, kneeling, at four meters, two bolts into the pale in the time it took the jaws to close twice. Lira with Oryn's — the crossbow she did not know how to wind and had learned in a week to brace one-armed against the coping and her knee and fire, badly, and at four meters into a thing the size of a cart badly was enough — two bolts, one into the pale and one into the water beside it, which was, at that range, one-armed, the best shot anyone had made in the hall.

It did not die.

He had not thought it would; four bolts in a thing that size were four splinters, and the log would say so. But the fixed point had stopped answering — he had let it go the breath after the fourth bolt, because a thing that stays and then does not is a thing that is *gone* to a hunter that reads weight — and the jaws closed on nothing, on water, on a place where the heaviest thing on the floor had been and was not; and the thing under the spring, with four bolts in its pale and a floor that had lied to it, let go of the place, and turned, and went down into the broken centre the way it had come up, and the water closed, and went flat, and did not open again.

He stood at the broken edge in water to the waist, spent, and waited for it to come up, and it did not.

"Walls," Karis said, from the coping. "I've the walls. Twelve sheets. I'm not getting another chance and I've not needed one."

They left it there.

---

The costs, outside the line, on the track's dry end, with Oryn's hands back at the stride.

She had gone over the stone first, with Brom's coat in her fist, and had stopped, and checked her hands, and said "Back," and had not said anything else, because Brom was on his knees on the track with his shoulder open front and back along the mark she had made three days before, and she had both hands on it before he had finished kneeling. An hour. Karis timed it. It was the second time she had mended that shoulder, and she said so, once, to the shoulder — "Twice. It'll hold twice. It will not hold three times this season, so don't ask it to." — and Brom said, "Arm," and she said, "No," and he didn't.

Then she sat down.

Not on the track. On the marsh's dry edge, back to the stone, boots in the reeds, wet to the waist, hands in her lap, face grey, eyes on the hall, and she did not say anything for a long while, and nobody asked her to, because the unit had learned at three lines what a healer looked like when she had spent the whole of a route's budget on two people in a morning, and it looked like this.

He catalogued himself, sitting in the track's rut with the stone at his back, because he did. Spent. Compression at contact against a thing the size of a cart and a half — the frame had held and the bill for holding was in the ribs, which had said nothing for a month and said something now. Anchor held against a thing that would not stop biting it, for the length of four bolts; he had never once held a point against that, and it had cost what the ledger would enter as *the whole of the rest*. Wet to the waist. Nineteen. The tenth fragment used on no one, because there had been no one to read and no reading that would have stopped a jaw.

Oryn spoke, after the long while, to the hall.

"You went in the water."

"You went in for Brom."

"Brom's on my route."

She looked at her hands, and turned them over, and looked at the road, and the hall, and the six of them on the dry end of a track at the edge of a marsh with a drowned thing in the middle of it, and said the rest in a voice that was not the register, because the register had been left on a coping and she had not gone back for it.

"So are you."

He did not have a column for it. Lira, on the track behind him, one-armed, with Oryn's crossbow across her knees and a bolt still in it, said nothing, which was a ledger line, and Brom said nothing, which was a weight, and Seln, on his feet at the stone, said nothing at all, and Karis, with twelve sheets under her arm and her boots wet to the knee, wrote one word in the notebook, and it was not a finding, and she did not show it to anyone.

Oryn got up. She came along the track to where he sat, and stood a stride and a half off, which was her distance and his, and looked at him the way she had looked at him in a steading's back room with her hands on a table's edge, and put the register back on, because she could.

She said: "Lie still. I want to read you."

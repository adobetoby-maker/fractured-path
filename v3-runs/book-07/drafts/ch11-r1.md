# Chapter 11 — The Quiet

He counted the floor going back across it, because he had counted it coming, and it was twenty-six strides both ways.

Positions, catalogued as they came off the ring with the light going: Seln first, over the floor and down the west lip into the scrub without a sound, gone toward the road. Karis after him with the book under her arm, the cord over her shoulder, charcoal to the wrists. Oryn, her leather roll on its strap across her back and her hands at her sides. Lira, on the taped ankle. Brom last but one, with the middle box on his back by its strap and the carter's crossbow across the top of it — Oryn's crossbow, which had ridden under her mule's panniers for two years because carters carried them through quiet ground, and which she had handed to Brom at the stone that afternoon without being asked, saying she had never once wound it at anything and did not mean to start; six bolts in a leather sleeve on his belt beside the hook. And himself, last, with nothing in his hands and nine things held to nothing, walking the last five strides backward.

The floor had gone white along its whole width at once, as it had the evening before, and it stayed white while the grass round it went grey.

They came up over the far lip onto the white as the six of them went down off the near one into the grey, and for the length of a breath they were the only things on the floor, and the floor showed them to him as a page shows ink. Low. Grey. Moving close to the stone, and no sound in any of them. He counted, because a count was what he had. Nine. He counted again with his heels in the grass below the lip, and it was nine, and then the white went out of the last of them and they were in the scrub either side of the floor, and he could not have said where any one of them was.

"Nine," he said, at the size Seln said things.

The stone was ten cords west of the floor's middle, along the straight line Karis had laid her cord down that afternoon; the road's rut ran a stone's throw north of that line and met it at the stone.

Seln's voice came back out of the scrub ahead, flat, with no more weight in it than a count.

"The road's cut."

Then, from further off: "The grass. Where the cord went."

There was a dog in the rut, forty meters out from the floor, between them and the stone, because there was always a dog on the road and it was never the one you were looking at. What he had found in the dark was the lane that was not cut — the cord-line, grass and low scrub, south of the rut and parallel to it, meeting the road again at the stone.

Cael looked at the rim of the floor behind them, a hand's height of turned grey stone with the last of the white going off it, and at Brom, who had already put the box down.

---

Brom held the lip.

He stood two strides off the rim on the grass, below it by the height of a hand, with the crossbow up and wound and a bolt in the groove, and he made no sound, and Cael, three strides behind him with one eye on the lane and one on the rim, saw the fight's whole shape in the way the first hound came to the edge of the stone and stopped.

It stopped because there was nothing in front of it. That was the cruelty of the thing and its gift, and he had known both since a river: a still body was nearly nothing to them, and Brom, who at the ford had learned in one contact that Iron Skin was a shout, was the stillest large thing on the ring. Then the hound went along the rim, east, toward the sound of the box settling on the grass.

They hunted by sound now, and every head that came to the rim came to a noise, and the six of them had been taught for four years of their lives to make none. The box. Lira's boots. The strap of Oryn's roll against her back. Every sound the unit made was a place a grey head turned to, and every silence was a place it turned away from.

The second one came over.

It did not stop at the rim; it came off the floor at the box, and the rim was a hand's height and turned over like a basin's, and its forefeet came down on grass a hand lower than its hind feet expected, and for one beat it had no drive in it at all. Brom did not shoot. He stepped into the beat with the hook's haft crosswise in both hands and put his weight through the haft into the animal's neck while its hind feet were still finding the stone, and it went back over the rim onto the floor, and stood up on the white, and could not find him.

A big man with good footwork and a lip of fused stone. That was what an Iron Skin practitioner was without Iron Skin, and Cael filed it, because Brom would not.

---

Lira was on the rim.

Not on the floor — on the outside of the lip, south of Brom, running its turned edge with the long knife low, because someone had to hold the south side, and she had been the fastest thing they had since she was fourteen. She ran as she had run the bar: everything going and nothing held, no burst at the end of it because there was no burst to be had, and she was fast anyway.

Her right heel came down on the rim's turned edge.

He heard it go, the third time, and the sound was the one it had made on a bar. She did not go down. She finished the stride on the other foot with the knife between her and the floor and her left hand coming up because that was where hands went, and the hound came over the rim at the sound of her landing and took the forearm.

It took it below the elbow with its whole jaw and did not let go.

She put the knife into its neck. He saw the blade go in behind the ear, all of it, and come out, and go in again, and the animal shook her by the arm as a dog shakes a rope, and she went down onto the grass under it with the ankle folding and the arm in its mouth, and she could not burst it off, because a burst was a shout and there was no shout in her here, only the shape of one going through her back and shoulders and arriving nowhere.

Brom's bolt went through its eye at four meters.

He heard the string — a flat wooden note, the first loud thing on the ring — and the hound's head went over sideways and the rest of it followed the head, and it lay across Lira's legs with her arm still in its jaws, and did not move again.

Oryn was on her in a breath.

She had been a stride and a half behind Lira the whole way along the rim, on the grass side, where the hurt would arrive, and she came down on her knees beside the dead hound with her hands already going, and Cael reached them a stride behind her and got his knife's back into the dead jaws and his fingers into the hinge of them and opened them. Oryn had the forearm in both hands before it was clear of the teeth.

Both hands. The count that went to ten, and he watched her face do what it had done over Brom's arm at the stone that morning: the count stop at the stride.

Not end. Stop.

"Nothing," Oryn said, to the arm. Her hands were to the wrist in what was coming out of it. "Nothing in my hands."

She said nothing else. She had the linen out of the roll on her back without looking for it, and got her knee up onto Lira's upper arm above the elbow, all her weight on it, and Lira made a sound through her teeth, and Oryn wadded a strip over the tear and wound another round it and twisted it hard and tied it off, and a third above that, tighter, without a Path in any of it. Cloth and knees. The bleeding slowed under the cloth. It did not stop.

"Move," she said. "I can't fix this here. Move."

He got Lira up. She came up onto the good foot with the left arm held against her chest and the knife still in her right hand, and he took her weight on the side of the bad ankle, and his own right calf caught the rim's turned edge as he turned them off it, and went hot along the old line where the ford had gone through, in and out. He filed it as reopened, and kept moving.

Behind them Brom spanned the crossbow with his hands, in the dark, in a hurry, and the string got out from under his fingers and came across them, and Cael heard him take one breath in a way Brom did not take breaths.

---

The lane, then, in the order Seln had put them: Seln somewhere ahead in the scrub; Karis; Oryn; Lira on Cael's shoulder with the ankle going down wrong every second stride; Brom at the rear with the bow wound and his right hand held oddly on the stock. Cael had the middle box on his own back by then, because Brom needed both hands and Karis needed the box.

A hundred and sixty meters.

The scrub came to the knee either side of the grass and it moved when they moved, north of the lane and south of it, at the pace Lira's foot came down. There was never a shape in it.

Item. They kept to the scrub. On the stretch the grey shape had never once crossed open gravel, and here it never once crossed the grass.

A hundred and forty.

Item, and this was the one that mattered: they were not careful. On the river the pack had tested and withdrawn and waited; the big grey at the bar's tip had stood chest-deep and watched. These did not watch. They came to the box, to the boot, to the roll's strap, and they came the whole way. Prey that walked into this circle could not shout, and had never once been able to, and there was not an animal in this scrub that had ever needed to learn what a shout was.

A hundred and twenty. A hundred.

Oryn, beside him, was looking at the arm, and he could see her counting the cloth going dark.

Eighty meters.

He held the nine. He had held them since a stone in a rut two days ago, on ground where nothing should have worked, and he held them now with a girl on his shoulder and a box on his back, because the thing that would happen if he stopped holding was the thing he had not found a sentence for, and a sentence was what he owed the five of them before he owed them anything else. Iron ran under his soles, because it was the cheapest thing he owned. The rest waited.

Sixty meters.

Karis went down.

---

She had come back along the lane to take Lira's other side, because Karis went where the work was, and the hound came out of the north scrub at the sound of her doing it and took her at the hip, and she went over into the grass with it on her.

He saw her hands go to its throat. Both hands, the scarred right and the left, under its jaw, holding the mouth off her own throat by the strength of her arms while the animal's whole weight came down on her chest, and its jaws came down on her throat across the backs of her own hands and closed as far as her fists let them, which was far enough, and she made a sound.

It was the loudest thing on the ring, and every piece of scrub between here and the floor turned toward it at once.

He counted them coming without seeing one. North scrub, three places moving. South, four. The dog in the rut, gone from the rut. Seven and the one on Karis, and one dead at the lip. There was no lattice in this circle for her to light, and she knew it, and her hands stayed on the throat.

He put Lira into Oryn's hands and let the box go off his back.

Shadow-adjacent. Once.

He had not used it on this side of the Line; he had held it through a country with a ledger and a ferry west, and there was no public here — four people who knew where it came from, a healer who had found nothing in him three times, and eight animals. He thinned. It was Seln's — the presence going out of him like a lamp turned down, the movement folded into the dark — and he crossed ten strides of grass through the middle of a pack turning to a woman's voice, and not one head turned to him. If it counted as a declaration, nothing on the floor came to it. He did not have a sentence for that either. He put it on the shelf.

He got the hound by the loose skin at the back of its neck and a hind leg and heaved.

The Iron read gave him the ground under his boots and he used all of it. The animal came off her chest with its jaws still working and its teeth coming off her throat, and he turned with it and threw it as far as a man throws a thing that weighs what he does, into the south scrub, and it landed and rolled and was up. Karis lay on her back with her mouth open, and no sound came out of it now.

Seven heads in the scrub, and the one he had thrown, were turned to where the sound had been.

He knew what they followed. He had learned it on a river from the thing that did not happen: the loudest declaration, and absent one, the loudest sound, and absent that, whichever thing on the floor committed first. They had never met a thing on this floor that could declare. He had been the only thing on it that could since the stone, and had told nobody, and the sentence he owed them was sixty meters west and he was going to have to run it before he wrote it.

He stopped holding.

---

Wind-adjacent, full.

The burst went through him as it had gone through Lira on the rim, the whole of it, the legs and the back and the breath — and did not land as nothing. It landed as twelve paces of grass in the time a breath takes, the scrub going flat and white either side of him with the wind of it, the loudest thing that had ever been inside this circle, on purpose, west, past the pack, along the lane toward a stone in a rut.

Every head turned.

Eight animals that had never in their lives sensed a declaration on this ground turned to it, all at once, as seven had turned on a river to a bead of white on a girl's fingers. Then he was in the landing lock — the fixed one-count, the beat he could not price a hit inside — and he heard the scrub come after him.

Wind was a burst and a lock and then a body running, and a body running was nearly nothing to them. So he did what he had not done since a matting floor against a Gold-tier woman: he spent everything, one after another, so that there was never a breath in which he was not the loudest thing on the floor.

Pressure-adjacent, because it was loud and he needed it for nothing else. Ember-adjacent, one point of ignition held out at arm's length as he ran, Karis's, a bead of white that lit the lane five paces round and made the scrub either side go still and then come on harder. Blade-adjacent, Ephram's, which had nothing to cut and rang anyway. Storm-adjacent, Daeva's, the pressure-differential seeded ahead of him along the lane so that the air went wrong for ten paces and every fragment in him that was not Storm rang with it as a struck pan rings. Anchor-adjacent, let off its hold, looking west. He ran through the middle of his own noise with the Iron read under his soles giving him every root and stone in the dark, and the calf hot, and the ribs stopping his breath at the tape on every stride, and behind him eight grey shapes came after the only declaration they had ever met on their floor.

One reached him in the second lock.

A second burst at forty, and the hound came out of the south scrub inside it, low, at the leg, and he took it on the shoulder instead — Compression-adjacent, Reydan's, at contact, the force of the animal going into him and through him and out along his own frame into the ground — and it went over him and was up, and he was up, and it turned with the rest and came on.

Twenty meters.

The stone in the rut ahead of him, two fists of it, dark against the grey; Seln a stride beyond it on the road, standing; Brom beyond Seln with Karis over his shoulder and the bow in his cut hand; Oryn and Lira on the road's far edge, Lira on her back, Oryn on her knees; the mule, hipshot, exactly where it had been left.

He crossed the line at a run.

Nothing in him changed, and he catalogued it at the run: the stride over the stone, going out, and the nine ringing in him exactly as they had rung a stride inside, and the place where an Arbiter would have been as dark on the road as it had been on the floor. Then he was past Seln, and heard Seln say "Back," to nobody, in the voice he used for a distance, and heard Lira, from the ground, say it a breath after him.

The pack came over the stone after the declaration.

He turned round to watch it, because it was the whole of the plan. They came across the line as they had crossed it every night of their lives, at the run, without care, eight grey shapes out of the scrub and over a two-fist stone in a rut into ground where prey could shout — and the first of them over took Brom's bolt through the chest at two meters and went down in the rut against the stone and did not get up.

Karis was on her knees at the road's edge, where Brom had set her down. She had no voice. She had her right hand out.

He saw the lattice before it lit, because Anchor-adjacent was still running and perceived fixed points, and hers were laid along the ground in a row so close they were one thing, as she had laid them across a mountain — along the line's inside edge, the meter of ground between the scrub and the stone that every hound in the circle had crossed every night without once needing to be careful of it. They lay along it as the stake had, to the width of a boot.

She lit it.

A wall of ignition stood up across the line, twenty paces of it, thirty, white, at the height of a hound's head, and the seven that were across turned to the loudest thing they had ever sensed and found it was behind them, between them and their floor, and found in front of them a big man who had just declared — Iron Skin, once, and off again — and a girl on the road who had just declared, Wind, lying on her back with one arm, the burst landing as a shout in the air, and a man beside the stone who had been there a breath ago and was not.

They broke.

Not back. The wall was there, and the floor was on the other side of it, and they had never once met fire on that meter of ground. They went out, north and south along the line's outside through the scrub, seven of them, at the run, and the sound of them going was the first sound any of them had made all night.

Karis's wall went out. She went forward onto her hands.

---

Two on the ground. One at the lip, inside, with a bolt through its eye; one in the rut against the stone, with a bolt through its chest, its forefeet across the line and its hind feet inside it. Seven gone.

Then he sat down on the road, because his legs had stopped taking instructions.

Oryn had Lira's arm in both hands.

She had gone to it the stride she was over the stone, and Cael, sitting in the rut with the nine gone to nothing in him — not held to nothing; gone — watched her face do what it had done at the stone that morning, going the other way. The count started. It had stopped inside, and here on the road with the same arm between the same palms it went from where it had stopped to ten, and her eyes went to a place and found it there.

"There," she said. "Back."

She did not say anything about the clock.

"Lamp," she said, to Brom, who had the mule's panniers open before the word was finished and the carter's lantern out of them and lit, and set on the road by Lira's shoulder, and Oryn said, "Don't move her," and nobody did.

She read it, the whole count, and said what she had.

"The vessels are open. Two of them. The muscle's torn through to the bone on the inside, and the bone's marked. The cloth held her this far and won't hold her to Thornwater." She did not look up. "I mend it here. On the road. It's the worst I've had my hands in for two years and it'll take an hour. Nobody talks to me."

Nobody talked to her.

He watched it, because he could not have stood up to go elsewhere. Lira on her back on the road with her face turned away toward the ring, breathing through her teeth in the rhythm she used for a thing that hurt in the right places. Oryn's face going grey by the lantern as it had gone grey on a mountain over a leg, and greyer, and the sweat standing on it in the cold. Brom, sitting on the middle box — Seln had gone back sixty meters into the dark for it while the wall was still burning, and come out with it — with his right hand open on his knee and the fingers of it laid open across the pads by the string, bleeding slowly onto the box's worn corner. Karis beside him with the notebook open on her knees and both hands shaking, writing, because she could not say anything: her throat under the jaw was going dark on both sides in the shape of a mouth, and when she had tried to say a word at the stone what had come out was breath.

He catalogued himself last, as he did.

Spent. Not tired; spent — the frameworks gone to nothing with a hollow in them where each one had rung, all nine, one after the other, as they had not gone since a matting floor against a woman who was better than him. The breath not coming past the tape. The calf open again along the old line and the trouser leg stuck to it. Two landing beats, paid in the usual currency and one of them with a dog in it. Eighteen.

Five people had watched him do it. Four practitioners with no Paths and a healer with no hands had watched the sixth one of them use his, inside the circle, all of it, and lead the pack out across a line with it — Brom from the lip, Seln from the lane, Karis from the ground with a dog on her, Oryn with Lira's arm in her hands, Lira from his shoulder — and not one of them had said a word about it, and not one of them was going to tonight, because the priority on this road was under a lantern with Oryn's hands on it, and everybody knew it.

He filed that. He did not look for a reason not to say more. The reason was on the road in front of him with its face turned away.

---

The hour was an hour. Karis timed it on the ridge's dark, and wrote the time down.

At the end of it Oryn took her hands off the arm and sat back on her heels, and then sat back further, onto the road, and put both hands flat on the ruts either side of her. Then she said the state, because she was a professional and a thing that was not said was not a finding.

"The vessels are closed. Both. The deep tear's closed; muscle to bone. The bone's marked and it'll stay marked and it's whole." She looked at the arm, and at Lira, and at nobody else. "She keeps it."

Lira did not move. Her face was still turned to the ring.

"Now the rest, because I've not got it in me to say it twice." Oryn's voice was level and had nothing left under it. "A mend that deep holds nothing for a season. Nothing. That arm is not to hold. It's not to strike. It's not to catch. Not a rope, not a knife, not a fall, not a friend. Not until I say. And I'll say at first snow, near enough, if it's let alone." She took Lira's left wrist and laid the arm across her chest, and bound it there with the linen, high, a sling from the shoulder. "One arm. From here to the snow."

"Yes," Lira said, to the ring.

"The ankle's a tape and a season. I've told you that before." Oryn lay down on her back on the road with her hands on her stomach, and did not get up for a while, and nobody asked her to.

Karis had written it all. She turned the book round to Cael on her knee, and he read it by the lantern: *Vessels closed. Deep tear closed. Bone marked, whole. Saved. No load for a season — not to hold, strike, or catch until she says. First snow. One-armed.* And under it, because Karis kept her own bill: *Throat: bruised both sides, jaw-shaped. Voice: none. Ninety-sixth day.*

He nodded. She took the book back and wrote something else and did not show him.

Seln came in off the stone at full dark, and put his knife away, and said, "Nothing," which from Seln was a whole report, and sat down on the road with his back to the mule's leg.

Brom said, "Hand," and held it out, and Oryn, from where she lay, said, "Morning. Wash it. I've nothing tonight," and Brom said, "No," which meant yes, and wrapped it himself with the linen she pushed toward him along the road, one-handed, badly, and Cael reached over and held the end.

---

He wrote it in the rut with the book on his knee at the edge of the lantern's light, in the plain hand, in a hand that hurt.

*Ninety-sixth day. The road, at the stone, outside the line. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Eighteen.*

*Inventory. Nine confirmed, one anomaly. Deployment inside the line, all witnessed by the circle and by Oryn and by no one else on the continent: Shadow-adjacent, once, ten strides, to reach Karis. Then everything. Wind full, two bursts, two locks, a dog in the second. Iron the whole way. Pressure. Ember, one point, running. Blade. Storm. Compression at contact. Anchor, perceiving. All nine. First time I've spent the whole suite since Daeva. Bill: spent to the road; ribs at the tape; right calf open again on the lip, the ford's line; two landing beats.*

*Injuries, all six. Lira: left forearm bitten through at the lip, mended at the stone in an hour, saved, no load for a season, one-armed to first snow, Oryn's order stands; right ankle, third time. Karis: throat bruised both sides, voice gone. Brom: right hand, fingers opened across the pads on the crossbow string. Oryn: nothing I can name and everything she had. Seln: nothing. Me: above.*

*The dogs: nine. Two dead, both Brom's bolts — one at the lip through the eye at four meters, one at the stone through the chest. Seven gone north and south along the line. Seven's not nothing; I'm writing it as open.*

*One sentence, because the ledger wants one: they live in there because nothing that walks in can fight, and I walked in, and I could, and I took their floor away by being the one thing on it they could follow out. The ford turned over: the declaration ran, and the killing was a line.*

*Everyone else's went to sleep at the stone. Mine has been asleep since I was fourteen. Out here that makes me the only one who's awake.*

*Five people saw all of it. Nobody's said a word. Lira's arm came first. It'll come first tomorrow.*

He closed the book.

Lira had turned her head. Not to him — to the ring, behind them in the bowl, where the eleven uprights stood on their floor in the dark with no light on them at all, and somewhere sixty meters short of the road a flat place in the grass where Karis had lain with her hands on a dog's throat. She lay with the arm bound across her chest and the ankle taped and the good hand flat on the road beside her, and she looked at it a long time before she said anything, to the ring.

"It worked in there."

A beat.

"Yours."

"Yes," Cael said.

"Later."

"Later."

# Chapter 23 — Bearing

Karis delivered the finding at the marsh in the morning, in the register she kept for findings she was afraid of, and he catalogued the register before he catalogued the finding, because he had heard it twice in his life and both times the thing under it had been true.

She had them come to the far fire. She did not bring the map to them. She stood over the middle box's lid with the carter's map flat on it under two stones, and the brass straightedge open along it, and the kit's ledger shut beside it with her thumb in the page, and she waited until the five of them and the mule had come the thirty strides along the track and stood round the box, and then she said it as she said a thing she had checked three times and would have given a year to be wrong about.

"The Ring." Her finger on the first circle, where Seln's charcoal ring lay over the carter's. "The Stair." The second. "The Hall." The third, where the marsh was, where they stood. "Three centres. I've paced two and cord-measured the third and the kit's radius for the Stair agrees with mine, so I know where the centres are to a stride, and I've put them on this map to a stride, which the carter didn't, because the carter drew what everybody knew and everybody knew *round about here*." She did not lift the finger. "The straightedge is on the three of them. Look at it. Not near them. *On* them. Three centres, and a brass edge, and it touches all three without a hair of daylight, and I've laid it and moved it and laid it again for two days and it does not stop touching all three."

She lifted the finger and put it on the straightedge itself, where it ran on past the Hall's circle toward the map's lower edge.

"And the bearing. The kit's. He wrote *third site confirmed on bearing* and stopped. I took the bearing off his first two sites, the way he would have, and it runs along this edge. It's the same line. He found the third by walking the line from the first two, and he was right, and it's here, and we're standing on it." Her voice did not change at all, which was how they knew she meant it as method and not as fear. "Three points make a line. Four make an argument."

Nobody said anything. Brom looked at the straightedge. Lira looked at Karis. Oryn looked at the map upside down, as she read everything, and Seln looked at the map's lower edge where the brass ran off it, and said nothing, because Seln had already seen what was there.

"The line doesn't stop at the Hall," Karis said. "It goes on. East and south. And the carter's fourth circle" — she moved the two stones and slid the map up under the edge, and there it was, at the map's lower corner, the carter's last blank, drawn careless and far, the one his copying had got tired at — "sits on it. Three days, or near it. On the line, near enough, and near enough is what the carter drew, and I'll know when I've paced it." She took her hand off the map. "Nobody has said a word about who or why and nobody's going to. I've a line. It's a fact about a straightedge. I'm asking to go and look at the fourth thing on it, and I'm not asking; I'm telling you where I'll be."

"That's a healer's sentence," Oryn said, from the map's far side, and Karis said, "I know whose it is," and that was the council, because the council had been held at a table at Thornwater eleven days ago and had ended *we find the others, all of them*, and this was the fourth of them, and there was nothing left to decide except the road.

---

The walk was the last of the year's, and the coldest, and he let it be both, because a man who catalogues a country he has come to love does not stop cataloguing it because he has a straightedge's worth of dread in his coat.

High autumn, at the back of it. The frost did not go off the track now until the sun was two hands up, and some mornings it did not go at all, so that the six of them walked on ground that rang under a boot and the mules' breath went up white in front of them. The void roads were emptying. They passed one cart in two days, going the other way with its bed full of hides and its carter walking beside it quiet, who looked at the six of them and the bow and the healer's roll and said nothing and lifted a hand, which was the whole of the etiquette and was, out here, a paragraph. The holds they passed had shut their gates early. The last of the grain was in. Smoke went up straight from roofs he had never seen and would not see again, and the country had the look it had when it was closing for the season, and he wrote it down at every halt because it was going, and he had learned at fourteen that a thing you did not write down while it was there was a thing you did not have.

He catalogued the six of them, too, at the halts, because they were going to be five.

Lira, one-armed, on the taped foot, walking as she had walked since the Ring — the arm across her chest, the good hand free, the stride not one hair shorter for it — and carrying Oryn's crossbow across her back on its strap, which she had not given back and Oryn had not asked for. Brom with the kit on his good shoulder and the middle box on the same one and the other carried still and forward, and no word about it, and Oryn's eyes on it at every halt. Karis with the map in her coat and the straightedge in her hand, open, because she had opened it and had not found a reason to close it. Seln ahead, always ahead, with the case on his back and the ledger inside his coat, reading a road nobody had walked for a season. Oryn with her mule's rope in her left hand and her right hand out of the coat, at every halt, as if a hurt might arrive at the fire and need it. And himself, with the ribs that had paid for the Hall saying so at every halt now that the cold had them, and a healer's two days on them gone to four, and ten things held to nothing and a still place at the middle of them that he did not touch, because a healer had said *stop touching it* and a man who had been told that by the only person who could read him did as he was told.

At the first night's halt, in the lee of a wall that had been a hold's once and was three courses of stone now, Brom sat with the kit beside him and the shoulder still, and Lira, across the fire, said, without looking at him, "Twice," and Brom said, "Twice," and Oryn, between them, said, "And not three," and neither of them argued, because she had said it to the shoulder at a marsh and the shoulder had heard. Karis did not come to the fire; she sat ten strides off with the straightedge on her knee and the map under it and looked at the ridge in the dark as if the line ran up it, which it did. Seln came in off the road at full dark and said "Nothing," and sat, and Cael wrote the halt down — the wall, the courses, the cold, the six — because it was the last night there would be six of them at a fire this year, and he knew it, and did not say so, because Oryn would not have wanted it said.

The fork was on the second morning.

---

It was a fork because Seln said it was: a place where the track they were on went on east and south along Karis's line, and a second track, older, went off north-west toward a gap in the hills that Oryn looked at once and did not look at again, because looking at a road you are about to take is not a thing a healer does in front of people.

"Thornwater's that way," she said. "Four days, by that gap. I'm eleven days late there, and eleven days is eleven days, and there's an old woman at Thornwater whose knee I said I'd see, and I said it in the spring." She had the mule's rope in her hand and the roll on her back and her face square to the north-west track, which was how she stood at everything. "I've been a stop on my own route for nine days. I'm going back to being the route."

He walked her to the fork.

Fifty paces, from where the five of them stood with the mules to where the two tracks parted at a stone that was only a stone, and he walked them beside her with his hands at his sides and did not say the thing he had had since the marsh, which was a sentence that asked her to stay, and which was a good sentence, and which he did not say, because the whole of her being with them at all had been built on a bank at Millrace out of the fact that nobody had asked her to come and nobody would ask her not to go. That was the condition. He had entered it at a ford in his own hand. A man who breaks the condition at the first fork it costs him has not understood the condition.

She stopped at the stone. She looked at the five behind him, once, the way she looked at a line of patients at a table's end — a count, and a finding — and then at him.

"Find me at Thornwater," Oryn said.

It was the sentence she had said at Oxhollow's gate with the road calling her north, five weeks ago, to a boy with three ribs taped, and it had meant *I want to read you when you're not broken*. She said it now at a fork, the same sentence in the same voice, and it meant the opposite, and he heard the opposite, and did not say so, because she had chosen the words on purpose and the purpose was that he would hear it.

"Bring the map," she said.

Then she turned the mule's head north-west and went, and did not look back, because a healer walking her route does not look back at a crew walking off it, and he stood at the stone until the gap in the hills had her, and went back to the five.

Nobody said anything about it. Lira, one-armed, shifted the crossbow on its strap, and Brom picked up the mule's rope, and Karis looked at the straightedge in her hand, and Seln, ahead, had already gone on along the line, and Cael took the log out and wrote one line standing, at the fork, because the fork was where it had happened.

*Hundred and twenty-second day, the fork. Oryn north. On her terms. I didn't ask. Five on the bearing.*

---

Five on the bearing, then, for a day and a half, and the country going from cold to colder, and the track going from a track to a thing Seln read off the ground.

He catalogued it because it was the last of it and he knew it. A ridge on their right for the whole of the afternoon, bare, with the first snow lying in its north-facing folds where the sun did not get, so that the ridge looked as if somebody had drawn white lines on it with a rule. The scrub gone brown. The sound the frost made under the mule's hooves. Brom eating as he walked, because Brom did, and putting bread in Lira's good hand without being asked, because he did that too. Karis not speaking, with the straightedge open in her hand and the map in her coat, looking at the ground ahead as if the line were drawn on it, which, to a stride, it was. Seln stopping every hour to read the track and finding nothing on it — no cart, no boot, no hoof but theirs — and saying so each time in one word, which was "Nothing," which meant that a country that had been full of roads all summer had gone empty in a fortnight and they were the last thing moving on this one.

He loved it. He put that in the log at the noon halt in four words, because it was true and because the dread was in the same coat and he wanted them both entered on the same page, so that whoever read it in ten years would know the two of them had been carried together.

*I love this country.*

The ridge turned east in the last hour of the light, and the track went up it, and at the top the ground fell away, and the fourth thing on the line was below them.

---

Larger.

That was the first word and the only one he had for the length of a breath, and he made himself count before he let himself have a second.

A bowl, as the Ring's had been a bowl and the Stair's had been a bowl, but not a bowl: a valley, or the head of one, wide, with the ridge they stood on curving round it on the north and west and the ground going away east into the dusk, and at its middle, far — further than the Ring's floor had been from its stone, further than the Stair's hole had been from its rim — a shape. He could not have said what shape. Grey, because everything on the line was grey. Standing, because it stood against the last of the sky where the Ring's uprights had stood against nothing and the Stair's floor had lain flat. A mound, or a hall, or a floor with something on it that was taller than a man and not a ring of uprights and not a stair, and the dusk took the rest, and he did not guess at the rest, because a healer had told him what guessing was.

"Line," Karis said, and it was not a question, and she was already off the ridge's crest with the cord.

Lira went with her, one-armed, on the taped foot, down the ridge's near slope, a stride in from where Karis walked, and at forty paces Lira did not say *gone*. At sixty she did not. At a hundred, on a slope where at the Ring and the Stair and the Hall she had gone dark at forty, she was still walking with her hand a little out from her side and her Arbiter with her, and she stopped, and looked back up at the five of them on the crest, and said nothing, and went on.

"Gone," she said, at a hundred and ninety paces, by Cael's count from the crest, and stepped back, and said, "Back," and Karis put a stone down.

Karis paced it in the last light, with Brom marking, because it was what she was for and there was a quarter of an hour of light to do it in. Not the whole circle — there was not the light for the whole circle, and she said so, and did what could be done, which was what she had done at the Ring: Lira on the line with her Arbiter gone, and coming back at a half-stride's stray, and calling it; Karis a stride outside her with her own sigil present, laying the cord end over end along the line westward from the stone; Brom setting a stone at the end of each, the count said aloud at each and Cael writing it from the crest; and nobody's boot a hair nearer the shape than the line was, until the light went off the shape at the middle and Karis stopped with the twentieth cord's last knot in her hand and her wrist turned the little it had turned at every cord.

"Twenty," she said, to the cord. "Twenty cords of the line, and it bent the same every cord, the way the Ring's did. Half as much. At the Ring, twenty cords was a third of the way round it. This is twenty, and it's a sixth." She coiled the cord, once, and did not say the number, because she was Karis and a number said once was a number said, and then she said it, because it was dark and the register was the one she kept for findings she was afraid of, and a finding that was not said was not a finding. "Half the bend is twice the round. Four hundred. Near enough. Twice the radius. The same shape, at twice the size, round a thing I can't see."

She came back up the slope in the dark with Brom and Lira and the stones marked behind her along the line, and did not say anything else.

---

Seln had found the stakes.

He had gone along the line while Karis paced it the other way, east along the slope where the perimeter ran, reading the ground, and he had not gone far, because he had not needed to. Ten paces. He was on one knee in the frost when they came to him, and he did not have a thumb on anything, because there was nothing to rub the scale off; there were too many.

A line of them. Iron, a hand's height out of the ground, registry-stamped, numbered — thirty-four on the nearest, and thirty-four on the next, and thirty-four on the next, with the small mark beside it, the stroke and the bar — set not a stride apart as at the Stair but close, a pace apart, a pace, a pace, running away along the perimeter's curve east into the dusk as far as the eye went and west along it the other way as far as the eye went, so that the line Lira had called with her Arbiter was drawn on the ground in iron, by somebody who had come with a great many stakes and a great deal of time, and had not measured a circle. Had *marked* one.

Seln looked along the stakes to the east, where they went into the dark, and along them to the west, and then at the nearest one, with its number and its small mark, and said the thing in the voice for an inventory, and the inventory had a weight in it that inventories did not have.

"That's not a survey. That's a cordon. They've been here more than once."

Nobody said anything. Cael counted the stakes he could see, because a count was what he had, and stopped at forty because the dusk stopped him, and they had not stopped.

---

The council was five voices and one silence, and the silence was his, and everyone on the crest knew whose it was and why.

Nobody had suggested crossing. Nobody had needed to. The five of them stood on the crest above a cordon of iron round a circle four hundred meters across with a shape at its middle that the dark had, and looked at it, and the thing they were looking at was not the shape. It was the line, and the fact that four of the five of them would go dark at it, and the fact that the fifth would not, and that nobody knew what a circle twice the size did to the four — whether it was the same dark at twice the distance, or a different thing — and that the one person on the continent the fourth site would let in without a mark on him was standing on the crest with his hands at his sides and a straightedge's worth of dread in his coat, and had not said a word.

Lira said it first, because Lira asked the honest question first. "Twice the size. Is it twice the *anything else*?"

"I don't know," Karis said. "I've paced twenty cords of it. I don't know what it does to a Path at the middle. I don't know what it does to a Path at the line. I know the line's where Lira said, and that's all I know, and I'm not going to know more from a crest."

Brom said, "The shoulder's mended twice," which was the weight named, and was, from Brom, an argument against. Then, because Brom said a thing once and let its weight do the rest but had learned from a healer that some things wanted saying twice, he said, "And she said not three."

Seln said, "There's forty stakes I can see and there's more I can't. They've been here more than once. A cordon's a thing you put round a place you mean to come back to." He did not say *and they know which crew walks out of quiet ground*, because he had said it at a plank and once was the number.

And Oryn was four days north-west, with a route.

That was the silence. Not the shape. Not the cordon. The five of them on a crest, and the one of them who could walk down that slope and over that iron and across four hundred meters of dark to a thing nobody had a word for, and come back, alone, because nothing on any of these floors had ever done anything to him — and the four who would stand at the line while he did it, with their Paths gone at a stride, and no healer's hands within four days if a thing came up under him that a floor had not taught them.

He said it. He said it as he had said a thing at a table at Lowmarch to five people, plainly, in the order he had it.

"Not without her. Not without knowing what a circle twice the size does to the four of you. And not without the map finished."

"It's finished," Karis said. "It's a line."

"Then it's a line that points somewhere. We go home and find out where."

Nobody crossed the line.

He catalogued that, because it was the first time in the book of the year that the six — the five — of them had stood at a line and not gone over it, and he wanted the count honest: three floors entered, one paced from outside, and the reason for the one written down in three sentences at a council that had taken the length of a sunset. Karis coiled the cord. Seln came back up the slope from the stakes with nothing in his hands. Lira shifted Oryn's crossbow on its strap and looked at the shape in the dark for a while and then at the north-west, where a gap in the hills had a healer in it four days off, and said nothing, and Brom put the kit down on the crest and sat on it.

They camped on the crest with the fire small and the cordon below them in the dark, forty stakes and more, and the shape at the middle of four hundred meters of quiet that none of them had a word for and none of them was going to make one, and in the morning they would turn for Lowmarch, five, with a kit and a ledger and fifty-two sheets of copied walls and a straightedge and a map with four circles on it and a line through all four.

He wrote the log on the crest, by the small fire, in the plain hand, with the fourth circle below him in the dark, and read it back once, and found the one thing in it he had written without meaning to, and let it stand, because it was true.

*Hundred and twenty-third day, the crest above the fourth. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed. Nineteen. Ten. Five of us; the sixth on her route.*

*Four sites. One line. The last one's twice the size and the Compact fenced it. The bearing runs through it and keeps going. Karis says four points is an argument. I say it's a bearing — the kind their maps draw as nothing. Home is Lowmarch now. I just wrote that without noticing. Nineteen, ten fragments, a still place at the center of me, and a line on a map that somebody drew before there were maps. We're not done. We're oriented.*

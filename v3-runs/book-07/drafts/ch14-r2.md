# Chapter 14 — Nineteen

He managed twenty-one lying on his left side on a plank table with the sun on the ceiling, and told her from the beginning, because the beginning was the only place he had ever found to start it.

Fourteen. A certification office in Denvash, a sigil that lit and went dark for eleven seconds and came back with a word on it nobody living had seen. A year in an Unranked District with a notebook. A training floor, and a girl with a Wind Path who did not know she was teaching, and a sidestep his body made before his foot decided to. He gave her the first notice, and the format of it, and that he had never once received the other kind. He gave her the count — nine before this morning — and the sources, by Path and not by name, because the names were the circle's and she had not been in the circle; and then, because she was a healer and had had her hands on three of them, he gave her three names anyway. Brom's. Karis's. Lira's. He told her what the conditions were as he had them, and that the model said real and not fight, and that until an hour ago nobody had ever tested that sentence.

She did not interrupt. She stood with her hands flat on the table's edge either side of his shoulder, leaning on them, and looked at the wall where the sun had been, and listened the way she had read: without a verdict attached, going along it.

"On your table," he said, "the conditions came together. I felt them come together, and I felt it start, and I could have stopped it, and I didn't. I reached. On purpose. The notice came while your hands were still on me." He said the notice to her, line by line, the way it had stood on the wall. "*Clinical.* It's never said that before. It's never said anything that wasn't a fight, or the next thing to one."

"Tide-adjacent," Oryn said. It was the first thing she had said in twenty-one minutes, and it was a word she was trying for weight.

"Yes," he said.

"Partial."

"They're all partial. They're all *adjacent*. I've never had the whole of anything." He kept his eyes on the ceiling; the stillness was easier with the eyes fixed. "There's a page. I wrote it last night, before, in the loft, so that I'd have to pay what's on it and not what I felt like paying after. It's in the satchel. It's yours to read. It says what I knew and what I didn't and why I didn't tell you before, and the last reason isn't a good one, and it's on the page with the good ones."

"Get it."

He got up off the table.

---

He went up the loft ladder and came down with the leather book. She had turned the stool to the window and sat on it, and she took the book without looking at him and opened it to the pencil.

He stood, and did not know where to put his hands, and put them at his sides, which was where she kept hers.

She read it the way she read Karis's turned notebook: once, the whole of it, and then the parts that had a count in them a second time. Her lips moved on the conditions. They did not move on the price. She read *she can't consent to what she can't know about* and stopped there for the length of one of her breaths, and read on, and read *the real one is that I don't know what she'd say*, and read on past that as if it were a wound she had already seen the extent of. Then *I want it. Write that too.* Then the last line. Then she closed the book on the pencil and held it shut on her knee with both hands, as she had held the arm.

"So I couldn't read you because there was nothing in you shaped like a Path for the reading to find."

"I don't know. Maybe."

"I don't know either. I'm a healer, not a theorist." She was looking at the book's cover and not at him. "What I know is you've now got a piece of the thing I read with."

"Yes."

The pause was a healer's. He had watched her take it over Lira's leg on the mountain and over Brom's forearm at the stone: the count going to its end, and the finding not said until it had.

"Then use it on yourself first. Before anyone." She looked up. "I want to know what you find, and I want to be the one you tell."

He had been asked for a great many things by a great many people about what he was — a demonstration, a transcript, a baseline, a sentence with the hole in the right place — and he stood in a steading's back room and catalogued that nobody had ever attached a condition to it before. Not a price; the Compact priced it. A condition: a thing he would have to do first, and a person he would have to tell, both hers to set, and both right. He had a page on her knee that said what he had chosen for her, and she had read it, and this was her answer, and it was the only one in the log that had ever come back with terms.

"Yes," he said. "First. And you."

"Good." Oryn gave him the book. "Now lie down again. Not for me. You're the colour of the wall and you've been standing on that calf for a quarter of an hour, and I've just been told I'm a patient's source, and I'd like a minute to sit with the second thing before I go and eat."

He lay down. She sat with it. Neither of them said anything for a while, and then she got up and opened the door, and the steading's woman was on the other side of it with a bowl, because the woman had known how long the long one took.

---

It came on at dusk, and it came in a register the log had no words for, so he built them on the first night and spent two days finding out whether they held.

The cascade was the cascade. He had banked for it since Ostrand, and the discipline held as it had held eight times: the circle told on the loft ladder before dark, in four sentences; nothing scheduled; water and the last of the bread within reach of the grain he lay on; and Brom on the ladder's head with the lantern turned down to a thread, because it was his turn, and the rota had never once been discussed.

What arrived was not a pull. He had had appetites before — motion, contact, the air, fixity — and every one of them had wanted something from him, and a want could be named and scheduled round and, in the end, put in an idle state and told to be quiet. This did not want. It *listened*.

He lay on his back on the sacks in the dark and heard himself.

Not with the ears. With the thing the tenth fragment had brought in from her hands: the sense of current, hers, turned on his own. At the edge of every thought, faint, there was a flow — his own — going along whatever it went along, and he could feel it as a man in a still house at night feels the house breathe. It did not stop. It had never stopped. He had lived inside it his whole life without once perceiving it, and now it was there at the edge of everything, and the strangeness was not that it was loud. It was that it had always been there, and that he was standing in a room he had lived in for eighteen years with the lights suddenly on.

Then the others came up out of it, one at a time, and that was the part he had not been ready for.

Wind, first. Not the framework, not the thing he deployed — the *flow* of it, the current that had been ringing faint since the Ring's second morning, audible now as a thing that ran, and ran a particular way, quick, and near the surface, so that he could have said where in himself it lay the way Oryn had said it from outside: *the water's too fast*. Pressure under it, slower. Iron, deep and dense and very quiet, running like water under a floor. He lay and listened to each of them come up out of the general flow and be itself, and by the small hours he had nine, each with a sound that was not a sound, told apart as he told the five people in this loft apart by their breathing.

He said so to Brom once, because Brom had asked him to say when it was bad, and this was not bad.

"It's not bad. It's *loud*. Not loud like Storm was loud. Loud like a room with people in it that I've been calling empty."

Brom considered that from the ladder's head. "How many people."

"Nine. Ten. I can't hear the tenth. It's the thing I'm hearing with."

"Mm." The lantern's thread did not move. "My father could hear the forge from the house. Every hammer. Thirty years. My mother said she'd never once heard it and he said that was because she'd never once listened." A pause. "He didn't mean it kindly. But it was true."

He entered it, and entered under it, in the flat hand the log kept for things it did not like, that he had no way of knowing what a thing heard for the first time was *supposed* to sound like, and that *loud* was the wrong word and the only one he had.

---

The second day was the worst of it and it was not, by any measure the log had ever kept, bad.

The listening did not stop with the light. He went down the ladder and the currents came with him, and he stood in Thornwater's yard in the cold with the well in front of him and heard nine flows go about their business inside him while a woman drew water, and found that the difficulty was not pain. It was *attention*. Every framework he owned had an idle state, and the idle state had been taught, over four years, by a hand that could not perceive what it was putting to sleep — like tuning a string in a room where you could not hear it, by the feel of the peg. Now he could hear the string, and a hand that can hear every string in a room wants to go to each one, and the going was the cost.

Storm resettled, as it always did, being the heaviest thing he owned. He felt it go mid-morning, standing in the yard — the whole architecture shifting a hair round the new presence — and for the first time in his life he *heard* the settling happen, from inside, as a change in a flow he could follow, and stood with a hand on the well's rim until it had closed, which it did, on its own, inside a quarter of an hour. He wrote *correctly*, and then that he still could not have said what incorrect would sound like, and that at least now it would be a sound.

The Wind economy went with the resettling, as it had gone above Ostrand, and the yard's ground arrived a half-beat late under a foot that had been counting on it, and he stood still until it came back.

Oryn watched him do that from the house door.

She had done the steading's hurts that morning at the rate she had — a man's split thumb, a child with a cough, the woman's own knee, unmentioned — and she came out into the yard with her hands drying on the coat and stood a stride off and looked at him as at a thing with a rule in it she had not been told.

"Where is it loudest."

Nobody had ever asked him that. Karis asked *what* and *how many*. Lira asked *is it bad*. This was a healer's question, aimed at a body, and he answered it as one.

"Here." The breastbone, where her front hand had been. "Under where you were. It's not louder there. It's *nearer*. Everything's nearer there."

"And you're standing with your hand on a well because."

"Because the floor was late."

She looked at the well, and at the foot he had put his weight on, and at his face, and nodded once, as at a finding that matched the last one.

"Does it hurt in the right places?"

"It doesn't hurt."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's in the right places," Cael said. "It's just that I didn't know they were places."

Oryn looked at the well, and at his hand on it.

"One more, while it's loud. When it's quiet enough to turn outward — and it will be; that's what it's for — you ask. Every time. Before your hand goes on anyone. I ask a dying man before I put my hands on him, and if he says no I sit with him and don't. You've got a piece of the thing I read with. It comes with that, or it doesn't come."

"Yes," Cael said.

"Say it whole."

"I ask first. Every time."

Oryn took all of it back into the house with her without writing anything down, and he entered it, because one of them had to.

---

Karis documented all of it, because that was her seat, and she did it on the afternoon of the third day in the loft, with the three boxes open round her and her voice back.

It had come back as Oryn had said: in pieces, hoarse, over days; and she had used it for one sentence in the loft the night before the reading and not again until the second morning, when she had said *charcoal* to Brom in a voice like a dry hinge and been so plainly pleased with it that Brom had gone and found her some. By the third day it was a low, torn, usable thing, and she used it, and the notebook lay open beside her for the words that would not come out whole.

"Tenth." She had the sheet on the middle box's lid, turned to face him: the curve she had been drawing since Greyvane, ten points on it now. "First non-combat. First clinical. First one taken lying down." That last was not on the sheet, and she did not write it. "Source-grade Iron. The arithmetic says two days to an idle state and it's been two and a half, and the half's the new register — you've never had one that listens, and there's no comparison class for what it costs to learn to stop hearing something. So. Holding. The curve holds." She put her finger on the tenth point. "For now."

"You always enter that."

"I enter it because the model has no ceiling and a model with no ceiling is a model. And I'm entering something else this time, at the same weight, and I want you to hear it in my voice and not read it in my hand, because it's the sort of thing that reads as a complaint and isn't." She turned the sheet a little, so that the light off the loft's window lay across the curve. "Ten. Ten sources, ten Paths, and the tenth one does nothing in a fight, and the tenth one is the first one that has ever let anybody — you — look at the thing I've been charting from outside for four years. I have ten points on a curve and no instrument. You have an instrument now. It's not mine. It never will be. I'm entering that I know that."

"Karis," he said.

"I'm not asking you to use it on me." She said it before he could say anything, and her voice went on the last word and came back. "I'm telling you I've noticed that you could. That's all. It goes in the record as an observation about the researcher and not about the subject, and then I'm going to close the box."

She closed the box.

---

The birthday fell on the third day, and Lira instigated it, one-armed, against the last of Thornwater's autumn — and both were true at once, because both were.

She did it at the house door at midday, to the steading's woman: "It's his birthday. Nineteen. We've got three days' marks and a healer and a mule, and I want your common room and whatever you'll sell us that isn't bread." The woman looked at the sling's knot, said "Well," which was a ledger line, and named a price, and Lira paid it out of the pouch one-handed without putting the pouch down, which she had been practising for a week and had not said so.

Brom acquired the food. He did it without any order ever being heard placed: he spoke to the woman's husband in the yard for a while about something that was not food, and came back an hour later with a boy carrying one end of a board, and on the board there were three kinds — a kid roasted whole from the hold's own pen, the first meat in a fortnight that had not been cut into sixths; apples baked soft with the last of a comb of honey in their middles; and a cheese, whole, in a cloth, from a hold that had three kinds of everything because it had a wall and a well and a woman who counted. He set the board down with the mended hand doing its share, looked at it, said, "Three," and sat.

The common room. He catalogued it, because he loved it, and that was how: a long room under the loft with the second chimney in it, the fire lit for them and not for the hold; the plank table; a bench either side; a window onto the yard with the light going yellow in it; a door that shut. Six places. Lira at the fire's end with the arm across her and the foot up on the bench, refusing to explain it. Brom beside the board. Karis with the notebook shut for once, on the bench, her throat gone from green to the yellow of the apples. Seln at the door end, where the two doors were, sitting like a man who had sat at tables all his life, eating what he was given. And Oryn.

Oryn sat at the table the way a woman sits who has sat at a great many tables and stayed at none of them. She had the seat that faced the yard, and she ate — properly, for the first time since the bend, as a person eats who has been spending and has stopped — and she watched the five of them be a unit, with the same look she had given the stone at the line: a thing with a rule in it. She said perhaps nine things all evening. Four of them were about food.

He was eighteen at the start of the meal and nineteen by its middle, and the middle was where he wrote it, at the table, in his own hand, with the pencil out of the spine and the book open on the board's edge between the cheese and the apples, per the custom now four birthdays old and the first kept without an institution's wall round it:

*Hundred and second day past the Line. Nineteen. Written at Thornwater, in the steading's common room, autumn, the fire lit for us. Witnesses: four of my own, and a healer who's a stop.*

"A stop," Oryn said, reading it upside down, as she had read the map. "Good. Write it down before it changes."

The carter came at the third apple.

The steading's boy put his head in at the door and said a word to Seln, who was nearest, and Seln went out and came back with a slip of thin paper folded to a palm's width, and put it on the board in front of Cael without a word. Pike's hand: the upright letters that priced the void roads, on the ledger's own stock, the copy the board-keeper sent east to a crew when a line went in, because the book was for him and the saying was for them.

*Recovery, Fallow road, broker's posting. Taken. Witnessed. Completed: incomplete — instruments not recovered — crew returned. Six went in. Six came out. No price paid.*

He read it twice, the second time for the count, because Pike's counts were the whole of Pike, and it said six. Then he set it beside a note he had read on a bluff, in a different hand, that had closed a file with the words *the instruments will not see its like*, and he laid the two of them side by side in the place he kept such things: one closed complete, and just, by a man walking his own paper across a counter; one closed incomplete, and true, by a one-armed man who priced a crew by what came back, and had written on the copy the only line that mattered to him: that the number going in and the number coming out were the same.

He put it in the satchel against the leather book, flat, where the others were. There was nothing else in the carter's bag with his name on it. The ferry was a month and had been late, and the chain from a shop in Denvash to a steading two days east of a town on no map was a long one. He did not look at the door twice. Lira, who had watched him not look, cut the cheese one-handed against her knee, and gave him the first piece, which was the size of two.

"Nineteen," Brom said, to the board, as a weight.

"Nineteen," said Karis, in the dry-hinge voice, and it went into the notebook after all, because some things did.

Seln, at the door end, looked at the satchel, and said, to nobody, in the voice for an inventory: "Six went in. Six came out. He's counted her." He went back to his plate. "So has the broker."

Nobody answered that, and Lira refilled his cup, which was an answer.

It ran until the fire was down to a working red. Oryn told them, when asked, the seven holds in order, and what each owed her and what she owed each, and it was the longest she had spoken since the bend, and she did it without once saying she was going back to it, because that did not need saying. Lira kept the taped foot on the bench and dared anyone to remark on the arm. Karis and Brom argued for a quarter of an hour, hoarse against deep, about whether an apple baked with honey in it was a thing the hold had *made* or a thing it had *had*, and Karis lost on the merits and said so. Seln left first, with a nod that took in the whole table and stayed on the healer a breath longer than the rest, and at the door he did not turn round.

He caught himself, past the middle of it, running the room as an instrument — six places, one board, two doors, a fire — and put the instrument down. Not the new one; he had no hand on that yet. The old one; the count. For the better part of an hour he did nothing with any of it, which was the second time this year and the first time on ground no registry had drawn.

---

He did the thing she had asked him to do that night, alone, in the loft, with the five of them asleep round him and the lantern out, because she had said *first* and *before anyone* and he had said *yes*.

He had not tried it in three days; the currents had come up out of the general flow on their own, and he had let them and learned their sounds, and had not once turned the new thing on himself on purpose, because on purpose was the reading, and the reading was hers, and she had set a condition on it. He met it now. He lay on his back on the sacks with one hand flat on his sternum and the other under his back as far as it would go, which was not far, and did what she had done: went in, and went along.

Ten. He counted them because he counted things, and it came out ten: the nine he had learned by ear in three nights, and the tenth, which he could feel now that he was using it, as a man feels his own eye when he shuts the other. He could not find their banks. That was the first finding and it was hers already, from outside; from inside it was the same. He went along Wind and could not say where Wind stopped and Pressure began; he went along Iron and found it running under everything, and could not find the floor it ran under. Ten flows, and no edge to any of them, and the not-finding was a fact about the reading and not about the thing read, and he made himself write that down before he wrote anything else, so that it would be on the page above whatever came next.

Then he went to the place session nine had logged.

He knew where it was. He had known for five years, in the way a man knows where a step on a stair is uneven: a place in himself that Brom's Iron read had caught the edge of once on a floor in Ardenmere, with no Tide practitioner in the city, at no stakes, and had never caught again. He went to it now with the new thing, slowly, the way she had gone along the ninth, and found it.

It was there.

He lay very still with his hands on himself and catalogued it as sensation, because he had promised her the finding and had promised the log no verb. It was not a current. That was the first thing and the plainest: everything else in him *went*, and this did not. It was not the tenth; the tenth was what he was feeling it with, and a hand is not the thing it touches. It was not any of the nine; he had their sounds now, and this had none. It was a place. Near the middle — under where her front hand had been, where everything was *nearer* — and the ten flows went round it. Not through. Round, the way the currents in a river go round a thing on the bed that they have gone round for so long that the going-round is the river's shape and not the thing's.

He did not name it. He did not have a name that was not a guess, and a guess was what the registry had done to him at fourteen.

He knew three things about it that were not guesses, and he counted them. It had been there before Oryn; session nine was five years old and she was two months. It had been there before Brom's read caught it, because a read catches what is already there. And it was not the Quiet — the Quiet was outside him, a line on the ground that stopped Paths, and this was inside, and the fragments went round it, and had been going round it for longer than he had had any of them.

He took his hands off himself, and felt, from his own side, the shape of the reading going, as he had felt hers go, and then that was gone too, and he was a man on a grain sack in a loft with five people breathing round him and a healer's condition met.

---

He wrote the inventory by the last of the lantern's oil, which the woman had refilled without being asked, in the plain hand.

*Hundred and second day, late. Thornwater, the loft. A hundred and eight in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Pike's copy in the satchel: six went in, six came out, no price paid. Nineteen.*

*Inventory. Ten confirmed. Wind-adjacent — Lira. Pressure-adjacent — the Bronze from Ardenmere. Iron-adjacent — Brom. Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Ember-adjacent — Karis. Shadow-adjacent — source protected; the seal holds out here as it held inside; used once past the Line, on a floor, in front of the circle and nobody else. Storm-adjacent — Daeva; resettled once this week round the new neighbour, and I heard it do it. Anchor-adjacent — unnamed; the hand with no face; it wanted the stake and it can want. Blade-adjacent — Ephram; the only non-hostile in the log until three days ago. Tide-adjacent — Oryn. Partial. Iron. Self and contact. Engagement: clinical. Does nothing in a fight, and is the first thing I've ever owned that lets me hear the other nine. Cost: three days of listening; the idle state learned by the third night, and it isn't quiet, it's just that I've stopped putting my hand to every string. Deployment notes current on all ten.*

*Karis says the curve holds, for now, and that I have an instrument and she doesn't. Both entered.*

*The condition, in its parts. Oryn: use it on myself first, before anyone — done tonight. Ask, every time, before it goes on anyone else — said whole in the yard, and not yet tested, because I've not turned it on anyone. Tell her what I find — owed, not done; she's asleep, and it's hers in the morning, and it goes below as what it is and not what it means, so that I tell her what's written and not what I feel like by daylight.*

*The anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Turned over — for the first time in five years, with a Tide-adjacent fragment in hand to turn it over with. The three words are retired. They were the right three for a thing I couldn't see and could only wait on; I've had them at the foot of every inventory since Ardenmere, and they've done their work, and I'm taking them out tonight for a reason, so here's the reason.*

*Still open. Still real. Now I can see it. That's worse. The tenth fragment lets me feel my own currents, and there's one in the place session nine logged, and it isn't the tenth, and it isn't any of the nine. It was there before Oryn. It was there before Brom's read caught it. I don't know what it is. I know where it is now. Patience is over; observation begins.*

*Daeva's rematch, Reydan's answer — both ride east. Nineteen. Ten fragments. Hesk's package is on a ferry somewhere. The last one said mind the shape. I'm starting to.*

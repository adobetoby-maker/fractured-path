# Chapter 5 — Route

The slip was in a hold's hand, and the hold was one he had never heard of.

He read it at the plank on the seventieth morning past the Line, in the order the board taught, which was every slip once and then the ones that paid twice: *Oxhollow ford. High traverse to Thornwater wanted scouted before the autumn carts. Shale bench above the gorge; two days under the void road; not walked three seasons, since the slide. Map wanted, and a word on whether it holds a cart. Seventy marks on the map, paid at Oxhollow.*

Oxhollow was a ford, north, a week by the void road; that was the whole of what the plank knew about it.

Thirteen slips. Two of them, the good two, wore Pike's HOLD and Teague's name across the top in the same pencil; the crew had been in off the autumn road and out again with the hold slips while the five of them were at the well on the south road. That left eleven: one hold's, four escorts at the carters' price, and fences and wells and a roof that wanted thatching. He sorted them by what each would cost the five of them, and the sorting put Oxhollow's slip at the top by itself before he had finished reading it the second time.

He counted why, because he did not trust a thing that sorted itself.

It paid. Seventy marks was the ford's price and three-quarters again, for a walk. Holds priced what a thing was worth to the hold, and a route two days under the void road was worth a great deal to a ford that sent carts south in autumn.

Teague's crew was gone on the hold contracts, a week out and a week back, and Oxhollow's slip had come in on a carter behind them and sat four days on the plank with nobody's finger on it. Pike had said, to the page, when Cael asked why: "Pays on the map, at Oxhollow. That's a week north to get paid for a week's walk, and the crews that could take it are the crews with the pick. Nobody's been on the board long enough to want it."

And the third reason, which he counted last because it was his: it was ground. Not teeth. A scouting run was a thing walked and measured and written down and handed to a man who would trust a cart to the writing, and he had done that every day of his life since he was fourteen, in a notebook, for nothing, at the edges of rooms where the work being paid for was somebody else's. He had never once been paid to catalogue. He read the slip a third time to be sure. It was what it said.

"That one," he said.

Seln, beside him, had already put a finger on it and moved it a quarter-inch on the plank.

---

Pike entered it as a price gets entered, flat, with the five names under it, and said the things he said.

"Three seasons nobody's walked it. Slide came down on the Thornwater end and the carts went round by the void road and stopped talking about the bench. Oxhollow doesn't want a crew that comes back and says it's fine." The pen did not stop. "They want a map they can put a cart on. Width at every narrow. Where it holds and where it doesn't. Where you'd camp." A pause, at the end of a line. "Take a cart. Smith has one, six marks against it coming back. You want to tell a ford it holds a cart, you'd better have had one on it."

He said one more thing, to Seln and not to the rest of them, when Seln stopped at the plank's end on the way out: "Ask me about the shale-backs before you go." Seln asked, there, with his back to the room, and Cael did not go to hear the answer, because a man who asked a thing quietly at the end of a plank had asked it that way on purpose.

The cart was the smith's second-best, two wheels and a bed and a pair of shafts with a chest-strap between them. Brom stood in the shafts and put the strap across his chest and leaned on it once, and it moved, and he nodded, and that was the cart's whole trial. The bound arm went inside the right shaft against his ribs. The good hand took the left.

The boxes went because there was nowhere to leave them. Lowmarch had no wall and a population that turned over with the season, and three boxes in a meadow belonged to whoever walked past them last. Karis roped them onto the cart's bed herself, the front box over the axle and the other two behind it, and put her hand flat on the middle lid when she was done, as if to see whether it was warm.

Inventory, taken on the smith's yard on the seventy-first morning.

The calf: closed, mostly. He had stopped counting on the flat, because the count had gone past twenty and a number that large was not a limp, it was a leg. Uphill it still said nine. He had not told anyone the uphill number, and there was a mountain in the slip.

Brom's forearm: bound wrist to elbow still, but the wrist had closed at last, the cloth coming off clean on the sixty-sixth day, and he had looked at the closed place with the lock face and wrapped it again as if it had not. The hand was allowed to hold. It was not allowed to pull. The chest-strap pulled.

Lira's ankle: the stick was gone, leaned against the inn wall on the sixty-fourth day and walked away from. What she had instead was cloth — the steading's boiled linen cut into strips, wound each morning from the arch up over the joint and tied off at the shin, tight, so that the ankle bent as far as the cloth allowed and no further, which was less than the other, and later. She called it taping. She did it before her boots, sitting, with her mouth shut.

Karis's hands: two lines across each palm, pink and raised, that she flexed against the pen every morning like a woman working a hinge. The letters had come back to their size. She did not look at the palms while she wrote, and Cael did not look at them either.

Five. All walking. None of it healed by anybody.

---

Two days up the Thornwater road to the fan, with the cart; the stretch walked at noon, quiet. They did not go up to Thornwater's gate. The slide had come down a mile short of the steading, and the road went over the toe of what it had left and the traverse went up its side, and there was a cairn, knocked half over, where the two parted. Seln set the stones back on it while the rest of them looked up.

The fan was the worst ground of the route, and Cael catalogued it from the bottom: a spill of broken shale the width of a field, lying at the angle at which shale stops, every plate the size of a hand or a door and none of them fixed to anything. Somewhere on the mountain above it a shelf ran along over the gorge, and the shelf was the traverse, and the fan was where a piece of the shelf had come down.

They took the cart up it on a rope, three ahead on the rope and Brom in the shafts holding the bed level, and it took the whole morning, and the calf said nine the entire way.

---

The bench, when they reached it, was a shelf.

He wrote that down first, at the first halt, because it was the fact everything else stood on. A shelf of grey shale along the mountain's face, a cart and a half wide where they came onto it and sloping toward the outer edge as a roof slopes — not steep, but not flat, so that anything set down on it wanted to go one way. On the left the mountain went up in the same stone, plated, and two hundred meters up, by the eye, a second shelf ran along it, greener than theirs: an upper bench. On the right the shelf stopped, and past where it stopped there was nothing for a long way down, and at the bottom of the nothing a white line that was a river.

The gorge had a sound. On the bench it resolved into water, far down, and wind coming up, and a third thing that was neither: the gorge took every sound they made and gave it back late, from the far wall. Brom set the cart's tail-board down at the first halt and the bang came back a breath afterward from across the gap, smaller, as if a second crew were working over there and were more careful. It was the loudest thing about the place, that it repeated you.

Lira went ahead.

She had taped the ankle at the fan's foot and again at the top, tighter, and then she went out along the bench at a run — not a burst; a burst was a shout, she had learned that on a gravel bar with seven dogs turning toward her — a hundred paces out over the shale with nothing under it but her, and came back at a walk, reading the ground with her feet. Shale that held a walker held nothing, she said; shale that held a runner held a wheel. She ran the bench in hundreds all afternoon and the cart followed in her prints, and at the evening halt she sat down before anyone and took the boot off and did not look at the ankle, and then looked at it.

Karis mapped.

She had paced a length of the void road against the folding rule on the first morning out of Lowmarch and had a number for her stride she trusted; now she walked the bench counting, the pen in the scarred hand, and at every place where the shelf narrowed she ran the cord across it edge to edge and wrote the width, and the count to it from the last width, and the pitch, which she took with the cord and a stone hung on it. Where the gorge came close she dropped a stone over and counted until the sound came up. What went into the book was a chain of numbers with a drawing beside it that looked like nothing at all — a line, kinked, with figures along it — and would look, to a man at Oxhollow with a cart, like the only true thing on the mountain.

Brom kept the cart on the mountain.

He said, at the second halt, that a cart on scree did not roll; it floated. The wheels sat on plates that sat on plates and the whole of it went where the slope wanted, which was over the edge, a finger's width at every turn of the wheel, unless the weight was over the axle and up-slope. He re-roped the boxes at every halt to keep the front one where it was, took the down-slope shaft himself as the counter, and walked the whole day with the cart's want against his chest, and it was — Cael watched for the word — a lock somebody else had built, being held shut by hand.

Seln was ahead of Lira.

Nobody saw him go past her; he was simply further along, and then further, and in the last hour of the light, when the cart came round a shoulder of the mountain, he was standing in a place where the bench widened to three carts and the rock above it leaned out over a floor that was flat and dry and out of the wind. He was standing in it as he was always standing in the place where they would stop, before anybody had said the word.

"Here," he said, which was the whole report.

Cael wrote.

At every halt, on the cart's tail-board or his knee, as he had written in a notebook at the edge of every bout he had ever watched: the width at the narrow, in Karis's number and then in his own paces to check it; the count of narrowings from the fan — one, two, three by the first camp; where Seln had stood. Nothing on the mountain asked him what he was. It was the exact shape of a thing he had done for nothing for four years, except that at the far end of it there was a ford and seventy marks and a man who would read it.

---

*Seventy-third day. The bench, first camp. Three narrowings from the fan, none under a cart's width.*

*Inventory. Nine confirmed, one anomaly, patience. Eighteen. Deployment since the Line: one, the ford, paid. Nothing drifting. Anchor wanted the inn door; there is no door up here. Calf: nine uphill, and the whole fan was uphill, and it's a leg again on the flat. Sixty-two marks earned. Forty-six in hand after the cart.*

*Lira runs it before we walk it. Karis has a number for everything and a drawing that looks like nothing. Brom says the cart floats. I've written eleven widths since noon and none of them mine, and it's the first day of my life I've been paid by the line.*

*The gorge says everything back late. That's the only loud thing on this mountain. Us, twice.*

---

He saw the first of them on the second day because it moved.

They had come round the fourth narrowing — a cart's width and a hand, Karis's cord said, with the outer edge crumbling — and the bench opened again beyond it, and on the upper shelf two hundred meters above, where the green was, a piece of the mountain lifted its head.

Lira, ahead, had stopped with a hand up; the cart stopped behind him with Brom's breath going out of it.

The colour of the shale, which was why he had not seen it. Then, because he knew what he was looking for, the next one, and the next, and he counted them as he counted a crowd, left to right and again, and got twenty-nine and then thirty-one and then thirty, and kept thirty. Thirty animals along the upper bench, every one of them with its face down against the rock. Plated across the back in slabs that overlapped like the stone of a roof, grey going to rust as the shale went; the head small and low for the body and the legs short and thick under it; each one, by the eye at that distance, the size of the cart with its load and Brom in the shafts. They were eating lichen — the green was lichen, on the upper shelf where the wind did not scour it — and the sound he had taken for the gorge since the narrowing was them: a grinding, patient and low, thirty sets of teeth on stone, coming down to the lower bench and then back from the far wall, late.

Placid was Pike's word for them, by Seln's report, and from below it was the word: they looked like animals that had not been consulted about anything in a long time and had not minded. Nothing about them had noticed a cart.

"Shale-backs," said Seln, from ahead, low, in the road's own quiet. "Pike's rule. He said it twice, so I'm saying it twice." He was not looking at the herd; he was looking at the bench under them and the bench above. "Never be below them. Never be *loud* below them."

Cael looked up at the herd and down at the shelf they stood on and did the arithmetic the rule required, which was short. The route was the lower bench. The herd was on the upper. There was no walking this traverse that was not walking below them; the first half of the rule was a thing Oxhollow's carts would break every autumn by taking the road at all. The first half was a carter's wish. The second half was the job.

"They'll follow the lichen," said Karis. She had the book open and was not writing in it. "That's the whole shelf."

"Then they follow it," Seln said. "Above us. All the way."

Karis said, without heat, as one asks for a thing already paid for: "I want a day."

He said yes because a day of her looking had always been worth more than a day of anything else, and for one reason more, which he did not say: he wanted the day too. Thirty of a thing nobody had a page on, standing still in good light. He had never in his life been given a day to look at anything that was not about to hit him.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Here. Seln?"

Seln looked at the overhang a hundred paces on and said "There," and it was a camp. They walked under the herd for the rest of the afternoon and the herd did not look at them once.

---

*Seventy-fourth day. Second camp. Five narrowings. Thirty shale-backs on the upper bench, two hundred meters up by the eye. Armoured. The size of the cart. They eat lichen off the rock and the sound of it comes across the gorge twice. Placid is Pike's word. Mine is that they haven't noticed us, and I don't think it's because we're quiet. I think a cart is nothing to them.*

*Seln quoted Pike: never be below them, never be loud below them. We are below them. Every cart Oxhollow ever sends will be below them. So the rule out here is the second half, and we've been keeping it since the Line without knowing it had a second half. Karis gets tomorrow. So do I.*

---

Karis sat on a flat plate at the camp's edge with the herd above her and the book on her knee and did not move for the first hour except her eyes.

He sat a length from her with his own book and catalogued the herd as he would have catalogued a fighter he was going to have to watch for a season: how they stood; what they did with the weight; what a head did before a body moved. Thirty animals, and not one of them made a movement in an hour that he would have called a decision. Lira had the day too, on the cart's tail with the boot off and the ankle in the sun.

Karis read out what she saw as it went in, to nobody.

"Spacing. Never closer than a body. Never further than three. Thirty, and the spacing holds." A while. "They've moved. The whole line's a length along the shelf from where it was at dawn. I didn't see one of them move. I saw the line be somewhere else."

At noon Brom let the tail-board go, and the bang came back off the far wall, and thirty heads did not lift. An hour after that a rock came off the mountain, loud on the way down, and went through the herd's line between two of them and into the gorge. The two it had passed between stepped a body's width from the place. Then they stepped back. Then they ate.

"Sound and a rock," Karis said. "Nothing. That's not flight. That's a step. The carters didn't make a rule about a step."

The thing she was waiting for came in the middle of the afternoon, and it was small.

An animal at the shelf's outer edge had its weight on a plate that had been holding its weight all day, and the plate tilted. Not far. It went a hand's width at the outer side and slid its own length down the shelf's pitch with the animal on it, as a floor-board goes when the joist under it is gone — and the animal walked off it, uphill, without hurry, and the plate stopped against the next plate and lay there.

The herd moved.

All of it. Thirty animals, along the whole length of the upper shelf that he could see, took a body's length uphill at the same moment, without a head coming up, without a sound that reached him, as if the shelf had been a table and somebody had tilted it and every cup on it had slid the same way at once. Then they stopped. Then they ate.

Karis read it out as it went in.

"A plate went under one at the outer edge. It stepped off, uphill. The herd went uphill, one length, all at the same time. No leader. I've been looking for one since dawn and there isn't one; there's no animal that moves first. They moved as a unit. Away."

"Away from what?" said Lira, from the cart.

"From where the ground went." Karis got up and went to the cart. The three boxes rode roped at its front, the foremost over the axle where Brom kept it, the middle one with a corner worn white where the rope ran; she untied nothing and put her hand under the middle lid and came out with the one thin page in Cael's hand that was the whole of her shelf on this side of the Line, and laid it beside the day's page. There was a rumor with two lines under it in that box too, near the bottom, and she did not take it out.

"The ford," she said. "Read it to me."

He read it, because it was in his hand. "They hunt discharge. They're near-blind to a still body. They turn as one to the loudest declaration, and otherwise to the loudest sound. Water masks it. They break toward whichever hound moves first."

"Whichever moves first." She put her scarred finger on the day's page, on *no leader*. "Those turn as one to the loudest thing and then follow the first to go. These go as one and there's no first. Same shape, upside down. Now the rule. Seln."

"Never be below them. Never be loud below them."

"Loud." Karis said it as a word she was weighing. "A rock off the mountain is loud. A tail-board is loud. They stepped. A step doesn't kill a cart, and the carters didn't make a rule about one. So *loud* in the rule isn't what a rock does. What does a carter mean when he tells a crew to shut up?"

Nobody had to say it. Brom said it anyway, because it was the shortest way. "Don't declare."

"Don't declare. The ford taught us what out here means by *loud*, and it isn't sound. The dogs hunt it. The carters walk quiet through the dogs' country and they walk quiet under these, and it's one word, and I'll enter that the word means one thing. Derived. The herd senses discharge — the ford's sense. The herd's answer is the ford's answer with the sign changed: a hunter comes to it; a grazer goes from it. Flight, not hunt. As a unit, with no first animal, so all thirty at once. Away from the declaration." She stopped the pen. "They went uphill from the slip because the slip was below them. If the loud thing is below them—"

"They go up," said Lira.

"And if it's *above* them."

Seln answered as he answered everything. "Then they come down."

"Thirty of them," Karis said. "The size of that cart. On this." She did not point at the shale; she did not need to. "On a slope, flight is a landslide with legs."

The gorge gave the last of it back to them, late, smaller.

She entered the rest with the pen going and her voice flat. "Not seen. Not tested. Not going to be tested; there's no trial I'd run under thirty of anything on scree. Entered as derived. If it's wrong it's wrong in the direction of being careful, and I'll take that." She underlined *derived* once. "That's the day."

"That's the rule," Seln said, "with the reason on it."

Karis closed the book with the ford's page inside it. "First primary source I've had on this side of the Line. It's got a hundred and twenty legs and it doesn't know I'm here." She looked at Cael. "Write it in yours, too. I'd like it in two hands."

He wrote it in his, beneath the count of thirty, and beneath that the thing she had not said: the ford had been theirs because they had known the rule before the dogs came out of the water, and this was the same kind of knowing, arrived at sitting down.

---

The bench narrowed on the fourth day.

Six, seven, eight — Karis's cord at each, and the widths coming down: a cart and a half, a cart and a hand, a cart. At the eighth the outer edge was not crumbling, it was gone, a clean break where a length of the shelf had let go some season past; Lira ran it first, twice, and came back and said *it holds a runner* and did not say the rest, and Brom took the cart across it with the down-slope wheel a hand from nothing and his whole weight on the up-slope shaft and his face shut, and it went across, and the tail-board did not bang, because nobody let it.

The herd came along above them. It drifted with the lichen, at the pace a cart makes on scree, which is not a pace; it was above them at every halt and above them at the next, thirty grey backs on the green, as if the contract had hired them too. They did not look down. They walked it as they had walked it since the fan: Lira in hundreds, Karis in numbers, Brom against the strap, Seln somewhere ahead in the place they would sleep, and the whole of it quiet in the one way that mattered, which none of them had to think about anymore and every one of them was thinking about.

He wrote at every halt. The widths. The count. The herd's position, by the narrowing it stood above. He was twice as careful with a page that somebody would pay for as he had ever been with one that nobody would.

The fourth camp was an overhang again, because Seln found overhangs. The fire was small. Lira taped the ankle by it, as she did at night now as well as in the morning, and nobody said anything about it.

---

*Seventy-sixth day. Fourth camp. Eight narrowings; the eighth is a cart and no more, and the edge is a break, not a crumble. Karis has it in the book with the break drawn. Two-thirds of the way by Pike's count of days, a little under by mine.*

*The traverse holds. So far. The map's good — Karis's, not mine; mine's the check. The herd's above the eighth narrowing tonight, thirty, grazing along. Karis says they go off declarations, as a unit, away, and on a slope away is down; which means the loudest thing on this mountain is us, and we've been quiet four days. Not one of us has had to try.*

*Nothing's drifted in four days. I'm writing that as a fact and not as a meaning. First honest job I've ever had that didn't have a fight at the end of it. I'm writing that before it's finished, which I don't do. Note that I did.*

---

The fifth day showed them the ledge.

They saw it from the ninth narrowing in the last of the light, because the bench had bent round a shoulder of the mountain and given them the next stretch whole: the shelf running on along the face, a cart wide, then less than a cart, then — a long way ahead, where the mountain bulged out over the gorge — pinching down to a lip that was, at that distance, a line. A ledge. Below it, the gorge, wider there than anywhere they had seen it. Above it, the upper shelf, coming round the same shoulder with the herd on it, thirty backs on the last green, above them and — he counted the narrowings back — behind them now, for the first time, by half a day's grazing. Beyond the ledge, the bench opened again and went on toward Oxhollow.

Karis stood with the cord in her hand and did not run it across anything. "I can't measure that from here. Morning."

"Here," Seln agreed, and it was, of course, an overhang.

Two-thirds. He did the count on the tail-board while the fire was lit: nine narrowings in the book with their widths; Pike's seven or eight days for a cart, and five of them walked. A map a hold could put a cart on, for two-thirds of a mountain. The last third began with a line.

Lira sat down by the fire before anyone and took off the boot.

She did it as she had done it every night on the bench: the day's cloth unwound and laid across her knee, the ankle turned in the firelight as far as it would turn, which was less than the other one, and later; and then the night's strip, from the arch up over the joint and off at the shin, pulled tight, tied. It was the ankle that had never been seen by anyone who could do more for it than boil the cloth. She had not stopped taping it.

She tied it off and looked, not at any of them, at the shoulder of the mountain with the ledge on the far side of it.

"I'll run it at first light," she said. "See if it holds a cart."

Brom, from the strap, which he had not taken off: "It'll hold you."

"It'll hold me, and I'll know what it holds." She put the boot on over the tape and stood on it, once, to feel where it bent. "That's what I'm for up here. That's the job. First light, before the sun's on it."

Nobody argued. It had been the plan at every narrowing since the fan, and the ledge was a narrowing with a name.

Seln was looking up the mountain at the herd. Brom took the strap off at last and sat with the bound arm inside the good one. The gorge gave the fire's small noises back to them, late, from across the gap.

He took the log out.

---

*Seventy-seventh day. Fifth camp, at the ninth narrowing. The ledge is in sight; Lira runs it at first light.*

*The traverse holds. The map's good. Karis says the herd moves off declarations, which means the loudest thing on this mountain is us, and we've been quiet for five days. One more day. First honest job I've ever had that didn't have a fight at the end of it. Sleep.*

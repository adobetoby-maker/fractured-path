# Chapter 5 — What the Records Say

They read it at a spring a mile past the shoulder hold on the morning of the two hundred and eightieth day, because the road widened there to let carts pass and the ground was dry and the wind was off the water, and because Brom's foot wanted an hour off it and Vastin's legs wanted more than that and would not say so.

He catalogued the halt before the flat was opened, because the flat was going to change the halt.

A spring out of the road's uphill bank, stone-lined by somebody paid to line it, with a trough under it for carts' animals and a flat slab beside the trough where a carter could sit and eat. The mule at the trough. Brom on the slab's end with the boot off and the foot up on the kit's case, saying nothing about it, which said everything. Lira on the road, above and below by turns, keeping it, because a road that somebody kept was still a road with a pack three hills behind it. Oryn on the bank above the spring with her roll beside her and her hands on her knees, watching a man with a stick not sit down. Vastin standing, because he had been told he was spent and did not yet believe it, with the stick planted and his weight on it and his face the colour it had been on the road's stone, a day paler. Karis at the slab with the flat.

And Seln, at the slab's other end, who had asked for the flat with his eyes and had been given it, which was a thing Cael had not seen Karis do for anyone.

Three of them read it. He catalogued that first, because it was the thing he had never seen: a file read three ways at once by three people who had each spent a life reading files and had never once read one together. Karis read it for the pattern — the sheets laid out on the slab in the six rows she had made on an inn table, weighted at the corners with stones from the trough, her hand going along a row and stopping and going on, the pencil in the other hand not yet used. Seln read it for what was not in it — he did not touch the rows; he stood at the slab's end with his shoulder to the wind and read the sheets where they lay, upside down or sideways as they came, and what he was reading was the gaps, the places where a sheet that should have been there was not, the dates that did not follow, the hands that changed. And Vastin read it for what it meant that the Compact had done it at all — standing, on the stick, looking down at six years of his own office's procedure laid out on a carter's slab beside a trough, and reading it as a man reads his own house from the street after he has sold it.

Cael read the three of them. It was the only thing at the slab he was qualified to read.

The rest of the halt went on around the slab as a halt goes on. Oryn came down off the bank once, to look at Brom's foot without being asked, and put two fingers on the binding and said, "It'll walk," and went back up, and did not look at the flat, because paper was not a thing she read and she had said so at a table. Brom looked at the flat the way he looked at a wall somebody else was pricing — with respect, and no intention of touching it. Lira went past on the road above, and down again below, and on the third pass stopped at the trough and drank from her two hands, and looked at the left one after, and went on. The mule ate. The spring ran. Seven people and an animal at a carter's halt on a kept road, with six years of a continent's hidden weather on a slab between them and a rise to the west with a city behind it, and nobody, Cael noted, looking west.

---

"Instruments," Karis said, first, because it was the first thing. "Not one man with a cord. An office's instruments, on an office's form." Her hand on a sheet in the second row. "This is a surveyor's abstract from — five years back, the Stair, the same hand as the kit. Radius, two hundred. Stake count. And here, in the column the kit's ledger doesn't have — the office's column — a note of what he carried and what he did with it: *cord, stakes, mallet; perimeter paced twice; stakes set at the fives.* Twice. He paced a floor in a bowl twice, five years ago, and set iron at every fifth stride, and wrote it in a form." She turned a sheet. "The Ring, four years back. Different hand. *Paced once; stakes set.* The Hall, three years back, from the marsh side — *perimeter paced along the bank; stakes not set; ground unsuitable.* They didn't go in. They walked the marsh's edge with a cord, as we walked the fourth's line, and got two hundred, and wrote it down." She put her hand flat on the row. "Six years. Instruments. Stakes at every one they could drive them at. They measured everything, and they measured it properly, and they wrote it in a form, and the form went on a shelf."

"That's what an office does," Vastin said, from the stick. "It measures. It is very good at measuring."

"It's good at measuring." Karis did not look up. "It's not good at asking what it measured. There isn't a column for that."

Seln said, "There's a column."

They both looked at him. He had not moved. He was looking at the fourth row, sideways, at a sheet near its end.

"Not for asking," he said. "For not asking. Fifth column from the left, every sheet, every year. The office's abbreviation. You read it as *total* at the inn — the sigil going out at the line, every practitioner, every site. Read what's beside it." He did not point. Seln did not point at paper; pointing was a thing that left a mark. "Same distance. Same instant. Every sheet. And in the sixth column, where a surveyor puts his remark, there's nothing. Six years, more than twenty sheets, and the remark column is empty on every one but one."

Karis found the one. It took her the length of a breath — the sheet in the fourth row, near its end, a hand older than the rest, a form of an older ruling with the columns a hair narrower — and she drew it out, and held it to the light off the water, and read the sixth column, which had four words in it, in a hand that had been dead, by the date at the sheet's head, longer than Cael had been alive.

She read it aloud once, at the size of a finding.

"*Behaves as device. Cannot be.*"

Nobody said anything.

"That's the whole of the remark," Karis said. "Four words. He wrote *behaves as device* and then he wrote *cannot be*, and he didn't cross either out, and he filed it, and nobody in the office wrote anything in that column again for the rest of the file." She looked at the four words for a while. "He saw what we saw. Every sigil, every Path, every person, going out at a line at the same stride and the same instant, the way a hundred lamps go out when — " and she stopped.

Cael saw her stop. He saw where the sentence had been going, because he had heard her not go there before, at a table at Thornwater with a ring on it, and he saw her put her pencil down on the slab and take her hand off the sheet and say, to the sheet, in the small voice, the thing she said when she needed a rule:

"A measurement is a fact about a ruler. Not about the hand that held it."

"You've said that before," Vastin said.

"I say it when I need it." She put the sheet back at the end of the fourth row, in its place, and did not write anything under it. "A dead surveyor wrote four words in a margin and didn't cross them out. That's a fact about a surveyor. I'm entering it as what it is: an observation I can't file. I've a page for those. It's getting long."

Vastin looked at her, and at the sheet, and Cael watched him reach for something — the first of them; he would count three before the halt was done — and it was there in the set of the man's shoulders and in his free hand coming up an inch from the stick: a directive. *File under—. Classification—. Refer to—.* The shape of an office's answer to a thing it could not answer, forty years deep in him, arriving in his mouth as it had arrived ten thousand times, and he opened his mouth on it, and there was no office to send it to, and it died there, and he shut his mouth on nothing, and looked at the trough.

Seln saw it too. Seln said nothing. Yet.

---

The grade second.

Karis found it because she was looking for it, and she had been looking for it since a kit's ledger by a marsh, and Cael knew the look on her when she found a thing she had been looking for, because it was not pleasure; it was the look of a woman who has been right about a shape and wishes she had been wrong about it.

"Here." Her finger above a sheet in the first row — the Ring — not on it. "Top corner, by the stamp. A mark. Three strokes and a bar." She moved along the row. "Same mark. Same mark. Every Ring sheet, every year." The second row. "The Stair — three strokes and a bar. The Hall — three strokes and a bar." Her finger stopped over the fourth row. "The fourth. Two strokes and a bar. Different." She went on down the rows, the ones that were theirs, the sites the crew had never found, and her finger went over them and she did not say what was on them, because she had not checked, and a number said once was a number said. "It's the kit's mark. The designation in the field ledger — the entry I couldn't read, in the index I don't know — it's this. The same strokes. The office puts it in the corner of every survey like a tier, and the kit's man put it in his ledger like a tier, and it *is* one; it's a grade; they've ranked these — by something — and the fourth's ranked different from the three, and I can't read it." She took her hand back. "I can see it orders them. I can't see what it orders them by. That's all I can say, and I'm not going to say more, because the next thing I'd say would be a guess wearing a finding's coat."

"The fourth's different," Brom said, from the slab's end. It was not a question. Brom had been listening with the foot up and the eyes shut and had heard one thing in it, which was the thing that had been four hundred meters to its middle and cordoned in iron.

"The fourth's different." Karis looked at the row. "Two strokes where the three have three. I don't know if two is more or less. I don't know if it's older or newer or bigger or something I haven't a word for. I know a surveyor put a different mark on it in the corner of every sheet for six years, and I know we stood on a crest above it in the dark and Cael counted forty stakes and stopped because the dark stopped him, and I know that nobody at this slab is going to say what two strokes means until somebody reads it to us." She put the row's stones back on its corners. "That's the shape of the thing. A grade I can't read, in a ledger I carried out of a hole, matching a grade I can't read on a shelf a marshal carried out of an office. Two copies of one ignorance."

"It's a shelf-mark," Vastin said. "An office puts a shelf-mark on — " and stopped, because it was not a shelf-mark and he knew it, and because the second thing had come up in his mouth — a classification; the reflex of a man who had spent a career deciding which shelf a thing went on — and he had heard it coming this time, and had stopped it himself, and stood with it stopped, on the stick, and Cael saw the stopping cost him more than the first one had.

"Two," Seln said, to nobody.

"What?"

"Nothing." Seln had not looked up from the rows.

---

The third thing, Seln found, because Seln was the one who knew where a file hid the thing it was really about.

He had said nothing while Karis worked the rows, and had said his one thing about the empty column, and had gone back to reading the file sideways from the slab's end with his shoulder to the wind, and Cael, who had stopped watching him because watching Seln was a thing you did when you did not trust him, did not see him move. He saw the sheet.

It was on the slab in front of him. Not in a row; in front of him, where he sat at the slab's corner with the log unopened on his knee, laid flat on the stone with its head toward him so that it could be read the right way up, which was not how he read things, and there was no hand near it and Seln was at the slab's other end where he had been, and had not, as far as anyone at the spring could have said, gone anywhere.

The technician's sheet. The narrow columns of figures. The wide one at the foot with the line in it and the bar of brown-black ink under the line, laid on with a brush, the width of a thumb.

He had read it at the inn. He had heard Karis read it aloud and he had watched a man say he could not get past the bar and he had watched Seln go still at a date and a word and had written down that he had seen it and had not known what it was. He had not, at the inn, read the date himself. He had let Karis read it, and had let the room do the arithmetic, and had not done it, because the room had done it for him and a room's arithmetic is not a thing a man has to write down.

He read the date.

It was a Compact date, a registry year and a day in it, and he had lived by the Compact's count for fourteen years before he had lived by the Line's for nine months, and a date was a date. He read the year. He read the day in it. He did the arithmetic, because a cataloguer does the arithmetic whether he wants the sum or not — that was the whole of what a cataloguer was, a man who could not not count — and he had the sum before he had decided to have it.

Six years back. Not near enough. Exactly.

A day he knew. A day he had a page for, in a log that was in Hesk's satchel on the ground by his boot, in the plain hand, the first entry in it that was not a schoolboy's, written at fourteen in a room with a door that locked from the outside. He did not open the satchel. He did not need the page. He had the day as a man has the day he was born, which is a thing you do not look up.

He sat with it. The spring ran. Brom, on the slab's end, said something to Oryn about the boot and Oryn said something back. Lira, on the road, went past above and came back below. Vastin stood on his stick and looked at the trough. Karis worked the fifth row. And Seln, at the slab's other end, with his shoulder to the wind, read the file sideways and did not look at him, and had not looked at him since the inn, and had set a sheet in front of him with its head turned the right way up and had gone back to what he was doing, and had said nothing, and was not going to.

Cael did not write it down.

That was the whole of what he did with it. He looked at the sheet for the length of the spring's running and did the sum twice more, because a sum done once was a sum done, and got the same day twice more, and did not open the log, because there were things a man did not write where a log could be read — by a Compact clerk with a warrant, by a rival at a plank, by a healer who had asked to read him and had the right — and he had learned that from a man who had spent fifteen years not writing things down, and the man was at the other end of the slab, reading a file, and had just taught it to him again without a word. He picked the sheet up. He put it back in the second row, at its end, where it had come from, with its head turned like the row's other heads, so that it lay in the file as if it had never been out of it.

Seln did not look up.

Cael did not look at Seln. There was a thing between the two of them now that neither had said and neither was going to, and it lay in the second row of a dead office's file with a bar of ink under it, and it was, he thought, the first thing he had ever owned that he had not written down, and he found that he could carry it.

---

Vastin's third came at the end, when Karis was gathering the rows, and it was the worst of the three, because it was the one he had spent his whole life being right about.

"This has to go up," he said.

He said it to the flat. Not to anyone. Cael understood the word, because he had grown up under it: *up* was where a thing went when a man in a hall could not answer it — to the next hall, and the next, until it reached a shelf where nobody had to answer anything, and the man who had said it had spent forty years being the last hall before the shelf. He had said *up* about Cael once, at seventeen, in a room with a table, and the thing had gone up, and had come back down as a designation, and the designation had followed a boy across a continent, and here was the man who had said it, at a spring, saying it about a file that had no *up* left in it, because he had carried the whole of *up* out of an office in a courier's flat and set it on a slab. He said it as a man says the one true thing he has left, and it was true — it was the truest thing that had been said at the spring — and it was also nothing, because there was no *up*, and he heard that in it the moment it was out of his mouth, and stood with it out.

"Escalate it," Seln said, not unkindly. He had come round the slab's end while Karis gathered the rows and was standing now a stride from the man with the stick, with his hands in his coat, looking at him as he had looked at him in a doorway. "That's the word. Directive, classification, escalation. I've been counting. Three." He waited until Vastin looked at him. "You keep drafting orders."

"Habit."

"Out here the habit's the tell." Seln said it flat, a thing he had learned at a price. "Draft nothing. Just look."

Vastin looked at him for the length of a breath. It was the first thing, Cael thought, that Seln had ever taught anyone — in the four years Cael had known him, in the fifteen before that if the fifteen were anything like the four — and he had taught it to the man who used to sign the assignments, at a spring, over a trough, with a mule drinking, and the man had heard it. That was the thing. He had heard it. He took the stick out of the ground and put it back in the ground a hand's width to the left, which was the nearest thing to sitting down he would do in front of them, and said, "Just look," to the trough, as a man repeats a thing he intends to keep, and did not draft anything else.

---

Oryn had come down off the bank while Seln was teaching. She stood at the trough with the mule's rope in her hand and looked at the road west, and then at the road's fork — a day on, past the rise, where a track went north to seven holds that owed her a visit — and did not look at it long, because looking at a road you are going to take is not a thing a healer does in front of people, and Cael saw her not look at it, and noted it, and said nothing, because that was the condition, and the condition had been written in his own hand on a bank at Millrace.

Karis tied the flat.

She tied it at both ends as it had come, with the rows in their order and the one page — the citation, a title and a place — on top, where she could reach it without untying anything, because she had decided at some point in the last hour that it was the page she would be reaching for, and she stood up from the slab with the flat under her arm and the notebook in her hand and the road going west past the spring toward a rise that had, on the far side of it, a city she had never seen, and said what she had.

"Six years," Karis said. "They measured all of it. They graded it — the sites are ranked by something, and it's the same something in the kit, and I can't read either one." She looked at the citation. "Everything I can't read points at the same place."

"Treswick," Vastin said.

"The archive under it. Cleon."

"Who's Cleon?" Cael said. He had not heard the name before, and it was not a name he could place by its sound against anyone the crew had ever met, and Karis had said it as she said the name of a thing she had been carrying a long time.

"The only historical-Path practitioner registered west of the Line. If the language is anywhere, it's under his hands. A day and a half." She closed the case. "And then I find out whether I've been right for five years or wrong for five years, and I don't know which I'm more afraid of."

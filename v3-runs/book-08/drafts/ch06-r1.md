# Chapter 6 — Oryn's Terms

The fork was a stone.

It was a stone because Seln said it was, and because the border road's makers had set one there four hundred years ago to say so: a squared post of grey rock at the road's edge on the evening of the two hundred and eightieth day, a mile past the spring's rise, with a mark cut in its west face that counted down toward the Line and a second mark, older and worn nearly smooth, cut in its north face, that counted toward nothing the Compact had ever measured. The road went on west over a long rise with the sun going down behind it. The track went north-west, off the kept surface and onto ruts, toward a gap in the hills that had the last light in it, and Oryn looked at the gap once as they came up to the stone, and did not look at it again, and Cael, who had watched her not look at a road before, at another fork, in another season, catalogued the not-looking and let it be.

They camped at the stone. Nobody said the word before dark. Nobody was going to.

He had stood at a fork with her before, and had written that one down, and he took the log out at the stone before the fire was lit and read what he had written, because a man who keeps a ledger reads the last entry before he makes the next. *Fifty paces. A stone that was only a stone. Two sentences, hers.* He had brought the map. He had found her at Thornwater. Both sentences were paid, in full, on the page, and he closed the log on them and put it back in the satchel and did not open it again until she asked him to, because the thing about a paid sentence is that it does not need to be read twice.

He catalogued the camp because a camp at a fork was a thing he had done once already and had written down, and he wanted the second one honest. Seven of them and the mule, on the road's north verge where the ground was dry, with the fire small and the rise black against the west and the gap black against the north. Brom with the boot off again and the foot up, saying nothing about it. Lira on the far side of the fire with her two hands round a cup, using both. Karis with the flat under her arm even now, because she had stopped putting it down, and the citation page on top where she could reach it. Seln at the edge of the light, where the light stopped being useful, facing the road. Vastin on the road side, with the stick across his knees, a day less spent and a day more silent, watching a crew make a camp as a man watches a drill he has never seen and does not intend to interrupt. And Oryn, with her roll beside her and her hands flat on her knees and her face square to the fire, which was how she sat at everything, and which meant — he had learned this at a marsh — that she was about to say a finding.

She let the fire burn down to working red first. Then she said it.

---

"I'm not an archive person."

She said it to the fire, plainly, at the size she said a finding, and it was one, and it was about herself, which was the only kind she made in front of people.

"I'll say the rest so it's said and nobody has to work it out from my face. That's the road north. Seven holds on it, and I've been to two of them this spring and I'm late at the other five, and late is a number at a hold; it's a knee that's gone another fortnight without being looked at, and a birth that happened without me, and a man with a wrist set wrong who's been told I'd see it and has been holding it wrong for a month so I'd have something to see." She did not look at the gap. "That's the route. It's the only thing I own. It doesn't run west."

Nobody said anything. That was correct. It was the condition.

"And west is a city." She turned the cup in her hands without drinking from it. "I've been in a city. I was certified in one. I'll not say which; it doesn't matter which; they're all the one city when you're a healer in them. I healed for a year inside walls, and the walls had a ledger, and the ledger had a column for the patient's tier, and I was assigned by the column. Bronze and above first. I was told that on the first day as if it were a kindness — *you'll not be wasted on the yard, girl* — and I spent a year mending Bronzes with bruises while the yard went septic under the window, and I left. I walked out of a gate with a roll on my back and I have not been inside a gate that shuts since, and I'm not going inside one now to stand in a square with a pass in my hand that says what rank I'm let to bleed on." She looked up from the fire, then, at Cael, and at nobody else. "That's not a complaint. I don't make those. It's a finding about a healer, so you'll know what kind you've got, and it's this: I'm not going to be talked into a city, and I'd take it as a courtesy if nobody tried."

"Nobody's going to try," Cael said.

"I know. I'm saying it so it's said."

Lira, across the fire, said nothing, and looked at the rise in the west where a city was, which was where Lira looked when the word *ledger* was said with a column in it, because Lira had walked away from a column once, and had not been fast enough to walk away from what it had made of her, and was still, at nineteen, on a road, finding out what she was without one. She did not say any of that. She turned the cup.

"So." Oryn put her cup down. "I'll run the north circuit while you go inside. Five holds. It's a month if the fords are down and three weeks if they're not. And I'm not leaving." She said it flat, in the voice she used for *lie still*, so that there was no other way to hear it. "I want that understood by everyone at this fire, because there's a way of hearing a healer walk up a north road that I'll not have. Leaving and routing are different verbs. I'm doing the second. I did it at the Ring and I did it at the fork and I'll do it at this stone, and every time I did it I came back down a road to a plank with the map in somebody's coat, and this'll be the same, and if anybody at this fire files it as the other verb I'll know, because I read faces, and I'll be disappointed in the face."

"Routing," Brom said, from the foot. He said it as a man says a price he has agreed to.

"Routing."

"Noted," said Karis, and wrote it.

Seln, at the edge of the light, said, "Three weeks," which was not a question and was not the fords; it was the length of time a man who had spent fifteen years counting absences would allow before he began to count this one, and Oryn looked at him across the fire and said, "Three weeks," back, and that was the whole of the arrangement between those two, and it was, Cael thought, more than most crews managed with a contract.

Vastin, on the road side, had not moved. Cael saw him watching — the stick across his knees, the stillness that was the one thing he had been trained to — and saw him understand that he was watching a thing no unit of the Compact's had ever done in his forty years of commanding units, which was to sit at a fire and let a member of it stand up and walk north because she had said she would, without an order, without a warrant, without a word from anyone that she should or should not; and Cael saw that the man could not have said, if asked, whether what he was watching was discipline or its absence, and that he was too honest to guess.

---

"There's a thing I want," Oryn said.

That was different. That was the register she kept for a finding she was afraid of, which he had heard three times in his life, and he sat up.

"You're walking into a city with a station in it." She was looking at him now, and only him, across the fire, and the five of them let her, because it was hers. "An Arbiter station. At the top of the tiers, with a light on it. I've never seen one from the inside and I never want to, but I know what it's for, because I was certified under one: it's the thing that reads a practitioner. It's the instrument. It's the biggest instrument on the continent, and there's one in every city, and it reads everyone who walks up its steps, and I have had my hands on you five times and got nothing three times and a floor the fourth and a cord the fifth, and I don't know what you are, and I'm a healer, and there is one rule a healer learns before she learns to set a bone." She stopped. She made herself say the next thing slowly. "You do not walk a body you can't read toward the one instrument on the continent that reads bodies. Not because I know what it'd do. Because I don't. I don't know what it'd do to you and I don't know what you'd do to it, and a healer who doesn't know what a thing will do keeps her patient away from the thing. That's the whole of medicine. That's the rule under all the other rules."

"I'm not going near it," Cael said.

"I know you're not. I'm not asking you. I'm telling you what I want, so it's said, and then I'm going to ask you for it in the only form I'll take it in." She put her hand flat on the ground beside her, the way she put it on a table. "You go inside, you don't go near the station. Not the steps, not the square. You promise me in that log of yours, because I've seen what the log does to you."

He looked at her for a while. The fire moved. He had known her a year, near enough, and she had asked him for two things in it — to lie still, and to ask first — and she had never once asked him for a thing that was not about the body in front of her, and she was not asking now, either; the station was a thing that read bodies and he was a body she could not read, and that was the whole of it, and she had said so, and she had said it in front of five people so that it would be in five ledgers and not one.

He took the log out of the satchel.

He did it in front of her, because that was the form. Hesk's satchel, the flap, the log in the pocket built for it, the plain hand. He opened it to the day's page — the two hundred and eightieth, the spring, the slab, the three readers, the sheet he had not written down — and under it, in front of the fire, in front of her, he wrote:

*The fork. Oryn north; five holds; routing, not the other verb. Her term, given at the fire, taken in the log because that's the form she'll take it in: inside Treswick I do not go near the station. Not the steps. Not the square. Written here so she can read it, and so I can.*

He read it back to her once, aloud, at the size he read a thing that was going to be kept, and she listened with her hands on her knees and her face square to him, and when he had finished she nodded, once, as if a thing had been entered, and said, "That'll do," and looked away, at the fire, and that was the covenant kept — not a promise, which was a thing a man said and could unsay; a line in a book, which was a thing a man wrote and could be read back to.

"And now the rest of it," she said, "because I'm not walking up a north road tomorrow leaving a crew with a shoulder in it and no hands."

---

She priced it for them. That was the word Brom would have used, and Cael used it, because she did it as a price was done — out loud, to the whole fire, each item with its number.

"Brom. That shoulder's been mended twice and I said not three times this season, and the season's over and the shoulder doesn't know it. It's bound, and it's held under the binding, and it's holding because you've carried it and not used it, and that's the whole of why it's holding. Inside walls you'll want to use it. Don't. Not a door, not a box, not a man. If you use it and it goes, there's no healer inside that gate who'll put hands on a Copper's shoulder, because inside that gate there's a column, and you're at the bottom of it. You'll be a man with a shoulder that's gone, in a city, until you're out of it. Carry it. Bind it fresh every fourth day; Karis has thread. Don't let him lie about it," she said, to the fire generally, "because he will."

"I don't lie," Brom said.

"You say *it's a boot*."

Brom did not answer that.

"The foot walks. It'll walk to a city and it'll walk in one. Keep it dry." She went round the fire. "Lira. The arm's fifteen days. It'll go slow for a month and it'll tell you when it's tired, and you'll not listen, and it'll tell you again. When it tells you the second time, stop. The foot's the same. You've two of everything now; don't spend it because you've got it." Lira, across the fire, opened the left hand and closed it and said nothing, which was yes. "Seln, nothing. You've never once needed me and you've never once said so, which is the only reason I like you. Karis, you'll not sleep, and there's nothing I can do about that from a north road, so I'll say it and leave it: sleep. Vastin." She looked at him. "That knee's carried you three hundred miles and you've not asked me. Don't start now; I've not the reserve for a knee tonight and I'd rather you carried it to Millrace and asked me there. It'll carry. It'll hurt. Those are different things and you know the difference better than anyone at this fire."

Vastin inclined his head, a finger's width, which was a Compact courtesy and was also, from him, a great deal. He had been priced, at a fire, by a healer with no rank, in front of a crew, in the same breath as a Copper's shoulder and a girl's arm, and had not been priced lower than either, and Cael watched him find that he could carry it.

"And Cael."

"I'm whole."

"You're whole. I can see it from here, and I said I'd read you when you'd slept a week and the ferry'd run, and you've slept a week and the ferry's run and I've not, because a road's not a table and I'll not read you at a fork with a city in front of you and a man's shoulder in the fire. That's on me. It's the one thing I'm walking up that road owing, and I'll pay it." She stood up, the way she stood up from everything, all at once. "Millrace. That's the second stop on my spring and it's a day south of Lowmarch on the Stair road, and it's the road you'll take east when you're done inside, whatever you find, because it's the only road east. I'll be there in three weeks or a month. I'll read the shoulder there and the knee there and I'll read you there, on a table, properly, the whole of it, when you're still, and if there's anything in you that a station's put there I'll know, because I know what's in you now to the hair, and a hair's the thing I read." She picked up her roll. "Bring me whatever walks out of that archive with you. All of it."

She did not say the other sentence. She had said it at a fork once and it had meant a thing, and it did not need saying at this one, and everyone at the fire heard her not say it, and Cael put it in the place where he kept the things that were true about her, which was getting to be a long page.

---

The stone at first light.

He catalogued it because it was the second fork of his life that he had stood at with her, and the first one he had written down had been a stone that was only a stone, and this one had four hundred years of a road's count cut into it and it was only a stone too.

Fifty paces, from where the six of them stood with the mule to where the track left the kept surface, and he walked them beside her, and did not say the thing he had had since the marsh, which was still a sentence that asked her to stay, and which was still a good sentence, and which he did not say, because the condition was the condition and he had entered it in his own hand on a bank at Millrace and again last night at a fire, and a man who breaks a condition at the second fork it costs him has not understood the condition any better than a man who breaks it at the first.

She stopped where the ruts began. She looked back at the five, once — the count, and the finding — and at the man on the road side with the stick, who was not in the count and was not out of it, and then at Cael.

"Not the steps," she said. "Not the square."

"It's written."

"I know it's written. I watched you write it. I'm saying it so it's said twice, because a thing said twice at a fork is a thing that walks." She took the mule's rope. "Millrace."

Then she turned the mule's head north-west and went, and did not look back, because a healer walking her route does not look back at a crew walking off it, and he stood at the stone and watched her go up the track toward the gap with the sun coming over the hills behind him and putting her shadow out long ahead of her on the ruts, a woman and a mule and a roll, getting smaller.

The five of them stood at the road's edge and watched, because it was a thing you watched. Brom with his weight on the good foot. Lira with her two hands at her sides, not in her coat. Karis with the flat under her arm and the page on top of it, looking at the north track as if it were a line on a map that she had not drawn and could not measure. Seln looking at the gap, where a road went that he had never walked and never would. And Vastin, a pace back, looking at the five of them watching, because that was the thing he had not seen before, and he was a man who catalogued too, in his way, and had just found a thing that was not in his catalogue.

He did not wait until the gap had her. He had done that once. He turned, at a quarter-mile, while she was still a shape against the thaw, and went back to the five, because the road west was there and the six of them — five, and one on a road — had a rise to go over.

---

The rise was long and the sun was on their backs going up it, and at the top of it the six of them stopped, because the country stopped being one thing and started being another, and a crew stops for that.

Treswick.

He had read the name on a page in a square hand, and heard it in a healer's mouth, and in a researcher's, and in a marshal's, and had built, without meaning to, a city out of the four of them, and the city on the horizon was not that city; it was a bluff, first — a long grey bluff standing up out of the plain to the west with the river going round its foot — and then it was the walls, three of them, one above the other, stacked up the bluff's flank in tiers as a hold stacks its terraces, each wall a ring and each ring smaller than the one below it, so that the whole of it went up the hill like a thing that had been made to be looked at from below. Roofs inside the rings. Smoke. A road going up through the tiers by gates, and he could see the gates from here, three of them, one in each wall, and the road going through each and switching back to the next.

And at the top, above the third ring, on the bluff's crown, a light.

Not a fire. He knew fires. A light with no smoke over it and no colour in it, pale, steady, the size of a coin at this distance, in the morning, with the sun full on it, which was a thing no fire did and no lamp did and only one thing on the continent did, and he had walked up its steps at fourteen in a room with a door that locked from the outside, and had not stood within a mile of one since a bluff above Ostrand, and it was there, on the crown of a hill on the Line, findable if you knew to look, and he knew.

He did not look at it.

He looked at the rise going down ahead of them, and the river, and the road, and the first gate, and the smoke, and the three walls, and Lira beside him looking at the three walls with a face he knew, and Brom looking at the road because a road was a thing you walked, and Karis looking at the whole of it with the flat under her arm as if the city were a page, and Seln looking at the gates because gates were what Seln looked at, and Vastin looking at the top of the bluff for one breath and then, deliberately, at the road, and he did not look at the light on the crown of the hill, because it was written, and because she had said it twice, and because he had a half-day of road in which to find out whether a thing a man wrote in a book at a fire could be kept by the man in the morning, and this was the first half-hour of it, and it could.

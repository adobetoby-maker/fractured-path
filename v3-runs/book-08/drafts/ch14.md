# Chapter 14 — Dividers

The runner came to the street door on the two hundred and ninety-second day with a thing wrapped in oilcloth the size of a hand and a letter folded round it, and said a name at the door that was not the crew's and was, and Brom, who was nearest, paid the runner a copper for the carrying and did not ask where it had come from, because the wrapping said Denvash in a hand Cael would have known across a yard.

He catalogued the parcel before he opened it, because a thing from Hesk was a thing you owed a description before you owed it a use.

Oilcloth, waxed, the kind the man wrapped a finished piece in for a customer who had a road to walk it on. Tied with the flat cord he used, in the knot he used, which was a knot that came undone with one pull and did not come undone by accident, and Cael had watched him tie it a hundred times at a bench with his hands going faster than his eyes and had never once managed it himself. A letter round the whole, folded twice, addressed on the outside in the dry square hand to *the boy with the log, at the edge, wherever the edge is this season*, which was the whole of the direction and had been enough, because a courier chain that ran from a bench in Denvash to a bench in Lowmarch and a river ferry and a broker's runner at a street door in the outer district of a city on the Line was a chain of people who knew what an instrument-maker's parcel looked like and passed it along by its shape.

Karis was not there. She had gone up to the registry at its opening and down with Cleon on the seventh day of the week he had asked for, alone with him by her own asking, and she had said at the yard door, without looking round, "Today he tells me," and had gone down the stair with her bound hand against her coat and the flat under her other arm and had not come up at noon, and nobody had gone to see why, because a woman three days from the thing she had hunted her whole life was not a woman you went to see about.

So it was five at the inn's long table, and the parcel, and the mule's chair.

He did not open it at once. He catalogued the five first, because a thing was about to be opened and he wanted the room from before, as he had wanted a back room from before a pack was opened: Brom at the table's end with the foot up on the mule's chair, which was what the mule's chair was for on a day the mule was in the yard; Lira across from him with her two hands on the wood, the left one open, because she opened it now without thinking, which was the whole of what twenty-seven days had done; Seln at the door end, facing the street door, because a street door in a city where a team knew where they slept was a thing somebody faced; and Vastin, on the knee, at the table's side, with the stick against the bench and his hands on his knees, at a crew's table on a working day for a thing that was not work, and not knowing what the thing was, and not asking.

He pulled the cord. It came undone with one pull.

---

Dividers.

He had not known what the parcel was and had known, from its weight in the runner's hand, that it was brass, and had thought *a second edge*, because a second edge was the thing a man might send to a boy who had one, and it was not. Two arms, the length of a hand, hinged at the top on a pin that turned without a sound, tapering to two points fine enough to prick a sheet and not tear it, and a small brass wheel on a screw across the arms' middle that held them open at a width and would not let the width change until the wheel was turned. He knew the tool. He had watched Hesk use one at the bench — walk it along a rule, point over point, to lay a length off a drawing and put the same length on a piece of work, and back, so that a thing built in a shop came out the size the drawing said — and had never once had one in his hand, because a divider was a maker's tool and he had been a boy who watched.

He opened it. The arms went apart without a sound, as a thing goes that has been made by a man who does not tolerate sound in a hinge, and stopped where the wheel held them, and he turned the wheel a quarter, and they went a hair wider, and stopped.

A second tool. Not a second edge; the thing that went with an edge. An edge told you a line was straight; dividers told you how long it was, and carried the length somewhere else, and put it down again the same. He sat at the long table with the thing in his hand and understood that Hesk had sent him, across a continent, in two parcels a year apart, the two halves of a single competence, in the order a maker teaches them: first the straight, then the measured.

He turned the wheel back. The arms closed. He opened them again, to the width of the straightedge's first mark, and walked the points along the table's edge, point over point, the way he had watched a man do it at a bench a hundred times — eleven steps to the table's end — and found that a table in an inn on the Line was eleven of Hesk's first marks long, which was a fact about a table that nobody had ever needed and that he now had, and he understood that this was what the tool was for: not the measuring; the *carrying*. A length taken here and put down there, the same. He closed the arms.

The note was three lines.

*A rule says a line is true. A divider says how far, and carries it. You've the first. Here's the second, and mind the points; they'll go through a thumb as easy as a sheet. Lines are for finding out how far a thing is from another thing. Everything is. — H.*

He read it twice. Not upside down; the right way up, as it came, because it was addressed to him and a thing addressed to you is a thing you read as it was written. Then he folded it, and put it in the satchel, flat, against the log, where the others were, and put the dividers in his coat, in the pocket where the straightedge was, and Brom, who had watched the whole of it with the face he used for a wall somebody else was pricing, said, "He makes good things," which was the whole of Brom's opinion of Hesk and was, from Brom, an essay, and the two brass things lay against each other in the pocket as they had lain, he supposed, in a maker's drawer, and made a small sound when he moved, and he found that he did not mind the sound.

---

The crew marked it the way the crew marked things.

He catalogued that because it was the family's register and he had never once written the register down, and a thing a family does without a name is a thing somebody should give a name to before it is lost.

Brom priced a meal. He went to the woman who kept the inn and came back with a number and said it — "Four, for five, and the mule's chair gets nothing because it doesn't eat" — and put four marks on the table, which was a thing Brom did only when a thing was worth marking, and the meal came, and it was the inn's meal, which was mutton and bread and the beer the outer district drank, and it was on the long table at noon on a working day with the door open, and nobody said what it was for.

Lira kept a seat. She put Karis's cup at Karis's place, and Oryn's roll — which was not there; Oryn's roll was on a north road — she marked with the second cup, at the far end, where a healer sat when she sat, and said nothing about it, and the two cups stood at two empty places through the meal and were not moved, and that was the whole of what Lira had to say about who was not at the table, and it was, from Lira, a paragraph.

Seln closed a ledger. Not the log; the crew's, the one Brom kept in his head and Seln kept on a slip in his coat — what was owed, what was earned, what had been paid at what gate — and he read it out, at the meal, in the voice for an inventory, from the first snow to the day: the winter's fence, the rope-house, the shed, the hold at the cut, the inn at Lowmarch and the inn here, the week paid to the day, a hundred and thirty in hand and nothing owed; and then he said, "Closed," and tore the slip in two, and put the halves in the fire, which was how Seln said a season was done, and he had not done it since a marsh.

And Vastin sat at the table.

That was the thing Cael had not had a catalogue for at a ferry landing and had one for now. The man sat at a crew's long table for a thing that was not work — a meal on a working day with the door open and two cups at two empty places — and did not know what it was for, and did not ask, and ate the inn's mutton with the good manners of a man who had eaten at a great many tables and had never once sat at one where nobody was in charge of it, and Cael saw him look at the two cups, once, and look at Lira, and understand, as he was learning to understand things, by watching, that a seat kept for somebody who was not there was a thing this crew did, and that nobody had told him, and that he was at the table anyway, and that the not-being-told was the invitation. He did not say anything. He ate. He was, Cael thought, very slightly less bad at being nobody than he had been the day before, and was learning it at a table with mutton on it, which was where most people learned it, and where an Archmarshal never had.

Nobody said what the meal was for. It was not for a day; he was nineteen, and had been nineteen since a steading in the autumn, and the autumn was a long way off and would not find him here. It was for a parcel, and for a man on a bench a continent away who had sent the second half of a thing a year after the first, and the crew knew what that was worth and priced it at four marks and two cups and a slip in the fire, and that was the family's whole register, and he had it now, and wrote it down at the table with the new tool's weight in his pocket.

---

He wrote the log inside walls.

That was the custom, and he kept it without the occasion, because the custom had never been the occasion. He had written an inventory at the end of a year on a floor above Ostrand and at the end of a year in a marsh, and at other times when a thing was worth an inventory, and the form was the form: the day, the place, the count, the money, the bodies, the things, and the one line under all of it that the year had earned. He had never written one inside a wall. He wrote this one at the long table, with the door open and the two cups at their places and the dividers in his coat, and it was the first entry in the plain hand that had ever been made in a city, and he noted that, because a first was a thing you noted.

*Two hundred and ninety-second day. Treswick, the outer district, the inn on the ring road, the long table. Inside walls. Five at the table; one below; one north. A hundred and thirty in hand; the week paid to the day; nothing owed. Brom's foot walks; the shoulder carried, bound fresh yesterday; the good one used wrong in a cut and told him so. Lira, two arms, twenty-seven days. Seln, nothing; a slip in the fire. Vastin, the knee bound, a month running; at the table. Karis, the hand bound, below, on the seventh day.*

*Twelve. Wind, Pressure, Iron, Compression, Ember, Shadow, Storm, Anchor, Blade, Tide; Iron Wall, from a plane in a cut; Rune, from a lamp. The still place at the middle of them, not read, not touched, not the Quiet, not a fragment. A cord that reads everyone but me. A row of sheets that counts one short in my hands. A straightedge and now the thing that goes with it.*

*Hesk sent dividers. Lines are for finding out how far a thing is from another thing. Everything is. I'll enter that as a rule and see what it's worth.*

*The promise stands. Not the steps. Not the square. Eight days inside and I've not been within four tiers of either.*

---

He went to the district's edge in the afternoon, because the log wanted a thing at the end of it that the table could not give, and because a man who has been handed a tool for finding out how far a thing is from another thing should go and stand where he can see the thing.

The district's edge was the first wall's inner face and the stair's foot and the first-tier gate, and from the ring road at the gate you could see, if you looked up the bluff past the second wall and the third, the crown of the hill and the old town's roofs and the one roof taller than the rest, and on it, in the afternoon, with the sun on the bluff, the thing that did not go out.

He had not looked at it since a rise. He had made a discipline of it, and had kept the discipline through a bridge and a gate and eight days and a cut, and he did not break it now; he stood at the stair's foot four tiers below the crown with his hands at his sides and the dividers in his coat and looked at the second gate, which was the thing in front of him, and let the crown be where it was, at the edge of his eye, the way he had let it be for eight days, a location he was aware of and did not look at.

And it did the thing.

He would have said, after, if anyone had asked him — nobody did — that he had not seen it. That was true. He had not been looking at it. He was looking at the second gate and the coat at it and the queue doing the thing with its hands, and at the edge of his eye, on the crown of a hill four tiers up, a pale steady light that had been pale and steady since a rise did a thing that a pale steady light does not do: it was not steady. For the length of a breath. Not brighter, not dimmer; *wrong*, in the way the cord had been wrong in his hands and the row had been wrong in his hands — a flicker that was not a flicker, because a flicker is a thing you see, and this was a thing he felt, at the edge of his eye and somewhere under his ribs at once, a pressure, small, the pressure of a thing turning. An instrument turning toward the thing it was for.

Then it was steady.

He stood at the stair's foot with his hands at his sides. Nobody on the ring road had stopped. A carter went past with a mule. The coat at the second gate read a pass and waved a hand through. The queue did the thing with its hands. Nobody, on a street with forty people on it, had felt a light four tiers up do a thing for the length of a breath, because it had not done it for them, and he stood and let that be a fact and did not make it into anything else, because he did not have anything else to make it into and was not going to invent one.

He had a page for it. That was the whole of what he did with it. He had a page in the log for things the instruments did wrong around him and it had three entries on it — a still place a reading could not read; a cord that counted forty-one and a piece; a row of sheets that came out one short in his hands — and he added a fourth, standing, without taking the log out, in the part of him that wrote things down: a light on a hill that was not steady for the length of a breath, once, for him. Deniable. Every one of the four was deniable. He had known since he was fourteen that he was the thing the instruments were not built for, and here was the biggest instrument on the continent, four tiers up, in the sun, doing the thing the small ones did, and he filed it where the small ones were filed, under the unreadability that had followed him across a continent, and let it stand, and did not cross.

He stood a while longer, because a thing that had done something once might do it again and a cataloguer waits for the second time before he enters the first as a pattern, and it did not do it again. The light was pale and steady on the crown of the hill in the afternoon sun, and the queue went through the second gate, and the carter's mule was gone round the ring road's bend, and nothing four tiers up did anything but be what it had been for longer than the walls. He waited the length of time a man waits at a stair's foot before a coat at a gate begins to wonder about him, and then a little less than that, because Seln had taught him the length, and turned.

He did not cross. He noted that too, because a healer had asked him for one thing in a log, and here was the first afternoon on which the thing she had asked for had cost him anything — he had wanted, for the length of the breath, to go up the stair and find out — and he had not gone, because it was written, and because a thing a man writes in a book at a fire is a thing the man keeps in the afternoon or it is nothing, and he was finding out, at a stair's foot, that it was not nothing.

He turned his back on it.

---

The outer district after the meal, in the afternoon, with the carts on the ring road and the smoke going up from the roofs inside the first wall and the brokers' boards nailed up outside their yards with the day's prices on them, and the stair going up the bluff to the second gate, and above the second gate the second wall, and above that the third, and above the third the crown, and on the crown, pale and steady again, four tiers above where he was allowed to stand, the light.

Cael, nineteen, keeping a healer's promise, not crossing, with his back to it — turned toward the way down, the carts' street to the landing and the yard door and the cellar stair and the iron door and the dark past it where a woman was reading a thing on Cleon's seventh day, the hole under the city where the machine that made that light could not reach, because everything down there was older than the light — and going down into it, with the straightedge and the dividers in his coat, brass against brass, making their small sound.

The light behind him.

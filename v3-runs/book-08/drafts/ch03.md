# Chapter 3 — Six Years

He catalogued the room before the pack was opened, because the pack was going to change the room and he wanted the count from before.

The back room of the lower inn, on the evening of the two hundred and seventy-seventh day, with the long table cleared of everything but the lamp, and the door shut, which it was not in the ordinary way of things, because the back room was Karis's and Karis kept the door open so that the sheets could breathe. The sheets were on the shelf tonight, in their oilcloth, all fifty-two. The kit was on the shelf beside them with its lid down. The map was in Karis's coat. The lamp was on the table's corner where the lamp always was, and the fire was small, and the window had the last of the frost on it at the edges and the dark behind that.

Six of them, and the man in the brown coat, and the pack.

He had carried it up from the door end himself, by its mended strap, and set it on the table's end, and stood behind it, and had not opened it, because Karis had not yet sat down, and a man who had been handing things over for forty years knew that the order in which a thing was handed over was the last authority he had left, and was not going to spend it before the room was ready.

Karis sat down. She sat where she sat at a source — the near side, the lamp to her left, the notebook flat, the pencil in the hand that did not turn pages — and she put both hands on the table, and looked at the pack, and did not look at Vastin at all, which was, Cael thought, the greatest courtesy she had ever paid anyone.

"Three things," Vastin said. "In the order I decided on the road. I'll tell you the order and then I'll keep to it. First the records. Then the page. Then the thing in the records that I could not get past, which is the reason for the other two, and which I would have told you first if I were a different kind of man."

"You're not," Karis said.

"No." He undid the strap.

---

The records first.

He catalogued them as they came out because a catalogue was what he had, and because the man taking them out was doing the same thing with his hands, and had been doing it for forty years, and it was the first time Cael had ever watched the Compact's method used against the Compact.

Not a case. He had thought *case*, on the landing, and it was not one; it was a courier's flat, the kind a broker's runner carries under one arm along the Line, oilcloth over pasteboard, tied at both ends, and inside it the sheets lay flat in the order somebody had put them in, and the somebody was not Vastin, because the sheets had been in that order for years, and the pasteboard had the shape of a shelf pressed into it.

Survey abstracts. He knew the form because the kit's ledger was the same form: the surveyor's hand, the radius line, the count of stakes, the registry's stamp in the corner. Not one. A stack a hand's depth, in more hands than one, dated, and the dates went back — he read them across the table — the dates went back six years. Sites. Radii. Stake counts. A column he had not seen on the kit's ledger, because the kit's ledger had been one man's field book and these were the office's fair copies: a column for what the office called the *anomaly*, which was the word the office used for the thing that happened to a practitioner's sigil at the line, and in that column, in every hand, in every year, the same abbreviation, three letters, which Cael did not know and Karis did not need to, because she had it from the first sheet.

"Total," she said. "At the line. Every one. The same distance, the same instant." She turned a sheet. Another. "They wrote it the same way six years ago that they wrote it last summer. Nobody in six years has put a second word in that column." She wrote it then, in the notebook — the abbreviation, the six years, every hand — on the page she kept for observations she could not file, and Cael saw that it was the second entry on that page, and that the first had been a ring at Thornwater.

"There wasn't a second word to put," Vastin said. "I asked, before I left. A technician who had been entering that column for four of the six years told me it was the only column in the office he had never had to think about."

Karis did not answer that. She had begun to sort.

He had watched her open a source before. He had watched her open the ring at Thornwater and the walls at the Stair and a kit's ledger by a marsh, and he had thought, each time, that he had seen the whole of what she did. He had not. She opened the Compact's six years the way a starving person opens bread — not fast; *completely* — and the sorting was the eating: sheet by sheet, by site and then by year within site, laid out along the long table in rows that had been in her head since the first radius line, the lamp moved twice to keep the light on the row she was building, the pencil never once picked up, because the sorting was a thing she did with her hands and the writing was a thing she did after. Nobody spoke. Brom moved the lamp the second time without being asked. Lira, at the far end, watched Cael, which was where Lira looked when a Compact hand was on a table. Oryn, across from Vastin, watched the man's knee under the table, and then his hands, and then the sheets, and then the knee again. Seln watched the sheets go down the table from the door end and read them as they went past him, at the speed Karis laid them, because Seln read paper at the speed it was shown to him and had never in his life needed it shown twice.

Six rows.

Karis stopped with a sheet in her hand and looked at the table.

"More than four," she said. She did not say how many more. She put the sheet down at the end of the sixth row and did not start a seventh, and looked at the rows, and Cael saw her decide not to say the number, because a number said once was a number said, and she had not checked it. "The Ring's here. The Stair's here — that's the hand from the kit; I'd know it in the dark. The Hall's here, surveyed from the marsh side; they never went in. The fourth." Her finger touched a row and came off it. "And the rest are theirs. Places we didn't find. Some of them are on the bearing and some of them aren't and I'm not going to say which tonight, because I'll say it wrong, and a wrong site is worse than no site."

"They knew," Brom said. Not a question.

"For six years." Karis put her hand flat on the first row. "They measured it. They mapped it. They wrote *total* in a column every summer and filed it and put a shelf-mark on the file, and then they sent a man with a kit and a stake to the Stair and sent a crew in behind him to bring the kit home, and the crew came home with it, and wrote it up, and the completion report went west." She looked at Vastin, then, for the first time. "The report went into this."

"It went into this," Vastin said. "It was the last sheet in the flat when I took the flat. I read it on the road. It is a very good report. It says exactly what a crew of six walked out of quiet ground with, and where, and who, and it does not say what worked inside, because the man who wrote it did not know."

Cael did not say anything. He did not look at the last sheet. He knew, without looking, which hand it was in.

---

The page second.

Vastin took it from inside his coat, not from the flat, because it had never been in the flat: a single sheet, folded twice, in the ordinary paper the Compact used for the ordinary business of a day, and the hand on it was his own — square, upright, a hand made for filling boxes on forms — and there were four lines on it.

He set it apart. He set it on the table's bare end, past the sixth row, and did not slide it toward Karis, and did not look at Cael, and Cael understood that the setting-apart was part of the order the man had decided on the road: the records were a stack; this was a page; a stack could be sorted and a page could only be read.

"I copied it from a record older than the section it was filed in," Vastin said. "The classified history is not one history. It is an office's habit, kept for a long time, of putting a thing on a shelf it does not want read, and the shelf is older than the office. The oldest things on it are not Compact records at all. They are what the Compact found when it built the office, and did not throw away, because throwing a thing away is a decision and filing it is not. This was on the oldest shelf. It is a reference — a line in a longer record, citing another record that the Compact does not hold — and it is the only line in the whole of the classified history that names a designer."

"Names," Karis said.

"Cites. There is no name. There is a word the record uses for him, which I have copied as it was written, and there is the place where the record it cites is said to be kept, which I have copied as it was written, and there is nothing else, because there was nothing else. I did not copy the rest of the line. The rest of the line is a clerk's hand telling a later clerk where a shelf was."

Karis reached across the sixth row and took the page.

She read it once, to herself, and then she turned it to the lamp, and then she read it aloud, at the size she read a finding, to the room, because a page on a table was the room's.

"*—of the Architect: see the lower archive at Treswick, which holds it in full—*"

She stopped. She read it again, to herself. Then she put the page back where he had set it, on the bare end of the table, apart, and took her hand off it, and Cael saw the hand come off the page as if the page had a heat in it.

"The Architect," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"It's a word I have never read in any stratum." She said it as a finding about herself, which was the only kind she would make about that word tonight. "Not in the confiscated record. Not under the Line. Not in anything I copied at three floors. It's a title, not a name — the record says *the*, and a record that says *the* is a record whose readers already knew who it meant. That's all the page says. That's all I'm going to say it says." She looked at Vastin. "Treswick's lower archive. You said Treswick on the landing."

"I said Treswick because that is where the citation points. I did not know, on the landing, that you had a reason of your own."

"I have a reason of my own," Karis said, and did not say it, and the whole table heard her not say it for the second time in a day, and the map stayed in her coat.

Cael looked at the page on the bare end of the table. Four lines, in a square hand, folded twice, a word and a place. He did not know what he had expected a name to look like when it came. He had expected it, he found, to look like a name. This looked like a shelf-mark. It looked like the thing at the bottom of a file, under everything, that told you where the rest of the file was, and he had spent five years and a continent staying out of files, and here was the oldest one on the continent telling him where it was kept, and it was west, and inside.

His hand was on the table. It was near the page. He noted the distance — a hand's width, a little less — and did not close it.

---

The third thing.

Vastin did not take it out of anything. It was already on the table. It had been on the table since the first row went down, because it was in the records, and he had known where, and had let Karis lay it in its place among the survey sheets and had waited for her to be done sorting before he said which sheet it was, because that, too, was the order.

"The sheet at the end of the second row," he said. "Not a survey. The office filed it with the surveys because the office did not know what else to file it with. Read the last line."

Karis found it without looking for it. She had laid it; she knew where every sheet on the table was. She drew it out from the end of the second row and held it to the lamp, and it was not a surveyor's form. It was a technician's — Cael knew the ruling of it from a hall at fifteen, the narrow columns for an instrument's readings, the wide one at the foot for a remark — and the columns were full of figures that meant nothing to him, and the wide one at the foot had a line in it, in a technician's hand, and under the line a bar of ink the width of a thumb, laid on with a brush, black, straight, and old enough that the black had gone brown at its edges.

Karis read the line aloud. She read it as she read everything, at the size of a finding, and she read it slowly, because it was a line that had been written by a man who did not know what he was writing down.

"*Deep layer. Sequence initiated* — and a date." She gave the date. It was a Compact date, a registry year and a day in it, and the room did the arithmetic without being asked to, because the room had lived by the Line's count for nine months and by the Compact's for their whole lives before that, and a date was a date. Six years back. Near enough six — a year before he had begun staying out of files, which he noted, and did not follow. "*Unreadable. Not halted. Running. Correlated with classification event* —" and then the bar.

"That is the redaction," Vastin said. "I could not get past it. I had forty years of keys and I put every one of them to that bar and it did not move. It is not a clerk's bar. A clerk's bar is laid on after; this was laid on the day the line was written, by the hand that wrote the line or by the hand standing over it, and the office has filed the sheet with the surveys for six years because there is no shelf in the office for a thing that a technician wrote and a technician's superior crossed out and nobody, since, has been able to read." He put his hands flat on the table. "Something in the layer nobody can reach began to run six years ago. It has not stopped. No one can read it and no one can halt it and no one in the Compact will say what it was correlated with, and I do not know, and I am not going to pretend to this table that I know, because I have spent a winter finding out what it costs to pretend."

Nobody spoke.

Cael watched the room, because that was what he did when a thing had been said that he did not have a place for yet. Karis, with the sheet in her hand, still, her pencil still on the notebook where she had not yet copied the line, because she was going to copy it and was making herself wait. Brom, looking at the bar of ink as a man looks at a wall he has been told not to lean on. Oryn, looking at nobody, with her hands flat on the table and her face in the register she kept for a finding she did not want. Lira, looking at Cael. Vastin, looking at the sheet in Karis's hand as a man looks at a thing he has carried three hundred miles and has just set down and cannot pick up again.

And Seln.

Seln had gone still.

Not the stillness he used on a road, which was a thing he did on purpose, so that a body that had been moving stopped being a thing anyone could see. The other one. The one Cael had seen twice in his life: once at a plank in a town on no map when a completion report had been read aloud to him, and once on a rim in the dark with four crossbows in the scrub and no faces. Seln, at the door end, with his shoulder on the wall, had heard a date and a word and had gone still in the way a professional goes still when he has just done a sum in his head and does not like the answer, and he did not say the answer. He did not say anything. He looked at the sheet in Karis's hand, and then at the bar of ink on it, and then — once, no longer — at Cael, and then at the window, and he did not say it.

Cael did not know what it was.

He knew Seln had a sum, and had done it, and had the answer, and had decided at the door end of a back room not to say it aloud, and he knew that Seln had made that decision before about a thing in a margin and had been right to. He did not know the sum. He wrote down, in the part of him that wrote things down, that Seln had gone still at a date and a bar of ink, and that he had seen it, and that he did not know why, and he let it stand, because a thing you do not know is a thing you do not know, and the log had a place for those.

Karis copied the line. She copied it into the notebook with the date in it and the word *correlated* and then the bar, which she drew as a bar, the width of a thumb, and under it, in her own hand, smaller, two words: *not read*.

---

Then there was nothing for the man to do.

Cael catalogued that too, because it was the thing at the table he had never had a catalogue for, and it was the plainest thing in the room. Vastin had brought three things to a table and set them down in an order, and had said the order, and had kept to it, and had told the table the one thing he did not know as plainly as the things he did, and now the three things were on the table and the flat was empty and his hands were flat on the wood, and the part that came next did not come.

The part that came next had always been his. A problem brought to a table; a response; a team, a warrant, a man sent, an order given before the problem had finished being spoken, because that was what the table was for and that was what he was for at it. He had done it ten thousand times. He did not know how to sit at a table where the problem had been set down and the response belonged to somebody else, and Cael watched him not know it — the hands flat, the stick against the table's edge, the eyes on the six rows going the length of the wood and on the woman at the near end who had already begun to own them.

Because Karis had. She had the notebook open and the pencil going now, and she was not writing the line again; she was writing the three questions she was going to ask the records in the morning, in the order she had them, and the questions were hers, and the records were hers, and the page on the bare end of the table was, whatever else it was, a place she was going to go, and the man who had carried all of it three hundred miles on a knee that said so watched her take it the way a man watches a thing he used to do being done by somebody else, and did not reach for it, and Cael saw the not-reaching cost him, and saw him pay it.

"You'll want to read them," Vastin said, to Karis, eventually. It was not an order. It was the nearest thing to one that he had left, and it came out as a thing a man says to fill a room.

"I'll want to read them," Karis agreed, not looking up. "I'll be reading them for a month. I'll be reading them on the road." She wrote. "You'll want to sleep. You've been carrying this since the Line, and the thing about a thing you carry that far is that when you set it down you find out how much of you it was holding up."

Vastin looked at her. Then he looked at Oryn, across from him, who had not moved and had not stopped looking at his knee, and Oryn said, in the voice for a finding, "She's right. Go and lie down, and don't take the stairs fast," and he stood up, on the knee, and took the stick, and went, and the door shut behind him, and the room let out a breath that nobody had known it was holding.

Lira looked at Cael's hand.

It was still on the table. It was still a hand's width from the page. He had not moved it in the whole of the last thing and the whole of the man's leaving, and Lira had watched it not move as she had watched it not move at a table at seventeen with a man in grey across from it, and she looked at it now, and then at him, and did not say anything, and did not need to.

"I know," he said.

"I didn't say anything."

"No." He took the hand off the table and put it in his lap, and the page stayed where it was. "You didn't."

---

Nobody said *west*. Nobody had to.

The lamp burned down toward its working red and Karis did not trim it, because she had stopped seeing it. The six rows lay the length of the table with the frost creeping in at the window's edges behind them, and at the bare end, past the sixth row, apart from all of it, the one page lay folded open on its own — four lines, a title, a place, and nothing else — and Cael's hand was in his lap now and had been near the page for the length of a lamp and had not once touched it, and the six of them knew the distance to a hand's width and did not say that either.

Outside, through the window, past the frost, the void roads ran east into the dark. He could not see them. He knew where they were. Nine months of roads with ruts a cart's width, going out past the ford and the fence-line and the bend, past a ring and a stair and a hall and a fourth thing nobody had a word for, under their last frost of the year, toward the sites and the line the sites made and whatever the line ran to. And on the other side of the window, on the other side of the town, past the landing where the ferry had gone from that morning, the one maintained road ran west, three days of it, laid and kept and measured by people who measured things, to a city on the Line with a registry on its old town and an archive under the registry that a page on a table had just named.

The lamp's light lay along the six rows and stopped at the page. He looked at the roads he could not see, east and west, and at the page he had not touched, and at the woman writing three questions in the order she had them, and did not write anything down himself, because it was not his to write yet, and a thing that was not yet his was a thing he could still refuse.

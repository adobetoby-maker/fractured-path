# Chapter 22 — The Still Place

They buried him at the east mouth, under stone, because the west mouth was under stone already and the pass had made the choice for them.

Teague's crew did it. Cael catalogued the order, because it was theirs and he wanted it kept as theirs: the tall woman first, with the spear laid down for the first time in a year and a stone the size of her head lifted in both hands; the broad man on his stick, who could not lift and did, one-handed, small stones, one at a time, from the stream-bed to the place, and did not stop when the older woman with the cloth bag put a hand on his good shoulder, so she took it off; Teague last, with the wrapped arm at his side and the other doing the work of two, laying the stone as he read a job, in order, from the feet up, as if the order mattered, which it did.

The six carried at his leave. He gave it without looking up — "Carry, if you're carrying" — and they carried, from the stream-bed to the mouth, Brom with the bound forearm and the good one, Lira with two, Karis with one hand and the case set down for the first time since a bridge, Vastin from where he sat, handing stones up from the ground to whoever was passing, because he could not stand yet and would not not do it, and Seln, who had come down off the shelf's lip when the ridge stayed empty and had not gone back up. And himself, with the ribs saying their one thing at every stone and the stones going on anyway, because the ribs were not the thing that wanted doing.

No one answered the boy's crew had not said. That was the rule and nobody had made it; a crew that has heard a name said once at a stream-bed, by the man it was not said to, does not say it back, and the six had heard it once and would not, and did not ask it again. The stone went on until it was a shape, and the shape was the size of a boy with a pack too big for him, and then Teague put the last one on, at the head, and stood, and the tall woman picked up the spear.

Oryn stood where she had stood. Three strides. Her kit closed. She had not moved from that distance since her hands had come off, and she did not move from it now, and when the last stone went on she did not look away from it, as she had not looked away from it in the stream-bed, and Cael understood that this was what she had instead of the thing she could not do, and that it was not nothing, and that she would not have called it anything.

Lira and Seln went back for the mule. There was a way. The team had used it: up the shelf's stair, along the ridge above the pass, and down the far side of the hills to the west, where the ground came round to the pass's west mouth from above, and the two of them went that way at Teague's last stone without being asked and came back by dusk, the mule between them with Karis's boxes on it and its head down, having stood tied to a rock all day on the far side of a fall with nothing to say about it, because a mule did not know what had happened on the other side of a heap of stone and a dead tree, and was the only one of the twelve who did not, as it had been the only one on a bridge.

Eleven, and a mule. He entered the count. It was the first time all year that the count had gone the other way. He had counted up at every halt since a landing in the snow — five, six, twelve — and had thought of the counting as a thing that went one way, as a ledger's lines do, and it did not, and he had known that in the abstract for five years and had it now in the particular, and the particular was a boy with a pack too big for him.

---

They camped on the plain's edge below the mouth, where the ground came down off the hills in its last long step and the plain began, flat and pale and hazed, with the sun going down behind the pass and the light going out across the plain ahead of them toward a place a half-day off where the haze had something in it, low, that nobody looked at long.

Oryn read the ribs at the fire before the fire was a fire.

She had come across the camp from the mule with the roll on her back and had stood over him and said, "Ribs. May I?" as she said it every time, because a healer asks every time or she is not one, and he had said, "Yes," and she had put two fingers on his wrist, and then the flat hand on the ribs, on the left, low, where it went, and gone in, and come out, and looked at him as she had looked at a knee.

"That's a line," she said. "That's not yours."

"It's mine now."

"Then it costs what his costs." She took the hand off. "You put a thing through yourself today that goes out along one line and comes back along it, and it came back into the ribs I've been reading since a marsh, because that's where the line stops in you, and it's going to stop there every time, and you know what a wrist looks like after twenty years of it." She did not say *don't*. He noted that she did not. "Lie still when I say. That's still the term. I'm not saying it tonight."

"Why."

"Because tomorrow's a site," Oryn said, "and I'll not set a term tonight I'd have to take back at a line. I've read you as deep as I go and I'll not read you again till you've stood where you're going, because that's when the finding changes, if it changes, and a healer doesn't take a reading the night before the thing she's reading for." She stood. "The ribs will carry. They'll hurt. You know the difference."

"I know the difference."

"Good," she said, and went to Brom's forearm, which was the next thing, and he sat with the ribs and the difference and watched her do the crew in order — the forearm; the broad man's shoulder, at the older woman's leave, her hands in the other's work again; Vastin's knee, which she read and said nothing about, because there was nothing on the twenty-seventh day of a month that had not been on the twenty-sixth; Karis's hand, which she rebound and said "You've been writing with it" and Karis said "Yes" and that was the whole of that — and did not do Teague, because Teague was at the fire's far side with his three and his back to the plain, counting his people, and coming up one short, and not saying the number.

Cael could see him not say it. A man who reads his crew in order, face after face, does not stop reading them because there is one fewer; he reads them in the old order and comes to the gap and goes on past it, and the going-on-past has a shape, and Cael had watched the man for a year and could see the shape from across a fire. He did not enter the number either. It was not his to enter.

---

He could not sleep, and he had known he would not, and he had a reason for not sleeping that was not the ribs and not the stone at the mouth, and he sat up with it against the mule's pack, with the fire low and nine people asleep or pretending, and Lira awake, across the fire, as she was always awake, looking at nothing.

He was going to stand in the middle of the oldest one tomorrow. He had said so at a fire and a table and a bridge and to a woman over a map and at the mouth of a pass, and he had meant it every time, and the last time it had cost a boy, and he had walked to the east mouth with the ribs and the haze and had understood that the line was not drawn in ink; and now, sitting against a pack in the dark, he understood the other half of it, which was that he was going to walk to the end of a line drawn in that, tomorrow, into the first quiet anyone had ever made, without knowing what the thing at the middle of him was.

He had one instrument left to ask.

He had asked the tenth. Six times now, counting a bench at a ford under a healer's hand: the current, his own, going down through the thirteen to the middle and going round, and coming back with the same finding every time, which was *nothing*. Not empty; still. A place the current was not. Oryn had asked with hers, deeper than he could, and come up with the same, and had said it was the bottom of what she was, and he believed her, because she had never once said a thing about a reading that was not so.

The twelfth was not for that. He had the card for it from a chamber under a city, in Cleon's words and his own: *made things only; not a river; not a man.* It read what somebody had built, and told you how, and did not tell you why, and it had turned on a bench by itself, without his leave, toward the place the tenth went round, and had come back with a thing he had entered as *set* and had not entered as anything else, because he had not had leave to look and a thing seen without leave is a thing you do not describe.

And Cleon, at a table under a city, with a cup of water in his hand, had said the thing that had leaned.

*If that fragment ever reads something for you that shouldn't be made and reads it anyway — Don't tell me. Don't tell anyone. Just remember I said it.*

He remembered it. He sat against the pack and remembered it as exactly as he could, because he was about to do the thing it was about, and a man who is about to do a thing he was warned against should at least have the warning by heart.

Then he turned the twelfth.

Not by itself, this time. He turned it, as Cleon turned it, with the eyes going to the unfocused place between a thing and himself, which in the dark against a pack was nowhere, and was easy; and with the lean found — the direction a maker puts in a thing; which mark bears, which is borne — and held; and he turned it inward, the whole of it, toward the middle, toward the place under where a healer's front hand had lain on a table in the autumn, the place the current went round, and let it look.

It looked.

*Still*, first. He had that from the bench and he had it now, and he held there, because it agreed with two readings by two people and a thing that agrees with a healer is a thing you can enter. Still. Not flowing. Not moving. The tenth's finding, in the twelfth's grammar.

Then *set*. He had that too. Not a heap; a thing put where it was so that a next thing could sit on it. He had entered it as *set* on a bench and had gone no further because he had had no leave, and he had leave now, his own, which was the only kind there was going to be, and he went further.

He held the twelfth on the still place as Cleon had held it on a row of six marks — the eyes on nothing, the lean found, the thing not described but *read*, left to right, bearer to borne — and it stopped being still and set and started being a shape.

Bones.

That was the first word, and it was not a word he chose; it was the word the shape was nearest, and he let it stand because he had no better one and because a cataloguer enters the nearest word and marks it *nearest*. Not a hole. A hole was what the tenth had found, because the tenth was for current and a place with no current is a hole to it, as a room with no light is dark to an eye. This was not that. This had *structure*. It went a way. It had a thing that bore and a thing that was borne, and rows, and the rows leaned, and the lean was the lean of the floors — the same lean; the one he had seen go along six marks on a board and along a Span's piers toward the east — and it was not a heap and it was not nothing and it was not a man's, because a man, Cleon said, did not read, and this read.

A floor with nothing built on it.

That was the second thing, and it came as the first had, as the nearest shape: a floor. Seamless. Level. Cut to bear a thing that was not on it — the rows all going somewhere and the somewhere empty; the bearers with nothing borne; the whole of it *ready*, as the Ring had been ready and the Stair had been ready and the Span had been, for four hundred metres of quiet round a made thing, ready for a thing that had not come or had gone. An instruction with no language. He had watched Cleon say that a row was an instruction and that he could not say what it instructed; this was that, and it was inside him, under the ribs that a healer had been reading since a marsh, and it instructed nothing he could read, and it *leaned*.

He did not name it.

He held it as long as he could, which was not long — the eyes were paying already; the fire across the camp had gone to a smear and Lira's shape beyond it to a darker one — and he took the twelfth off the place as a man takes his hand off a stove, not fast, because fast is a decision and he had not decided anything, and sat against the pack with the fire a smear and the ribs saying their one thing and the eyes saying theirs, and did not think.

Then he thought one thing, because it was the only thing that was a finding and not a guess, and he entered it exactly: *the same kind of thing, to that instrument, as the sites were to Cleon's.*

Not the same thing. He had no instrument that could say that and no leave to guess it. The same *kind*. A made thing's inside, read by a Path built for made things, returning what that Path returned when it was turned on a floor: a lean, a structure, a readiness, an instruction it could not read. Cleon had turned his on a chamber's worth of floors and a leaf and a board and had come up with *this is a stair* and had never once said what the stair was for. Cael had turned his on the one place in himself that nothing else could read and had come up with the same grammar, and he sat with that and did not turn it into a sentence, because the sentence was six words long and he had watched a woman refuse a six-word sentence at a fire the night before and had entered the refusal as the whole of what a year had been for.

He understood what had just happened. He did not need it explained. A man at a table with a cup had said *if it ever reads something for you that shouldn't be made and reads it anyway*, and it had, and he had felt it read, and the reading was on the page now in the only words he was going to allow it, and the man had said *don't tell anyone*, and he was not going to.

Not Lira, across the fire, awake, who was always first and would be first with this too, one day, when there was a day for it. Not Oryn, whose finding was hers and was *nothing* and was true, and who had said at a ford that a thing not for fighting was in him and she did not know what it was for and would not ask, and had kept to that. Not Karis, who would have put it on a third page and not drawn a line to it, and who did not need a third page tonight. Nobody. That was the instruction, and for once in his life he had been given an instruction by a man who knew exactly what he was instructing, and he was going to keep it.

---

He checked the other things, after, because a cataloguer who has found a thing he cannot file files the things beside it, and because the eyes came back slowly and there was an hour to fill with something that was not the shape.

The Arbiter first. He checked it as a man checks a lamp he knows is out: the mark given at the kindling, at fourteen, in a hall, that had lit for every boy on that floor and had not lit for him, and had not lit since, in five years, at any floor, at any line, in any quiet. It was dark. It had always been dark. He went to the place where it should have been and there was the place and nothing in it, as there had been on a floor at the Ring with every other sigil in the party going out at a stride and his not going out because it had never been on. Nothing in him for the Quiet to take. There had never been. He entered *unchanged*, and the date, because a finding that has not changed needs a second date and not a second description.

Then the instruments.

He had the numbers from Lowmarch in the log, from the first week of the winter, in the plain hand, and he read them by the fire's smear before he touched anything: *the yard, forty-two strides for everyone and forty-one and a piece for me, three times; the Ternhall edge touching three and the fourth a hair short in my hand; Hesk's edge a hair long.* A cord and two brass edges that read everyone but him. He had had it then as a hold, with Karis's rule beside it, and he had carried it since, and at the Span, on the crown, Karis had counted a row of sheets near his hands and it had come out a sheet short, again, as at a spring, and she had looked at his hands, once, and said nothing, and he had entered that under the same hold.

He took the cord out of the kit's case, quietly, and paced the camp's edge with it in the dark. Twenty strides, by the cord, from the mule to the last stone of the fire's ring. He paced it back. Nineteen and a piece. He paced it a third time. Nineteen and a piece. He put the cord down, and did not pace it a fourth time, because three was the number and a fourth would have been wanting a different answer.

Then the edges. He laid the Ternhall edge along the map's line by the fire's last light, as he had at Lowmarch, and it touched three of the five circles and missed the fourth and the fifth by more than a hair — he sighted along it and there was daylight, a hair and a half, two, where at Lowmarch there had been one — and he laid Hesk's edge, which was true to a hair by the man who had cut every gauge in a shop in Denvash, and it was long, and it was longer than it had been on a landing in the snow, and he sat with both edges in his hands and the fire going out and did the thing Hesk had taught him to do with any two numbers taken at two times, which was to put them side by side and say which way they went.

They went up.

At Lowmarch a hair. Now more than a hair. A cord a piece short in the winter and a whole stride short tonight. The instruments read wrong for him and for nobody else, and they read *wronger* than they had, and he had two dates and two numbers and a direction, which was a trend, and Hesk had said that a man who catalogues everything eventually needs to note the trend, and he noted it, and could not source it, and did not try. He put the two facts beside each other on the page — *the instruments read wronger; the oldest one is a half-day east* — and did not draw a line between them, because a line was a thing you drew when you had measured both ends, and he had measured one and was going to stand on the other tomorrow, and standing was not measuring either.

The fire went out. The plain was dark to the east, and the haze had gone with the light, and there was nothing to see where the thing was, and he did not look, because it did not know he was coming and he did not need to look at a thing that did not know.

He opened the log against the pack, by feel, and wrote in the plain hand, in the dark, as he had learned to on roads, at a size he would be able to read in the morning.

*Three hundred and sixth day, night. The plain's edge below the pass. A hundred and twenty-three in hand; nothing owed. Nineteen. Thirteen. Eleven of us, and a mule, and a boy under stone at the east mouth whose name I was told and am not going to write. Ribs: a line. Brom's arm. The broad man's shoulder. Vastin on the ground. Teague one short and not saying it.*

*Turned the Rune fragment on myself. Cleon said don't. The reading only ever told me the still place was nothing — no flow, no current, a hole where a man should have a middle. The Rune sense says it's not a hole. It says it's *built like something* — like one of those floors with nothing standing on it. Same shape to that fragment as the sites are to Cleon's. I'm not going to write what that might mean, because I don't know, and because the last time I wrote something true in this log a healer made me swear an oath about it. The instruments read wronger for me than they did at Lowmarch. The trend's climbing. My Arbiter's still dark—nothing in me to go quiet, same as always. Tomorrow, the oldest one. I've spent the whole year wanting to stand in the middle of it. I think part of me already knows what I'm going to feel. Nothing. I think I'm going to feel nothing, and I think that's going to be the loudest thing that's ever happened to me.*

# Chapter 11 — The Marks

By the third day down he had learned the lamp.

That was the whole of his trade in the chamber, and he learned it as he had learned to hold a cord's far end for a woman who was pacing: where the light had to be for the reader, which was never where the reader was looking but a hand to the left of it, so that the hand did not shadow the line; when to move it, which was before she reached for it and not after; how to hold a sheet flat on the table's edge with two fingers at its corners so that a man could read the whole of a carving-copy without the paper's curl putting a false line across a true one. Labour. He was good at it by the second hour of the second day and very good at it by the third, and Cleon had stopped saying "Lamp" and had started simply turning his head a finger's width, and the light was there, and Cael found that he did not mind being a thing a man turned his head for, and wrote that down at the bell, because it was true and he had not expected it.

The two hundred and eighty-sixth day. The chamber under the registry, the lamp that was always lit and the one he held, Cleon in the cushioned chair and Karis in the one that had none, and on the table between them the Ring's eleven and the Stair's twenty-nine and the Hall's twelve, and beside the Hall's, on its own square of cloth, the thing Cleon had kept on the shelf behind him for ten years.

He catalogued it because Cleon had let him hold it, once, to move it into the light, and a thing you have held is a thing you owe a description. A board. Not a leaf; a flat piece of a wood he did not know, the length of a forearm and a hand wide, gone the colour of old iron, with a face to it, and on the face, cut in with something finer than a chisel, lines. Not letters. He knew the Compact's letters and had seen four scripts that were not the Compact's on the shelves in three days, and these were none of the five, so far as he could tell: lines that went across the board's face in runs, some straight and some bent at the ends, with the bends the same at the ends of some runs and different at the ends of others, so that the whole of the face looked, if you held it far enough from your eye, like a wall Karis had copied. He had thought that on the first day and had not said it. On the third day he did not need to, because that was the day Cleon put the board beside the Hall's fourth sheet and said, "Now."

---

"You'll want to watch this," Cleon said, "because it's the only thing I do that's worth watching, and I've never done it in front of anyone."

He said it to the sheet. He had his right hand flat on the board and his left flat on the copy, a finger's width apart on the table, and his eyes were not on either; they were on the place between them, unfocused, like a man listening to two things at once and trying to hear whether they are the same sound.

"A carving's a made thing," he said. "Somebody cut it. Somebody decided where every line went, and every line's there for a reason, and the reasons aren't equal. Some lines carry. Some lines are carried. And some — " his finger moved on the board, not touching, above a short run at the edge — "some are nothing. Ornament. A man's hand doing what a hand does at the end of a row. I see which is which. That's the whole of the Path. I put my attention on a made thing and I see the bones of it — which mark's holding the others up, which mark would bring the rest down if you cut it out, which mark you could cut out and nothing would notice." He moved his hand to the sheet. "Here. This one." Above a mark on the Hall's fourth copy, one of the forty-one kinds, a bent run with a hook at the end. "That's a bearer. Cut it and this whole row's nothing. And this — " a shorter run beside it — "is carried. It rests on the first. And this — " the smallest, at the row's end — "is a man finishing a line with his hand because the stone had room. Ornament. Nothing." He looked at Karis. "Do you see it?"

"No," Karis said. "I see forty-one kinds of mark in three strata. I don't see which ones hold."

"No. You wouldn't. It's not in the strata; it's in the make." He put his hand back on the board. "Now the board. Same thing. This run bears. These three rest on it. This at the end is a hand finishing a row." He was quiet a moment. "Same bones. The same three kinds in the same order, bearer and carried and ornament, in the same places in the row. I've read this board for ten years and I've known it had a structure and I've never once had a second thing with the same structure to put beside it, and now I have, and it's a floor."

Cael held the lamp a hand to the left of the board and did not move it, with Brom and Lira at the rock wall behind him and Vastin on the knee beside the case, all three of them as still as labour is still when it has nothing to carry, and watched a man read a made thing's skeleton, and understood, at arm's length, at the level of the hands, what the instrument was.

It was not the reading. Oryn's hands went into a thing and followed what flowed. This did not go in. It stood outside a made thing and saw how it had been put together — saw load — as the Iron read, on a floor, had told him where a thing the weight of a cart was pushing. But the Iron read felt weight. This saw *design*: which of a maker's choices were the ones the thing stood on. He had wanted, against a post across the river, an instrument for what did not move. This was an instrument for what had been *built*, and it was standing three feet from him, in a man's hands, being used on a wall.

"There's a second thing," Cleon said, "and it's the thing that matters, and I'll say it once and I'll say it plain, so you'll not go away thinking the Path does more than it does."

He sat back. He took both hands off the table.

"A made thing's either a record or an instruction. It describes, or it commands. A ledger describes: this much, on this day, to this man. A recipe commands: do this, then this. They're built differently — the bones are different — and I can see which is which as you can see whether a wall's holding a roof or just standing there. These" — the sheets — "command. All fifty-two. Every run on every floor is built as an instruction's built, with the bearers where an instruction puts them. They're not the marks somebody cuts to say *we were here*, and they're not the marks somebody cuts to make a floor handsome. They're orders." He looked at the board. "This is an order too. Same make."

Karis had not moved. Her pencil was on the notebook and had not touched it.

"And here's the limit, and I want it in your book in my words so nobody can say I claimed past it." He said it to her, and he said it slowly. "I can tell you these are orders. I can't tell you what they order. For that you need the language, and the language is in the text, not the stone. That's her work, not mine."

He looked at Karis when he said *her*, and she wrote it down, in his words, and Cael watched her write it, and saw her underline *not the stone*, once, because that was where the limit fell and she wanted the line under it.

"Structure," Cleon said. "Not meaning. It works on a made thing — a carving, a wall, a document, a thing with a maker's choices in it. It doesn't work on a river, and it doesn't work on a man, and it doesn't work on a tree, because nobody chose where the branches went. It costs me my eyes for an hour after and nothing else. It's no use to anyone in a fight, which is why I'm Bronze at sixty and have never once minded. And it can't read. I want that understood. I've had this board ten years and I can tell you every bone in it and I cannot tell you one word it says, because it doesn't say words to me. It says *load*. The words are in the text, in a hand I can half read, and the text's your work, and I've been waiting thirty years for someone to walk in and do it."

Karis asked him one thing, then, in the voice for a finding. "The three floors. Same make, all three?"

"Same make. Same bearers in the same places. The Hall's rows are longer and the Stair's are cut deeper, but the bones are the bones. Somebody cut three floors two days' walk apart in the same hand, or in three hands taught the same thing, and the thing they were taught is the thing on this board." He looked at the board. "I'll not say more than that. I've said more than I've said in thirty years."

"Noted," Karis said, and wrote it, and underlined nothing, because there was nothing in it yet to underline.

Cael held the lamp still.

*It doesn't work on a man.* He had it. He had wanted, without letting himself want it, to ask the question he had never let anyone hold — to put the one instrument on the continent that read built things onto the thing in the middle of him that did not flow and see what it said — and the man had just answered it without being asked, at a table, as a limit stated plainly so that nobody could claim past it. *Nobody chose where the branches went.* A man was not a made thing. The instrument did not go on him.

He noted that he had wanted it to. He noted that the wanting had been there since a post by a river, and that he had not said it, and that he was not going to, and that a thing which read only what had been built could not be turned on a person, and that he had been sorry, and that the sorriness was a thing he did not have a column for and was going to have to carry down a stair and up again with a box. He did not say any of it. He moved the lamp a hand to the left when Cleon turned his head, and the light was there.

---

Brom's offer came at the bell.

Cael was in the yard for it, because the bell had sent the hands up and out of the cellar in the order they had gone down, and Brom had been first down and was first up, and Cael, with the middle box, came through the inn's yard gate a dozen strides behind him and stopped, because there was a cart on its side in the yard and a man of Brom's size standing at it with a box set down carefully at his feet.

A broker's cart. It had come through the yard's gate too fast, by the evidence — a wheel in the drain, the bed over, the load on the stones — and the carter was at the beast's head with the traces fouled, and the broker's factor, a man in a coat that had a colour, stood at the cart's tail with a ledger under his arm and the look of a man pricing a loss.

Brom set his shoulder to the cart's bed.

He did it without a Path, because a healer had said not a door, not a box, not a man, and Brom kept a price; with the good shoulder and his back and his legs, and the foot with the tooth-hole in it taking the brace on the stones, and the bed came up, and hung, and the good shoulder said something Cael saw in the set of it and Brom did not, and the cart went back onto its wheels with the load on and stood there. Brom stepped back from it and put a hand on the box at his feet as if the box were the thing that wanted seeing to.

The factor watched him do it. He watched him set the box down first and touch the cart second, and Cael saw him price it — the size of the man; the care; the Copper's paper that a man in that trade could see on a stranger as a smith sees a shoe — and the factor did not wait, because a broker who sees a thing worth having does not.

"You've paper," he said. It was not a question.

"Copper," Brom said. "Circuit-rated."

"I've a yard here and a second on the second tier, and I've no one in either who could have done that without a Path, and two who couldn't have done it with one." He named the wage. It was more than the five had earned in the winter, and he said it as a man says a thing he knows is enough. "Guard's post. A pass — not the district's nothing; a working pass to the second tier with my name on it, and a bed inside the second wall. You'd be ranked, here. You'd be what you are."

Brom listened to the whole of it. Cael, at the gate with the middle box, watched him listen — the shoulder still, the face he used for a wall somebody else was pricing — and knew from the face that Brom was pricing it honestly, because Brom priced everything honestly, and that it was a good price. It was the life. It was the one his family had wanted from him, at fourteen, with a Gold in their eye and a Copper in his hand; the one he had walked away from into a circuit and then into a road and then across a Line; the one that had been waiting, it turned out, in a broker's yard on the Line for a man big enough to put a cart back on its wheels. It was clean. Nobody at Lowmarch's plank had ever offered him anything clean.

Brom picked up the box while the factor was still talking. He put it on the shoulder that was not the good one, because the good one had just paid for a cart and he knew it, and he would carry the bound one wrong for a day and say nothing, and Cael entered the debt where debts went.

"I'll think on it," Brom said, which was courtesy, because Brom did not lie.

Then he picked up the first box, and put it on the good shoulder, and went back across the yard toward the inn's door and the morning and the stair and the hole under the city where a woman was reading a wall, and the factor watched him go with the face of a man who has priced a thing correctly and been declined by it, and did not understand, and did not need to.

That was the whole statement. Cael wrote it as one, at the bell: *Brom was offered the life. He picked up the box.*

He asked Cael one thing about it, later, on the stair, going down, with the boxes.

"You think Karis is right? That it's all built on something?"

"I think we're about to find out."

Brom shifted the box. "Good. I'd hate to have turned down a real job for a wrong hunch."

He was joking. Cael heard the joke. He also heard the other thing, under it, which was a man who had put a cart back on its wheels with his back because a healer had said not to use his shoulder, and had been offered a bed inside a wall for it, and had picked up a box, and wanted, very slightly, to be told that the box was going somewhere. Cael did not tell him. He did not know. He said, "So would I," which was true, and they went down.

---

Seln walked the tiers that night.

Cael did not see him go and did not see him come back, and knew both, because a man who has slept in the same room as Seln for a year knows the shape of the room with Seln in it and the shape without, and woke at the second shape, some hour after the city's bell had gone for the last time, and lay in the dark and did not get up, because the thing Seln was doing was Seln's.

He knew what it was. He had known since the ferry. The cache was on Seln's back — had been on Seln's back for four years, across a hearing and a Line and a rim with four crossbows in the scrub, carried and never opened and targeted once and kept — and it had walked, four days ago, into the first governed city Seln had stood in since he had walked out of one with it. And up the hill, on the crown, past three gates and a square, there was a registry, with a book in it and a counter and a careful young man with a pen, and behind the registry, somewhere in the west, the office the registry answered to, which would have paid for what was in the cache in a currency Seln had stopped taking, in a room he had stopped standing in, on a day he had stopped counting from.

He had not decided anything. That was the thing Cael understood in the dark, without a word from anyone: that a man who carries a thing for four years without opening it is not a man who has decided to keep it. He is a man who has decided not to decide, every day, and has found that the not-deciding is a thing you can carry, and has carried it, and has walked it now into the one place on the road where the decision had a counter to be made at. Seln had gone up the hill to look at the counter. Not to use it. To look. As he had looked at a landing door for a winter, and at four crossbows in scrub, and at a sheet with a bar of ink on it: to know where a thing was, exactly, so that he could keep on not going to it on purpose instead of by accident.

How he went up three tiers at night with no pass was not a thing Cael asked, then or after. He had a guess. The stairs cut in the bluff had rails, and the rails ended where the walls began, and the walls were old, and Seln had told a room once that a city's walls were built by people who wanted to keep things out and not by people who had ever tried to get in. He had a guess and he kept it, because a guess about Seln was a thing you kept.

Seln came back before the light. Cael heard the room change shape and did not move, and Seln crossed to his blanket and lay down, and was still, and then said, to the dark, in the voice for an inventory, very low, so that only the one person in the room who was awake would have it:

"I looked at it."

That was all. Cael did not answer. He lay in the dark and had, from a man who said a thing once, the shape of the thing, which was as much as Seln ever gave, and the grit on a boot, which Cael had heard on the floor and not seen, said a roof: that Seln had gone up in the night — not to the crown; Seln did not stand beside a thing that read bodies; to somewhere under it, a roof in the district or the foot of a stair, with the cache on his back and the station's pale light above him — and had looked up at the door his old trade would have walked through, and had decided nothing, and had come back down, seen by nobody, and had let one person know that he had decided nothing, which was the nearest thing to trust that Seln had ever handed anyone, and was, Cael thought, a great deal.

He wrote it down in the morning in six words, because six was what it was. *Seln looked at it. Decided nothing.*

Lira, who had also been awake — she was always awake when the room changed shape, and had been since a ford — said nothing about it at all, then or after, which was the other half of the thing: a crew that keeps a man's not-deciding for him without once asking him to decide.

---

Vastin, that week, was learning to be nobody, and was bad at it.

Cael catalogued the badness because it was honest and because he had never once seen a man try. The man carried the kit's case down the stair each morning on the knee and set it down against the rock and stood beside it, and did not draft anything, and did not offer anything, and did not say his name to anyone, and the not-doing sat on him like a coat cut for a bigger man. He had stood at the head of things for forty years, and standing at the foot of them was a thing his body did not know the shape of: he stood too straight for a hand; he watched the readers like an officer at a briefing and not like labour watching a woman work; and when Cleon turned his head for the lamp, Vastin's hand moved before Cael's did, every time, and stopped, because the lamp was Cael's, and the stopping was the whole of his week.

On the fourth day he said, to Cael, at the rock wall, very quietly, "How long did it take you."

"To be nobody?"

"Yes."

"I was fourteen. I'm nineteen." Cael moved the lamp. "I'm not there yet either."

Vastin took that. He did not draft anything. He stood at the foot of a thing, badly, and kept standing.

---

Late on the fourth day, with the bell an hour off and the lamp low, Cleon put the Hall's fourth sheet beside the board for the last time that day and sat looking at the two of them for a long while without reading either, and then said, to Karis, in a voice Cael had not heard from him — not the voice for a limit; the other one, the one a man keeps for a thing he has carried alone:

"You've been reading the strata. Old, older, oldest — I know, I read your face, you've dated these past anything the Compact admits." He tapped the board. "Here's what the strata can't tell you. These aren't the ruins of something. This is the *documentation* of something. Somebody wrote these down so the thing could be *built the same way twice*." He sat back. "In thirty years I never had the stone to match the text. You walked in with three floors." A beat. "I don't think you're going to like what you've found, Karis. I've had a piece of it for a decade and I've never once slept well since."

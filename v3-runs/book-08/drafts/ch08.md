# Chapter 8 — The Line at Treswick

The bridge was older than the walls.

He knew it before he was on it, because the walls were ochre and the bridge was grey, and grey was a colour he had learned to read in a year of floors: not the fused grey of a thing that had never had a join; the grey of stone that had been cut and laid and had stood in a river long enough for the river to give up on it. Five arches, at first light on the two hundred and eighty-second day, with the water high and brown under them and the road going over the crown of the middle one in a hump that a cart had to take slowly, and a toll-house at the far end with nobody in it, because the toll-house was for the road and the road's toll was taken at the gate.

He catalogued the bridge because it was the last thing outside.

Six of them on it, and the mule, in the order they walked. Seln first, over the hump, and stopping on the far side to look at the gate as he looked at gates. Lira. Brom on the foot. Karis with the flat, reading the bluff above the bridge as it came closer and got taller and turned, at the last, from a thing you looked at into a thing you were under. Cael. And Vastin, at the back again, where he had walked into every town on the road, because a town was a place with a plank in it and the man had learned what a plank did to him.

The gate was in the first wall, at the bluff's foot, where the bridge road met the ring road that went round the bottom of the city, and it was a gate that shut: two leaves of iron-bound oak on hinges the size of a man's thigh, standing open, with a bar leaning against the wall beside them that it would take two men to lift, and a man in a coat that was not grey sitting on a stool inside the leaves with a book on his knee, who looked at the six of them and the mule as they came under the arch, and counted, and wrote, and did not ask.

He did not ask. Cael had braced for the asking from the far side of the bridge — the way a body braces for a step that is not there — and the asking did not come, and he walked under the arch and into Treswick with his satchel on his shoulder and his hands at his sides and nothing said to him at all, and understood, three strides inside, why.

The gate-keeper had not asked because the outer district was where paper was not asked for. That was its whole function. A city that ran on rank had to have a place at its foot where things that had no rank could be unloaded, and this was the place: the ring road, and the streets going off it into the space between the first wall and the second, and every one of them full of the things a rank does not have — carts, mules, bales, brokers' yards with tally-boards nailed up outside, a ferry landing on the river side with the season's first hides stacked on it, inns with two doors, a smith, a rope-walk, men and women moving between all of it with the walk of people who were paid by the load and not by the tier. The unranked district. The city's foot. He had lived at the foot of a city once, at fourteen, for a season, and had not known then that it was a mechanism; he had thought it was where he had ended up. It was not where anyone ended up. It was where the city put you, so that you could carry things for it without walking on its stairs.

He looked up, then, once, because a man who is going to keep to a place should know the shape of what he is keeping out of.

The second wall, above the roofs, with its gate in it — a smaller gate than the first, with a man in a coat at it that had a colour, and a queue of people going through it who each did a thing with their hand as they went that he could not see from here and could guess. A pass. The third wall above that, its gate smaller still, its queue shorter. And above the third wall the bluff's crown, and the roofs of the old town on it, and one roof taller than the rest with a square in front of it that he could not see and knew was there, and on the roof a thing he was not looking at.

He was not looking at it. He looked at the ring road, and the smith, and the ferry landing, and the mule.

The mule did not care. He noted that with something that was not quite gratitude. A mule at the foot of a tiered city was exactly what it was at a ford or a marsh: a thing that carried, and was fed, and was not asked. The city had a column for the mule. It was the same column it had for him, and the mule had walked into it without braking for a step that was not there, and he found, watching the animal put its head down to a trough by the gate, that there was a lesson in that he did not want and was going to take anyway.

---

They took two rooms at an inn on the ring road that had a door on the street and a door on a yard, because Seln chose it, and Seln chose inns for their doors. Brom paid — two nights, in front, a mark a night for the six, which was the outer district's price for a room with a floor — and the woman who kept it counted six people and one mule and wrote six in a book and did not write what they were, because her book had no column for that, and Cael read her book upside down across the counter out of a habit he had thought a winter would cure, and it had not.

Then the crew did the thing it did in a new place, which was to go and look at it, each as each looked.

He catalogued them going, because he was staying.

Karis first, out the street door with the flat under her arm and the page on top, toward the second gate, because the registry office was on the old town and the scholars' registers she had read years ago said the provision was kept in a book at the registry and she wanted to see the outside of the building the book was in before she saw the inside of anything. She could not go through the second gate. She was unranked by choice, which was a thing the city priced exactly as it priced unranked by birth, and she knew it, and went to look at the gate anyway, because a gate was a stratum.

Lira, out the yard door, toward the ring road's far side where the street went up toward the second wall in a stair — an actual stair, cut in the bluff, with a rail — because Lira had seen the stair from the bridge and had looked at it as she looked at a thing with a top. She could have gone up. Iron, first rank, formal, on paper from a circuit — the second tier was hers if she showed the paper, and she had the paper in her coat where she had carried it across a continent, and she did not take it out, and he watched her not take it out and go to look at the stair from its foot, with her two hands at her sides.

Brom, nowhere, which meant the smith, because Brom priced a town by its smith.

Seln, gone, as he went, which was not out of either door that anybody saw.

And Vastin, who had not gone anywhere. He sat in the inn's long room with his stick against his knee and his back to the wall, and when Cael sat down across from him he said, without being asked, "I have papers I will not show. I want that said once, so that nobody asks twice. They would open every gate on that hill and the station's door at the top of it, and I carried them out of an office because a man does not leave his own name behind on a desk, and I will not show them to a clerk in a city on the Line to save myself a stair." He looked at the stick. "I am in the district where the unranked are allowed, and it is the first honest district I have stood in."

Cael did not say anything to that. There was not anything to say to it that the man had not already said, and he had said it once, and once was the number.

Brom came back from the smith with the foot re-bound and a price for iron in his head and said, "They shoe by the tier," which was the whole of Brom's report on Treswick and was, from Brom, an essay: the smith at the city's foot shod carters' mules for coppers and had a second forge, up a stair, that shod ranked practitioners' horses for silver, and the same man worked both, and walked between them by a pass. "Same hands," Brom said. "Different price. He doesn't like it either." He sat down with the foot up and did not say anything else, because he had said the thing.

---

He walked the district, because a man who is going to keep to a place should walk its edges.

He walked them the way Karis would have paced a perimeter: the ring road round the bluff's foot from the bridge gate east along the river to the ferry landing and past it to where the first wall came down to the water and stopped, and back west along the wall's foot the other way, past the smith and the rope-walk and the brokers' yards to where the wall came down to the water on the far side. A circle, near enough. A line, with the city inside it and him outside, and everything on his side of the line a thing that had no rank and everything on the other side a thing that did, and the line was a wall, a real one, ochre, with a gate in it that a man with a coloured coat sat at, and he could not have crossed it if he had wanted to, and he did not want to, and the not-wanting was the strangest thing he had felt in a year.

He had spent nine months in a country that did not read him. It had not asked. It had not measured. It had priced what he did and written it in a book and stopped numbering him when the number got wrong, and he had written *no file I can see* in a log until the sentence had gone soft with use, and had found, in the winter, that a country that did not read you was also a country that did not answer, and had not liked finding it. And here was a country that read. Here was the whole machine — the tiers, the gates, the passes, the office on the old town, the thing on the crown — stacked up a hill in front of him in three rings with the sun on it, and it read everyone who walked up its stairs, and it would read him the moment he set a foot on one, and he stood at the foot of it with his hands at his sides and felt the walls as walls, which was a thing he had not felt since a bluff above Ostrand, and found that he had missed it, and did not know what to do with having missed it, and wrote that down.

The road up to the registry went through the square.

He worked that out at the second gate, from below, with the ring road under his feet and the stair going up the bluff above him. The stair went to the second gate. The second gate opened on the second tier, and the second tier's main street went up through the third gate to the old town, and the old town's main street ran to the registry office, and the registry office's door was on the square, and the square was at the station's foot, and the station's steps came down into the square, and there was no way to the registry that did not cross the square, because the old town had been built with one square in it and had put everything that mattered on it.

He could not have gone up the stair. But if he could have, that was the road, and it went through the square, and a healer four days north had said *not the square*, and he had written it, and read it back, and here was the first place on the continent where the promise and the road were the same thing, and the promise held, because he stood at the foot of a stair he was not allowed on and did not try the other way round the bluff, which a man who wanted the registry badly enough might have tried, and which he catalogued himself not trying, and let stand.

He went back along the ring road to the inn, by the smith, where Brom was, past a broker's yard where a man with a tally-board was counting bales by the load and not by anything else, and sat in the yard, and waited, because the district where the unranked were allowed was the district where the unranked waited, and he was going to find out what waiting was worth.

---

Seln came back at dusk, by the yard door, and had the whole of it.

He gave it at the long table as he gave a report — in the voice for an inventory, standing, without notes, in the order the thing was shaped and not the order he had found it — and Cael wrote it down as it came, because Seln said a thing once.

"The archive's under the registry. Not near it; under. The old town's built on the bluff's crown and the crown's hollow — quarried, before there was a Compact, for the stone the first wall's made of — and when they built the registry they built it on the quarry's roof and used the quarry for shelving. Nobody staffs it. Nobody catalogues it. It's Compact property in the sense that the Compact owns the building it's under, and in no other sense, because the Compact has never once sent a clerk down the stair, and the stair's behind a door in the registry's cellar that the registry keeps locked because a locked door is cheaper than a decision.

"There's one way in that's legal. The old scholars' provision. It's older than the standardization and they never repealed it because nobody uses it: a registered historical-Path-studies practitioner may enter the pre-Compact archive for the purposes of study, with a working party. The provision's kept in a book at the registry, and the book's kept by a clerk, and the clerk's form for the provision has three columns on it — the practitioner's name, his registration, and the count of hands."

"Count," Karis said.

"Count. Not names. Not tiers. A number, up to four, entered by the practitioner and answered for by him. The form's four hundred years old and nobody has ever redrawn it, because nobody has ever needed to, because nobody has used the provision in living memory but one man, and he uses it alone." Seln looked at Karis. "Cleon. Registered historical-Path practitioner, the only one west of the Line. Rune Path. Bronze. Sixties. He's been going down that stair for thirty years and coming up with things nobody reads, and the clerk knows him the way a gate knows a cart. He has a house in the old town and a table in a tavern on the third tier where he eats at noon, alone, and reads."

"The clerk," Cael said.

"Young. Careful. Keeps the book because the book's a thing that's kept, not because he knows what's in it. He has never once, I'd say, had a working party entered on that form, and the first time he does he'll look at the count and look at the hands and he'll want to ask a hand for paper, because a careful clerk asks, and the provision doesn't say he can't." Seln's voice did not change. "That's the exposure. It's exact. The bar on the boy isn't lifted by the provision; it's not consulted, because the form has no column for it. If a clerk asks a hand for paper the bar applies and the practitioner answers for having signed for him. So the clerk has to be handled, and he has to be handled so that he never thinks to ask, and he has to be handled by somebody with a reason to be standing at that counter, and that's not me and it's not the boy." He looked at Karis. "It's you. You're the working researcher. You attach to the practitioner. You stand at the counter with him and you talk about strata until the clerk wants you both gone, and he enters the count, and the count goes down the stair."

Nobody said anything. Karis had the notebook open and had not written in it, which was how Cael knew she had known the provision already and had wanted to hear a professional say it.

"I read the provision years ago," she said. "In a register, when I was reading everything. I never thought I'd stand at the counter it's kept at. I never thought I'd have a reason." She looked at the flat under her hand, and the page on it. "Four hands."

"Four," Seln said. "By count."

Cael understood it then, the whole of it, in the order Seln had shaped it, and he sat with it for a breath because it was worth a breath.

The only legal way for him to reach the oldest record of what he was, was to be entered on a four-hundred-year-old form as a number. Not a name. Not a tier. A *hand* — an unranked body carrying a box down a stair for a registered man, which was the one thing a Compact city allowed an unranked body to be, and was exactly what the city, looking at him from the top of its stairs, thought he was worth. He would go down into the quarry under the registry as labour. He would carry Karis's boxes. He would be counted, once, at a counter, by a careful young clerk who would not ask, and the count would be *four*, and one of the four would be the thing the whole building on top of the quarry had been built to keep out, and the building would not know, because its form had no column for that.

Lira had come in during the last of it, by the yard door, and had stood at the wall and listened, and when Seln had finished she said, to nobody, "Four hands," and then, "I've two," and looked at the left one, and Cael understood that she was pricing herself as labour, on purpose, in a city that would have taken her paper and put her on the second tier by noon, and that she had decided which of the two things she was going to be here before anyone had asked her, and had decided it at the foot of a stair.

He wrote it down. He wrote: *Entered by count. That's the door.* And under it, because the log did something to him and he wanted to find out what: *It's the right price. It's what I'm worth here. I'll carry the boxes.*

---

Karis went up in the morning, because the provision was worked in the morning when the registry opened, and because a thing that has been waited five years for is not waited a day longer than it has to be.

She went out the street door with the flat under her arm and the page on top and the notebook in her hand and nothing else, because a working researcher attaching to a registered practitioner carries her work and not her boxes, and she stopped at the door and looked back at the long room, at the five of them and the mule's chair, and at Cael, who was sitting at the table with the log open and the pencil in his hand and his back to the wall, in the district where the unranked were allowed.

He got up and walked her to the ring road, because it was fifty paces and he had walked fifty paces beside people at the edge of things before.

They stood at the foot of the stair. The stair went up the bluff to the second gate, and above the second gate the second wall, and above that the third, and above the third the crown of the hill with the old town on it and the registry's roof, and above the registry's roof the one taller roof and the thing on it with its light, and he did not look at the light. He looked at the stair, which he could not climb, and at Karis, who could not either, and who was going to stand at its foot and wait for a man in his sixties to come down it at noon to eat, and attach to him, and go up.

"Last time we walked into a place like this," Cael said, "it took four years and a hearing to walk out."

"Last time we didn't know what we were looking for."

"Do we now?"

Karis looked up the bluff. She looked at it as she looked at a page — the strata; the walls as layers; the registry on the old town on the quarry on the crown — and she did not look at the light either, and he saw her not look at it, and put that in the place where he kept the things that were true about her.

"A name and a reason," she said. "Four hundred years old, under our feet, and nobody's read it because nobody's had a reason to look and a person who could read it in the same lifetime." A beat. "That's us. That's the whole reason it's still buried. We're the first ones who are both."

She went to find the clerk.

He stayed in the district where the unranked were allowed, at the foot of a stair, with a satchel on his shoulder and ten things in him and a promise in a book, and waited.

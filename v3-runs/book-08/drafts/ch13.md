# Chapter 13 — The Reader

They went down by the carts' street on the two hundred and ninety-first day, in daylight, in the open, with the boxes on a broker's handcart that Seln had borrowed for a copper and a look, because a working party that had been met in a cut at dusk was a working party that went home by the way everyone could see, and a thing everyone could see was a thing nobody could hold.

He catalogued the change because a change of route was a change of state, and because Seln had made it without a word at first light, and the crew had taken it without a word, and the not-saying was the crew.

Six of them, then, on a street with carts on it. Karis with the flat under her right arm and the left hand bound in a strip of Brom's shirt and her own thread, holding it against her coat as she held a thing she did not intend to use until it was mended and did not intend to mention until then either. Brom with the good shoulder carried carefully now, because he had used it wrong on a stave's man and it had told him so in the night, and the bound one carried as it had been carried since a marsh. Lira with two arms and a knife she had wiped twice. Vastin on the knee, with the stick, a day past a plane held across a cut, going up three tiers on it anyway, because a healer had said carry it to Millrace and the man had decided that a hand who could not carry was not a hand. And Cael with the satchel, and eleven things in him now, and the eleventh with nothing to do, because a plane across a cut was a thing you held when something was coming through it, and nothing was.

The clerk counted four at the yard door and did not look at anyone's face for longer than a box's. He had looked twice. He had decided, Cael thought, not to look a third time, because a careful man who has looked twice at a thing and not asked has told himself a story about why, and does not want to be made to tell it a third time.

Karis went up to the counter with Cleon as she had every morning, and came out by the yard door with the pass in his hand and her bound one held against her coat, and the clerk looked at the strip of shirt on it as he had looked at Brom's shoulder on the first morning — a careful man's eye going to the thing carried wrong — and did not ask, and Cael understood that the clerk had now not asked three things, and that a man who has not asked three things has a fourth in him somewhere, and that the fourth was a thing to be gone from the city before.

Down. The iron door. The dark, and the lamp, and the man at the table who was already reading.

He set the middle box against the rock where it went and stood by it, and Brom set the first box down and sat on it with the foot out, and Lira put the third box down and stood, and Vastin set the case down and did not sit, on the knee, because sitting was a thing he had decided a hand did not do until the reader did, and nobody had told him that and he had worked it out from six days of watching Cael, which was, Cael thought, the first thing the man had learned by watching rather than by being told, and was not nothing.

---

"Today I'll show you the inside of it," Cleon said, "because tomorrow I'm going to tell you a thing you'll not believe from the outside, and I want you to have seen how I see before I tell you what I saw."

He said it to Karis, over the Hall's ninth sheet, on the sixth day of his week, with the board beside the sheet and the bound leaf of his own notes open on the other side, and Cael stood at the table's corner with the lamp a hand to the left of the sheet and held it still.

"You've watched me read the bones," Cleon said. "Bearer, carried, ornament. That's the Path from outside — a man pointing at a wall. Now the inside. Sit where you can see the sheet and not my hand, because the hand's a habit and the habit's not the thing."

Karis moved. Cael did not. He was labour, and labour held the lamp, and the lamp was two feet from the sheet and two feet from the man's hand, and he had held it there for six days and was, he found, the person in the room best placed to watch a thing from inside, because the man had stopped seeing him.

"A record," Cleon said, "sits. Every mark's where it is because a thing happened and somebody put the mark down after. You read it and the marks are *still*. They describe. Nothing in them wants anything." His hand went flat on the board, on his ten-year piece, and his eyes went to the unfocused place between the board and the sheet. "An instruction *leans*. Every mark's where it is because a thing is meant to happen next, and the mark's pointing at the next thing. You put your attention on it and it's not still. It has a direction. It's built so that the eye goes *along* it, bearer to bearer, in an order, and you can feel the order as you can feel which way a stair goes in the dark by where the treads are. That's what I see. Not words. The lean."

He read the sheet.

Cael watched him read it, from two feet, holding the lamp, and had, for the length of the reading, the thing he had had beside a plane in a cut: the sense of a craft being done at the edge of his eye by a body that was not hiding it. Cleon's hand did not move. His eyes did not move, either — that was the thing Cael had not seen from the wall on the other days — they held the unfocused place and did not travel the sheet as a reading eye travels, and it was the *attention* that travelled, and the attention went along the sheet's fourth row bearer to bearer, and Cael could not see it go and could see that it went, because the man's breath changed at each bearer, a little, as a man's breath changes on a stair when his foot finds the tread.

"There," Cleon said, at the row's end. "Left to right. Six bearers, in order. It leans. The whole row goes somewhere. A record doesn't go anywhere; a record's a heap. This is a stair." He took his hand off the board. "That's the inside. That's the whole of the Path, and I've never once shown it to anyone, and I've shown it to you because tomorrow you'll need to have seen a thing lean, so that when I tell you which way the whole of it leans you'll believe your own eyes and not mine."

"Noted," said Karis, and wrote, and Cael saw her hand shake once and steady, because she had seen a thing lean and had not known that a made thing could.

She sat with it for the time it takes a woman to decide whether a thing she has just seen changes the shape of ten years' work, and decided that it did, and did not say so; she said, instead, in the voice for a finding, "The strata lean too. I've never called it that. The oldest layer under a floor's not a heap either; it's laid so the next one sits on it. I thought that was weight." She looked at the sheet. "It's not weight. It's the same thing you just showed me, in stone, from below." And Cleon looked at her for a moment as a man looks at somebody who has just said a thing he has waited thirty years for someone else to say first, and said, "Yes," and nothing else, because a thing said by somebody else does not need saying twice.

And Cael stood with the lamp, and had it.

---

He knew the moment this time.

He had not known it in a cut; a cut had been a minute of standing still with a crossbow above him, and the thing had gone in somewhere in the middle of the minute where he was not looking. He had not known it on a table in the sun with a healer's hands on him, because that had been her reading and not his watching. He knew it now, in a chamber under a city, with a lamp in his hand, at the row's end, when the man said *there* and the attention that had gone along six bearers came back to the man's eyes and stopped — because in the breath after it stopped, the row on the sheet, which Cael had looked at for six days as forty-one kinds of mark, *leaned*.

Not much. The way a floor leans that you have stood on for an hour before you notice it is not quite level. Six marks in a row that had been six marks, and were now six marks with a direction, left to right, the eye going along them bearer to bearer whether he told it to or not, and the row going somewhere, and the somewhere not a word — he could not have said what the row said; it did not say — but a *lean*, a made thing's lean, the thing a maker had put in it so that the next thing would happen. He looked at the fifth row. It leaned. He looked at the board. It leaned the same way.

He did not use it. He had not used the eleventh either. He stood with the lamp in his hand and the twelfth thing in him and did not turn it on anything but the sheet it had come off, because a thing that had come to him from a man working in front of him was a thing he owed the man the knowing of before he owed himself the using, and because there was one place in him he was not going to turn it, and he knew that place, and he had been told by a healer not to touch it, and had been told by this man, two days ago, that the instrument did not work on a man, and he was going to find out which of those two things was true by asking neither of them tonight.

He held the lamp. Cleon turned his head a finger's width, and the light was there, and Karis, across the table, who had watched him not turn the twelfth thing on anything, looked at him once, as she had looked at him on a road with a cord in his hand, and said nothing, and wrote nothing, because it was not hers to enter and she knew whose it was.

The rest of the day was the rest of a day in the chamber, and he catalogued it because a day in which a man had come to own a twelfth thing should be catalogued as an ordinary day, so that the twelfth thing would not think itself the whole of it. Cleon and Karis at the table with the fourth row and the fifth and the board, the pencil going now, Karis's, in the notebook, faster than he had seen it go since a marsh. Brom on the first box with the foot out and the good shoulder held as a man holds a thing he has used wrong and does not intend to use again for a while. Lira standing, then sitting on the third box, then standing, because a room with three galleries going off it into the dark was a room she did not sit in for long. Vastin on the knee, beside the case, not sitting, being nobody a little better than the day before, which Cael measured by the number of times the man's hand moved toward the lamp and stopped: four, on the fourth day; two, today. And the twelfth thing in him, with nothing to do, leaning at every mark on every sheet on the table whether he asked it to or not, so that the chamber was full of small directions he had not had yesterday and could not now stop having, and he found that a made room was a thing that pointed, everywhere, at what its makers had wanted next, and that he was going to have to learn not to look, as he had learned not to look at a light on a hill.

---

He told him at the bell.

That was the form. The nine had been taken in fights, or in a yard, or on a road, from people who had been using a thing at him or beside him, and the telling had been a thing he did after, when he could — to Lira first, always, and then the circle. The tenth had been given, at a woman's direction, with the cost written down in advance, and he had told her on the table with her hands still on him because that was the covenant and the covenant was the only thing that made the tenth not a theft. The eleventh had been a plane held across a cut for him, and he had told the man on a step, in the only currency the man took, which was a ledger. And the twelfth was a craft, shown by a craftsman to a researcher with a boy holding a lamp, and it had come to the boy because the boy had stood there, and it was not a reading of a person and it was not a fight, and the covenant for it was lighter than any of the others and it was still a covenant: you tell the man. Honesty was the only strategy that had ever survived contact with what he was, and he had learned that at fifteen from a woman with a ledger and had not once found it wrong.

He told Lira first, on the stair up at the bell, in three words at her shoulder — *Twelfth. His. Lean.* — because Lira was always first, and had been since a hall, and she said, "Made things?" and he said, "Made things," and she said nothing else, which was how she kept it. Then he went back down.

So he told him. At the bell, when Karis had gone up the gallery to the stair with Brom and the flat, and Lira had gone with them, and Vastin had picked up the case and gone, slowly, and the chamber was the man and the lamp and the boy, he set the lamp down on the table's corner and said it plainly, in the order he had it.

"There's a thing you should know before I go up, because it's yours as much as mine and you'd want it in your book." He waited until Cleon looked at him — the first time, he thought, in six days that the man had looked at his face and not at the light he was holding. "I'm not a hand. I'm the boy on the paper. [SHATTERED], from Denvash, five years ago. I've a thing in me that the classification doesn't have a column for: when I stand beside a Path being used, long enough, close enough, I come away with a piece of it. Not the Path. A piece. Partial. Eleven pieces, before today. This morning, when you read the fourth row from inside and said *there* — I have the lean. I looked at the fifth row after, and it leaned. I didn't take it. I stood where you'd put the lamp. But I have it, and you should know that I have it, and that I'll not turn it on anything you wouldn't, and that I'll not turn it on you."

Cleon did not say anything for a while.

He looked at the lamp first, which Cael had set on the table's corner, because it was the thing he had been turning his head for all week and it was, Cael understood, the thing he was seeing now — a boy who had stood at his shoulder for six days holding a light, and had been, the whole six days, a thing the classification had no column for, standing in a room the classification had never been lowered into, taking a craft off a man's hands by holding a lamp for them. He looked at the sheets. He looked at the board, and at his own thirty years on the shelf behind him. He did not look at Cael. Not yet.

Then he said, "You did what."

It was not a question. It was the sound a careful man makes when a thing he has spent thirty years being careful about has just stood up in front of him and said its name.

"Twelve," Cael said. "Yours is the twelfth."

Cleon looked at him. He looked at him properly, for the first time — not at the carvings; at the boy — the way he had looked at Karis on a ring road when she had said *off floors*: as a man looks at the thing that proves the shape he has been right about, and finds that being proved right is a thing with a cost in it. And then he said, slowly, in the voice a man uses for a sentence he is building a word at a time and does not want to finish:

"That's not in the classification system. That's not *supposed* to be possible. That's — "

He stopped.

Cael watched him stop. He had watched Karis stop at a slab, with a sentence about lamps in her mouth, and had watched a marshal stop at a spring three times, and this was neither of those; this was a careful man arriving at the edge of a thing he had read a piece of, on a board, for ten years, and seeing the boy in front of him standing on the far side of it, and choosing, with the whole of thirty years' care, not to say the sentence that would have joined them.

"No," Cleon said, to himself. "No. I'll not say that one. Not down here. Not to you." He put his hand flat on the board. "I've had a piece of a thing for a decade and I've kept it in a hole because I've learned what happens to a thing the Compact finds interesting, and you are the most interesting thing I have ever had in this room, and I am going to be very careful indeed."

He looked at the boy a moment longer. Then, deliberately — Cael saw the deliberateness; it was the same as a woman's hand coming off a page — he looked back at the carvings.

---

Vastin was on the landing when Cael came up to it with the lamp, after, with the case at his feet and the stick, waiting, because a hand who could not carry a case up a stair alone on that knee had learned this week to wait for the hand who could, and had not once said so, and Cael took the case's other strap without a word and they went up the stair together, the two of them, with the thing between them that a plane in a cut had put there, and the man said, on the fourth flight, without looking round, "You told him," and Cael said, "I told him," and Vastin said, "He looked away," and Cael said, "Yes," and the man nodded, once, as a man nods who has spent forty years watching people be told things and knows what a careful man's looking-away is worth, and they went up.

Cael took the log out, before he went up the last flight, because a thing that has come should be entered where it came, and he put his attention where the twelfth thing was, and it rendered.

*Rune-adjacent. Cleon. Partial. Bronze. A made thing's lean — the direction a maker put in it; which mark bears, which is borne, which is nothing; whether a run sits or goes. Structure, not meaning: it will tell me a thing is an instruction and will not tell me what it instructs. Made things only. Not a river. Not a man. Costs the eyes, after. No use in a fight. Engagement field: inscriptive — the third field that is not a fight, after clinical; the second that was given, not taken. Tier equivalent unknown. Deployment: none. Twelfth.*

He read it once, in the plain hand, in a chamber under a city, with a man across the table who had just given him a thing by working in front of him and had then, deliberately, looked away from him, and he understood that the looking-away was the covenant's other half — that a craftsman who had been told a thing and had chosen not to see the boy who told it was keeping the boy the only way a careful man could — and he entered that too, in four words, under the notice, because it belonged there: *He looked away. Kept.* *Not a man.* He let it stand, and did not look at the place in himself where the ten went round, and closed the log.

---

Cleon had a cup on the table. He had had it there all week — water, from the spring at the gallery's head, in a clay cup that had been a cup for thirty years — and he had not once, in Cael's seeing, drunk from it while he read, because a man who reads with his whole attention does not have a hand to spare for a cup. He picked it up now. He drank. He set it down.

"I'm going to pretend you didn't tell me that," he said, "for your sake, not mine. But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you can apparently take my whole trade by watching me work it for a minute." He set down the cup. "Everything I read is *made*. Stone, ink, seal — somebody built it, so I can see how. If that fragment ever reads something for you that shouldn't be *made* and reads it anyway — " He stopped. "Don't tell me. Don't tell anyone. Just remember I said it."

He went back to the carving.

He put the log in the satchel, and the satchel on his shoulder, and picked up the box, because the bell had gone and a hand carries a box up at the bell whatever else he has carried down.

Cael did not tell anyone. He remembered.

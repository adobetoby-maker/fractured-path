# Chapter 12 — No Authority

The alley had steps in it, and that was the whole of the geography, and he had it before the first man moved because he had walked the alley eleven times with a box on his shoulder and a cataloguer walks a thing eleven times so that he has it on the twelfth.

The first tier's stair came down off the bluff to a landing at the first wall's foot, and from the landing the route to the ring road was not the wide street the carts used but a cut between two rows of houses that Seln had found on the second day and used since, because a working party that went home by the carts' street was a working party that could be counted going home. The cut was stepped — twenty-two steps, in five flights, each flight a stride and a half wide and walled on both sides by the backs of houses with no doors in them, three men high, sheer — and it fell from the landing to the inn's yard gate in a straight line that a man at the top could see the bottom of, and a man at the bottom could see the top of, and nobody in between could see anything but wall. A choke. He had thought that on the second day, and had said it to Seln, and Seln had said, "Yes. That's why. Nobody holds a choke they can't get out of, so nobody holds this one," and had been right for eight days.

On the two hundred and ninetieth day, at dusk, with the boxes, coming down the third flight, he saw that somebody had decided to hold it after all.

Four at the top. He counted them as he had counted hounds and hoods — without seeing one clearly — as they came out of the landing's shadow onto the first flight above and behind the party: four figures, not carters, not in a colour, with their hoods up in a city where nobody wore a hood at dusk in spring, and one of them with a thing at his shoulder that was a crossbow, in a city, which was a thing nobody carried in a city unless they had brought it for a purpose. And two at the bottom, coming through the inn's yard gate from the ring road, which they had gone round by, with staves, into the fifth flight, so that the cut had a lid on it and a floor, and the party — Karis, and four hands with four boxes, and the cache on a man who was not there — was in the middle three flights, on steps, with wall on either side, and no room to do anything a Path did except be loud.

He catalogued it because a fight is drawn before it is fought. Six of them. A crossbow above. Staves below. Twenty-two steps, sheer walls, dusk. Karis on the third flight with the flat. Brom above her with the first box on the good shoulder and the bound one carried. Lira on the second flight with the third box on two arms. Vastin on the fourth flight, below them all, with the kit's case and the stick, on the knee, nearest the two coming up. And Cael on the third flight beside Karis with the middle box and the satchel under it, ten things in him and one of them a burst that would have been the loudest thing in the cut and would have brought a bolt down it from the top in the beat after, which was, he understood, what the crossbow was for: they had come for practitioners. They had come for a crew that walked out of quiet ground declaring, and they had built the trap for the declaration, and the trap was a stone cut with a bolt at the top of it.

Seln was not anywhere. Good.

"Boxes down," Cael said. Not loud. "Karis, against the wall. Nobody declares."

The boxes went down. That was the sound of it, four boxes on stone steps, and the four at the top heard it and came down the first flight fast, because a party that put its boxes down was a party that was going to fight, and a crossbow that has been brought to end a declaration does not wait for it.

Vastin turned round.

---

He rooted the choke above them.

Cael had seen it done once, on a road, across a cut with ice in it, and had learned then what a wall was and what it cost, and had thought, since, that he knew the whole of it. He had not. On a road a wall was a plane across a line with nothing on either side of it but scree, and a pack had gone round it on the loose slope, slow, and the man holding it had been the loudest thing in a country with nothing behind him but a man with a rock. This was a cut. This was a stride and a half of stone between two walls three men high with no door in either, and Vastin turned on the fourth flight — turned uphill, with his back to the two coming up from the yard gate and his face to the four coming down from the landing, which was the wrong way round for a man defending himself and the only way round for a man defending a party — and went past Lira and Brom and Karis and Cael on the third flight, on the knee, with the stick, faster than a man with that knee had any business going up steps, and stopped on the second flight below the four, and set his feet, and the plane went up.

Across the cut. Wall to wall, because the cut's walls were a stride and a half apart and a plane knows what it was told, and the walls were sheer and the plane went from one to the other without a hair of a gap, and from the second flight's top step to somewhere above a man's head, and it was, Cael saw — from six feet below it, looking up — the first thing he had ever watched Iron Wall do that the ground did not argue with. On a road the ground had said *go round*. Here the ground said *there is no round*. The four at the top came down the first flight at a run and hit the plane as eight hounds had hit it, and did not go over each other, because they were men and men do not; they went into a thing that was not there and stopped as if it were the cut's own stone, and the crossbow, which had been coming up to a shoulder, went off into the plane a hand from the man's face, and the bolt did what the ice's bolts had done on a floor, which was nothing, and fell.

And the man holding it could not move.

He knew that already. He had known it on a road and had made a plan around it and had watched the plan need every one of them. But he saw the other half now, in a cut, which the road had not shown him: that in a corridor with sheer walls, a wall that cannot move does not need to. There was no flank. There was no scree for a hound to take slow. There was the plane, and the four above it, and the four could go through it or not, and they could not, and every other way to the party was round by the ring road and up through the yard gate, which was a hundred strides and a minute, and a minute, in a fight, was the whole of a fight.

"Hold it," Cael said, which was not an order, because the man was going to hold it whatever anyone said; it was a count, so that the man would know somebody was counting.

"I'm holding it."

The two from the yard gate came up the fifth flight.

---

That was the second thing, and it was the thing a road had taught the crew and the team had not learned, and it was the whole of the difference.

They came up with staves, two of them, hooded, fast, into the fourth flight where Vastin had been and was not, and they came up ready for what they had been told to be ready for, which was a declaration: a burst, a hardening, a lattice, a thing that would tell them where to swing and how hard. They had built a trap for a crew that walked out of quiet ground declaring. They had a crossbow at the top for the loud one and two staves at the bottom for the rest, and the whole of their plan was that a practitioner, cornered, declares, and a declaration is a place to hit.

Nobody declared.

Brom came down off the third flight to meet the first stave, and did not go hard, because a healer had said not a door, not a box, not a man, and because he had learned at a ford that Iron Skin was a shout; he came down as a very large man with a rock in his good hand, which he had picked up off the fourth step where the cut's makers had left a loose one a long time ago, and put his shoulder into the stave's man as a man puts a shoulder into a cart that is on its side, which was a thing he had done once this week already, and the stave's man went into the wall, and the stave went down the steps, and Brom stood on it. He did not hit the man again. He had not needed to. He stood on the stave with his bound shoulder carried still and forward and his good hand with the rock in it and looked at the second man coming up, and the second man, who had been told to expect a practitioner, looked at a big man with a rock standing on his friend's stave, and did not know what to hit.

Lira told him.

She had no room. That was the cut's whole price for her: a woman who was fast had a stride and a half of stone and nowhere to run in it, and she had known it since the second day and had walked the cut anyway, and now she did the thing a fast woman does when there is no room, which is to use the one direction the walls do not close: down. She came off the second flight's edge onto the man's shoulders from above, two-armed, with the knife, and the knife did not go into him — Cael saw that, and saw her choose it — it went into the stave's shaft where his hands were, and the man let go of a thing that had a knife in it, and Lira came down off him onto the steps on her own foot and put the flat of the knife across the back of his knee, and he sat down, on the fourth flight, hard, and did not get up, because a man who has just been landed on from a height does not.

Two staves on the steps. Two men on the steps. Nobody hit twice. Nobody declared.

Cael had not moved. That was his whole part in the second thing, and he catalogued it because it was the thing he had learned on a road and was doing on purpose: he had stayed on the third flight, at the plane's foot, beside the man who could not move, with the knife out and the satchel under his arm and his back to Karis against the wall, and had been the reason. The four above the plane could see him. He was the boy — the one the completion report had named, the one who had walked out of the Quiet declaring — and he was six feet from their crossbow with nothing but a plane between, standing still, not declaring, being the thing they had come for and giving them nothing to hit, and every one of the four wanted through that plane worse than they wanted anything, because he was on the other side of it, and the wanting was the cost of the plane, and he was the cost.

*Someone else has to be the reason.* He had the sentence now. He had had it since a road. He was it.

---

Seln took the top of the cut from the roof.

Cael did not see it. He saw the result, which was the thing you saw with Seln, and drew the rest afterward, because a fight is drawn after as well as before.

The four above the plane had a fifth. Not on the steps; on the landing at the cut's head, in the shadow where the stair came down, standing where a man stands who is not going to fight and is going to tell the others when to — and Cael had counted six and had had six, four and two, and had been wrong by one, because the sixth had been the one you do not count until he speaks. He spoke. He said one word up the cut, from the landing, to the crossbow, a word Cael did not catch and did not need to, because a word said from the back of a fight to the front of it is a signal, and a signal is a coordination, and a coordination is a thing you can remove.

Seln removed it.

He was on the roof of the house on the cut's east side — he had been there, Cael worked out later, since the party had come down the first flight, because Seln went home by a roof when a cut had two ends and he could only watch one — and the landing was under the roof's edge, and the sixth man was on the landing, and a man on a landing in a city at dusk does not look up, because nobody in a city looks up. A cord. That was all it was. A weighted cord, from above, round the sixth man's arm and the rail he stood beside, so that the arm he signalled with was tied to the stair, and then Seln was not on the roof any more and the sixth man was pulling at his own arm in the shadow and saying nothing, because a man tied to a rail does not say the next word; he works at the cord. Nobody died. Nobody was cut. The signal stopped.

And the four at the plane, who had been waiting for the word that told them when to go round by the ring road and take the party from below with the two who were now sitting on the steps, did not get the word, and stood at a plane with their hoods up and a crossbow they had spent and a leader who had gone quiet, and were, for the length of a breath, six people who did not know what to do.

That was the third thing. The cache was on the roof. It had been on the roof the whole time, on a man's back, unopened, and the four had come for it, and it had been above them, and they had never once looked up.

---

The fourth thing was that they left.

He catalogued it as the ford had taught him to catalogue a pack that has lost its first mover: not a rout; a *decision*, taken all at once by six people who had built a plan for a declaration and had got a wall, a rock, a knife in a stave, a cord round an arm, and a boy standing still, and had understood, in the length of a breath, that the plan was not going to happen and that the next plan would be made somewhere else. The four at the top went back up the first flight into the landing's shadow, and one of them cut the sixth man's cord with a knife, and the six of them were six shapes on a landing, and then they were not, because a landing in a city has three ways off it and they took all three. The two on the steps got up — the one with the knee, slowly — and went down past Vastin's plane on the wrong side of it, which was the party's side, because the party let them, because Brom stood on the stave and Lira stood on the step and neither of them touched a man who was walking away, and the two went through the inn's yard gate into the ring road and were gone into a district that had a thousand people in it and no faces.

None taken. No faces. Nobody dead. Two staves and a spent bolt on the steps, and a weighted cord on a rail, and six hoods that had gone into a city like water into ground.

The plane came off.

Vastin went down on the second flight as he had gone down on a road, both knees and then a hand, except that it was worse — a Silver-tier plane held across a cut for the length of a fight is not a plane held across a road for the length of a charge — and the knee that had gone under him on a road eleven days ago went under him again on a step, and he sat on the step with his back to the cut's wall and his face the colour of the plane and did not say anything, and could not have, and Cael, who was six feet below him with a knife in his hand, put the knife away and went up the six feet and sat down on the step beside him, because the man could not move and somebody should be where he could not move to.

---

He catalogued the costs on the step, because the costs were the ledger and there was no healer to catalogue them for him.

Karis: a cut, on the back of the left hand, shallow, from the stone — she had gone down against the wall when the boxes went down and had put her hand on a step's edge and the edge had been a step's edge — bleeding as a shallow cut on a hand bleeds, which is more than it should. Brom bound it. He did it with Karis's thread and a strip off his own shirt, on the steps, with his good hand and his teeth, badly, like a man who has watched a healer do it a hundred times and has never once been the hands, and Karis held the hand out and looked at the flat under her other arm and said, "The sheets are dry," which was her whole report on the cut. No healer inside the gate would put hands on an unranked woman's hand. Oryn had said so, at a fire, and had been right, and the cost of it was a strip of shirt and Brom's teeth, and Cael wrote the cost down as she had priced it, because she had priced it exactly.

Brom: the shoulder, carried through the whole of it, still and forward, not used, and Cael saw what the not-using had cost — a man who had put his shoulder into a stave's man with the wrong shoulder, the good one, from the wrong side, because the right one was bound, and had done it badly and had done it anyway — and Brom sat on the stave with the rock still in his hand and said, "Cart was heavier," and did not say the other thing.

Lira: nothing. Two arms, twenty-five days. She was standing on the fourth flight with the knife wiped, opening the left hand and closing it, and she caught him looking, and did not stop this time.

Seln: back, by the yard gate, with the cache on his back and nothing in his hands and the cord left on the rail because a cord on a rail was a thing that could be found and a man could not, and he came up the steps and looked at the two staves and the bolt and said, "They'll go to ground and think. They know where we sleep now. They knew before, but now they know we know." He looked at the plane's place, where the air had stopped being stone. "They came for a declaration. They got a wall and a rock. They don't know what to make of that, and they'll want to."

And Vastin.

Cael did not have a catalogue for it, and had not had one on a road either, and had one now, because he had sat beside it for the length of the man's breath coming back.

He had stood two feet from it. That was the fact. He had stood on the third flight at the plane's foot for the length of a fight — a minute; he had counted it, because he counted things, and it had been a minute and some — with a crossbow above and his own body the reason the crossbow wanted through, and had watched, from two feet, a Silver-tier Iron Wall held across a corridor by a man who could not move and would not stop, and the plane had been *there*, the whole minute, at the edge of his eye, a place where the air had been told it was stone and had believed it, and the believing had been the man, and the man had been the plane's foot, and Cael had stood beside the foot and been the reason the plane was needed, for a minute, under real threat, with nothing in him declared.

And he had it.

He did not know when. Somewhere in the minute. Not at the start, when the four hit the plane; not at the end, when it came off; somewhere in the middle, in the long part where nothing happened and the plane held and the four stood at it and the crossbow was spent and he stood still, the thing had gone into him the way the nine had gone in — not taken; *learned*, at two feet, by a body that learned the shape of a thing by standing beside it long enough — and he had felt it go and had not looked at it, because he was being the reason and the reason does not look away. He looked now. On the step, beside a man who could not move, with the costs catalogued and the cut empty, he put his attention where the thing was, the way he had been taught to on a table, and it rendered.

*Iron-Wall-adjacent. Vastin. Partial. Iron. Held. A plane, not a body: the ground told not to give. Distinct from the third — Iron-adjacent, the bed; this is not the bed hardened, this is a line drawn on it and kept. Engagement field: defensive; rooted. Tier equivalent unknown. Deployment: none. Eleventh.*

He read it once and let it stand and did not use it. There was nothing to use it on; the cut was empty. He sat with it. The man beside him breathed.

"You held it," Cael said.

"I held it." Vastin's voice was the ledger's, with nothing left in the ledger. "There was nothing behind it. I have never once in my life held a line with nothing behind it. There's always been — " He stopped. "A field. A formation. The Compact. I held it because I decided to, and that was the whole of what was behind it, and it held."

"It held."

"It nearly didn't. I could feel the place where it wouldn't have." He looked at his own hands, on his knees, on a step. "I have put that plane in front of a great many people in forty years. I have never once put it in front of anyone the Compact would have restricted." He did not look at Cael. "I know what you are, on the paper. I have read the paper more times than any man alive. I put the wall in front of it anyway. I'd like that entered somewhere, since I'm not the one who keeps the ledger any more."

Cael did not say anything for a while. Then he said, "It's entered," and took the log out, on the step, and wrote it, and did not tell the man what else he had entered, because a fragment is a thing you own and a thing a man gave you is a thing you owe, and he did not yet have a word for the distance between those two, and was not going to make one up on a step.

---

*Two hundred and ninetieth day. Treswick, the cut between the first-tier stair and the inn's yard. Six of them, faceless, hooded, a crossbow and staves; a sixth on the landing to say when. They came for a declaration. None taken. Nobody dead. The cache kept — it was on a roof. Karis, left hand, a cut, bound with thread and a shirt. Brom, the shoulder carried; the good one used wrong. Lira, nothing, two arms. Seln, nothing; a cord on a rail. Vastin, spent past a road; the knee again. Me, the burst not used, on purpose; nothing declared; a minute at a plane's foot, being the reason. A hundred and thirty-six in hand.*

*Eleventh. Iron Wall, from Vastin, in an alley, while he held a line for us with nothing behind him but the decision to. It's not Brom's — Brom gets harder, this holds. I've spent five years learning to give ground and disappear. Now I've got the opposite in me, from the man who spent forty years being the wall they put in front of people like me. He held it for us instead. I don't have a word for what that is. I wrote it down anyway.*

# Chapter 10 — The Deepstacks

The dark did not give the lamp back, and then it did, a little at a time, in the order the lamp asked for it.

Rock first. The quarry's own rock, cut in the days before anyone cut rock square, in galleries that went off from the landing in three directions at a height a man could walk in and a width two men could pass in, and the cut-marks still on every face of it, the marks of tools nobody made any more, running as the quarrymen had run them and not as a mason would. Then the shelving, which was not shelving as the Compact shelved a thing — no cases, no doors, no labels — but timber pinned into the rock floor to roof along both walls of every gallery, grey with age and dry as bone, and on the timber, the records.

He catalogued them by their shapes because he could not catalogue them by anything else. Rolls, the size of a man's forearm, tied, with the ties gone to dust and the rolls staying rolled by habit. Boards — flat pieces of wood the length of a hand with a face to them, a hundred to a shelf, standing on their edges like slates. Bound leaves, thick, sewn along one side with something that had been cord, in covers of a leather that had been a colour once. Things that were none of those: a sheet of hammered metal with marks on it, standing against the rock; a stone, squared, with a face cut into it; a bundle of sticks tied in a pattern that was, he understood after a while, the pattern itself, and not a bundle. Four centuries of a country's paper, if it was paper, in scripts that were not the Compact's and had not been anyone's for longer than the Compact had had a name, kept because a decision had never been made about them, in a hole, in the dark, under a building whose whole purpose was to decide.

And the smell. He had no catalogue for it. It was the smell of a thing that has been left alone for so long that the leaving-alone has become the thing, and he stood on the landing with a box on his shoulder and breathed it and understood that he had never once, in nineteen years, stood in a place where nothing had been decided, and that this was what it smelled like.

The lamp went on into the middle gallery, and there was a second light at the end of it that had not come with them.

---

Cleon was already reading.

That was the whole of how he was introduced, and Cael would not have had it another way, because it was the truest thing about the man and the first: they came down the middle gallery with the lamp and the boxes, six people and the sound of six people in a place that had not heard six people in four hundred years, and at the gallery's end, where the rock opened into a chamber that the quarrymen had left when they hit a seam they did not want, there was a table, and a lamp on it that was always lit, and a man at the table with a bound thing open in front of him and his finger on a line, reading, who did not look up.

He had come down ahead of them by an hour. Seln had said he would. He had a chair with a cushion on it that had been a cushion for thirty years, and a second lamp on a bracket in the rock above the table that a hand had put there to throw light where a reader wanted it, and behind the table, along the chamber's back wall, shelving that was not the archive's — newer timber, squared, with a man's own order on it: bound leaves in one hand, a hand Cael knew from a ledger in a coat, hundreds of them, thirty years of a man writing down what he had read in a room nobody else read in, and the room's own things pulled from the galleries and stood on the new shelves where he could reach them, in an order that was his and would die with him.

He read to the end of the line. Then to the end of the page. Then he put a scrap of leather in it and closed the thing and put both hands flat on it, which was, Cael saw, exactly what Karis did, and looked up, and looked at Karis, and at nothing else in the chamber.

"Put them there," he said, and moved the bound thing off the table's middle.

Karis put them there.

She did it the way she had put a ring on a table and a wall on a floor and a marshal's six years on a slab: without saying she was going to, and all at once, and in the order that was in her head. The oilcloth opened. Fifty-two sheets, in three sets, laid out along the table's length in the order of the floors — the Ring at the left end, eleven; the Stair in the middle, twenty-nine; the Hall at the right, twelve — with the lamp moved once to put the light where the sets met, and the charcoal on the sheets grey against the paper the way it had been grey on a floor in the dark, and she did not say a word while she laid them, and neither did he, and the four hands stood with the boxes against the chamber's wall and watched a man look at fifty-two sheets that he had, Cael thought, been looking for in some part of himself since before any of the four had been born.

He did not touch them. He looked. He looked at the first set for as long as it takes a man to read a page he already knows, and then at the second, longer, and then at the third, and his hand came up off the table and went toward the third set and stopped, a hand's width off, and stayed there, and Cael saw it stay and had seen a hand do that before, on a table, near a page.

"Three floors," Cleon said.

"Three floors. Two hundred meters each, to the line. A fourth we didn't go in."

"A fourth." His hand had not moved. "And the word."

Karis said it. She said it the way she had said it at a table in the autumn, once, as a stratum's limit and not as a meaning — the old word, the retired one, the one from the confiscated record that she had carried across two books and a Line and had put in a sentence exactly once — and Cleon closed his eyes.

Not for long. For the length of a man deciding whether a thing he has hoped for and dreaded in equal measure for thirty years is the one or the other, and finding that it is both, and that both is not what the young man who started the work had imagined it would be. He opened them. He looked at the third set, at the Hall's twelve sheets, at a mark on the fourth of them that Cael could not have told from any other mark, and said, quietly, to the sheet and not to Karis:

"I have a piece of that. I've had it ten years. It's on the shelf behind me in a hand nobody in the Compact can read, and I've read it, and I've never once had the stone to put beside it, because the stone's out there past the Line where a man with a Bronze pass and a registry's tolerance doesn't go." He looked up. "You've brought me the stone. Three floors of it. Do you know what that is, to a man who's been sitting in a hole for thirty years being tolerated?"

"I know what a marsh with a kit's ledger in it was to me," Karis said. "I'd guess it's that, longer."

"It's that, longer." He put his hand down flat on the table beside the third set, not on it. "I'll tell you the thing I tell nobody, because you've earned it by walking in, and because you'll need to know it before you sit down in this chair, which you're going to. Being right for thirty years alone is a thing a man can carry. Being believed — " He stopped. "I had thought it would be a relief. It's not. It's the end of being able to be wrong. I've been wrong, safely, in this room, for thirty years. Nobody read the work, so the work could be anything. Now it's going to be read, and it's going to be *this*" — the hand a finger's width above the sheets — "and if I've been right, then what I've been right about is a thing I have not slept well beside since the day I found the piece of it." He looked at her for a moment longer. "Give me a week. Give me a week with these and the text and I'll tell you what you've got. And then you'll not sleep either, and I'm sorry for it, and I'm not going to stop."

"Noted," Karis said, and sat down, in the second chair, which had not had a cushion on it, and which Cleon had brought down that morning.

---

He set the box down.

He catalogued that, because it was the whole of his function in the room and a man should know his function to the hair. The middle box, against the chamber's wall, beside the first and the third and the kit's case, lid up, so that Karis could reach the sheets in it — the fair copies, the ledger, the map, the two brass edges — without asking, and then nothing. Nothing to carry. Nothing to count. Nothing to be, in a chamber under a city, but a body that had carried a box down a stair and would carry it up again at the bell, standing against a rock wall with three other bodies while two people who could read the room began to.

He had not been nothing inside walls before. He had been a case, a hearing, a designation, a boy with a number on a sheet; he had been the thing every room he stood in was about. He stood against the rock and was not the thing this room was about, and it was, he found — the finding arrived slowly, over the first hour, as the lamp's light had arrived — the first place inside a wall he had ever stood where nobody could file him. Not would not. *Could* not. The stone was older than the Compact. The Compact's reach was a thing that went down through its own foundation and stopped where the rock changed, because the rock had never been told what the Compact was and the Compact had never once sent a man down the stair to tell it. Everything in this chamber was older than the machine that filed. The paper was. The shelves were. The dark was. He was standing in the one hole in the whole of Valdris that the classification had never been lowered into, and he was standing in it as a number, as labour, as a hand, and the number had stopped at the door because the door was older than numbers.

He wrote that down, later, in the fixed hand. At the wall he only stood in it.

Brom, beside him, had set his box down and had sat on it, with the shoulder still and forward and the foot out, and was watching Karis work with the face he kept for somebody else pricing a wall: respect, and no intention of touching it. Lira was on her feet, with her two hands at her sides, and was not watching Karis; she was watching the galleries going off into the dark in three directions, the way she watched a floor for the place where a thing would come from, because a hole was a hole. And Vastin had set the kit's case down and stood beside it on the knee, with the stick, and was looking at the shelving behind Cleon's chair — the thirty years of one man's hand — with an expression Cael had not seen on him and had, after a while, a name for: it was the look of a man who has spent forty years in offices reading what other men filed, and has just understood that this man had filed the whole of his life in a hole where no one would ever read it, and had done it anyway, and had been right.

At the table, Cleon had drawn the Hall's fourth sheet toward him and had a bound thing open beside it, and was reading the two together, and his finger was on a mark on the sheet and his other hand was on a line in the text, and he was not reading what the mark said. Cael could not have said how he knew that. He knew it the way he knew a thing about a floor with the Iron read on it: the man's attention was not on the mark's meaning; it was on the mark's *bones* — on which of the lines that made it were holding the others up — and he was checking the bones against the text's bones, and nodding, once, to himself, and going on, and not saying anything, because a man who has been reading alone for thirty years does not say the thing until the thing is checked.

The bell came down the stair at the end of the day, faint, a city's bell four tiers up, and they went up to it with the boxes, and left Cleon at his table with the lamp that was always lit, reading.

---

The second morning was where the door nearly closed.

He catalogued it because it was the chapter's cost and the cost was not his, and a man who keeps a ledger keeps other people's costs in it too.

The provision was worked once and stood for the week; that was the form. But the yard door was a door, with a keeper, and a keeper had a book, and the book wanted the count each morning that the count went through it — *four*, again, entered again, by the same careful young man with the same pen, who had looked at four faces yesterday for as long as he looked at four boxes and had written the number. He was there again on the two hundred and eighty-fifth morning, in the yard door, with the pen, and the four of them were in the lane with the boxes, and Karis was inside at the counter with Cleon, and Seln, who walked the party to the door each morning and did not go through it, was at the lane's mouth with his hands in his coat.

The clerk counted. One, two, three — and stopped on the fourth, which was Vastin, and Cael saw why: the man had the kit's case on his shoulder and the stick in his other hand and the knee had been asked to stand in a lane for a quarter of an hour on a stone floor, and the knee had said so, in the way a knee says a thing, and the clerk, who was careful, had seen a hand who did not stand like a hand, and had let his pen stop.

It was a half-breath. It was the place where the form had no column and a careful man could ask.

And Vastin — who had stood in front of a thousand clerks from the far side of a thousand counters, and had never once in forty years been on the near side of one with a pen stopped on him — did the thing forty years had built him to do, which was to end the pause with the fact that ended pauses.

"Vastin," he said. "Archmarshal, Compact enforcement. You'll find the name is — "

Seln was there.

Cael did not see him come from the lane's mouth. He was simply at Vastin's shoulder, between the man and the door, close, with a hand on the kit's case as if to steady it, and his voice was the voice for an inventory, low, and he said, over the man's sentence and through it, to the clerk, "He's the carter's man. He was a factor once, up west; the name's a habit and the title's a joke he tells. Four hands, same as yesterday. The researcher's at your counter and she'll want to be told we're through," and the clerk — who had heard a word he did not know the weight of, in a lane, from a man with a stick, and had heard another man explain it in the voice that explains things, and was young, and careful, and had a woman at his counter who would not stop talking about strata — looked at Vastin for one more half-breath, and at Seln, and wrote *four*, and said, "Down. Out by the bell," and went in.

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Seln took his hand off the kit's case, and looked at Vastin, and did not say anything, and the not-saying was the worst thing Cael had ever seen Seln do to a man, because Seln said a thing once and this was the once and he was not spending it.

Vastin stood in the lane.

He had heard himself. That was the thing Cael catalogued, because it was the thing that cost: the man had heard the sentence come out of his own mouth — a name, a rank, the Compact's own title, spoken aloud in a lane behind a Compact registry in a Compact city on the Line by a man who had resigned from it, to a clerk, to smooth a door — and had heard it land. Not as authority. The clerk had not known the name. The rank was nothing at this level; the level had no column for it, and a rank that the form has no column for is a noise. And the third thing, the thing he had heard last and understood first: that a former Archmarshal invoking the Compact inside a Compact city, in front of a clerk who kept a book, with an unranked boy at his shoulder who had a piece of paper in an office in the west that said what he was, had just come within one careful man's question of closing every door on the hill, and had been stopped from it by a defector's tradecraft and a lie about a carter.

He stood with it. He stood in the lane with the kit's case on his shoulder and the stick in his hand and his face doing the thing a face does when a man has just found out that the reflex he is proudest of — the one that had ended every pause in his life, the fact that ended pauses — is not a tool any more but a blade with no handle, and that the only way he could help the four people in the lane with him was to be no one, and that he did not know how to be no one, and had not known that he did not know until a half-breath ago.

"I'm sorry," he said. To the lane. In the ledger voice, which had never in Cael's hearing said that sentence before.

"Don't be sorry," Seln said. "Be nobody. It's the only thing down here that's worth anything, and you can't buy it and you can't be given it. You have to stop being the other thing." He went back to the lane's mouth. "Down. She's waiting."

---

They went down. Cael carried the box. It was a long stair and the box was the middle one and he had carried it up and down twice now and knew its weight to the hair, and he carried it behind Brom and ahead of Lira with the satchel under it and the man with the stick behind Lira, going down slowly, on the knee, in silence, and at the iron door with the mark on it at the height of a man's eye he set the box down to open the door, because the hand nearest the door opened it, and that was him.

He looked at the man behind him while he did it.

Vastin was on the landing, with the case, looking at the iron. He was not looking at the mark. He was looking at the door as a thing that opened on a place where the name he had just spoken was worth nothing, not because the place had decided that, but because the place was older than the name, and he was, Cael thought, working out how a man walks into a place like that, and finding that he had never once in his life had to.

Cael had. He had walked into every room of his life as the thing the room could not use, and had learned, at fourteen, in a room with a door that locked from the outside, that being nothing to a room was a thing you could stand in if you stood in it on purpose. He had been no one for five years. He had been no one on purpose for nine months. He was, at that moment, on a landing under a city, the nearest thing on the continent to an expert in the exact thing a fifty-three-year-old man was standing behind him failing to do, and he did not say so, because a man who has learned a thing at a price does not hand it to the man who set the price for free, and because it was not his to hand; the man would learn it or he would not, and the learning was the man's.

He filed it. He filed the fellow-feeling, which was the strangest thing he had felt in a year that had had a good deal of strange in it — that he and the Compact's own held line had a thing in common now, on a landing, and the thing was a door that neither of their names would open — and he pushed the iron, and it went in on its pins without a sound, and the lamp went ahead of him into the dark, and he picked up the box.

---

*Two hundred and eighty-fifth day. Under Treswick. The Deepstacks — the old quarry under the registry, shelved, unread. Second day down. Four hands; Seln above. A hundred and thirty-six in hand; the inn paid to the week.*

*Karis has been looking for this room her whole life. Cleon's been sitting in it for thirty years waiting for her. Vastin tried to open a door with his rank and nearly closed all of them — an Archmarshal's worth nothing here, which he's taking about as well as I took [SHATTERED]. Down here the stone's older than the Compact, so the Compact doesn't reach. First place inside walls I've ever stood where nobody can file me, and it's a hole under a city, and it's because everything down here is older than the machine that files. Karis says give her a week. I've got a box to carry and a promise to keep about a station upstairs. I can wait.*

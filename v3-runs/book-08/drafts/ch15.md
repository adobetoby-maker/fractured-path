# Chapter 15 — Before the Paths

She had found it on the seventh day, and she did not say so until the eighth, because a thing found on paper is a thing you read three times before you say it once, and she had read it twice by lamplight in a hole under a city, and a third time at first light, with a man beside her who had had a piece of it for ten years and had said nothing while she read, which was, Cael understood afterward, all that the man had left to give.

The two hundred and ninety-third day. The chamber under the registry. The lamp that was always lit and the one he held, and the other three hands at the rock wall with the boxes, and Cleon in his chair with both hands flat on nothing, and Karis at the table with a thing in front of her that had not been on the table before.

He catalogued it because it was the thing, and because a thing you are about to hear read aloud once is a thing you owe a description before the reading, so that the description is not the reading's.

Not a board. Not a roll. A leaf — a single leaf, the size of two hands, of a stuff that was not paper and not skin and had been, Cleon said, both once and had stopped being either a long time ago: stiff, the colour of old horn, with a face to it that took a mark and a back that did not, so that it had been written on one side and had lain, for as long as it had lain, with its written face down against the next leaf of a book that had been a book once and was now this leaf and eleven others in a bundle tied with a cord that had gone to dust. The writing on it was not the Compact's and was not the four scripts on the shelves and was not the board's; it was older than the board's, Cleon said, and the board's hand had learned from it as a child's hand learns from a master's — the same lean; the same bearers; the runs going left to right and the bends at the ends of the runs the same shape — and where the board had been cut with a tool, this had been written with a point, in a black that had gone brown, and had been written by somebody who wrote for a living, because every line was the same height and every run started at the same margin, and a hand that does that is a hand that has been paid to.

And in the middle of the leaf, a third of the way down, a place where the face had been scraped.

He saw it from the lamp's distance before he knew what it was: a patch the width of four or five of the runs' letters, if they were letters, where the horn-colour went pale and the surface went rough, as a table goes rough where a knife has been dragged across the grain — the face taken off, back to the fibre, by a blade, or by a thumb over a very long time, and at the patch's left edge, still there, the first stroke of the first mark that had stood in it, a single upright line, and after it nothing, and after the nothing the writing went on as if the patch were a word.

"Somebody's name," Karis said. She had followed his eye. "There was one. There's the first stroke of it. There's nothing else, and there's not going to be, because it's not faded; it's *gone*. Scraped. Whether a man did it with a knife the day after it was written or a thousand thumbs did it over the years going to that one place on the page to touch it, I can't tell and Cleon can't tell and nobody ever will." She put her bound hand flat on the table beside the leaf, not on it. "I've read the rest. I read it yesterday, with the board and the Hall's fourth sheet as the key, and again last night, and a third time this morning, and I'm going to read it aloud now, once, because that's how a finding's said, and then I'm not going to say it again, and none of you are going to say it back to me, because I want it to be the record's and not mine."

The silence held.. That was correct.

He had time, in the nothing, to catalogue how she had found it, because she had told him on the stair going down that morning, in eleven words, and eleven words from Karis on a stair was a report. Cleon had pulled it from the galleries thirty years ago — a bundle of leaves in a script he could read the bones of and not the words — and had set it on his own shelf in his own order and had read it, alone, for three decades, as a thing with a lean in it and no language; and she had walked in with three floors and a word, and the floors had given the language its letters and the word had given it a place to start, and on the seventh day the leaf had stopped being a thing with a lean and had started being a thing that said something, and she had read it, and had said nothing to the man beside her, and he had said nothing to her, and they had read it again by lamplight before the bell, and had come down again in the morning to read it a third time, because a thing found on paper is read three times before it is said once. That was the method. It had taken thirty years and a year and a week, and it had taken eleven words to report, and he had them.

"Lamp," Cleon said, which was the first thing he had said, and Cael moved the light a hand to the left of the leaf, and held it, and Karis read.

---

She read it as she read everything, at the size of a finding, and slowly, because it was a thing written in a hand centuries dead in a language the Compact had never converted, and she was reading it through a board and a floor and ten years of a man's notes, and she gave every word its own weight and did not give any word more than its own, and Cael held the lamp still and listened as the cataloguer listens, which is to the words and not to what they are about.

"*In the years of the unmaking, when the un-bound walked in the land and no wall stood against them and no law held, and the cities of the east were emptied and the cities of the west were emptied and the rivers ran wrong* — that's the record's phrase; I've read it four times and it says what it says — *there came one who saw the shape of it, and set himself to the ordering. And he made the measure, and the seat, and the tier, and the mark that is given at the kindling, that each should know what he is and none should be what he is not. And he set the apparatus over all the land, and the land was ordered, and the un-bound came no more.*"

She stopped. She looked at the scraped place.

"*And the* — " and her finger went above the patch, not touching it — "the name. Four letters, five. The first stroke. Nothing." She went on. "*— set the apparatus over all the land, that the un-bound should not come again, who had near unmade the world before the ordering of it. And this is the reason of the apparatus, and this is all of its reason, and let none who come after forget it, though the un-bound be forgotten: that they should not come again.*"

She stopped reading.

The lamp did not move. He had held it still through all of it and he held it still now, because his hand was the one thing in the chamber he had charge of and he was not going to let it do anything the rest of him had not decided on.

"That's the passage," Karis said. "There's more on the leaf — a list of the measure's parts, the seat's, the tier's; I've the shape of it and not the words yet; it's the same instruction-make Cleon read on the floors, and it's older than the floors, and the floors were cut from it or from a thing like it. But that's the passage. That's what the citation on Vastin's page pointed at across a continent. That's the thing under the registry." She took her hand off the table. "I'll say what it says, once, in my own words, and then I've said it. It says the thing the Compact calls the Path system was *made*. By a person. The record's word for him is the one on Vastin's page — the Architect; it's a title, not a name; the name's gone. It says he made it about four hundred years ago, by the date at the leaf's head, which is a date I can read because the board taught me the numerals. It says he made it *for a reason*, and the reason's the only thing on the leaf the writer cared that anybody remember, and the reason is the un-bound." She looked at the word on the leaf, where it stood, in the antique hand, live, a thing somebody had written down as the name of a thing that walked in the land. "That's the word from the confiscated record. That's the word I've carried since a stratum. It's not retired here. It's not a limit. It's a *kind*. There were people the record calls that, before the measure and the seat and the tier and the mark, and the whole of the apparatus was set over the land so that there would not be any more of them."

She sat back.

"Older," she said. "Made. Us."

She did not say the third word to anyone. She said it to the leaf, in the small voice, the one she kept for the finding she had wished to be wrong about, and Cael understood that she had chosen the word on the stair that morning and had carried it down and had put it at the end so that it would be the last thing said and not the first, and that she had put it there because it was the true one and because she was Karis, and Karis did not put a true thing anywhere but where it went.

---

He catalogued the room, because it was the only thing he was going to let himself catalogue.

Karis, with her hand off the table and her face the colour it went when a finding was one she had been right about and had wished to be wrong, sitting very straight in a chair with no cushion on it, not writing, because the thing was said and the writing was after. Cleon, across from her, in the chair with the cushion, with both hands flat on nothing and his eyes on the leaf and not on anyone, a man who had had a piece of this for ten years and had just watched a woman get all of it, and had said one word in the whole of the reading, which was *lamp*, and was not going to say another, because there was nothing to say that the leaf did not say and a man who has not slept well for a decade knows the difference between a thing that needs saying and a thing that has been said. Brom, on the first box, with the foot out, looking at the leaf as he looked at a wall somebody else had priced — respect, and no intention of touching it — and something under the respect that Cael did not have a name for and would have called, in anyone else, fear, and would not call that in Brom. Lira, standing, with her two hands at her sides, not looking at the leaf. Looking at Cael. She had been looking at Cael since the word *kindling* and had not stopped, and he did not look back, because if he looked back he would have to have a face, and he did not have one yet.

And Vastin, beside the case, his weight off the bound knee, still.

Not the stillness of a man being nobody. The other one — the one Cael had seen on a rim and a landing and in a lane: the stillness of a man who has just heard a thing that is about him, and has understood that it is about him, and has not yet decided what his face is going to do, and has forty years of practice at not letting a face decide before the man does. The Compact's own held line, standing in a hole under a Compact registry, hearing a leaf read. He did not move. He did not draft anything. He stood with the stillness on him like a coat, and Cael looked at him and looked away, because that was Vastin's, and it was not this morning's, and the man knew it.

Lira moved once. She did not go anywhere; she shifted her weight from the foot that had been mended to the one that had not needed it, which was a thing she did before a floor and had done, he thought, without knowing she had, because there was no floor in a chamber under a city, only a leaf; and the shift put her a half-stride nearer the table's corner where he stood, and she stopped there, and stayed, and he did not look at her, and she did not need him to.

He did not catalogue himself.

That was the discipline, and he kept it, at the table's corner, with the lamp: he did not put his attention on the thing at the middle of him where ten things went round, and he did not say the word the leaf said, and he did not do the arithmetic that the leaf invited — a kind, before the measure; a thing that walked in the land; a boy at a table's corner with twelve pieces of the measure in him. He had the sum. A cataloguer could not not have it. He did not do it. He held the lamp, and the light lay on a leaf where a name had been scraped off and a word had been left on, and he was, for the length of the room's silence, exactly what a working party's hand was for, which was to hold a light where a reader wanted it, and nothing else, and he found that he could be that, and that being it was the hardest thing he had done in a city, and that it was not going to hold past this room, and that it did not have to; it had to hold in this room, and it did.

---

"There's a second thing," Karis said, eventually. "Smaller. I'll say it so it's in the book and then I'll not say what I think of it, because I don't think anything of it yet and I'd rather have nothing than a guess."

She drew a second leaf toward her — not from the bundle; from a different place on the table, a thing on its own square of cloth, smaller, of the same horn-coloured stuff but thinner and more broken, with its edges gone to crumbs and a hole through its middle the size of a thumb, so that what was on it was two-thirds of what had been.

"A chronicle. Later than the first — the hand's the board's hand, or the board's teacher's; the same bearers, but the lean's softer; it's a record, not an instruction. Somebody, a long time after the apparatus, writing down what was remembered of the man who set it. Most of it's gone. There's a run here" — her finger above a place at the hole's edge — "that Cleon read the bones of and I read the words of, and I'll give it you as it stands and no straighter."

She read it. It was shorter than the first, and she read it faster, because it was a record and a record sits.

"*— and of him it is said that he did not die in the way of men, but went into the ordering he had made, and is in it —*" She stopped. "And there's the hole. Whatever came after *is in it* is a thumb's width of nothing." She looked at the leaf a moment longer, and took her finger back. "I'm marking it. I'm not reading it. I've written it down as it stands, with the hole drawn as a hole, and I've written nothing under it, and I'm going to leave it that way until somebody hands me a second source that says the same thing plainer, and if nobody ever does, it stays marked."

"Marked," Cleon said. It was his second word. He said it as a man says a thing he has said to himself for ten years about the same run of marks, and had wanted, for ten years, to hear somebody else say.

"Marked," Karis said, and wrote nothing.

---

Vastin spoke once.

He had not moved through all of it, and he did not move now; he said it from where he stood, beside the case, to the leaf and not to anyone, in the ledger voice, and it was not a question and it was not a finding; it was the thing a careful man says when he has arrived at the edge of a sentence and can see the other side.

"The apparatus," he said. "The measure, the seat, the tier, the mark. He made it and he set it over the land. And the mark that is given at the kindling — "

"Is a thing a record says a man made four hundred years ago," Karis said. She said it at once, and plainly, and not unkindly, and she said it looking at Vastin and not at the leaf, because it was a thing that had to be said to a face. "That's what the record says. It says he made a thing and set it over the land and it says why. It doesn't say what the thing he made has to do with yours, or with mine, or with the thing on a station's roof four tiers up, and I'm not going to say it either, because I've a leaf and a board and a floor, and a leaf and a board and a floor are facts about a leaf and a board and a floor." She put her hand flat on the notebook. "A measurement is a fact about a ruler. Not about the hand that held it. I've said that at a slab and a marsh and I'll say it in a hole. I've two findings this morning, and they go on two pages, and I'm not drawing a line between them, because I can't see the line and I'm not going to guess one."

Vastin looked at her for a while. Then he inclined his head, a finger's width — the Compact courtesy — and said, "Two pages," and did not finish the sentence, and Cael watched him not finish it, and understood that he had just watched the Compact's own held line decline to draw a line, at a table, on a researcher's word, and that the man had done it because the researcher was right and not because she was in charge, and that this was the first time in forty years the man had done that, and that it had cost him nothing, which was the strangest part.

Cael catalogued the two pages, because he could see them from the corner: the notebook open, the bound hand flat on the left-hand page and the pencil on the right, and the left with the leaf's words on it in her small hand and the right with the column's four words and a date, and the gutter between them, a finger wide, with nothing in it. He had watched her not write in a gutter before, at a slab, with a wind lifting the sheets. He watched her not write in it now, in a hole with no wind, with a marshal across the table who had just asked her to, and understood that the not-writing was the same act in both places and had cost more in the second, and that she had paid it without looking up, and he entered the gutter as the thing the morning had found, and did not write in it either.

Cleon said nothing. He had said *lamp* and *marked*, and that was his whole ledger for the day, and he sat with his hands flat on nothing and looked at the leaf where the name was not, and Cael, who had watched him for nine days, saw that the man was not looking at the scraped place. He was looking at the word that had been left on. And his face was the face of a man who has been right for thirty years and has just found out, at a table, in a hole, with five strangers, that being right was never going to have been the relief he had thought, and that he had known that, and had gone on anyway, and would.

---

She copied it before they went up.

That was the last thing, and Cael held the lamp for it, because a copy wanted light more than a reading did, and because it was the thing the chamber had been for and he wanted to have held the light for it.

She copied it into the field notebook, in her own hand — the small square hand that had copied fifty-two sheets of a wall and a kit's ledger and a marshal's six years — from the leaf, a run at a time, with the board beside her for the letters she was not sure of and Cleon's finger going above the leaf ahead of her pencil where a bearer was, and she did not copy the whole leaf; she copied the passage, because the passage was the finding and the rest was the work, and the work was for the road. She copied the scraped place as it was. She did not leave a gap on her page and she did not write a word in it; she drew the patch, the width of it, with its rough edge, and the one upright stroke at its left, and the nothing after, so that a reader of her page would see what a reader of the leaf saw, which was a name's length of a thing that had been taken back to the fibre, and would not be able to say, any more than she could, whether a knife had done it or a thousand thumbs.

And under the passage, when the copy was done and checked against the leaf twice, she wrote three words, in her own hand, smaller, the way she wrote every finding she did not yet dare to interpret, and Cael read them by the lamp he was holding, and did not read them aloud, because she had asked that nobody say it back to her, and a thing written was not a thing said.

*"and the"* — then the gap: a name's length of the page scraped back to the fibre, four or five letters' worth, the first stroke of the first letter still showing — *"set the apparatus over all the land, that the un-bound should not come again, who had near unmade the world before the ordering of it"*

*Older. Made. Us.*

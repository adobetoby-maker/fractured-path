# Chapter 24 — The Center

He counted the strides, because it was the only instrument he had left that read true in his hands, and because a man walking alone across the oldest floor on the continent should be doing something with his mouth that is not talking.

Five hundred and six to the middle. He had the number from the cord's half, and he had it in the register he used for a price, and he walked it as he had walked a Span's arch and a Stair's terraces and a Ring's floor, on stone that had no seams and no give, with the last of the light lying along it from behind him, and his shadow going ahead of him toward the place, longer than he was, which was a thing he noted and did not make into anything.

Ten. Twenty. The stumps behind him going from a rim to a row to a line, and the eleven at them going from people to a shape, and he did not look back, because he had said he would not and a thing said at a line is a thing kept. The thirteen rang in him faint as they rang at every rim — the ten that ran and the three that held, the bed and the plane and the line from heel to hand — as they had rung on four floors, doing nothing, because doing was not what a held thing did. The still place at the middle of them did what it did. And the thing under it, the thing that had turned at the line, turned with him: a stride at a time, as a needle turns when the lodestone is carried past it, not pulling, not pushing, *facing*.

Fifty. He noted that the floor did not change. He had thought it might — a mark, a seam, a place where a thing had stood — and it did not; it was the same grey from the rim to the middle, level, worn to nothing on its surface by whatever had worn the plain, and the only thing on it in any direction was a man walking and his shadow.

A hundred.

He stopped there, because he had decided to, at a fire the night before, and had not told anyone, because it was a thing that was going to cost the eyes and a healer had told him she would not set a term tonight and he was not going to give her a reason to.

He turned the twelfth on the floor.

Not on himself. On the floor: on the thing under his boots, as Cleon had turned it on a board and a leaf and the inside of a chamber's walls — the eyes to the unfocused place, which on a floor a thousand metres across at dusk was everywhere; the lean found; the thing not described but read. He had turned it on a Span's piers from a stride and had it agree with Cleon's board. He had turned it on the still place against a pack in the dark and had come up with bones and a floor with nothing built on it and an instruction with no language, and had entered the nearest words and marked them nearest. He turned it now on an actual floor with nothing built on it, and let it look, and it looked.

Still. Set. The rows — there were rows; the floor had them, under the wearing, in the grain of the stone if stone had grain, bearers and borne, leaning, from the rim toward the middle and from the middle toward the rim, a lean that went both ways and was therefore not a direction but a *shape*. An instruction with no language. The same reading. Not like the still place's reading; the *same* reading, in the same grammar, returned by the same instrument, for a thing under his feet and a thing under his ribs, and he stood a hundred strides in with one Path built for made things telling him that the two made things it could reach from where he stood were the same kind, and could not tell him what kind, and did not have to.

The eyes went. He had known they would. The floor became a smear and the light on it a longer smear and the middle, four hundred strides on, a place he could not see and could count to, and he counted to it, half-blind, because that was permitted and had been priced, and a man does not stop walking to a thing because he cannot see it when he has known where it was since he was fourteen.

Two hundred. Three. The needle facing. Four.

Five hundred and six.

---

Nothing.

He had predicted it. He had written it down the night before, in the plain hand, in the dark, and had thought, writing it, that it was the truest thing he had put on the page in a year, and he stood at the middle of the oldest quiet anyone had ever made, with the eyes coming back slowly and the floor coming back with them, grey, level, to a rim of stumps a thousand metres round with eleven people at it who were too small to count, and there was nothing.

Not nothing *happening*. He had had that at four floors and he knew the shape of it: the fragments ringing on while every sigil in the party went out at a stride, and the difference between the two — his on, theirs off — a thing he could feel as one feels a draught, an edge in the air, the place where a room's warm stopped. An anomaly. He had thought about it every time. He had entered it every time in the same words, because it had been the same every time: *mine worked while theirs died.*

It was not that. That was the thing, and it took him a while standing there to have it, because it was a thing that had to be had by its absence, and an absence is the hardest thing there is to catalogue.

There was no edge.

Not *his worked and theirs did not*; there was nothing here for his to work *against*. The thing that stopped Paths at a stride at every rim on the bearing, that had put out a hundred lamps in an office's column and eleven at a line an hour ago, that Karis called the boundary and Lira called the line and the leaf called nothing because the leaf had been written before it needed a name — it was not here, to him. Not weak. Not held off. *Absent*, as a thing is absent that was never in the room; and he stood at the exact middle of the thing itself, with the thirteen ringing faint and untroubled and the still place doing what it did, and understood that at every other floor he had been feeling the edge of a thing from inside it, as a man in a warm room feels where the warm stops, and that here, at the first one, at the centre, there was no edge to feel because the room had no wall.

He knew why. He did not have the word for it and he knew why.

The twelfth had told him, at a hundred strides and against a pack: the floor and the still place read the same, to an instrument built for made things. The same grammar. The same lean. A made thing and a made thing. And between a made thing and a made thing there is no edge, as there is no edge between two stones cut from the same face and laid together by the same hand; the edge is where a made thing meets a thing that was not made, and he had been feeling that edge at every rim for a year and calling it an anomaly, and it was not an anomaly. It was a seam. And there was no seam here, because whatever had been cut here to sit on the oldest stratum on the plain and whatever had been cut in him to sit under his ribs had been cut by the same tool, in the same grammar, and laid so that the next thing would fit.

He said his own sentence. He said it aloud, at the middle of the floor, to nobody, because he had written it three times in the log and a thing written three times and then found to need more is a thing you revise out loud, so that you hear yourself do it.

"Not the Quiet."

That stood. He heard it stand. The still place was not the thing that stopped Paths; he had known that at a marsh and he knew it now with the thing itself under his boots and the place under his ribs and the two of them not the same and not fighting.

"Not a fragment."

That stood too. He had thirteen and he knew what they felt like and it was not one.

And then the thing he added, which was the only thing he had leave to add, because it was the only thing the instrument had returned and not the only thing he could guess.

"The same kind of made."

He did not say what kind. He did not have it. There was a word for it somewhere — he could feel the shape of the word as he could feel the shape of the rows under the stone, a thing with a lean and no language — and it was not his yet, and he did not reach for it, because he had watched a woman refuse a six-word sentence at a fire and a healer refuse to read a thing the night before the thing, and he had learned the discipline from people who had paid for it, and he was not going to spend it at the middle of a floor because the floor was quiet and nobody was listening.

What he felt was nothing.

He entered it exactly, standing there, in his head, in the words he was going to use that night and not one more: nothing. No draught. No edge. No warm stopping. No exclusion, which he had felt at a gate at fourteen and at every gate since. No prevention, which he had learned the word for at a fire from a leaf a fortnight ago and had carried across a Span and a pass. No wall — and he had stood behind a wall two days ago and knew exactly what one felt like from the inside, and this was not the inside of anything. He stood at the exact centre of the exact place a man had built four hundred years ago to make certain that a kind should not come again, at the end of a line that ran through five made things toward the first, and the place did not know he was there, because a made thing does not know, and he did not feel prevented, and he did not feel excluded, and he did not feel anything at all, and the not-feeling had a name he had never once in his life had cause to use about a place, and the name was that he fit.

It was not an accident. He had that as a fact, whole, and did not decorate it. A leaf had said a thing was built so that a kind should not come again; a woman had said the sites were graded and ordered and the order ran oldest-last; a man in a door had said an instruction leans; and he stood where the instruction had first been written, at the oldest end of it, in the one place on the continent that his instrument could not tell from him, and it was not a coincidence, and he did not know what it was, and he was not going to say the third sentence.

He stood a while longer, because a thing that has happened once should be given the chance to happen again before it is entered as a pattern, and it went on being nothing, steadily, as a level floor goes on being level; and then he turned round, and looked back across five hundred and six strides of grey to a rim of stumps with a shape at it that was eleven people and a mule, and started back, counting, because the count was the same in both directions and he wanted to know that it was.

---

Oryn read him at the line.

She had said she would not read him again until he had stood where he was going, and he had stood there, and he came over the line between two stumps with the straightedge from Denvash in his hand and the eyes nearly back and the ribs saying their one thing, and she was there, a stride outside, with her hands out of her coat, and she did not ask; she put two fingers on his wrist and the flat hand on the ribs and went in, in front of ten people, as she had never once done in a year, because a finding taken the minute after the thing is a different finding from one taken the night before, and she wanted it the minute after.

It took less time than a bench. She came out and took the hand off and looked at him.

"The same finding," she said.

"Nothing."

"Everything, round it." She did not say the rest of what she had said at dusk. She had said it once, and he had not answered it, and she looked at him now as a healer looks at a man who has come back from a thing with the same reading he went in with, and understood that the reading being the same was not the same as nothing having happened, and did not say that either, because it was not a finding. "Lie still when I say."

"Noted."

Lira was first. She had been first since a hall and she was first at a line at the end of a bearing, at his shoulder, with her two hands at her sides, and she said the one word she had said to him at a fire in a yard when the leaf had been read: "Well?"

"I'll tell you on the road," he said. "If there's road."

She looked at him a moment. It was her own line, or a healer's, and she knew it, and took it, because it was the kind of answer she gave and she could hardly refuse it from him. "Right," she said, and that was the whole of what she asked, and it was, from Lira, an act of the same discipline a woman had shown over a map, and he entered it as one.

He told nobody what the centre had felt like. Not at the line, not at the fire that night at the rim, not in the morning. There were no words for it that were his; the nearest ones were a leaf's and a board's and a healer's, and the true one was somewhere past a third sentence he had not written, and a man who has been given an instruction by someone who knew what they were instructing keeps it — and some findings you carry out of a place before you say them, because the saying is a thing that happens on a road, if there is road, and not at a rim, and not to a crew who have stood at a line with their hands dark for the length of a man's walk and are owed a count before they are owed a conclusion.

He gave them the count.

---

The ledger of the year, at the rim, by a small fire, with the floor dark behind them and the plain dark ahead and the stumps between, in the plain hand.

*Three hundred and seventh day, night. The court's rim. A hundred and twenty-three in hand; nothing owed; nothing earned. Nineteen. Thirteen: Wind, Pressure, Iron, Compression, Ember, Shadow, Storm, Anchor, Blade, Tide, Iron Wall, Rune, Force. One anomaly, the still place, read by two instruments — the tenth, which says nothing, and the twelfth, which says a shape — and understood by neither. Not the Quiet. Not a fragment. The same kind of made. That is as far as the instruments go and I am not going past them.*

*Stood in the middle of the first one. Nothing. Entered.*

The letter to Hesk he wrote after, on the last of the good paper, at the size Hesk wrote, because a man who has been written to in four lines all his life does not answer in forty.

*Nineteen. Your grandmother's line was already drawn. I followed it to the end; both edges agreed. Mind the shape, you said. I'm minding it. — C.*

He gave it to Teague, folded, in the morning, because Teague's crew was going west and the last runner off the deep edge was a captain walking home.

Teague took it with the good hand and put it in his coat without reading the outside, because the man did not read and had said so, and looked at Cael across the dead fire with his three behind him — the tall woman with the spear, the broad man on the leg that bore, the older woman with the cloth bag — and the gap where a fourth had stood, which he had read past every morning for three mornings and read past now.

"Spring's over," Teague said.

"Noted."

"Next spring." It was not an offer. It was a term, stated once, at a rim, by a man who kept his own ledger and had put a line in it in a stream-bed and was going to walk back past the stone that line was under, and who had said at a plank that it was a contract and not more than that and had been wrong about the *more*, and knew it, and was not going to say so, because a captain does not. "Whatever's on the next line. My crew comes."

"Noted," Cael said, and it was the whole of it, and it was enough, and Teague turned his three west, past the pass and the stone and the Span and the marsh and the bowl, toward a plank where a man kept a wall, and the alliance went with him, real, and grieving, and there.

Oryn's mule was already facing north.

She had turned its head while the letter was being written, and had taken Karis's boxes off it herself and set them by the case, and put her roll on it, and stood with the rope in her hand at the rim's north side, where the plain went off toward hills that had, three days back along the bearing, a road to three holds that owed her a visit, and she had said at a ford what she would do and when, and this was when.

"The day I have to," she said.

"Noted."

"Everybody says that." She looked at him once more, the look she had given a knee — a thing read, and priced, and not finished — and then at the ribs, without touching them, and said, "They'll carry. They'll hurt," and did not say the rest, because he knew the difference and she had taught it him. "Spring. Bring the map. Lie still when I say." And she turned the mule's head, and went, square to it, on her terms, which were the only kind, and did not look back, because a healer walking her circuit does not look back at a crew walking off it, and the crew did not need her to, and watched her anyway until the plain had her.

Six, and no mule.

He catalogued them at the rim, because it was the last halt of the year and he wanted the count. Lira with the third box and two arms. Brom with the first and the bound forearm and the good one, pricing the stumps a last time by eye, because they were the largest made thing he would ever price and he wanted the number kept. Karis with the case, and the map in the bound hand with six circles on it and a line through all six and nothing past the sixth, folded, and the notebook she had been told not to write in, open. Seln at the plain's edge with the cache, giving the count as he gave it at every halt, to the plain, flatly — "Six. Six and no mule. Nothing behind. Nothing beside." — and not saying *nothing ahead*, because for the first time in a year there was a thing ahead that was not on any line, and Seln did not count what he could not see.

And Vastin. On the stick, on the twenty-ninth day of a month, with the sigil on his hand lit again a stride from the stumps and dark a stride inside them, standing with the five as he had stood with them at a fork and a counter and a cut and a shelf and a stream-bed, unadmitted, an ally who had not asked, and did not ask now, and was not asked, because the asking was a thing that would happen on a road, if there was road, and the man had learned in a season what a road was for.

"West," Cael said.

"West," said Brom, and picked up the box.

He had spent the whole year learning that the world was built to prevent him — four hundred years of tiers and registries and a machine that lit for everyone but him, all of it laid down so that what he was would never come again. And at the center of the first place they ever made, the oldest silence on the continent, the one built before there was a word for it, he had felt no exclusion and no prevention and no wall. He had felt at home. He did not have a word for that yet. But he knew, now, walking back out across a floor older than the system that had expelled him, that it was not an accident — that the place built to end him was the one place he fit — and that the difference between *the system was built to exclude me* and *the system was built to prevent me* had a third sentence past it, waiting, that he was not ready to write. Someone had made the pattern. He was, he was beginning to understand, the pattern. That was as far as he could say.

# Chapter 4 — The Border Road

The slip was on the plank when he came down on the morning of the two hundred and seventy-eighth day, and it had not been there the night before, and Pike, who had pinned it, did not look up when the six of them came in and read it, one after another, standing, as a crew reads a slip it already knows it is going to take.

*Clearance. The cut below the shoulder hold, on the border road, two days west. Stillhound pack in the sink at the cut. Three carters lost since the thaw. Hold pays. Witness: the hold's factor.* And a price — twenty — which Brom read twice.

"Border road," Lira said.

"It's a road." Brom was still looking at the price. "It's the road."

That was the whole of the argument, and everyone at the plank heard it for what it was. The border road ran west from the ford three days to the Line and the city on it, and it was the only road that did, and a slip on Pike's board that paid a crew to walk two days of it was a slip that paid a crew to walk two days toward Treswick, and nobody at the plank had said *Treswick* aloud since a page had been set on a table's bare end, and nobody was going to say it now, because saying it was a decision and taking a slip was a job.

"Ours," Karis said, before Brom could.

Brom looked at her. Then he looked at Pike, and said, "Ours," because a price was a thing Brom said, and Pike wrote HOLD and the five names under it in the order he always wrote them, and then, because the board wrote what it saw and Pike had seen a healer's roll come through his door on the two hundred and sixty-eighth day and had not seen it go, he wrote a sixth line under the five, shorter, and did not write a seventh.

Cael read the sixth line upside down. It was a healer's mark and a route's, which was how Pike wrote a woman who was a stop on her own circuit, and it was ink, and a thing Pike wrote was a thing.

At the door end, the man in the brown coat had watched the six of them read a slip and take it in two words, both of them *Ours*, and had not moved, and was not on the sheet, and picked up his stick when they went out, and came.

---

He catalogued the border road because it was the first road he had walked in nine months that somebody kept.

It ran west from the ford along the river's north bank for a mile and then left the river and went up through the scrub to the first shoulder, and it was a road as the void roads were not: a surface, laid and mended, with the winter's ruts filled in with broken stone by somebody paid to fill them; a waypost every mile, a squared timber with a mark cut in it, the marks counting down toward the Line; a ditch on the uphill side that somebody had cleared of last year's leaves. A kept road. He had walked kept roads for four years inside the Line and had stopped seeing them, and nine months of ruts had taught him to see one again, and he did not like what he saw, which was a country that measured its roads.

Seven of them on it. Six, and the mule, and a man with a stick who walked at the back of the column where a man walks who has not been told where to walk and has decided that the back is where he can do the least harm. Oryn walked beside the mule with her hand on its rope and her roll on her back and the road west under her feet, which was not her road — her road went north from a fork on the far side of the shoulder hold, seven holds on it, and she had said nothing about the fork and nobody had asked her, because that was the condition. Lira walked ahead, on her own foot, with two arms swinging, thirteen days released and still, Cael saw, putting the left hand into her coat and taking it out again to make sure it went. Brom with the shoulder carried and the kit on the other one. Karis with the records flat in their oilcloth under her arm and the map in her coat and the two brass edges in her pocket, walking as she had walked the void roads with a straightedge open in her hand, looking at the ground ahead as if a line were drawn on it, which, on this road, there was: somebody had drawn it a long time ago, with instruments, and somebody else had been mending it since.

Seln ahead of Lira, and then not.

The first day was road and nothing else, and he wrote it as that at the halt, and slept, and the second day was the cut.

---

The shoulder hold was a hold on a shoulder above the road, walled, with a gate that shut, and a factor at the gate who had lost three carters since the thaw and said so in the voice a man uses for a thing that has cost him money and will cost him more.

"The cut," he said. He pointed down the road, west, where it left the shoulder and went into a fold of the hills. "Half a mile. The road goes through a cut in the rock — it's the only way down off this shoulder that a cart can take — and there's a sink on the north side, below the road, where the runoff pools, and it's frozen still, in there, this late, because the sun doesn't get in. They've denned in the sink. They come up onto the road when a cart goes through." He looked at the seven of them and the mule. "Carters declare, on the ice. You know that? And a den beside a road learns the road — lately they come up at whatever crosses, and most of what crosses declares. A man's cart slides on the ice in the cut and he throws something at the wheel to hold it — a Path, whatever he's got — and that's what they come for. Three carts. Three men. We found the carts."

"How many," Seln said.

"Seven. Eight. Nobody's stood still long enough to count."

Seln nodded, once, as if seven or eight were a number he could work with, which it was.

They went down to look at it from above before they went into it, because Cael had learned at fourteen that a thing you walked into without looking at first was a thing that looked at you, and he catalogued it from the lip of the shoulder with the six of them beside him and the man with the stick a pace back.

A cut. The road went into the rock where the shoulder broke, straight, between two walls of grey stone that somebody had cut back long ago to take a cart's width and a hand on either side, a hundred strides of it, falling toward the west. Down the middle of the road, where the road's crown should have been, a runnel — the winter's runoff, frozen, a ribbon of grey ice a stride wide that had not thawed because the walls kept the sun off it. The walls themselves not sheer: scree, on both sides, steep, loose, the kind of slope a body could go up on hands and feet and could not go up fast. And on the north side, halfway along, below the road's level where the wall broke and fell away, the sink — a hollow in the rock the size of a hold's yard, floored with ice, in shadow, with the dark mouths in its far side where the runoff had cut under the stone and made a place a grey thing could lie down in.

He drew it in his head as Karis would have drawn it: a line, the cut; a ribbon down the line, the ice; the sink a bowl off the line's side, fifty meters below and north; the scree the line's two banks. The pack would come up out of the bowl onto the line. The line was a stride and a hand wide with ice down its middle. Anything on the line was on the ice or beside it.

"They come up onto the road," Lira said. "From the sink. Below us, north side, halfway."

"Onto the ice," Brom said.

"Onto the ice." Lira looked at the runnel. "They can run on it. We can't."

"We don't run on it," Cael said. "Nobody's on the ice. Brom's on the road above the sink, on the stone, south side, with a rock. Lira's on the scree, south wall, above him — the slope's slow for them too. Seln's where he'll be. Karis and Oryn at the top of the cut, east end, behind us, and the mule. I'm on the road, west of the sink, past it, so that when they come up they've a choice between the thing on the road that's moving and the thing on the road that isn't." He looked at the man with the stick. "Vastin."

"I'm listening."

"They hunt discharge. A Path is a shout. A still body is nearly nothing to them and a declaration is everything, and they turn to the loudest one, all of them, at once, and they follow whoever moves first." He said it as Karis had said it at a ford in the autumn, as a finding, because it was one. "We fight them silent. Nobody declares unless it's on purpose and unless they're the one who's meant to be loud. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"You're telling me not to fight."

"I'm telling you how this crew fights. I don't know how you fight. I've only ever seen you stand."

Vastin looked at the cut for a while. "Where do you want me."

"Behind Brom. South side. On the stone." He paused. "And still."

"I can be still," Vastin said. "It's the one thing I've been trained to."

Oryn had said nothing. She had looked at the cut, and at the sink, and at the scree, and Cael saw her arrive at the place where the casualties would be, which was the road's south edge above the sink, where Brom would stand, and file it; and then she took the mule's rope, and went with Karis to the cut's east end, and stood where a healer stands, which is not in it.

---

They walked the cut silent.

The silence was a thing he had learned on the void roads and had thought he would not need on a kept one, and he counted it now as he counted anything he needed. The seven of them in the order he had set, with the ice down the road's middle and the walls close, and nobody's boot on the ice, and nobody's breath loud, and the scree ticking once under Lira as she went up it and then not ticking, and the sink below the road's north edge, halfway, in shadow, with the dark mouths in its far side, and nothing coming out of them.

Forty strides. Fifty. Brom at his place above the sink, on the stone, south side, with a rock the size of a loaf that he had found at the cut's mouth and carried in, and his shoulder still and forward and his other arm loose. Vastin behind him, a stride back, on the stone, with the stick, still. Cael past the sink now, west of it, twenty strides down the road, on the south edge with the ice at his left boot, turned to face back up the cut, alone, which was the point.

Nothing.

Then the pack came up out of the sink the way water comes up out of a hole, all at once and without a sound, grey and low, over the road's north edge and onto the ice, eight of them, and turned — every head at once, the way the ford had taught him and the Ring had taught him twice — toward the loudest thing in the cut.

The loudest thing in the cut was a wall.

He had not seen an Iron Wall go up. He had read the Path's name on a page at fifteen and had stood in front of the man who carried it for four years and had never once seen it used, because the Compact's senior enforcement officer did not need to use anything in a hall. It went up now, two feet from Brom's back, across the road, wall to wall, the width of the cut — the road's width; the scree above it was not road, and a plane knows what it was told — not a thing he could see, exactly; a *plane*, a place where the air had been told it was stone, and had believed it — and the whole of the pack, on the ice, all eight, turned from the road and the moving boy and the man with the rock, and went for the wall.

It held. He catalogued that first, because it was the first fact: eight stillhounds hit a plane of nothing at a run, on ice, and stopped as if they had hit the cut's own rock, and the two in front went down and the six behind went over them, and the plane did not give a hair. A wall. Ground that did not give. He had watched Brom go hard a hundred times and had never seen a thing like it: Brom was a man made harder; this was a *line*, drawn across a road, and nothing on the road was going past it.

And the man who had drawn it could not move.

That was the second fact and it came a breath after the first. Vastin stood behind Brom on the stone with his feet apart and his weight down and the stick on the ground beside him where he had dropped it, and his hands open at his sides, and his face gone the colour a face goes when a man is holding a thing up, and he was rooted: a plane across a road is a plane that is *held*, and the holding is the man, and the man is the plane's foot, and a foot does not walk. Every hound in the cut had its head toward him. He was the loudest thing in it. He had made himself the loudest thing in it on purpose, because that was what a wall was for — to be the thing the enemy came to and could not pass — and in a field, with a line of Compact steel behind it, that was the whole of the craft.

There was no line of steel behind it. There was Brom, with a rock, and a boy on a road, and a woman on a slope, and a man who was not anywhere.

"He can't move," Lira said, from the scree, not loud. She had seen it as fast as Cael had. "He's standing there and he can't move."

The pack came off the plane and came again. They did not stop, because nothing about the wall had stopped being loud; a stillhound went to the loudest thing until the loudest thing stopped being loud or stopped being there, and the wall was the loudest thing there had ever been in that cut, and the eight of them hit it a second time, and the two in front went down again on the ice and the six went over.

Brom moved.

Silent. No Path. He had learned that at a ford in the autumn — that Iron Skin was a shout, that every head turned when he went hard — and had never once forgotten it, and he went in now at the wall's south end where the plane met the scree, with the rock, at the hound that came round the plane's edge by the scree, slow, the loose slope taking its feet at every step, and put the rock into it, and the hound went down, and Brom went back to the stone with his shoulder still and forward and the rock in his other hand, and did not declare, and the pack did not turn to him, because he had not been loud.

Lira came down the scree. Not running — the scree would not let her run — but fast, her own fast, the one without Wind, on her own foot, on two arms, and she took the hound that had followed Brom's round the plane's edge, from above, with the knife, and went back up the scree before the next one had finished turning, and was not there.

Seln was not anywhere. Cael had stopped looking for him because looking for Seln was a thing you did when you did not trust him.

Six on the ice, at the plane, going at it a third time, and the man holding it going the colour of the ice.

He did not have the sentence yet. He had the shape of it: a rooted man who could not chase and a pack that would go at him until he fell, and a road, and a lesson from a ford: they follow whoever moves first.

He moved first.

Wind, open, the burst — the loudest thing he could be on purpose — twenty strides down the road from the plane, AWAY from it, west, a shout in a cut that had just heard nothing but a wall. Every head turned. All six. Off the plane and down the road toward the boy who had just declared, on the ice, fast, water going downhill, and Vastin, behind them, still holding a wall that nothing was hitting, could not follow, and did not need to.

He let them come. Ten strides. Eight. The lock was on him — the beat after a burst that he could not price a hit inside, the one the ford had put a hound into — and the six were closing across it, and he did the thing the ford had not taught him, because the ford had not had a wall in it: he went sideways off the road, out of the lock and not through it, onto the south scree, three strides up it, and *stopped*, and went still, and was nothing — a body on a slope, not using anything — and six stillhounds on ice, running at a shout that had stopped, did what ice and a stopped shout made them do, which was to go on past the place where he had been, down the road, and turn.

Back toward the wall.

Because the wall was still the loudest thing in the cut. It had never stopped being. The boy had been louder for three breaths and had gone quiet, and a stillhound did not remember three breaths; it went to what was loud *now*, and what was loud now was a plane across a road with a man behind it going grey, and the six of them turned on the ice, on the slope of the road, and went back up it at the wall, at a run, on ice, and Cael came down off the scree behind them, silent, with the knife.

The rooted thing they could not move was the thing they broke on. One sentence, for the log: he took away the pack's ability to converge on the rooted man by being louder than the wall for three breaths and then not, so that the wall was what they had left to go at.

They hit the plane a fourth time, all six, at the speed of ice and a slope and a shout behind them, and the plane held, because that was what it did, and the six stillhounds who hit it at that speed did not go over each other this time; they went into it, and down, on the ice, in a heap of grey against a wall of nothing, and for the length of a breath there was no pack in the cut. There was a heap.

Seln was in the heap.

He had been on the north scree, above the sink, the whole time — Cael understood it afterward, drawing it — a body on a slope not using anything, ten strides above the plane's north end, and he had come down the scree onto the road in the breath after the heap, and was in it, with the knife, in the runnel, on the ice, where the pack was, doing the thing he did without a sound. Brom came in from the south end with the rock. Lira came down the scree a second time. Cael came up the road from the west with the knife into the back of the heap, and it was not a fight then; it was the runnel, and steel, and four people who did not declare, and a wall.

It took the length of a slow breath. When it was done there was ice with grey on it, and three hounds going back over the road's north edge into the sink at a run because a pack that has lost five is not a pack, and Brom on the stone with the rock, and Lira on the scree with one hand on the slope and the other in the air where she had put it to keep her balance and had not needed to, looking at the hand.

And the wall came down.

Not down. *Off.* The plane stopped being stone and was air, and the man who had held it went down on the stone behind Brom like a man who has been holding a thing up and has been told he can stop, both knees, and then a hand, and then he was sitting on the road with his back against the cut's south wall and his eyes open and his face the colour of the ice, and Oryn was already coming down the cut from the east end with the mule's rope dropped behind her, because she had seen where the casualties would be and had been right.

---

The costs, while she worked, because the costs were the ledger and the ledger was his.

Brom: a hound's tooth through the boot, the right one, through the leather and into the foot above the small toes, not deep — the hound had been dying when it bit — and Brom sitting on the stone with the boot off and the foot in Oryn's hands saying, "It's a boot," in the voice he used for a thing that had cost him money, and Oryn saying, "It's a foot," and binding it. The shoulder: carried through the whole of it, still and forward, and not once used, and Cael saw Oryn look at it while her hands were on the foot and say nothing, again, which was twice.

Lira: nothing. Two arms. She had used both. She was standing at the cut's south wall opening and closing the left hand and looking at it, and she caught him looking, and stopped.

Seln: nothing, and the knife wiped, and gone back up the north scree to look into the sink at the three that had gone into it, and come back, and said, "Three. They'll den somewhere else. Tell the factor the sink's clear, and tell him it's clear because it's empty, not because it's safe."

Himself: the burst's bill, which he knew the price of to the copper — breath, gone; the ten frameworks knocking together in him like a cart on a bad road, which would last the day; the ribs, which had been quiet since the marsh and were quiet still, which he noted, because a thing that stayed quiet was a thing you wrote down. The still place at the middle of them, untouched, unmoved, going around nothing. He did not read it. He had not been asked to.

And Vastin.

Oryn came to him last, because he was the one who was not bleeding, and knelt on the road in front of him with her hands on her knees and looked at him — the count, and the finding — and said, "How long did you hold it."

"Until it was done."

"That's not a length."

"I don't know the length." He said it plainly, in the ledger voice, the one thing he was still certain of. "I have never held it longer than a field needed. A field needs it for the length of a charge. This was not a field."

"No." Oryn put two fingers on the inside of his wrist, which was the one thing she did to everyone and the only thing she had ever done to him, and held them there, and took them away. "You're spent to the floor. And the knee's gone — not broken; gone the way a knee goes that's been asked to be a wall's foot on stone for as long as that was. That's the wound, and it's a real one, and it'll be a real one for a month. You held a thing across a road with nothing behind it but a man with a rock, for as long as eight animals wanted to come through it, and you'd have held it until you fell, and you nearly did." She stood up. "Don't do that again. Not here. In a field, whatever you like. Here, a wall with nothing behind it is a man alone in a road being loud."

Vastin looked past her, up the cut, at the ice with the grey on it, and at Brom on the stone with his boot off, and at Lira on the wall with her hand, and at Seln coming back down the north scree, and at Cael, standing on the road where the boy had stopped being loud.

"The wall held the line," he said. Not to Oryn. To the road. "And someone else had to be the reason they wanted the line." He was quiet a moment. "I have never once in forty years needed someone else to be the reason."

"You do now," Cael said.

"Yes." Vastin looked at his own hands, open on his knees. "I know fields. I don't know packs." He picked the stick up off the road, and did not stand, because his legs had not agreed to it yet. "I'll learn or I won't."

Nobody answered that. It was not a thing that wanted an answer. Oryn went back to Brom's foot, and Karis came down the cut with the mule and the records under her arm and looked at the ice and the grey and the plane that was not there any more, and said, to the notebook, "Note the day," and wrote it, and the cut was a cut again, with a road through it that somebody kept.

---

*Two hundred and seventy-ninth day. The cut below the shoulder hold, on the border road. Clearance: done; the hold's twenty. Five. Three into the sink; the factor told the truth about it. Witness: the factor, at his gate, who paid what the slip said and wrote it in a book of his own and did not ask for a name. A hundred and thirty-eight in hand. Nothing owed.*

*Injuries, all seven, because I do this: Brom, right foot, a tooth through the boot, bound; the shoulder carried, not used, not read. Lira, nothing; two arms; used both. Karis, nothing. Seln, nothing. Oryn, nothing; she was where she said she'd be. Me, the burst — breath, the frameworks for a day, a locked landing beat with nothing in it this time because I'd left the road before it landed, the ribs quiet. Vastin, spent to the floor, and the knee — the wall's foot, on stone, for as long as it took — gone under him; Oryn bound it and said a month. That's the wound.*

*Vastin fought. He rooted the road and nearly got eaten for it — a wall's no good if you're the only thing making noise behind it. He knows fields. He doesn't know packs. He'll learn or he won't. Watched an Iron Wall go up two feet from me. Filed it. Note the day: an Archmarshal took a wound out here and there was no one to report it to. He looked almost free. Almost.*

# Chapter 16 — What It Was For

He told Lira on the stair.

Not the thing. The thing was on a leaf under a registry and in a notebook under Karis's arm, and it had been said once, aloud, in a hole, and was not going to be said again by anyone who had heard it, because a woman had asked that it be the record's and not theirs and the five of them had agreed to that without a word. He told Lira the only thing that was his to tell, which was that there was a thing, and that it was on a page, and that she should read the page before anyone said anything to her about it, and she said, "Now?" and he said, "At the fire," and she went up the rest of the stair ahead of him with her two hands at her sides and did not look back, because Lira did not look back at a thing she was going to have to look at properly later.

The two hundred and ninety-third day, at the bell. Up the cellar stair and out by the yard door past a clerk who counted four and did not ask a fourth thing, and down the carts' street in the last light with the boxes on a borrowed handcart, and along the ring road to the inn, and into the yard behind it, where Seln had already lit the brazier, because Seln had been above all day and had read the crew's faces coming down the street from the yard gate the way he read anything coming toward him, and had lit a fire before anyone asked for one, because a fire was what this crew lit when a thing had to be said and the inn's hearth was a public one.

He had told Seln on the street, because Seln had been above and had not heard the leaf, and a thing that is going to be said at a fire is a thing the man at the gate should have before the fire is lit, so that he can face the road while it is said. He had told him in the fewest words a thing could be told in, walking, at the handcart's tail, and Seln had listened as he listened to a report, and had said, at the end of it, "Made," and then, "For a kind," and then nothing, and had gone ahead to the yard and had the brazier lit before the cart was through the gate.

He catalogued the fire because it was going to be the place, and a place should be had before the thing that happens in it.

The inn's yard, walled on three sides, a shed on the fourth, empty. A brazier on legs, iron, with the inn's kindling in it and a broker's broken crate on top of that, burning low. Six people round it and the seventh not there, on a north road, with a cup that Lira had brought out from the long table and set on the wall at the far end where a healer sat when she sat. Karis on an upturned bucket with the notebook shut in her lap and her bound hand on it and her face the colour of the ninth day of a week that had been asked for as seven, sitting the way a woman sits who has stood up from a table for the first time in ten hours and has found that her legs have opinions. Brom on the shed's step with the foot out. Seln at the yard gate, facing the ring road, because the ring road was where a thing would come from. Vastin on the wall, on the knee, with the stick across his knees. And Cleon.

Cleon had come up.

That was the thing Cael catalogued twice, because it was the thing he had not expected. The man had never once, in nine days, left his table before the bell, and had never once come up the stair with the party, and had never once been seen above ground by any of them except on a stair at noon going to a table on the third tier to eat alone; and he had come up with them tonight, at the bell, with the leaf left on his shelf under the registry and nothing in his hands, and had walked down the carts' street behind the handcart like a man who did not know how to walk behind a handcart, and had come into the yard, and had stood at the brazier's edge in a coat that had been good once, and had said nothing, and nobody had asked him why, because the why was on a leaf.

Cael took the notebook from Karis. She gave it to him without opening her eyes. He opened it to the page — the passage in the small square hand; the patch drawn as a patch, with its one upright stroke; the three words under it — and crossed the yard to Lira, at the brazier's far side, and put it in her hands, open, and did not say anything, and stepped back.

---

She read it.

He watched her read it because it was the one thing in the yard he was going to let himself watch, and because he had watched her read a great many things in four years — a posting on a board, a page in a hearing, a line on a wall sheet — and had never once watched her read a thing that was about him and had been written before either of them was born.

She read it the way she did everything, which was fast, and then she read it again, which she did not do with anything, and her face did not change, because Lira's face did not change when a thing mattered; it changed when a thing did not. She read the three words at the bottom. She looked at the patch drawn as a patch. She closed the notebook, and held it a moment, and handed it back across the brazier, and he took it, and she looked at him — not the count; not a finding; the look she had given him in a hall at sixteen when a marshal had gone inside to read a page — and said the one true thing, which was not comfort, because Lira had never once in her life mistaken comfort for the truth.

"So the whole time they weren't afraid you'd break something. They were afraid you'd *exist*."

Nobody said anything.

He had known, coming up the stair, that she would say the true thing, and had not known what the true thing was, and now he had it, in nine words, from the person who had always been first, and it was worse than the leaf and it was better, because the leaf was a record and this was Lira, and a record sits and Lira did not.

"Yes," he said.

"Right." She put her two hands at her sides. "Then that's what it is."

That was the whole of Lira's part, and it was, from Lira, the whole of the thing.

---

The circle, then, because that was the order: Lira first, and then the circle, and the circle took a thing in the order it took things, and had done since a hall.

Brom priced it. He did it from the shed's step with the foot out, looking at the fire and not at anyone, in the fewest words a thing could be said in, which was how Brom said a thing that had cost him money and would cost him more. "So it's not that they didn't have a column for him." He turned it over once. "It's that the whole sheet was drawn so there couldn't be one." He was quiet. "That's a bad sheet. I've turned down a real job on the strength of it being a bad sheet, and it's worse than I priced it." He did not say anything else, and did not need to, because Brom had said the thing in the ledger's terms and the ledger's terms were the ones the crew kept.

Seln said one thing. He said it from the yard gate, with his back to the fire and his eyes on the ring road, in the voice for an inventory, and it was about the cache, and it was the nearest thing to a decision that Cael had ever heard him make aloud. "There's a thing on my back that a registry would pay for," he said, "and the registry's built on that leaf. I'd wondered, for four years, what I was keeping it from." He did not turn round. "Now I know what the office is *for*. That's worth knowing. I'll keep it a while longer." That was all. He had looked at a door at night and had decided nothing, and had heard a leaf read, and had decided nothing again, and had said so, which was a thing Seln did not do, and Cael entered it where he entered the things that were true about Seln, which was a short page and had just got longer.

Karis had two pages, and said so, and said nothing else, and the saying was the last thing she did that day with her eyes open, because a woman who has read a leaf three times in a hole and copied it once by a lamp another person held has spent the year and the week she had, and the crew knew it, and let her spend it, and did not once say her name to wake her, because a seat kept is a seat kept whether the person in it is asleep or on a north road.

She said it with her eyes shut, from the bucket, with her hand on the notebook that Cael had given back to her: "Two pages. The leaf says a man made the apparatus and why. A dead surveyor says a hundred lamps go out on one wire. I'm not drawing a line. I've said it in a hole and I'll say it at a fire. I've a year and a week of not-guessing behind me and I'm not going to spend it tonight because I'm tired." She opened her eyes and looked at Cael, once, across the brazier. "And I'm not going to say the third word again. I said it once, to the leaf. It's yours now. Do what you want with it." And she shut her eyes, and was, he thought, asleep before the sentence had finished being in the air, sitting up, on a bucket, with her hand on a book, which was a thing he had seen her do at a marsh and had not seen since.

Cleon said nothing.

He stood at the brazier's edge with his hands at his sides and looked at the fire the way he looked at a leaf — not at it; at the place between it and himself — and Cael, who had watched him for nine days, saw that the man was not thinking about the leaf. He had thought about the leaf for a decade and had done all the thinking a decade allowed. He was thinking about the yard: six people and an empty shed and a cup on a wall for somebody who was not there, and a boy with twelve pieces of a craft he had been told about in a hole, and the thing that the man had been right about for thirty years, sitting on a bucket asleep and standing at a gate and sitting on a step with a foot out, being what the leaf said, and none of them looking at him for an answer, because a leaf does not need a man to answer for it. He had not slept well for ten years. He was not going to sleep well tonight. He had come up a stair he did not come up, to stand at a fire he had not been asked to, because a thing he had carried alone had just been carried into a yard by six people, and a man who has carried a thing alone for a decade wants, once, to see it carried, and then he can go back down.

He stood a while longer. Brom, who did not talk to strangers, said to him, from the step, "You came up," and Cleon said, "I came up," and Brom said, "Good," and that was the whole of the exchange and was, Cael thought, the first time in thirty years anybody had told the man that a thing he had done was good, and the man did not know what to do with it, and stood with it, and kept it.

He went back down, later. He said, at the yard gate, to nobody, "Tomorrow the rest of the leaf. The list of parts. It's work, not a finding," and went, and Seln let him out, and Cael put the man's whole ledger for the night in four words — *lamp, marked, work, gone* — and let it stand.

---

Vastin spoke when the fire was at its working red.

He had not moved. He sat on the wall with the stick across his knees and the stillness on him that Cael had seen in a hole that morning, and had not shifted it, and had said nothing through Lira and Brom and Seln and Karis and Cleon, because none of it had been his, and he was a man who had learned, this week, at some cost, not to say a thing that was not his. And then it was his, and he said it, to the fire, in the ledger voice, with nothing in the ledger but the sum.

"I believed it was imperfect." He said it as a man reads a line from a file he wrote himself. "That's what I told a boy across a table in a borrowed room in Ostrand, in the autumn, and it was the truest thing I had, and it had taken me forty years to arrive at. Imperfect. A machine with a fault in it, that had filed a boy wrong." He was quiet. "In Ostrand I learned it was corrupt. Not a fault. A hand on the scale, at the top, deliberately. I resigned over that, or walked, and I have spent a winter thinking that was the bottom of it, because a man who finds out the thing he served was corrupt thinks he has found the bottom." He looked at the fire. "Now you're telling me it was *aimed*."

Nobody said anything. That was correct. The man was not done.

"At a category." He said the word carefully, as a man says a word from a form. "That is what the leaf says. I heard it read. The un-bound. A kind. The whole of the apparatus — the measure, the seat, the tier, the mark — set over all the land so that a kind should not come again. I have administered that apparatus for forty years. I have signed the mark at more kindlings than I could count if I had a winter to count them. I believed, every time, that I was measuring what was there." He stopped. "It was never for measuring. It was for *preventing*. And the category — " He looked, then, for the first time since the fire was lit, at Cael. "Which turned out to be a person. Who's sitting across the fire."

Lira, across the fire, said, to Vastin, the only thing anyone said to him that night, and it was not kind and it was not unkind; it was Lira: "You signed the mark at kindlings."

"I did."

"His?"

"No. I read it, after. I did not sign it." A pause. "I would have."

"Yes," Lira said. "You would have." And she let him have that, because it was true and he had said it himself, and a man who says the worst thing about himself before he is asked has paid for it, in the crew's ledger, and does not have to pay twice.

He did not say anything else. He did not draft anything. He sat on a wall with the stick across his knees and the Compact's own held line looked across a brazier at the thing the line had been drawn to prevent, and did not look away, and did not ask for anything, and Cael looked back, and did not give him anything, because there was nothing to give and the man had not asked, and the two of them sat with it, and the fire burned down.

---

He had it. He had had it since the leaf, and had held it at a lamp for a day and up a stair and across a yard and through five people, and he had not let himself have it until they were done, because a thing you take should be taken after, and not in the middle of somebody else's taking.

He took it now. He did not catalogue it.

He stood up first. He did not know why, and did not ask himself; he stood up from the wall he had been sitting on and stood at the brazier's edge with the fire at his shins, because a thing you are going to take you take on your feet, and the five of them let him stand, and did not look at him, because the family's register for a thing being taken was to leave the taker his face. That was the discipline, and it ran the other way tonight: he had spent a day not letting himself count a thing, and now he let himself have the thing and did not count it, because a man who counts a thing like this has found a way not to have it, and he had promised a healer he would find out what the log did to him, and here was the one thing he was not going to put in the log first.

He had come inside believing the system had no place for him.

That was true. He had believed it since a room at fourteen with a door that locked from the outside, and he had written it in a plain hand at the end of every year since — *no tier, no rank, no file I can see* — and it had been, at the end of the last year, a relief, on a landing in the snow, and it had gone soft with use over a winter, and he had walked into a city with it in his coat like a thing he had stopped noticing the weight of. *No place for me.* An omission. A machine that had been built for other people and had not, when it came to him, had a column, and had filed him under the nearest thing, and had shut a door on him that it would have shut on anyone the machine could not read. He had been on the wrong side of the door by accident of what he was. That was the sentence. He had lived in it for five years and it had a shape he knew to the hair.

The leaf did not say that.

The leaf said the door was him.

Not shut on him. *Built* — the whole of it, the measure and the seat and the tier and the mark at the kindling, every gate on every tier of every city, every hall he had stood in at fifteen and seventeen, every page a marshal had read and declined to read, four hundred years of a whole continent's ordering — built, the leaf said, and set over all the land, for one reason, and the reason was written once and the writer had cared about nothing else being remembered: that a kind should not come again. Not *excluded*. A thing that is excluded is a thing the makers forgot to make room for. A thing that is *prevented* is a thing the makers built the whole house to keep from being born in it.

He was not the case the machine could not parse.

He was the reason the machine had been built to parse.

He sat with that. He did not turn it into a count. He did not put it beside the still place, or the cord, or the light on a hill, or the row that came out short, because those were entries and this was not an entry; this was the page the entries were written on. Five years. He had thought, for five years, that the system had forgotten him. It had not forgotten him. It had been *waiting* for him, since before the walls, with every door shut, and he had walked up to the first one at fourteen and it had done exactly what it was built to do, and had called that a fault, and had filed him, and he had carried the filing across a continent thinking it was an accident.

It was not an accident. That was the whole of it. It was the most deliberate thing anyone had ever done.

He looked at the fire. He thought of a landing in the snow with a ferryman's ropes on it, and a man who had stopped numbering him because the number had got too small, and a woman on a north road who had said she did not know what he was, and a healer's two fingers on a wrist, and a clerk's pen going across four faces at the speed of four boxes, and he found that every one of them was still true, and that none of them was any smaller for the leaf, and that the leaf had made them, if anything, a thing worth having in a way they had not been before, because a man who is prevented and happens anyway has a different ledger from a man who is merely forgotten, and the entries on it are the same and the sum is not.

Lira was across it, with her two hands at her sides, looking at him, and she had said the thing, and he had it, and the having was not a change; he did not permit himself a change declared at a fire, only a difference filed, and he filed it, once, aloud, in the only sentence he was going to allow himself, and said it to the fire and not to anyone.

"For five years I thought the system forgot to make room for me," he said. "It didn't forget. It was *built* so there'd be no room. Four hundred years of a whole world, so that I wouldn't happen." He turned it over once. "I happened anyway."

Lira, across the fire: "Yeah." A beat. "Now what."

"Now we find out how old the oldest one is." He took the straightedge out of his coat, and the dividers came with it, and he put the dividers back and kept the edge. "Karis has a bearing and the marks, and the records to read them against. There's a site at the end of the line that's been quiet since they built the machine." He looked at the straightedge in his hands. "I want to stand in the middle of it."

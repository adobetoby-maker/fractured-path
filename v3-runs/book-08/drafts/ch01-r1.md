# Chapter 1 — Winter's End

*Hundred and thirty-first day past the Line. Lowmarch.*

*The ferry went west yesterday with the Line's lead on the sack and our two letters in it, and this morning the skin ice on the shallows had not broken where the bow broke it, because nothing went out to break it. The ferryman has taken his ropes in. He says the river will be a road by the month's end and a river again in five, and that between the two it is nothing anyone should be on. So: five months. I am dating the winter by the Line because the Line is the only thing out here that keeps a count, and I am writing down the day the count stopped moving.*

*Inventory, because I do one at the start of anything, and a winter is a thing. I am nineteen. Ten confirmed. The still place: not a fragment, not the Quiet, read three times, and not touched since a healer said stop. Ninety-six in hand, with the inn's six for the back room where the sheets are already out of it. Nothing owed. Three hundred entered on Pike's sheet as taken and never to be paid, which is a kind of money too.*

*No tier. No rank. No file I can see. I checked from the landing this morning, both directions, out of habit, and there was snow both directions, and a ferryman with his ropes.*

*Hundred and thirty-third day.*

*The two inns have priced the winter. The upper inn wants a mark a head a week and the lower inn wants the same, and Pike, who keeps the board's room at the lower inn and has chosen the smoke over the cold, said to the book without looking up, "Meadow rate is four a week for a crew that's on my sheet," and the woman who keeps the lower inn said, "Pike," and Pike said, "Eleventh," and that was the price: four a week for five, twenty for the five months, paid in front, which leaves seventy-six. Brom paid it out of the pouch and counted it twice and the woman counted it once, and the five of us have a room with a floor and a fire and a door that shuts, and Karis has the back room for the sheets, and the boxes are under the bed where the mule can't get at them, and the mule is in the ferryman's shed for a mark a month that he has not asked for and will.*

*Teague's crew have the upper inn. Four of them and the healer of no Path, at the table by the window, with the winter road's pay on it. Teague looked across the yard at the five of us going in under our own roof and lifted his chin a finger's width, which is the whole of the etiquette between crews in a town with one street, and is, in that town, a sentence.*

*Hundred and fortieth day.*

*Pike's board in winter. Eleven slips where there were forty. A roof. A well-house. Wood cut and stacked for the upper inn. A fence, ours, forty, held over from the autumn. Nothing that needs a Path and nothing that would let you use one, and the board does not care, because the board has never once asked what a crew was and is not going to start when the river is a road.*

*We walked the fence. Two days out on the winter road to the axe-head hold, with the ground iron underfoot and the posts Teague's crew had set in the autumn standing in a line to where they had stopped, and Brom read the line as he reads a wall, and said, "Straight," and we set the rest. Forty-one posts. Seln found the last two of Teague's posts a hand off true and said nothing to anyone but Brom, and Brom reset them, and that is the kind of thing that goes in a ledger nobody reads. The hold's factor paid forty at the gate, in silver, and wrote it in a book of his own, and did not ask for a name, because the slip said Pike's five and that was the name.*

*A hundred and sixteen in hand. Fence: paid. Pike wrote PAID and the five names under it and the line under that, and that was the tenth line under our names on his page, and he did not say tenth, because the season line above it is still blank, and he was not going to write a number under a blank he had left on purpose.*

*Hundred and sixty-first day. The river is a road.*

*Carts on it. Not many. A man walked a mule across this morning where the ferry ran in the autumn, and the ferryman watched him do it from his door with his arms folded, like a man watching somebody use his house without asking.*

*The kit is on the table.*

*It has been on the table since the first week, because Karis put it there, and nobody has moved it, because moving it would mean deciding whose it was, and it is not anyone's; it is the Compact's, recovered under a contract that paid nothing, and it sits on a table in an inn where the Compact has never been and is not going to come. The case, road-made, registry-stamped. The pacing cord with its knots at a stride, twenty strides to a cord, and the cord's end-loops worn to the shine that a thing gets when one pair of hands has used it for a season. The stake — thirty-two, by its number — with its point still bright from the twice it was driven at nothing. The mallet. The field ledger, in the surveyor's square hand, with the radius line in it, and the line under that, and the one entry that says what the third site is: a designation, in an index, that nobody at this table can read.*

*Karis has been learning it. Not the index; the kit. She says a tool teaches you the hand that used it, and that she wants to know that hand better than she knows her own before she meets it, and I do not ask her where she thinks she is going to meet it. She has paced the meadow with the cord in the snow, and the yard, and the inn's long room, and laid the two straightedges on the carter's map every night and moved them and laid them again, and they do not stop touching all four, and she does not stop laying them.*

*Hundred and sixty-eighth day.*

*Something to write down.*

*Karis had us all pace the yard with the cord, for the learning of it — the cord, the count, the stone at each end, the way it was done at the Ring — and the yard is forty-two strides by the wall and forty-two by Brom's count and forty-two by Lira's and Seln's, and forty-one and a piece by mine. Twice. Three times. With the same cord and the same wall and the same stones, and Karis at the far end holding what she held for everyone.*

*Then the straightedges. Karis laid the Ternhall edge along the map's bearing and it touched four. I laid it, and it touched three and the fourth by a hair short — a hair — and Lira laid it and it touched four, and I laid Hesk's edge and it was a hair long. Brom said it was the cold in the brass, and it might have been, except that the brass was the same temperature in his hand as in mine, and touched four in his.*

*"A measurement is a fact about a ruler," Karis said, "not about the hand that held it." She said it as she had said it at the Stair, as a rule, and then she looked at the cord in my hand for a while, and said it again, and it was not a rule the second time.*

*I am writing it down as what it is: the third instrument this winter that reads everyone but me, if you count Oryn's hands as an instrument, and I do. Claim: something. Evidence: a cord, two edges, a hair. Ruling: hold. It is the same unreadability, probably. The instruments the Compact makes cannot parse me and never could, and a cord is an instrument, and I have known since I was fourteen that I am the thing the instruments were not built for. Nobody at the table said that. Nobody said anything, and we went in to the fire, and the yard stayed forty-two strides for everyone else.*

*Two hundred and thirtieth day. The river is a river again at the middle.*

*Two hundred and sixty-fourth day.*

*The ice is going. The ferryman has his ropes out on the landing, coiled, not rigged, and says a week, and has said a week for a week. The Thornwater road is open — a carter came down it yesterday with the first hides of the year and said the ford at the bend is running high and clear and a man can walk it if he does not mind his knees.*

*Karis has said her sentence about west. She has been saying it since the fence, in pieces, and she said the whole of it last night, and I have not answered it, because there is a thing I owe north before I owe anyone anything west, and it is two days up a road, and it is a map.*

---

They went up on the two hundred and sixty-fifth day, the two of them, because the map was the five's and the promise was his and Lira's arm was hers, and Karis said, "Bring it back," meaning the map, and Brom said, "Bring her back," meaning nothing he would say twice, and Seln said nothing and put two days' bread in Cael's pack without being asked.

The road in thaw was a different road. He catalogued it because he had catalogued it in three weathers and this was the fourth: the ruts full of brown water and the water full of sky; the scrub coming up green under the dead brown, an inch, like a thing that had been told to wait and had waited; the ford at the bend running fast and knee-high and cold enough to take the feeling out of a foot in the length of a breath, so that they crossed it fast and sat on the far bank and rubbed their feet until they came back. Lira, beside him on the bank, with the sling gone the colour of the road and her right foot out of its boot, the tape on it grey and the ankle under the tape the shape it had been since the traverse — turned out, a little, from a winter of standing on it so that it did not say anything.

She had not said the word for five months. He had counted. She had walked six days from the fourth site on it and two days up this road on it and had said *foot* and *boot* and *tape* and had not said the other, because a healer had said she would say when, and Lira, who took orders from nobody, had taken that one and kept it past the first mile and past the winter.

"She'll say when," Lira said, to the river, because he was looking at the tape.

"Yes," he said.

"If she says no, I'm not asking twice."

"I know."

"Good," she said, and put the boot back on, and stood, and the foot went down flat, as it had gone down on every road since the autumn, as if it were somebody else's.

Thornwater in thaw. The fence-line dark with wet, the gate open, the yard mud and straw and a smoking chimney; the well with a boy at it who looked at the two of them and went in without a word, which was the etiquette, and was, from a boy, a paragraph. The house with two chimneys and one of them going. And at the house door, the steading's woman, with her arms folded, counting them as they came in, and arriving at two, and looking at the map-case under his arm and at Lira's arm across her chest, and saying nothing at all, and stepping aside.

Oryn was in the yard behind the house with her hands in a bucket.

She had a man on a bench with his shirt off and a rope-burn across his shoulders the width of a cart's trace, and she was washing it, and she did not look up when they came round the house, because she had heard them come round the house and a healer with her hands in a bucket does not lift them out for anything that is walking. She finished the burn. She dried her hands on a cloth that had been white. Then she looked up, at Lira first — the arm, the foot, the face, in that order, a count and a finding — and then at him, and then at the case under his arm, and she said, in the voice for a distance, "You brought the map."

"You said to," he said.

"I did." She put the cloth down. "Sit her on the bench. Him, off it." The man got off the bench with his shirt in his hand and went, and Lira sat, and Oryn came and stood in front of her, and did not touch her, and looked at the arm across her chest for the length of a breath, and then at Lira's face, which had gone still in the particular way it went still before a bout.

"Five months," Oryn said.

"Five months and twenty days."

"You counted."

"He counted. I didn't."

"That's a season," Oryn said. "Take it off."

Lira did not move. Oryn waited, and Cael understood that the waiting was part of it — that a healer who had put an order on a woman in the autumn was not going to take the order off with her own hands, because then it would have been the healer's and not the woman's — and Lira understood it a breath after he did, and put her right hand up, and undid the knot at the back of her neck one-handed, which she had learned to do because she had had to, and let the sling fall in her lap.

The arm came down. Not fast. It came down the way a thing comes down that has been held up so long that the holding has become the arm, and stopped a hand's width from her lap, and hung there, and she looked at it as if it belonged to a stranger who had been polite to her.

"Move it," Oryn said.

She moved it. The fingers, then the wrist, then the elbow, then the whole of it, out to the side, up, across her body to the other shoulder; and the forearm under the sleeve had two scars on it the length of a finger, one on each side, where a hound's teeth had gone in on the ninety-sixth day and a healer had spent an hour at a line to make them come out, and the scars were white, and the arm moved.

"Grip," Oryn said.

Lira gripped Oryn's wrist. Oryn's face did not change. "Harder." Lira gripped harder, and Oryn's face still did not change, and then she said, "That'll do," and took her wrist back and rubbed it, once, and said, "It's an arm. Use it. It'll be slow for a month and it'll ache in the wet for a year and then it won't. Don't fall on it." And that was the word, and she had said it, and Lira sat on a bench in a yard with two arms in her lap and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been about herself, and Lira did not talk about herself.

"Now the other thing," Oryn said.

"There isn't another thing."

"Boot," Oryn said.

Lira looked at her. Oryn looked back. It went on long enough that the steading's woman, at the house door, found something to do inside.

"You said a tape and a season," Lira said. "You said you wouldn't touch it because you hadn't the reserve, and I'd walk it flat, and I've walked it flat."

"I said that on a road with a man's shoulder to mend and a bank to sleep on. I've had a winter." Oryn crouched in the mud in front of the bench, which a healer did without thinking about her knees, and put a hand on the boot, and did not pull it off, and said, "Ask me."

Lira, who had not asked anyone for anything since Norhold, looked at the top of Oryn's head for a while.

"Will you mend it."

"Lie still," Oryn said, and took the boot off.

He had watched it done at the Ring's line, in the dark, with the light going, on an arm that had been chewed. He had never watched it done in a yard in the sun on a foot, with nobody dying and nothing under the water, and it was the same thing and it was not. The tape came off in a grey spiral. The foot under it was a foot that had been walked on for five months by a woman who had decided it was somebody else's, and it was thinner than the other, and turned, and Oryn took it in both hands, as she took everything, and went still, and Lira went still, and the yard went still around the two of them, the stillness of a yard where somebody is doing a thing that costs.

Cael did not count it. He wanted to and did not, because she had not asked him to, and because a thing he had learned from her in the autumn was that there were readings a man did not take just because he could. He looked at the fence instead, and the smoke, and the boy at the well who had come back out and was not drawing water, and when he looked back the foot was straight in Oryn's hands and Oryn was sitting back on her heels in the mud with her face the colour it went, and Lira was looking at her own foot as if it had said something to her.

"Stand on it."

She stood on it. She put it down flat, out of habit, the somebody-else's way, and then she stopped, and put it down again as a foot goes down that is hers, and the ankle took it, and she stood on one leg on it, and then she did a thing he had not seen her do since the autumn, which was to go up on the ball of it, once, her floor-testing rise, and come down.

"Slow for a month," Oryn said, from the mud. "It'll tell you when. Listen to it. You never have."

"No," Lira agreed, and then, because it was Lira, and because the thing had to be said by somebody and she was the one it had happened to: "Thank you."

"Route's whole," Oryn said, which was not an answer to that and was. "The knee held. Nobody died of three weeks." She got up out of the mud with a hand on the bench, and looked at Cael, and at the case under his arm, and said, "Table."

---

The map on Thornwater's table, where the ring had been laid flat in the autumn: the carter's sheet, the four circles, the bearing in pencil through all of them and off the edge, and the two brass edges laid along it, Ternhall's and Denvash's, brass on brass, touching.

Oryn read it upside down, across the table, because she read everything upside down. She did not touch it. She looked at the four circles and the line for as long as she had looked at Lira's arm, and he watched her not say the thing that anyone would have said, because Oryn did not say the things anyone would have said; she said findings, and this was not hers to find.

"That's a line," she said.

"Yes."

"I don't read maps. I read what walks in off them." She looked at the fourth circle, the big one, and at the line going off the sheet. "Karis wants to go where that goes."

"Karis wants to go west first. There's an archive. She says the answer to what these are is in a place with a registry on top of it."

"West is inside."

"Yes," he said.

Oryn looked at him, then — at him, the way she had looked at Lira: the face, the stance, the ribs under the coat, in that order. "You're whole," she said. "I can see it from here. I'll read you properly when you've slept a week and the ferry's run and you're not standing in my yard with a road on you. Not before. I don't read a man who's just walked two days; I'd be reading the road."

"There's a thing you should know first."

"Then say it."

He told her about the cord. He told it as he had written it, because that was the only way he knew to tell a thing: the yard, forty-two strides for everyone and forty-one and a piece for him, three times; the edges, a hair short in his hand and a hair long, and true in everyone else's; Karis's sentence about a ruler, said twice. He told her because she was a healer who could not read him and had said so as a finding, and a man who is owed a finding owes the instruments back — every one that cannot read him either, as they turn up. She listened with her hands flat on the table, a chest under them for all the difference it made to her.

"A hair," she said.

"A hair," he said. "In my hands. Only mine."

She thought about it. He watched her think about it and not theorize, which was the thing he had walked two days to watch, though he had not known that was what he was walking for. "I put my hands on you four times," she said, "and got nothing three times and a floor the fourth. Now a cord gets nothing. I don't know what that is." She took her hands off the table. "Neither do you. Write that down too."

"I did."

"Good," she said, and looked at the map once more, and then at Lira, who was standing by the door on two feet with her hands at her sides as if she did not know what to do with the second one, and then at him. "Lowmarch is the first stop on my spring. Millrace is the second. I'd have been at Pike's plank in six days with or without a map. I'll be there in two. Get the mule."

Nobody had asked her. He noted that — he noted everything — and put it in the place where he kept the things that were true about her, and went to get the mule.

---

Six at Lowmarch on the two hundred and sixty-eighth day, with the ferryman's ropes rigged on the landing and the river running black and loud between banks that had stopped being white, and the meadow above the ford with the first three tents on it, and Pike's board with fourteen slips where there had been eleven, and the sixth of them standing at the plank with her roll on her back while Pike said, "That one," without looking up, and wrote a mark by a slip, and the room's five men who did not know her looked at the healer's roll and went back to their beer, because a healer was a healer and a plank was a plank.

He catalogued the six of them at the inn's table that night, because it was the first table there had been six at since a marsh, and he wanted the count honest.

Lira with two arms on the table and a foot under it that went down flat because she chose to, and Oryn's crossbow on the wall behind her where she had hung it in the autumn and had not been asked for it. Brom with the pouch — a hundred and eighteen in hand, the mule's shed paid, five, and the ferryman's rope-house roof done for seven in the last week of the ice — and his right shoulder carried as it had been carried since the binding, still and forward, which Oryn had looked at once across the yard at Thornwater and had said nothing about, which meant she would. Karis with the three copies in their oilcloth on the table's corner and the kit's ledger under her hand and the map under the ledger and both straightedges on the map, because Karis did not put a thing down that she was still using and she was still using all of it. Seln at the door end with the case against his leg and his eyes on the landing door, which had a spring's worth of strangers coming through it now, three tents' worth, and every one of them was a face he had not seen, which was the thing Seln did with a face. Oryn with her hands flat on the table and her roll on the floor by her chair and her route in her head, two stops of it here and one a day south, and the rest of it north, waiting.

And himself. Nineteen. Ten. The still place at the middle of them that he did not touch. No tier, no rank, no file he could see, in a town where nobody had asked him for one in nine months, and where the not-asking had stopped, some time in the winter, being the thing it had been in the autumn. He could not have said when. A country that does not read you is a country that does not answer, either, and he had written that down in the hundred and sixties and had not liked it and had let it stand.

Karis made her case then, because there were six, and she had been waiting for six.

She made it plainly, in the order she had it, with her hand on the ledger. The carvings were instructions. She had known it at the Hall, from the shape of them — a record describes and an instruction commands, and these commanded, in the way a ledger's columns command — and she could not read a word of the language they commanded in, and neither could anyone on this side of the Line, because the language was older than the Line. The kit's ledger carried one entry for the third site that was not a measurement: a designation, in an index, in the surveyor's own hand, and she did not know what the index was, only that it was one, because a man does not write *index* in his own field book for his own convenience; he writes it because somebody will check it against a book he has never seen. The bearing ran east off the map and the answer to what it ran toward was not east.

"It's west," she said. "Treswick. The last Compact city on the Line. There's a registry office on the old town, and under the old town there's an archive that was there before the Compact was — the Deepstacks, the scholars call it, when they call it anything — records nobody converted because nobody was ordered to and nobody needed to. Whatever these are" — her hand on the oilcloth — "if there's a word for them anywhere on the continent, it's in a book under that registry that nobody has opened since before there was a registry to open it. And there's a provision. The old scholars' provision. It lets a registered historical-studies practitioner in. I'm not one, out here. There's a man there who is."

Nobody answered. Brom looked at the pouch. Seln looked at the door. Lira looked at Cael, because Lira always looked at Cael when the word *registry* was said in a room, and had done since Norhold, and did not need to say why. Oryn looked at the map upside down.

"Inside walls," Oryn said.

"Inside walls."

"With a station in them."

"At the top of the town. He doesn't go near it."

Oryn said nothing to that, which was not agreement, and was not the other thing. It was the kind of nothing that a healer says when a patient she cannot read is about to walk toward the one place on the continent a healer's whole training says to keep an unreadable body away from, and has not asked her, and she has not decided what she would say if he did. Cael saw it. He did not answer it. It was hers, and she would say it when she said it, and the ferry had not run.

Pike, at the plank across the room, wrote a line in his book and blotted it, and did not look up, and the room's noise went on round the six of them like a river round a stone.

---

*Two hundred and seventieth day. Lowmarch, the lower inn, by the fire. Ice going. Six of us.*

*A hundred and eighteen in hand. Fence paid. Rope-house paid. Mule's shed paid. Nothing owed. Lira: two arms. A foot that's hers. Brom: the shoulder bound and carried and not yet read. Karis: three copies, one ledger, one map, two edges. Seln: the case. Oryn: her route, two stops of it here. Me: nineteen; ten; the still place; a cord that reads everyone else.*

*The ferry runs when the ice goes. Karis has a bearing, an index she can't read, and a language she can't read, and she says the answers are in a border archive nobody's opened since before the Compact. I've spent five years staying out of registries because a registry is where they file you. She wants me to walk into the oldest one on the continent. The kit reads everyone but me. So does the world, mostly. Nineteen. Ice goes next week. West, and inside, for the first time since the academy above Ostrand — which is a sentence I did not think I'd write again.*

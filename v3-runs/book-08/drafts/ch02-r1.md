# Chapter 2 — No Sanction

The ferry came in on the two hundred and seventy-seventh day past the Line, at first light, with the river running high and brown under it and the Line's lead on the sack at its bow, and he was on the landing to see it because he catalogued arrivals, and Seln was beside him because Seln read faces off boats.

He catalogued the ferry first, because it was the first of the year and he wanted the count. The same flat hull, tarred over the winter and smelling of it. The same rope, new-spliced where the ice had frayed it, singing in the ferryman's hands. The boy at the stern pole. Hides, six bales, a broker's mark on four of them. The tarred sack, the lead on it, the season's first mail, which would have in it whatever the Line had decided to say to the edge over a winter of not being able to. And one passenger, at the bow, standing, with a pack at his feet and a stick in his hand, who was not a carter and not a broker's man and was looking at the landing the way a man looks at a place he has read about in a file.

Cael knew the stance before he knew the face. He had stood in front of that stance in a hall at fifteen, and at a table at seventeen, and at the top of a bluff at eighteen with a satchel on his back and nothing anywhere deciding what he was, and a body learns a stance the way it learns a stair. Feet apart. Weight down. The hands still. A man built to hold a line, standing at the bow of a rope ferry in a coat that had never been grey, holding a stick.

"Vastin," Seln said, beside him, in the voice for an inventory, and it was not an inventory.

"Yes."

"Alone," Seln said.

"Yes," Cael said.

Seln did not say anything else. He watched the ferry come in as he watched everything come in, and Cael watched Seln, because Seln had spent fifteen years being the kind of thing that man deployed and had never once, in Cael's hearing, said his name.

---

He catalogued the man as the ferry grounded, because a catalogue was what he had, and because the man he was looking at was not the man he had a catalogue for.

Fifty-six, or near it. The hair gone from iron to something with less iron in it. A traveling coat, brown, road-made, the kind a carter's factor wears, with the elbows already through and the hem cut short for mud. Boots that had been good boots a long way west of here and had done three hundred miles since. A pack, one strap mended with cord. A stick, ash, cut for walking and not for show, with the top worn to the shape of a hand. And the walk, when he stepped off onto the landing with the pack on one shoulder — the left leg taking the weight and the right coming after it a half-count late, a knee that had been asked for more miles than it had agreed to and had kept the account.

No grey. No seal. No clerk behind him with a book. No two men in road-colour a pace back and a pace to either side. He came up the landing alone with the ferryman's boy in front of him carrying the sack, and the boy did not look round at him, because to the boy he was a man who had paid his passage, and that was all a man was on a ferry.

The sack went up the landing ahead of him. Cael catalogued it going, because a sack was the only thing the Line sent that could not be argued with: the lead, the ferryman's knot under it, the weight of it on the boy's shoulder — a winter's worth of what the west had to say, and none of it, he already knew, for the five. Hesk wrote to the courier chain in the spring when the chain was running and not before, and Vell wrote when there was a thing to write, and the Compact did not write to a man it had no column for. Whatever was in the sack was for brokers and holds and the two inns. He noted that he had checked, and that checking was a habit he had thought a winter would cure.

He saw Cael at the top of the landing and stopped.

It was a short stop. A half-stride. The stance did not change and the face did not change, and a man who had not stood in front of that face for four years of his life would not have seen it, and Cael had, and did. Then Vastin came on up the landing, and stopped again, at the distance a man stops at who has come a long way to speak and has not yet earned it, and said, "Cael."

"Archmarshal."

"No." He said it without weight, as a correction to a ledger. "Not that. Not for some months. Vastin will do; it did for my father." He looked at Seln, and Seln looked back, and neither of them said what they were to each other, because it had never once been said aloud and was not going to start on a landing. "I'd like to speak to the five of you. Six," he said, and Cael saw him count, and saw him arrive at the number, and saw him not ask about the sixth, which was a thing the man he had a catalogue for would have asked about at once. "When it's convenient. I've come a long way to say a short thing, and I'd rather say it once, to everyone, than twice to anyone."

"There's a plank," Cael said. "Everybody who lands here goes to the plank first."

"So I've been told. Three times, between the Line and here, by three different people who didn't know each other." He shifted the pack on his shoulder. "I'll go to the plank."

---

Pike's board at the thaw. Fourteen slips, and the room half full at first light because the ferry was a thing the room came down for, and Pike at the plank with the sack open in front of him and the season's mail sorted into two heaps by the time the man with the stick reached him, because Pike sorted mail like crews, without looking up.

Vastin stood at the plank. He did not say his name. Cael, at the door end with Seln, saw him not say it and understood that the man had learned it somewhere between the Line and here, on a road, from somebody who had priced him at nothing and told him why.

"Room," Vastin said. "One. For a man alone. And the board, if a man alone can stand on it."

Pike looked up. He looked at the coat, and the stick, and the knee, and the hands, which were the hands of a man who had signed things, and then at the face, for about as long as he looked at a slip.

"What'd he do?" Pike said. Not to Vastin. To the book.

"Nothing," Vastin said. "Out here. Nothing yet."

"Then he's nothing yet." Pike wrote a line in the book, which was not a line about Vastin, and blotted it. "Meadow's free. Upper inn's full — Teague's crew has it — and the lower's a mark a night for a man alone, and the board's a plank; anyone can stand at it. It doesn't ask. It writes what gets done." He looked up once more, and for a breath Cael thought he had seen something — the name, or the stance, or forty years of a thing coming off a boat — and then Pike said, "You'll want to eat. Door end," and went back to the mail.

Vastin stood at the plank a moment longer than he needed to. Then he picked up his pack and went to the door end, past Cael, past Seln, and sat at the table by the road door with his back to the wall and his stick against his knee, and did not look at anyone, and a woman brought him bread and he paid for it, and Cael watched the man who had commanded the Compact's enforcement for the length of Cael's life count out a copper and a half on a plank in a town on no map, and put the change in his coat.

Teague's crew came down from the upper inn while the bread was still on the table, because a ferry was a thing every crew came down for, and they came in by the road door in the order they walked in — the tall woman with the spear first, the broad man with the crossbow and the limp, the young one, Teague last, the healer of no Path not with them — and each of them, passing the door end, looked at the brown coat for exactly as long as a crew looks at a man alone at a plank town, which is the length of a step, and did not look again. Teague looked a step longer. He had spent a career reading what a man's hands had done, and the hands on the table had signed things, and Teague saw that, and filed it wherever Teague filed things, and went to the plank to read the fourteen slips as if there were forty.

Vastin ate his bread. He did not look at Teague's crew and he did not look at the fire, and Cael understood, watching him, that the man was doing a thing he had done ten thousand times in a career and never once for himself: sitting in a room as the least of the people in it, and letting the room decide when it was ready.

Lira came in from the yard with the mule's bucket and stopped in the door.

She had watched Cael measured by men in grey since Norhold. She had stood in a hall at fifteen while a marshal read a page about him, and at a table at seventeen while the same man declined to read one, and she had a face for men in grey that she had never once had to use on a man out of it, and she used it now, on a brown coat, and it did not fit, and she stood in the door with the bucket and let it not fit.

"He's alone," Cael said.

"I can count."

"He came through the board."

"I heard." She put the bucket down inside the door. She did not go to the table. She went to the fire, and put wood on it that it did not need, and stood with her back to the room and her two arms folded, which was a thing she had been able to do for eleven days and was still doing on purpose.

---

They heard him at the long table in the back, at midday, because Karis said they would hear him, and nobody had said no, and the not-saying was how the six of them decided things they did not want to decide.

Six of them and the mule's empty chair, which was what they called the seventh chair at any table, and a man in a brown coat standing at the end of it with his pack on the floor and his stick against the table's edge, waiting to be told to sit, in a room where nobody had ever told anybody to sit.

Seln was in the doorway. He had come to the doorway and stopped there, on the room side of it, with one shoulder on the frame, and he looked at Vastin for a while, and Vastin let him, because a man who has been read by clerks for forty years knows when he is being read by something better.

"You came through the board," Seln said.

"I came through the board."

"Then you already know it doesn't care who you were."

"I'm learning," Vastin said.

Seln came in and sat down at the door end, and that was the whole of what those two had ever said to each other, and it was, Cael thought, more than most men managed in a life.

Vastin sat. Nobody had told him to. He sat at the end of the table in the chair that had nothing in front of it, and put his hands flat on the wood, and Cael saw Oryn, across from him, look at the hands, and then at the knee under the table, and then at the face — the count, and the finding — and say nothing, because the finding was hers and she had not been asked for it.

"I'll say the short thing first," Vastin said. "I am no longer the Compact's. I resigned, or I walked out, and I have spent a winter deciding which and have decided that the distinction is one only the Compact would care about, and I no longer file with them. I have no rank. I have no seal. I have no warrant, no team, no clerk, and no one behind me. I am aware of what that makes me at this table. I have had three hundred miles to be aware of it."

Nobody said anything. Brom looked at the boots.

"The long thing." He did not open the pack. His hands stayed on the table. "Before I left, I read the Compact's classified history. All of it that I could reach with what I had, which was more than anyone has reached in a long time, because I had forty years of keys and I used every one of them on the way out. Most of it is what you would expect. Two things are not." He looked at Karis, because Karis had leaned forward, a finger's width, and he had seen it. "A name. And a locked layer."

Brom spoke before she could. Brom priced things, and he had been pricing this one since the landing, and he said it to the table and not to the man, because that was how a price was said. "He walked here. Alone. In spring mud, on that knee, with a pack he mended himself." He looked at the boots once more. "Three hundred miles is a demonstration. Out here that's the only kind there is." And that was Brom's whole vote, and it was, from Brom, an argument for hearing a man out and not one hair past it.

Karis did not vote. Karis wanted the name and said so, which was a different thing. "I want the name," she said. "I've wanted a name since a table at Thornwater with a ring on it. I'll hear the layer first because you said it first and because I think I know it. Say the layer."

"The Arbiter evaluation protocol has a layer beneath the one the Compact administers. It is not a Compact layer. It is older than the standardization directive and the directive was written into it, not over it."

"Under every station on the continent," Karis said. "I said so at a table in the autumn." Her voice did not change. "That's not news, Marshal. That's a thing I've carried since the Compact's Hand, and said aloud at Thornwater with the ring flat on the table. Say the part that's new."

Vastin looked at her for the length of a breath, and Cael watched a man revise an estimate. "No living official can reach it," he said. "That is the part that is new. Not *will not*. *Cannot.* There is no key. There has been no key in living memory, and the Compact has stopped noticing that there is no key, because a thing you cannot open becomes, after a century or two, a wall, and nobody files a complaint about a wall. I found the record of the last man who tried. It is a very old record. He did not succeed and he did not say what he saw, and the Compact filed him."

Karis wrote. She had the notebook open before he finished the second sentence and she wrote as she wrote at a source, without looking down, and when she stopped she said, "And the name."

"The name is in the history. It is a citation, not a person — a reference in a Compact record to a designer, a single individual, that the record does not identify and the Compact has never identified, and the citation's trail runs out of the Compact's own archives and into one it does not hold." He said the next word carefully, as a man says a word he has only read. "Treswick. A pre-Compact archive at a border city on the Line. The trail dead-ends there. I could not follow it from inside. I am not certain anyone can follow it from anywhere, but if it can be followed, it is followed from there, by someone who reads what the Compact never converted."

Karis's pencil had stopped. She did not look at Cael, and she did not look at the map, which was in her coat, and she did not say *west*, and the whole table heard her not say it.

And Vastin, having said it, had nothing.

Cael catalogued that, because it was the thing at the table he had not had a catalogue for. The man had brought a problem to a table, which he had done all his life, and had set it down, which he had done all his life, and then the part that came next did not come, because the part that came next had always been his — the team, the warrant, the order given, the response owned before the problem was fully spoken — and there was no team and no warrant and nobody at this table was going to wait for him to give an order, and he sat with his hands flat on the wood and his stick against the table's edge and watched Karis own the response, because Karis was already owning it: the notebook, the pencil, the three questions she was going to ask in the order she had them. He watched her the way a man watches a thing he used to do being done by somebody else. It was not a small thing to watch. Cael had seen it once before, on a bluff, in a man who had just been told what his own file said; he had not expected to see it in this one.

"Why us," Lira said.

It was the first thing she had said. She had sat down last — after Seln, after Oryn, after Brom, who had sat when Vastin sat because Brom sat when a man he was going to have to price sat — and she had sat at the far end, as far from the brown coat as the table allowed, with her two arms on the wood, and she asked it flat, the way she asked the honest question.

"Because I am not going to take it to a council," Vastin said, "and I will tell you why in a moment, when the question is asked properly, because I think it is going to be. But the short answer to yours is arithmetic. The Compact spent four years trying to file this boy. It could not. I know, because I was the one who tried. Everyone else on the continent who has read what I have read is inside the Compact, and everyone inside the Compact who reads a thing like this buries it, because burying it is the job, and I know that too, because that was also me." He looked down the table at Cael, once, and away. "The only people I could think of who would do anything with a name and a locked layer but bury them were the six people the Compact could not file. So I came to them. It was not a difficult piece of reasoning. It was only a long walk."

Oryn said, then, to nobody, in the voice for a finding: "That knee's carried him three hundred miles and he hasn't asked me for anything."

"I didn't know you were a healer."

"You do now."

"Then I still haven't asked."

Oryn looked at him a moment longer, and nodded, once, as if a thing had been entered, and looked away.

"What follows you," Lira said.

"Nothing that can cross the Line." He said it as fact, in the ledger voice, the one thing at the table he was still certain of. "The restriction on him is pending jurisdiction. There is no jurisdiction here. There is no one behind me and no one after me, and if there were, they would not be after me — I am a man who resigned, and men who resign are filed, not followed. What follows this boy is a piece of paper that stops at a river." A pause. "What followed you to the Stair in the autumn is another matter, and it is not mine, and I do not know whose it is, and I would give a great deal to."

Cael had not said anything. He had not said anything since the plank, and the five of them had let him not say it, because the man at the end of the table had come to speak to six and had spoken to five, and the sixth was the one the whole of it was about, and everyone at the table knew that the sixth would say what he said when he had it.

He had it. He had had it since the landing. He remembered a table in a residence room on a bluff above Ostrand, and the man in front of him now in grey, and a sentence, said once, after the longest silence he had ever sat through: *I knew the system was imperfect. I didn't know it was designed to be.* And then nothing. No alliance, no files, no hand across the table. The man had said the sentence and gone back inside and stayed there, and Cael had carried the sentence across the Line in Hesk's satchel as the only true thing the Compact had ever handed him, and had never once expected to hear the voice that said it again.

He did not say any of that. He said, to Karis: "Ask him the thing you want to ask him."

Karis looked at him. Then she put the pencil down, and put her hands flat on the notebook, and asked it plainly, because she was the only one at the table who would ask it plainly, and the table had been waiting for her to.

"Why did you bring this to us," Karis said, "and not to a council, or a court?"

"Because a council would file it," Vastin said, "and I spent forty years being the man they sent to make sure the filing held. I know exactly how deep a thing has to be buried before no one goes looking. This is deeper than that." He was quiet a moment. His hands did not move on the table. "I helped bury smaller things. I'd like to dig one up before I'm done."

Nobody answered him.

Behind him, through the road door that Pike kept a hand's width open for the smoke, the ferry pushed off from the landing with the season's first sack going west, and the rope sang once in the ferryman's hands, and the boat turned in the brown water, and went.

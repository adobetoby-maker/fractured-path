# Chapter 9 — Inside Walls

Cleon came down the stair at noon on the two hundred and eighty-third day, because Seln had said he would, and Seln did not say a thing about a man's habits unless he had watched the habit twice.

He came down it alone, with a book under his arm and his eyes on the steps, in the walk of a man who has taken the same stair to the same table for thirty years and has stopped seeing it: sixties, grey, a coat worn past noticing, and old ink in the creases of the right hand, which Cael, at the stair's foot with the log open, catalogued before the face, because the hands were the thing.

He did not look at any of them. He came off the last step and turned along the ring road toward the tavern that Seln had said was his, on the third tier's landing, where the stair went up again, and Karis, who had been standing at the stair's foot with the flat under her arm for an hour, fell in beside him and said, "Cleon," and he said, "Yes," without slowing, and she said, "I have three sets of carvings off fused-stone floors that nobody on this side of the Line can read, and a word from a stratum older than the standardization, and I've been carrying both for a year," and he stopped.

He stopped like a man who has been asked a question he has been waiting thirty years to be asked and has stopped expecting: not all at once; a step late, with the foot still going, and then the whole of him arriving at the stop after the foot. He did not turn round. He looked at the tavern's door ahead of him for a while.

"Off floors," he said.

"Fused. Grey. No join. Three of them. I copied every mark on all three. Fifty-two sheets."

"Fifty-two sheets." He turned round then. He looked at her — not at Cael, not at anyone else on the ring road; at her, at the flat under her arm, at her hands. "I eat at noon," Cleon said. "I read while I eat. You may sit across from me and not talk until I've finished the page I'm on, and then you may tell me where the floors are, and then I will decide whether I have been wasting thirty years or not. Those are the terms."

"Noted," Karis said, and went up the stair with him, on his pass, which he did not show, because the keeper at the second gate had read it every noon for thirty years and had stopped.

Cael stayed at the foot of the stair. He wrote: *He stopped a step late. That's the whole finding. A man who stops a step late has been waiting for the question.*

He waited the afternoon there, because the district where the unranked were allowed was where a hand waited for a researcher, and he had decided at a long table to be a hand and meant to be a good one. The stair's foot had a bench on it for people who waited, and he sat on it with two carters' boys sent up with messages, and was, for an afternoon, exactly what they were, and watched the second gate do the thing it did.

---

The provision was worked on the two hundred and eighty-fourth, at the registry's opening, because Cleon said it would be, and Cleon, it turned out, was a man who said a thing once.

He catalogued the machine because he was going to be a number in it, and a man should know the form he is entered on.

The registry office on the old town: a building older than the Compact with the Compact's face put on it — a portico, a stamp cut over the door, a counter inside the door with a clerk behind it and a book on the counter and a rail between the clerk and the room. The counter was on the square. He knew that because Seln had told him and because he could see, from the lane behind the office where the four of them stood with the boxes, the corner of the portico and the light off the paving beyond it, and no more, because Seln had chosen the lane for exactly that: the registry's yard door opened on it, the door the office's own carters used, and a working party's hands went in by the carters' door and not by the portico, because hands did.

He had not set foot on the square. He noted it. He had come up the bluff by the tier gates under a pass that was not his, as a number. Cleon had walked them up himself, ahead, with the pass in his hand and not shown, until the second gate, where a party was behind it and the keeper in the coloured coat held out a hand for it; and the keeper read it, and looked past it at the four of them with the boxes, and counted heads with the pen's end — *four* — and wrote the number, and waved them through without looking at a face, because a working party under a registered practitioner's pass was a thing the gates had a rule for and the rule was a count. The keeper at the third gate did the same, with a longer look at Brom's shoulder — a look that priced a man who carried a box on one side of himself and knew why — and none at all at the boy with the box, who had spent five years being looked at by gates and had never once been passed through one as a thing not worth the looking, and found, on the third tier's stair with the box on his shoulder, that it was a different weight from the other and not a lighter one; and then the old town's streets, narrow and older than the walls, and Seln — who had not been on the stair, and had gone up by a way of his own an hour before, and was in the lanes when the four came out of the third gate — walking them ahead of the four as if he had been born in them, left and left and left again, keeping the square on the far side of a row of houses the whole way, so that Cael saw it only as light between buildings and never as a place his feet were on — and the not-setting-foot had cost the four of them a longer walk with boxes and had been done without a word, because a thing written in a log at a fire was a thing the crew had read too.

Four hands. Cael, with the middle box. Brom, with the first box on the good shoulder and the bound shoulder carried, still and forward, exactly as a healer had priced it. Lira, with the third box, on two arms, who had said at a long table *I've two* and had meant it as a price and was paying it. And Vastin.

Vastin had said nothing about it. He had stood in the inn's long room while Seln laid out who went and who did not, and Seln had said, "I'm not on the count. A count's a thing a clerk remembers, and I'm not a thing I want a clerk remembering," and had looked at the man with the stick, and had said nothing else, and Vastin had understood that he was being asked whether a Silver-tier Archmarshal with papers he would not show would consent to be entered on a Compact form as a hand, by count, under a Bronze's pass, and had said, "Yes," in the ledger voice, before anyone could ask it aloud, and had picked up the kit's case, which was the fourth box, and had carried it up three tiers on the knee without a word, and stood now in a lane behind a registry with it on his shoulder, the least of the four, and Cael watched him stand there and did not know what the man was thinking and understood that the man did not either, and that he was going to find out at a counter, and that the finding out was not this morning's.

Brom set his box down in the lane, carefully, on the good side, and stood with his hand on it and his shoulder still and forward, and said nothing about the three tiers and two gates he had carried it up on a foot with a tooth-hole in it, because he had said *it's a boot* once and had been told what that was worth. Lira stood with hers on her two arms and did not set it down, because she was pricing the arms and wanted the price honest. Vastin stood with the kit's case on the knee. Cael stood with the middle box and the satchel under it and the lane's wall at his back, and the four of them made, he thought, exactly the picture a working party made in a lane behind a registry, which was the picture of four people nobody was going to look at, and the not-looking was the machine, and they were inside it. He had wanted, at fourteen, to be looked at by it and passed. He had wanted, at seventeen, to be looked at by it and refused to its face. He stood in a lane at nineteen with a box on his shoulder and was not looked at by it at all, by a rule, on purpose, and the rule was the only door in the city that had ever opened for him, and he entered that, and did not know yet which column it went in.

Karis was at the counter with Cleon. They could hear her.

Not the words. The shape of them — Karis's voice in the register she used for a source, going on, level, with the smaller sound of a man's voice under it saying a thing now and then that she agreed with and went on from, and under both of them the third sound, which was the clerk, who was young and careful and had a form in front of him with three columns on it that he had never once filled, and was being talked to about strata by a woman who had been reading strata for ten years and a man who had been reading them for thirty, and wanted, Cael thought — he could hear it in the pauses — very much for them to stop.

It went on for the length of time it takes a careful man to stop being careful.

Then the yard door opened and the clerk was in it.

He was young. He had a pen. He looked at the four of them in the lane with the boxes on their shoulders — at the boxes first, because the boxes were what a working party was — and then at the count, which he made with the pen, not touching them, one two three four, and his eyes went across the four faces as they went across the four boxes, at the same speed, without stopping at any of them, and stopped, for half a breath, on Brom's shoulder, because a careful man's eye goes to the thing that is carried wrong; and then went to the form in his other hand, and Cael watched him look at the form and look at the four and feel — it was on his face; a careful man's whole trouble is on his face — the place where the form did not have a column, and the thing a careful man does at that place, which is to ask.

He did not ask.

He did not ask because Karis's voice came out of the office behind him, level, in the register for a source, saying, "— and the third stratum's the one you'll want to see before you'll credit the second, because the second's the one that makes the argument and I'd rather you disbelieved it in the right order —" and the clerk's face did the thing a careful man's face does when he has been talked to for an hour about a subject he cannot follow by two people who can, and would like the hour to end, and has a pen in his hand and four hands in a lane and a form that says *count*, and he wrote *four* in the third column, and said, "Down the cellar stair. The practitioner has the key. You'll be out by the bell," and went back in, and shut the door.

Cael breathed out.

Lira, beside him, had not. He saw her not breathe out — the box on her two arms, the paper in her coat that would have made all the morning unnecessary for her and had stayed in her coat — and saw her let the breath go a count after his, on purpose, so that it was hers and not his, and understood that she had stood in a lane with a clerk's eye going across her face at the speed it went across a box and had felt, as he had, the place where a careful man could have asked, and had priced what it would have cost her to answer, and had decided that the price was the point.

Vastin had breathed normally throughout. Cael noted that. A man who had stood in front of clerks for forty years on the far side of the counter did not hold his breath at one; he watched the clerk the way a smith watches another smith, and what he had watched, Cael thought, was a young man decide not to ask, and had found it, from the near side of the counter, more interesting than he had expected.

He had not known he was holding it. He had stood in a lane with a box on his shoulder and a satchel under the box and ten things in him and a piece of paper in a Compact office six streets and a river and a continent away that said what he was, and a careful young man with a pen had looked at his face for exactly as long as he had looked at Brom's and Lira's and a Silver-tier Archmarshal's, which was no time at all, and had written *four*. The bar had not been lifted. It had not been consulted. It had stood where it always stood, on a form in an office in the west, and this form, in this office, had had no column for it, and a careful man had looked at the place where the column was not and had decided, because a woman would not stop talking about strata, that the place was not his to fill.

*That's the door*, he had written. It was. It was a young man wanting an hour to end.

---

Lira did not go down at once.

That was the thing he catalogued, afterward, as the day's finding, though it had not been the thing the morning was for. The cellar door was open and Cleon was on the stair with a lamp and Brom was going down after him with the first box and Vastin after Brom with the fourth, and Karis with the flat and the lamp's light going down with them, and Cael at the door with the middle box, and Lira, with the third box on her two arms, stood in the registry's yard and did not go down, and looked up.

Not at the crown. She had made the same discipline of the crown that he had, unasked. She looked at the tiers.

From the registry's yard on the old town you could see all the machine from above, which was a thing you could not see from the ring road at its foot: the third wall below, and its gate with the queue at it, and the second wall below that with its longer queue, and the first wall at the bottom, and inside it, between it and the second, the outer district spread along the bluff's foot like a thing that had been unloaded there, and between each wall and the next the stairs, cut in the bluff, with the rails, and on the stairs the people going up and down them who each did the thing with the hand at each gate — the pass, shown, read, waved on — and the coats at the gates with their colours, and all of it working, in the sun, as a thing works that has been working longer than anyone has counted and has never once had to ask itself why.

She looked at it for a while, with the box on her arms.

He knew what she was looking at, because he had known her since the day they were both measured and had watched her look at a ladder before. At Norhold there had been a ladder and she had walked away from it with him, with her whole life on the rungs above her, and had not been fast enough to walk away from what the ladder had made of her, which was a person who measured herself by the next rung. At the academy above Ostrand the ladder had been all the argument — *become something they haven't seen* — and she had climbed it with everything in her, and it had not been enough, and she had walked out of a gate with a satchel that was not hers to carry and had asked a fire in the autumn, on a void road with no ladder anywhere on it, *what am I becoming if nobody's measuring*, and had not had an answer, and had fought a ford without declaring and the Hall one-armed and had been fast anyway, and had come, on two arms, up three tiers of the most measured city on the Line, as a number, carrying a box, to the top of the ladder, where all of it lay below her in the sun with its gates and its passes and its coats, and she could have been on the second tier by noon on the paper in her coat.

She looked at it. He watched her look. He did not say anything, because it was hers.

And he saw it not pull.

That was the finding. He had watched Lira look at a ladder for five years and every time the ladder had pulled — he had seen it in the set of her shoulders, the weight going forward, the thing in her that had been built to climb going up the rungs ahead of her body — and she stood in a registry's yard with the whole ladder of Treswick under her feet and a box on her arms and the weight did not go forward. It stayed where it was. She looked at the tiers as she had looked at the stair from its foot the day before, as a thing with a top, and then as a thing, and then she looked away from it, not deliberately, not as a discipline, but as a person looks away from a thing that has stopped being about them, and looked at the cellar door, and at Cael in it with a box, and at the dark going down behind him.

"Four hands," she said.

"Four."

"Right." She shifted the box on her two arms, and came across the yard, and went past him down the stair into the dark, and did not look back at the tiers, and did not write anything down, because she was not the one who kept the log, and he was, and he wrote it: *Lira looked at the whole ladder from the top of it and it didn't pull. She priced it. She's carrying a box.*

---

The door at the bottom was iron.

He catalogued it because it was the last thing before the thing they had come for, and because it was older than everything above it, and a man should know the age of the door he is going through.

The registry's cellar stair went down through the building's own foundation — Compact stone, cut and squared, the registry's own centuries of it — and then through the older foundation under that, which was not squared, which was the quarry's own rock with the quarrymen's marks still in it, and then it stopped at a landing with a door in it, and the door was the Deepstacks' gate: iron, a single leaf, hung on pins driven into the living rock, older than the registry and older than the Compact and older, Cleon said, holding the lamp to it, than the walls, because the walls were built of what had been quarried from behind it. It had a lock that Cleon had the key to and a bar that he did not need, and it had, cut into the iron at the height of a man's eye, a mark that Cael had never seen and Karis had, because she put her hand flat on the flat under her arm without looking at it, which was where the fifty-two sheets were.

Below every tier. Below the registry, below the old town, below the third wall and the second and the first, at the one level of the city where rank had run out, because the stone was older than rank and had never been told about it.

Cleon put the key in the lock and turned it, and it turned the way a thing turns that has been turned every day for thirty years by one hand, and he pushed, and the door went in on its pins without a sound, and the lamp went in ahead of him, and the dark on the far side of the door took the lamp's light and did not give any of it back, because it was the kind of dark that has had longer than the walls to learn how.

Brom went in with the first box, ducking under the lintel because the door had been cut for men who were shorter than the men the Compact bred. Vastin with the fourth, on the knee, without a word, and did not duck, because he had measured the lintel with his eye from the landing and had known he would clear it, which was a thing Cael had watched him do at every door since the ferry. Karis with the flat and her hand still on it, and the cellar's lamp in the other hand, taken off its hook at the stair's head, because a hand carries boxes and a researcher carries the light. Lira with the third, on two arms, past him, and down.

Cael stood on the landing with the middle box on his shoulder and the satchel under it and all the tiered city on top of him — the walls, the gates, the passes, the office, the square he had not set foot on, the thing on the crown that read bodies, all of it stacked up over his head on a hill in the sun — and went through the door as a number, as a hand, as the one thing the city thought he was worth, into a dark that had been there before any of it was, and Cleon, behind him, pulled the iron to, and the tiers closed, and the dark opened.

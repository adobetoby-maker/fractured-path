# Chapter 21 — The Last Reach

Seln came back from the pass at first light on the three hundred and sixth and said what he had said he would say if he liked it less, which was that he liked it less.

"They're on the shelf." He said it to the fire, crouched, with the cache beside him and the wet of the stream-bed on his boots to the ankle, because he had gone up the pass's floor in the dark as far as a man could go and not be on it. "West side. There's a shelf, two-thirds up, the width of this fire, and the scree below it goes all the way down to the floor at a slope you could climb if nobody was looking at you and nobody ever will be. There's a tree that shouldn't be where it is." He did not look up. "A dead pine. Wedged over a stack of stone on the shelf's lip, roped. The stone's been stacked. Nobody stacks stone on a shelf for the view."

"A fall," Brom said.

"A fall. One cut and the shelf comes down the scree onto the floor, and the floor's a stream-bed a hundred strides long with a wall on the east side you'd need a ladder for. I counted nine on the shelf and there'll be more I didn't. Three crossbows that I saw." He looked at the notch, then, in the growing light, black and square. "It's the bearing. There's no round. I've looked for a round since noon yesterday and there isn't one, and I'd tell you if there was."

Nobody spoke for a while. The fire went on.

"Then it's through," Teague said.

"It's through," Cael said, and drew it, because a fight is drawn before it is fought, and this one had been drawn by the other side first and he wanted his own drawing laid over theirs before anyone walked into it.

A hundred strides. The floor a stream-bed, dry, loose stone the size of a fist and a head, that turned under a boot. The west wall a scree slope, the shelf two-thirds up it with the rig on its lip and nine on it, or more. The east wall sheer. The mouth at this end, a cart's width and a half; the far mouth the same. Nowhere in the hundred strides to stand that was not under the shelf. He said it plainly, in the order he had it, and then he said the doctrine, once, to Teague's four, because it was the six's and not theirs and they had not been under it.

"Vastin puts a wall across the mouth. A plane; not a body — you'll see it and you won't, and you'll know it's there because bolts stop. Everyone behind it. *Everyone.* It doesn't move. It can't. He can't, once it's up. Behind it is behind it and a stride outside it is outside it, and outside it is where a bolt goes." He looked at the young one, because the young one was looking at him as the young look at a thing being explained. "Behind it. Noted?"

"Noted," the young one said, and nodded as the young nod, which is with the whole head and none of the rest of them, and Cael entered the nod, and did not know yet that he was going to read the entry again that night and find it the heaviest line on the page.

"And then?" the tall woman said. It was the first thing she had said to any of the six in a year.

"And then Seln goes up," Cael said, "and we find out what nine people do when the ground they built for is under somebody else."

---

They went in at full light, in the order the pass took them, and he catalogued the order because it was going to be the order the costs came in.

Vastin first, with the stick, on the twenty-seventh day of a month, because the wall went where he stood and he had to stand where it went. Cael at his shoulder. Brom at Vastin's other shoulder, bare of everything but himself. Teague and the tall woman. Lira. The broad man on his stick with the crossbow across his back, and the young one beside him, because a man on a stick has a man beside him. Karis with the case and the notebook, and behind her Oryn and the older woman with the cloth bag, with the roll open across Oryn's arm already, because a healer who is going to be needed does not wait to be needed to open the roll. The mule at the mouth with Karis's boxes, tied to a stone, because a mule does not go into a place a man has drawn.

Seln nowhere. Seln had gone at first light back along the hills' foot to the west, to a place he had looked at from the stream-bed in the dark, and was not on the count, and the count was eleven, and a mule.

Twenty strides in, the crossbows opened.

Three. Seln's count. From the shelf, two-thirds up the west wall, at an angle a man cannot answer from a stream-bed, and the first volley came before anyone on the floor had seen a hood, because a team that has learned a barricade does not wait at one; it shoots from where it cannot be reached. Nobody declared. The bolts came down.

"Wall," Cael said.

Vastin planted the stick, and set the bad knee, and put the wall up — not a wall; a *plane*, as at a cut, a thing the eye did not see and the body knew, but laid this time at a slant, like a lean-to's roof: its high edge against the west wall above the scree's foot, ahead of them and between them and the shelf, and its low edge on the stream-bed behind them, a stride high, at the mouth they had come in by, and the eleven under it, on the floor, with the slant between them and the crossbows and nothing under it but the way out at their backs. A plane that faces the thing that shoots at it is a plane, and a plane that does not is a line on the ground, and this one faced the shelf. The second volley hit it. Cael heard the bolts go off it — a flat sound, like stones on ice — and skip up the east wall and fall in the stream-bed, spent, and the eleven were behind it, on the floor, down, with the loose stone under their hands and the shelf above them and nothing coming through.

Brom had a bolt in the forearm.

The first volley. Before the wall. He had turned into it as he turned into everything, and the bed of him had gone hard under the coat, and the bolt had gone into the forearm above the wrist where the coat's cuff had ridden up, because Iron is a bed and not a sleeve, and he sat down behind the plane with the arm across his knee and the bolt in it and looked at it as he had looked at a boot, and said, "Through," meaning that it had gone through and not that they should, and Oryn was already at him with the older woman's hands holding the arm still, and the bolt came out backward the way it had gone in, and the arm was bound before the third volley, which was fast, and he entered it as fast.

That was the first exchange. The team had the shelf and the crew had the floor and a wall, and the wall was doing the thing a wall was for, which was everything, and it could do nothing else.

---

The pine came down at the fourth volley.

He heard the cut. A rope, up on the shelf, going as a rope goes when a blade is on it and the weight is waiting, and then the pine — dead, grey, the length of three men — turning on its wedge over the stack's lip and going, and the stack going with it: the stone, head-sized and larger, that nine people had spent a season putting on a shelf for this, coming off the lip in one body and down the scree in a sound that filled the hundred strides wall to wall.

It came down onto the wall.

He saw it hit. He had seen bolts hit the plane and go off it, and a crossbow's whole spring spent into it at a cut, and he had never seen this: a shelf's worth of stone and a tree coming down a slope at the speed a slope gives a thing, onto a plane a man was holding across a stream-bed with a stick planted and a knee that had been told a month. The plane held. That was what it was for. The stone hit the slant of it and went along the slant, as water goes along a roof, and the fall went down the plane's face, from its high edge at the scree's foot to its low edge at their backs, and off it, and piled — behind them. Across the mouth they had come in by, under the low edge, filling the one stride the plane had left open. The pine on top of it. A cart's width and a half of the pass, closed, with a dead tree across it and the stone to the height of a man, and eleven people and a healer's roll on the floor between that and the shelf, and no way out of the hundred strides but forward, under the rig, past the crossbows, and Vastin, who could not move, at the plane's foot with his face the colour of the stone.

That was the second exchange, and the ground had made it, and the rig had done exactly what it was built to do, which was to put the crew where it could not leave.

"Held," Vastin said, to nobody. It was all he had breath for.

"Held," Cael said.

Then he looked at the scree.

The fall had swept it. That was the thing he catalogued in the stream-bed with the dust still in the air, because it was the thing the drawing had not had and the other side's drawing had not had either: the rig had been built to bring the shelf down the scree, and it had, and the scree was now a slope with everything loose taken off it, a bare grey face from the floor to the shelf's lip, with the stack's footing gone and the pine's wedge empty, and nobody on the shelf was looking at it, because nobody looks at the thing their own trap has just finished with.

Seln was on it.

He was a third of the way up before Cael found him, because a man who has spent fifteen years not being where he is looked for does not become easy to find on a bare slope; he was flat to it, moving when the dust moved and still when it did not, with the cache on his back and nothing in his hands, on the one slope in the pass the team had ruled out because the team had built it to be ruled out, and he was going up it at the speed of a man who has looked at a thing since noon the day before and decided.

The wall was buying him the climb. Cael understood it as he watched: every bolt that came down onto the plane was a bolt that was not looking at the scree; every second Vastin held was a second the shelf spent on the thing in front of it; and Vastin, at the plane's foot, spent past the road and past the cut, was holding all the team's attention on a slant of nothing so that one man could go up a slope behind it, and did not know he was doing it, and would have done it anyway.

The broad man was hit in the fifth volley.

He was at the plane's high edge, where the slant met the scree's foot — under it, not outside it; but a slant that rises to the west wall leaves a strip of stream-bed at its foot that the shelf can see, and a man on a stick who has been down on loose stone and is getting up again does not put every part of himself under the slant. The bolt took him in the shoulder, high, from above, and put him down on the stream-bed at the plane's foot on the outside of the line, on his face, with the stick under him.

The young one went to him.

He did not think. Cael entered that, honestly, because it was all that happened and the whole of why: the young one did not think, because the broad man was down and the broad man was his crew and a man of his crew was on the ground outside a thing he had been told about at a fire that morning in a doctrine that was not his crew's, and he had nodded with his whole head, and the nod had not gone into the rest of him. He went out from behind the plane — a stride, one, into the outside — and got the broad man under the arms, and pulled, and the broad man came, backward, a stride, toward the line, and the young one was at the plane's edge with his back to the shelf and the broad man's weight in his hands.

The sixth volley took him through the chest.

Oryn was already moving. She had been moving since the broad man went down; she had the roll off her arm and was on her knees on the stone before the bolt had finished, and she had her hands on him as he came down, and Vastin could not. That was the thing about a wall. Vastin was three strides from the boy with a plane in his hands that could not move, and if he moved it the eleven were under three crossbows in a stream-bed, and he did not move it, and Cael watched him not move it, and watched his face, and entered that too.

Oryn's hands on the boy's chest. Both of them, flat, the whole of her going in as it had gone into a leg on a slope and a knee at a ford and a man's ribs on a bench, the current running *to* the thing that was hurt and arriving —

She stayed there a while.

Then she took her hands off, and put them on her own knees, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that a healer says, and the boy in the stream-bed had stopped being a thing that could be read, and she did not pretend that he had not. That was the first time Cael had seen her hands come off a person with nothing done, in a year of watching them, and he had not known until he saw it that a healer's hands coming off a person was a thing with a sound, and it was no sound at all.

The broad man, on his face at her knee, with a bolt in his shoulder, said the boy's name.

It was the only time anyone said it. Cael did not enter it. It was not his to enter, and he found — he had not known this about himself either — that there were things he would not write down, and that this was one.

---

Seln came over the shelf's lip at the seventh volley, behind the crossbows.

There were nine on the shelf and the nine were looking down. He had a cord — the weighted one, from a roof in a city — and he put it round the nearest crossbow's stock and the arm that held it and pulled, once, and the bolt went into the shelf's floor, and then he was among them, and the shelf was a shelf the width of a fire with ten people on it and one of them not where anybody had put him, and a crossbow is a thing you shoot from a distance, and there was no distance.

Cael was on the scree by then.

He had not decided that either. He had looked at Seln going over the lip and at the second stack — there was a second; the rig had two, one spent and one still on the shelf's far end, timber-braced, waiting for its own rope, above the place Seln was — and the thirteenth had turned in him toward it as the twelfth had turned toward a still place on a bench, because it was a thing to push against and the thirteenth had been listening for one since a crown, and he had gone up the bare slope after Seln without a word to anyone and was under the second stack with his palm flat on its brace before the ribs had finished telling him what it would cost.

He set his feet. He had watched a man do it twice: square, a stride apart, the weight on the balls of them, the whole of himself lined up from the heels through the hips through the shoulder to the flat of the hand as if a straightedge had been laid down him, and he found the line, and it was his — not Teague's; a stride's distance from Teague's; the adjacent thing, the thing that lived beside a Path and was not it — and he pushed.

The brace went a body's length. Which was his, from heel to palm, and it was enough: the timber went out from under the stack and the stack went, not down the scree, because he had set the line to take it along the shelf and not off it, but *along*, onto the shelf's far end, where three hoods with crossbows had been standing and were not, because a thing that has been holding a shelf for a season and starts to move is a thing that everyone on the shelf moves away from. The high ground was Seln's. The far end was stone. The near end had the shelf's own stair down it, cut for the team's coming and going, and Teague was on the stair with the tall woman, and Lira was past both of them, because Lira was faster than anyone in the pass and had been since a hall.

The recoil came back along the line.

He had known it would. He had it from a card and from a meadow and from a Span, and he had it now: the cost going back the way the push had gone, through the hand and the arm and the shoulder and down, and stopping — not at the wrist, as Teague's did, because he had not been doing it for twenty years and the line had not yet found its weak place in him — in the ribs. The ribs that had been quiet since a marsh. They said so. They said it once, in the register Oryn used for a finding, which was plainly, and he heard it, and did not sit down, because Seln was on the shelf and the stair had people on it.

Teague came up the stair with the other hand.

Cael saw it from under the shelf's lip: the man's wrapped arm at his side, useless by a healer's order, and the other hand out, and the frame lined up the other way round, left-handed, awkward, correct, and a hood at the stair's head braced across it with a stave, and Teague's palm on the stave's middle, and the stave and the man behind it a body's length back onto the shelf and down, and the stair open, and the tall woman through it with the spear reversed, the butt and not the point, because nobody on the crew had been told to kill and nobody did. Lira was already on the shelf, with the flat of the blade, at the speed she had.

The team broke.

Along the ridge, east, above the shelf, where there was a way for people who had built the shelf and knew it: nine hoods, and then eight on the ridge and one on the shelf's floor with a cord round his arm who got up when Seln let him and went after the others, because Seln let him. None taken. No faces. They went along the ridge above the pass's far mouth and over it and were the hills, and nobody on the shelf or the stair or the stream-bed went after them, because the pass was the thing that had wanted doing, and it was done.

Seln had removed the team's ability to hold the high ground by climbing the slope its own rig had swept clean, and the wall had bought him the climb. Cael wrote it that night, in one sentence, and it was true, and it was the least of what he wrote.

---

Vastin let the wall go when the ridge was empty, and went down with it, as he had at a cut and on a road, to the stream-bed, on his side, with the stick under him and the knee where the knee was, and lay there, and said nothing, and Karis — Karis, who did not do that — came out from behind where the plane had been and sat down on the stone beside him with the case still under her arm and put her bound hand on his shoulder and left it there.

The pass, after.

He catalogued it from the far mouth, where he had walked to because it was east and east was where the thing was that he had come for, and because he could not stand where the others were standing and be any use, and because the ribs wanted him to walk and not to bend.

Brom on a stone with the forearm bound, looking at the fall that had closed the mouth behind them, pricing it, because Brom priced everything and the fall was a thing that had been built by hands. Lira on the shelf's stair, sitting, with the blade across her knees and her two hands on it, looking at nothing. The tall woman on the shelf with the spear grounded, not looking down, because a crew does not look at its captain when he is doing the thing he was doing. The older woman with the cloth bag kneeling by the broad man, whose shoulder she had bound, whose stick was beside him, who was not looking at anything either. Seln on the shelf's lip with the cache, watching the ridge, because somebody had to and it was always him.

And Teague, in the stream-bed, on his knees by his own dead.

He knelt as he read a job. That was the thing Cael had watched him do since a board's room in the autumn — read his people in order, one face after another, before a thing was decided, and move them to where it was going to be — and he was doing it now, with one of them, on the stone, in the order he had always done it, except that the face did not read back, and the man knelt there anyway with the wrapped arm at his side and the good hand on the boy's chest where a healer's had been, and did not say anything, and did not look up, and the pass let him.

Oryn stood back from it. Three strides. Her kit closed, the roll on her back by its strap, her hands out of her coat and at her sides, doing nothing, because there was nothing for them to do and she did not pretend there was. She had healed a leg on a slope and a hand at a ford and a knee at a cut and ribs on a bench and had never once been anywhere she could not do the thing she owned, and she stood three strides from the first place she could not, and did not look away from it, and did not go nearer, and that was a healer's whole verdict, and Teague did not need it said.

He said one thing, later, when he stood. He said it to the stone and not to anyone, at the size he said a term, and Cael was at the far mouth and heard it the length of the pass away, because the pass carried sound and because it was the kind of sentence that carries.

"A wall keeps everyone behind it, and one of mine wasn't."

That was the lesson. It was the captain's, and he had priced it, and he was going to carry it, and Cael entered it in the man's words and did not add to them.

He stood at the far mouth of the pass with the ribs saying their one thing and looked east.

The ground went down from the mouth in long steps to a plain, and across the plain, a half-day by the office's distance, at the end of a line that two brass edges agreed on and a dead man's grade and an office's date and a wall's lean, the thing he had wanted since a woman said, on a landing in the snow, *someone made this*. He could not see it. There was a haze on the plain where the morning had not burned off yet, and the thing was in the haze, quiet, as it had been quiet since there was paper to say so, and it did not know he was coming, because it was a made thing and made things do not know.

He had wanted to stand in the middle of it. He had said so at a fire, and at a table, and on a bridge, and to a woman over a map the night before, and he had been honest every time, and it had cost a boy with a pack too big for him, who had nodded with his whole head, a stride outside a wall, in a stream-bed, on the three hundred and sixth day, at the end of a line that somebody had drawn a long time ago through five made things and a great many people who had not been asked.

He had thought the line was drawn in ink. He had thought that because ink was what he read it in. He stood at the mouth of the pass with the haze on the plain and the sound of a captain getting up off his knees behind him, and understood, for the first time, that the line ran through the pass as it ran through the sites, and that what it was drawn in was not ink and never had been, and that he was going to follow it to the end anyway, tomorrow, and that he was going to write that down tonight in the plain hand, and it was going to be the truest thing on the page, and he did not want it to be.

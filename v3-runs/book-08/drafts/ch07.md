# Chapter 7 — The Reading, Inward

The city grew all day at the end of the road, and he let it, because a thing that is going to be looked at from inside tomorrow should be looked at from outside today, honestly, for as long as the road allows.

He catalogued it by the wayposts. At the post that said nine miles to the Line it was a bluff with three lines drawn on it. At the post that said six it was walls, and the walls had gates, and the gates had the road going through them in a switchback that he could trace with a finger if he had wanted to lift one. At the post that said three the walls had a colour — not grey; he had thought grey from the rise, and it was not; it was the ochre a stone goes when a river has been at its foot for longer than anyone has counted and the sun has been on its face for the same — and the roofs inside the rings had a colour too, slate, and the smoke was a colour, and the whole of it stood up out of the plain on the far side of the river like a thing that had decided a long time ago how it wanted to be seen and had been seen that way since.

He did not look at the top of it. He had looked once, from the rise, and had not looked since, and he found that not looking at a thing was a discipline like any other — like not declaring, like not touching — and that he was good at disciplines, and that this one had a cost he had not priced, which was that a thing you do not look at is a thing you are always aware of the location of.

Six of them on the road. The mule with Karis's boxes on it, and the place on the mule's other flank where a healer's kit had been for nine days and was not. Lira ahead. Brom on the foot, which walked, as she had said it would. Karis with the flat under her arm and her eyes on the bluff, reading it as she read everything, for the strata. Seln not anywhere he could point to. And Vastin — who had walked at the back of the column for four days, where a man walks who has not been told where to walk — walking, since the fork-stone, beside him, because the road was wide and kept and there was nowhere else on it to be, and because, Cael thought, the man had made a decision about the back of the column somewhere on the rise that morning and had not announced it, which was a thing the man was learning.

Lira came back down the road once, from ahead, and walked beside him for a post's length, and said nothing, and looked at the city, and he saw her look at the three walls as a thing with a top, and then look away from the top, deliberately, as he had, and understood that she had made the same discipline of it without being asked to and without a healer's term in a log, because Lira had a thing of her own about the tops of walls, and had had it since a hall at sixteen, and was still carrying it at nineteen on a kept road with two arms. She went back up. She did not say anything, and that was the whole of what she had come down to say.

They did not talk for the first two posts. That was correct. A man who has filed you for four years and a man who has been filed do not have a conversation ready; they have a distance, and the distance has to be walked before anything else can be.

---

He ran the reading at the six-mile post, at the halt, with his back to the squared timber and the river below him and the city across it, because he was going to walk into a place that read bodies tomorrow and a man who is going to be read should read himself first, and because he had not done it since a marsh, and a healer had said stop, and he had a thing to work out about what she had meant.

He worked it out first, before his hands moved, because the working-out was the part he owed her.

*Stop touching it.* She had said it at a marsh with her hands on him and her face the colour it went, and she had said it about the still place — the thing at the middle of him that the reading found and found and found and could do nothing with — because she had watched him go at it three times in a week like a tongue at a tooth, and had said what a healer says to a patient who has found a thing in himself and cannot leave it alone. She had not said *do not read yourself.* The reading was his. She had taught it to him at her direction and had written the cost down in advance and it was the tenth thing he owned, and a man does not stop counting his own inventory because a healer told him not to pick at one item in it. He would read. He would read around. He would not touch the thing she had told him not to touch, and if the reading returned what it always returned he would write that down and not go back for a second look, and that was the whole of the distinction, and he had it now, and could begin.

Both hands. Not on anything — on himself there was nothing to put them on; he put them flat on his own knees, which was the form she had shown him, and let the tenth thing do what it did.

It did what it did. He had learned, in the autumn, that there was no better way to say it, and had stopped trying to find one.

The current went out of his hands into him the way water goes into a channel it has been given, and he followed it, because following was the reading and there was no other part. Ten of them. He named them as he came to them, in the inventory's words, because the inventory's words were the only ones he had that did not explain anything.

Wind, first, because Wind was always first: the burst's channel, wide and fast and shallow, the thing that had been loud in a cut two days ago and was quiet now, with the quiet of a road after a cart has gone over it. Pressure, beside it — the Bronze from Ardenmere's, narrow, deep, a channel cut for one thing. Iron, which was not a channel; Iron was the bed, the thing the other currents ran on, and it was quiet too, and had been quiet since a hall. Compression, which sat where Iron sat and pushed back. Ember, small and hot and exact, Karis's, that he had never once used and could feel the whole length of. Shadow, which cost from waking and was costing now, a thin draw that never stopped, the seal's whole logic in one steady leak. Storm, the suite's, Daeva's, wide and rough and asleep. Anchor, which did not flow so much as *hold*, a thing with a grip in it, and he passed over it carefully because Anchor was the one that had been used both ways in a year and still had the feel of both. Blade, Ephram's, sharp and short. And Tide, the tenth, which was not a current he could follow, because it was the following — the instrument, the hands on the knees, the thing doing the reading — and he had learned in the autumn that you could not read the reader, and had stopped trying to, and let it be the one entry in the inventory with no description under it.

He had not run it since the marsh, and the marsh had been the day after a hall, with two of the ten spent to the frame and the ribs saying so, and the reading then had been a reading of damage — of where the current pooled and broke and ran wrong, which was what a healer's instrument was for. This was not that. Nothing was broken. The ten ran as they ran when nothing had been asked of them for a day, which was easily, and the reading, which had been built to find the place where a body was wrong, went along ten things that were not wrong and found nothing to do, and he felt it find nothing to do, and felt, under that, the first edge of the thought that would arrive at the end: that an instrument built to find a fault reports *no fault* as its whole vocabulary, and that *no fault* is not the same as *nothing there*.

Ten. All flowing, or holding, or asleep, or costing. All going somewhere.

And at the middle of them, where the ten went round, the still place.

He read around it. He had said he would and he did: the current went along the ten and came to the place where the ten bent, and he felt them bend — Wind going round it, Iron going round it, all ten going round a thing the way a river goes round a stone that the river has never once moved and has stopped trying to — and he did not follow the current in. He stopped at the bend. He held there, at the edge, with the ten going round under his attention and the thing they went round returning what it always returned, which was nothing: no flow, no change, no temperature, no edge that he could feel from outside except the edge the ten made by going round it. Not the Quiet. He had written that three times and he did not need to write it a fourth; the Quiet was outside a man and turned Paths off, and this was inside him and turned nothing off, and the ten went round it and worked. Not a fragment. A fragment flowed. Older than the log. Observation continues.

He held at the edge for the length of a slow breath, and then he took the hands off his knees, and the current went back into them, and he was a man with his back against a post looking at a city.

---

That was the reading. It was the fourth time he had run it and it had returned the fourth same nothing, and he sat with the nothing and, for the first time, did not go back for a second look, and in the not-going-back he found the thing he had not found the other three times, because the other three times he had been too busy going back.

The reading felt current.

That was the whole of it. That was what Oryn had taught him on a table in the sun: *listening with my hands to how you're built* — but the listening was for flow, for the thing that moved through the architecture, for where it pooled and broke and ran wrong. It was an instrument for movement. It was the best instrument for movement he had ever seen, and it had been built by a Path whose whole name was *current*, and it could no more read a thing that did not move than a cord could measure a thing that had no length.

He had been putting a cord on a stone and writing down *no length*.

He sat with that. The river ran below him. The city stood across it with its three walls and its gates and the thing on its crown that he was not looking at, and he sat against a post with his hands on his knees and understood, plainly, that four readings had told him exactly one true thing about the still place, which was that it was not a matter of flow, and that he had been reading that one true thing as *nothing* for six months because the instrument had no other word for it.

He tested it, because a finding that was not tested was a guess wearing a finding's coat, and he had heard that said at a slab two days ago and had agreed with it. He put the hands back on his knees and ran the reading a fifth time — not to the middle; to the ten — and asked it, in the only way it could be asked, what it could tell him about a thing that did not move, and it told him what it had told him four times: the ten went round; the going-round was real; the thing they went round was, to an instrument for current, a place where the current was not. A cord on a stone. He took the hands off. Five readings, one finding, and the finding was about the cord.

You cannot measure a stillness with a thing that only feels movement.

He did not have another instrument. He had ten things that were not a Path and every one of them was a thing that moved, or held, or burned, or cut, and not one of them was for reading a thing that did none of those. He had a cord that read everyone but him and two brass edges that read a hair off in his hands and a healer four days north who had put her hands on him five times and got a floor, and a man across a river who, Karis said, read how things were built.

*How things are built.* Not how they flowed. Built.

He noted the word. He did not do anything with it, because it was Karis's word for a man he had not met, and a word for a man is not an instrument; and because he could feel, at the edge of the noting, the shape of a thing he did not want to have arrived at yet — that he was going to walk into a city tomorrow and keep a promise about a station and hand the one question he had never let anyone hold to a stranger with a Path he had never seen, and that the reason he was going to do it was that he had just found out, against a post, that he could not answer the question himself, and never had been able to, and had spent six months mistaking the instrument's silence for the thing's.

He was afraid of that. He wrote it down, later, in the plain hand, because it was true, and because a healer had said the log did something to him and he wanted to find out what it did with a sentence like that in it. But he did not write it down at the post. At the post he sat with it, with the city across the river, and did not catalogue it, and did not turn it into a count, and let it be what it was, which was a nineteen-year-old with ten things and a hole in the middle of them that he had been measuring with the wrong tool, on a road, a day from the first registry he had walked toward on purpose in his life.

---

Vastin had sat down.

That was new. He had not sat in front of them since the cut, on his knee; he had stood on the stick at every halt and had let the standing say what it said, and now he had sat, on the road's edge, ten strides from Cael's post, with the stick across his knees and his back to nothing, and was looking at the city too, and Cael understood, after a while, that the man had sat so that the two of them would be sitting, and that it was the nearest thing to an invitation the man had in him, and that he had been at it for some time.

Cael got up and went and sat on the road's edge a stride from him.

They looked at the city.

"I read your file for four years," Vastin said, eventually. He said it to the city. "I want to say that first, because I have never said it to you and it is the only fact between us that is entirely mine. I read it when it was two pages and I read it when it was a case. I read the report from the hearing and the report from the tournament and the report I wrote myself, at a table, when I declined to read another. I know the date you Kindled and the date you were expelled and the date you crossed the Line, and I know the name of the man who carried your satchel out of the residence, because I ordered it looked into. I know more facts about you than anyone alive except possibly the girl with two arms, and I have understood none of them." He was quiet a moment. "I am telling you this so that you know what I am, sitting here. I am the instrument they used. I am the thing that read you and could not parse you, and I am sitting on a road with the thing I could not parse, and neither of us has a rank, and I find that I do not know how to sit."

Cael looked at the city.

"You were the instrument," he said. "I was the error."

"Yes."

"That's the whole of it. That's every room we were ever in. They built a thing to read people, and you were the part that read, and I was the part it couldn't." He turned it over once. "And now there's no thing. There's a road. There's you with a stick and me with a satchel and a cord that reads a hair wrong in my hands, and nobody on this road is going to read either of us, because the only instrument on it is across a river, and I've promised a healer I won't go near it."

"I heard you promise."

"Then you know what I'm walking into tomorrow with. Ten things and a promise."

Vastin looked at him, then, for the first time since the fork — the count, and not a finding, because the man did not make findings any more; he had given that up somewhere between an office and a ferry and had not yet found the thing that went in its place. "I will tell you one thing I never put in the file," he said, "and then I have said everything I came up this road to say, and we can walk. I declined to read you a second time, at a table, when I was ordered to. You know that. The file says I found the first reading sufficient. That is a lie I wrote in my own hand, and I have written very few. I declined because I had read the first one twice, alone, at night, and had understood that if I read it a third time I was going to have to decide what it meant, and I was not — at fifty-one, with a career behind me — going to be the man who decided that. I sent it up. I let it become a designation. I let a boy carry my not-deciding across a continent as a piece of paper." He looked back at the city. "That is the thing I am. Not the instrument. The man who declined to be one, and called it procedure."

Cael did not say anything for a while. He had carried the designation across a continent as a piece of paper and had known, in the way a man knows the weather, that somebody had signed it, and had not once, in four years, put a face to the signing, because a signature was a thing the Compact did and a face was a thing a man had, and the two had never been the same thing to him until a man on a road's edge had just made them the same thing on purpose. He did not forgive it. He had not been asked to. He noted that the man had not asked, and that the not-asking was the only part of it that a Compact officer would have got wrong, and that this one had got it right, and that getting it right had cost him something Cael could see and could not price.

The river ran. Lira, up the road, was standing with her two hands at her sides looking at the walls, and Brom was sitting with the foot up, and Karis was reading the bluff, and Seln was somewhere.

"I'll tell you one," he said. "Because you told me one and that's the only exchange rate I know." He looked at his own hands. "I've a thing in the middle of me that I've been reading for six months with an instrument that can't read it. I found that out just now, against that post. Every reading I've ever run on it came back nothing and I wrote *nothing* down four times, and it wasn't nothing; it was the tool. I've been putting a cord on a stone." He was quiet. "Nobody has that. Not Karis. Not the healer. Not the file. You're the first person I've said it to, and I've said it to you because you've no file to put it in."

Vastin took that. Cael watched him take it — the stillness, the hands on the stick — and watched him not draft anything, which was a thing he had been taught at a spring and was keeping.

"No," Vastin said. "I haven't."

That was all. It was not friendship. He knew what friendship was; he had four of it and a fifth on a north road, and this was not that, and might never be. It was the ground the thing grows in, which is two men on a road's edge who have each told the other a fact that no institution holds, and have found that the road is still there under them afterward. He got up. Vastin got up, on the knee, with the stick, and did not need a hand, and they walked.

---

They camped short of the river, at the last post, with the city across the water close enough now to have a colour and the colour going out of it as the light went, and the light on its crown not going out, because it was not that kind of light.

He did not look at it. He looked at the log.

*Two hundred and eighty-first day. The last post short of the river. Six; the sixth on a north road. Brom's foot walks. A hundred and thirty-eight in hand.*

*Ran the reading on myself again. Same nothing at the center. I think the reading can't do it — you can't measure a stillness with a thing that only feels movement. I need an instrument for what doesn't move. Karis says there's a man in Treswick who reads how things are built. Maybe that's the instrument. Maybe I'm about to hand a stranger the one question I've never let anyone hold. Tomorrow. The walls are close enough now to have a color.*

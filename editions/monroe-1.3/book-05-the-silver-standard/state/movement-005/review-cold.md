## 1. Unscored reader response

**Where interest caught.** Immediately, and for the right reason: the coast coach dropping back from her column to look at Cael "like a person checking a delivery against its invoice," then nodding once and riding off. "I've been inspected." / "You've been weighed." That is a concrete reason to care before anything has been explained, and it pays off twice later (she nods at Ephram after his bout). The corner game in the walled yard is the intellectual peak of the movement — "She was not building him a corner. She was charging him for every road that did not end in one" — and the moment Lira uses it blind, by ear, in her first bout ("Price his roads") is the kind of payoff a reader feels in the chest. The rise above Norhold ("The prospectus was counting people. It wasn't counting that"), the woodcut sheets with Seln cut into the block, Umber alone with the seals, and the blank demonstration card at the foot of the draw boards are the other places I leaned in.

**Where it slipped.** Three places, none fatal. (a) Chapter 28 after Withrow's speech: Cael's Log works out what she meant across five paragraphs ("We are not her argument because we're better than the paper says…") — it explains a line the speech had already landed. (b) The middle of chapter 30: Rooke's speech about Umber at the rail, followed by Cael's list of honest men, tells me Umber is trustworthy a chapter before chapter 31 shows me, better, with a glass and cold tea. (c) The second half of chapter 32 is carrying a great deal — two Brom bouts, Ephram's bout, the filings, the team-trial rule, the city, Havel, the steward — and by the Havel cutaway I had already been told the seat-twelve fact once (Vastin) and was about to be told it a third time (Cael with the glass).

**Which person matters.** Lira, by a distance, for the Zerin line drawn three rows under her own; then Cael for the ring that waits for volunteers; then Karis, because the Ivenne beat is the one the chapter handles most tenderly (Withrow moving to stand between Karis and the crowd without a word). Brom's "Five. All right." and his fifth exchange made me fonder of him than I expected. Ephram's rail confession ("I'd rather be trial caller of a house that walks in with you…") is the best character turn in the movement.

**What I expect next.** Cael versus Rhagen's Shield captain in four days, fought as "a survey," with Cael's private "I think Shield is solved" about to be tested; Lira versus Zerin in the quarterfinal; whoever sits in seat twelve; the team trial, with Karis's sleeping clause ("Again.") and Daeva on the other side of it.

**Would I continue?** Yes, without hesitation. The movement does the thing it was built to do: by the end I believed in the size of the place and I wanted the first ring bout.

## 2. The five questions the owner asked

**Is the scale felt — eight thousand seats, fourteen delegations?**
Yes, and it is felt more than counted. The devices that do the work are physical: the flag a week out at a crossroads; the freight moving to the verge for the columns with "nobody anywhere making them"; the road stopping at the top of the rise; Brom's village fitting in one tower "with the cows"; fourteen painted panels and Halcenvane "eleventh from the top, in letters no larger than anybody else's"; the crowd humming the procession tune through the stone of the tunnel; the tiers becoming "a single sloping wall of faces"; the whole Concourse standing for the cloth and sitting for the houses; the silence so complete Cael "could hear the lamps along the lowest tier." The draw taking two hours and "a dozen accents doing arithmetic aloud at once" is the right size for fourteen houses. Of the fourteen I can name or place about eight as a cold reader (Auremont, Rhagen, Ternhall, the coast house, the onion/green house, the lake house, the hill house, a southern house in yellow, a river house), and the rest are a mass, which is correct. One caution: the figure "eight thousand" is *stated* roughly seven times across the six chapters (Withrow, Rooke's briefing, Umber twice, the tunnel, the procession, Karis's bank speech). The felt moments carry it; two of the statements could go without loss.

**Is the new scoring format clear by ear the one time it is stated?**
Yes. The italic paragraph Karis reads at the long table (chapter 30, "Bracket bouts at the continental finals are scored by the exchange…") can be followed aloud on one pass: five exchanges; a clean touch is a point; an exchange ends at the bell or at three; points add; early finish when the fighter behind cannot catch up; ties to the figures. What makes it stick is that the table immediately translates it into character ("Every exchange is money"; Brom: "I've never fought a fifth"; Rooke on the man two up who holds and the man two down who throws everything), and then every bout uses the same two-number call — "Two to one. Two to three on the bout." — so the reader learns the notation by repetition without being taught it. I checked the arithmetic of every bout against the rule: Lira (0-2, 2-1, 3-1, 3-0 → 8-4, over in four), Karis (8-1 after three, over), Brom I (9-0 after three, over), Ephram (2-1, 1-1, 0-2, 3-0, 3-1 → 9-5), and Brom II (1-2, 2-1, 1-3, 3-0, 2-1 → 9-7). All the totals are right. The one place the *prose* and the *score* disagree is Brom's fifth exchange — see Repair brief, Priority 1.

**Are the opening-round bouts distinct?**
Yes — each has a different problem, a different fix, and a different source on the road. Lira versus the Ash miser (veils spent only where they hide something; she stops looking and listens to the honest floor; then prices his roads blind) is the best bout in the movement and the only one that runs to full length, which it earns. Karis versus the Rune scribe is summary, three sentences, and that is the right weight for a cutaway. Brom I is comedy — the old edition of his file, the mended seam, "It's an old edition." Brom II versus the hill circler is stillness against motion, resolved by Rooke's four words ("Nobody stands still. Walk.") that call back the cold-term habit of walking to keep feet warm. Ephram versus the coast Blade is the paying bout — two points spent to learn the length of a backward stride that Karis had read on the road from the stirrup leathers. Rooke's evening lecture on the trade is a touch long but earns its place. Two small tics across the set: three losers in a row "stood… looking at his hands" (the narration even notes the repetition once), and three bouts end on a warm handshake; vary one of each.

**Are the three cutaways identifiable?**
Yes, each in its first sentence. Vastin: "The Archmarshal Vastin read the Norhold files on a Fourth-day evening, in his room in the Compact's house, a week before the opening." Umber: "Chief Adjudicator Umber sent his clerks home at the ninth bell on the eve of the opening, and then sat down alone with the case." Havel: "On the fourth evening Assessor Havel sat in the second seat of the Compact's row." A cold reader never wonders whose head she is in. Umber's is the strongest piece of prose in the movement ("You checked them last night. So your hands may shake. The seals did not."). The problem is not identification but duplication: Vastin and Havel are the same scene — an official reads line twelve of the seating order, notices a hand that aches, and declines to let a thought run ahead of its evidence — and then Cael does the seat-twelve beat a third time with the man and the pie. The matching aches (Vastin's left hand, Havel's right) read as a deliberate plant to a cold reader, but the plant does not need the whole scene repeated to carry it. See Priority 2.

**Does Daeva, seen only at a distance, make you afraid for what comes?**
Wary, and by the last line of the procession scene, yes, afraid — but it is a cold fear, not a hot one, and I think that is the design. The build is disciplined: Cael turns his back on the Auremont column in the street ("I decline to review the publishing"), the woodcut is "all storm and no face," and then the hundred-yard read asks the body four questions and the fourth has no answer. "Her body carried on as though she were crossing an empty yard in the rain" is the image that stays. The moment that actually produces dread is the head-turn: it goes "straight across the hundred yards to the north side, to the eleventh block, and moved along Halcenvane from one end to the other and back again… She was counting what we'd brought." That is the right beat and it is placed correctly. What limits the fear is that only Cael registers it; nobody else in the block reacts, Rooke says nothing about her anywhere in the movement, and the reader does not yet know the route by which Cael and a Gold could meet (the team trial is implied by Rhagen's "captain of Rhagen's squad for the team trial" and Karis's roster clause, and a cold reader does assemble it, but late). One reaction line from the block — Lira, who talks about everything, saying nothing; or Brom's shoulder arriving at Cael's — would turn the notebook entry into something the whole house felt. Not a priority; a suggestion.

## 3. Two lenses

**A fluent thirteen-year-old.** Would race through the corner game (and probably reread the third game once to see how the pricing works, which is a good reread), the woodcut table, "With the cows on the upper floors," the barrow-man's "The sinister one sells better," Brom's "Five. All right.", and all five bouts. Would come away wanting the Rhagen bout and worried about Zerin, not Daeva.

**An adult genre reader.** Would admire the pricing idea and its blind reuse; Umber's seals as the moral spine of the movement; "This city is the system with the roof off"; "Cheaper than deciding"; "Scheduling is opinion"; and the discipline of withholding Daeva's face. Would notice the seat-twelve triplication, the two aching hands, the single-sentence-paragraph habit flattening cadence through the orientation and Umber's ledger pages, "eight thousand" as a recurring figure, and the "looked at his hands" tic. Would clock the hidden-cost thread ("the one that stood on no record anywhere… costing what it always cost from the moment he woke") as carried exactly right — present three times, never explained.

The hip "line" is named as the floor's ordinary bill and nothing more; the right shoulder is explicitly unasked; the unrecorded cost is carried three times without disclosure. Consistent.
- **Scoring arithmetic.** All bout totals and early-finish calls obey the stated rule (checked above). The single prose/score disagreement is Brom's fifth exchange. The eleven-minute figures interval is consistent across three bouts.
- **Counts.** Karis's sheet count (Cael four, Lira two, Brom one, Karis one) matches the six sheets described. Halcenvane "eleventh of fourteen" on the board matches the procession. Lira's "There'll be thirteen more of her" — the coast coach is one of thirteen *other* houses, so strictly twelve more; a careful reader may blink.

## Repair brief

**Priority 1 — Brom's fifth exchange reads as three touches while the call says two.**
- *Location:* Chapter 32, Brom v. the hill fighter, fifth exchange: from "Once the hill fighter went for the left door, fast, with everything he had left" through "Two to one. Nine to seven."
- *Observed issue:* The paragraph narrates Brom's point, the hill fighter's sweep, then "Then Brom took another, plainly, through the middle, a breath before the bell" — and then a new paragraph begins "The last of it came with the bell nearly up" and describes Brom's "other fist" coming through "impossible to stop." Read sequentially that is Brom 3, hill 1, which under the stated rule closes the exchange early and makes the bout 10–7, not 9–7. If the two sentences describe the same touch (summary then close-up), the "Then… Then…" sequencing hides it.
- *Effect on the reader:* At the climax of the movement's hardest bracket bout, the reader who has just learned to count by ear stops and recounts, and the rule the movement has worked hard to make audible wobbles exactly where it should be most trusted.
- *Proposed scope:* One or two sentences. Either cut "Then Brom took another, plainly, through the middle, a breath before the bell" so the close-up fist is the second point, or recast it as the lead-in to the close-up ("The second came with the bell nearly up…"). Do not shorten the exchange; the millstones image and "found Brom already turning into it" are the best lines in the bout.
- *Strength to preserve:* The whole walking turn — "a moving man does not have to start moving" — and the final "slow and plain and impossible to stop."

**Priority 2 — Seat twelve is passed over three times, and two of the passes are the same scene.**
- *Location:* Chapter 29 closing section (Vastin reads the seating plan); Chapter 32 "On the fourth evening Assessor Havel sat in the second seat…"; Chapter 32 closing section (Cael, the steward, the man with the pie).
- *Observed issue:* Vastin and Havel each read line twelve, each has a hand that aches "as it did most evenings now," and each declines to let a thought run ahead of its evidence. Cael then re-establishes the held seat a third time. The matching aches read as a plant and should stay, but the Havel scene restates Vastin's beat almost line for line, and by its third telling the fact has stopped being news.
- *Effect on the reader:* The second and third passes are met with "I know" rather than unease; the dread of the empty chair flattens into repetition, and the thirteen-year-old skims the Havel page.
- *Proposed scope:* Keep Vastin whole and Cael's steward scene whole (the pie, the brushed seat, the screw holes, "Blank pages are the ones that get written last" are the movement's last and best image of the chair). Cut Havel's section by half and give it one thing Vastin's did not have — he already knows which man holds that grade, and what it would mean for that man to come; or he looks down from the row at the ring sockets rather than along it at the chair; or he writes nothing and the reader sees the pen stop. Keep the aching hand in both as the plant it is.
- *Strength to preserve:* "the difference between a pattern and a guess wearing a pattern's coat," and the notebook's rule that it holds what happened and nothing of what he made of it.

**Priority 3 — Chapter 30's day runs out of order, plus two mechanical errors.**
- *Location:* Chapter 30: the scoring-rules section ends "They sat up late over the program that night… 'Five,' he said, to himself. 'All right.'" and the next section opens "Rooke took Cael back to the main floor rail at the end of the afternoon," followed by "Before the light went" and "The draw was made at dusk that same day." Also Chapter 32, the city section: "There were and colour-sellers with the fourteen houses' ribbons on long poles." Also, optionally, Chapter 27: "There'll be thirteen more of her."
- *Observed issue:* A late-night scene is placed before the afternoon and dusk of the same day; the reader rewinds without being told to. "There were and colour-sellers" is a dropped noun. "Thirteen more" counts fourteen other houses where there are thirteen, one already seen.
- *Effect on the reader:* A clean-transition stumble in a chapter that is otherwise a model of orderly arrival; a sentence that cannot be read aloud; a small arithmetic blink on the first page of the movement.
- *Proposed scope:* Move the "sat up late" paragraph to follow the draw (it also lands better after Brom has seen Marek's name), or change "that night" to "over the midday meal" and let "Five… All right." close the section there. Supply the missing noun ("ribbon- and colour-sellers" or similar). Consider "twelve more of her."
- *Strength to preserve:* Brom closing the program with his hand flat on the cover; Karis reading walking "with Lira steering her round the posts by her elbow."

*Not in the brief, noted for the author's discretion:* trim two of the seven statements of "eight thousand"; vary one of the three "looked at his hands" bout endings; consider one line of reaction from the Halcenvane block when Daeva's head turns, so that the count she makes is felt by the house and not only recorded by Cael.

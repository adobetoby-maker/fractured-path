# Chapter 27 — The Corner Game

On the third day out the road came down off the last of the hill pastures into a broad wet valley, and a column passed them.

They heard it before they saw it: a jingle of good harness somewhere behind the second wagon, and a steady trotting rhythm that did not falter when the road went soft. Then it came round the bend. There were four matched greys pulling a long blue wagon with painted sides, two more wagons behind that, and six riders spaced along the verge at equal intervals, as if somebody had measured them out with a rule that morning. Every horse was the same height, and every rider sat the same way.

The wagoner pulled over to let it by without being asked. There was no question of doing anything else.

"Coast house," said Karis. She was sitting on the second wagon's tailboard with her map of Norhold's provinces folded small on her knee, and she did not need to unfold it. "Look at the colours. And look at their stirrups."

Cael looked. Every rider's stirrup leather had been let down a hole further than an inland rider would have it, so that their legs hung long and straight against the horses' sides.

"Long in the leg." Karis was pleased with them. "Everything on that coast is long in the stride, and they don't know it, so they've bought their saddles to suit."

The column came level. Its fighters were in the painted wagon, a dozen of them sitting in two rows with their coats buttoned to the throat against the wet, and they looked across at Halcenvane's two plain wagons with open interest. They took in the mud on the wheels and the kit roped under canvas, the reserves on the second bench, and Gault's case wedged in among the bags with its handle sticking up. A girl near the back murmured something to the boy beside her, and he laughed, not unkindly.

Then one of the riders dropped back from the head of the column and let the painted wagon go on without her.

She was a lean woman of perhaps fifty, in a dark coat with the coast house's colour at the collar, and she rode alongside the second wagon at a walk for a quarter of a mile. She did not look at the kit, or the reserves, or Gault. She did not look at Lira, though Lira's name stood on every Iron sheet on the continent by now. She looked at Cael, and at nothing else.

It was not a stare. It was the long, unhurried look of a person checking a delivery against its invoice, item by item, before she signs for it. She took in his hands, resting on his knees, and his boots on the tailboard, and the way he sat, and last of all his face, and she gave each of them its proper time. When she had finished, she was sure of something.

She dipped her head to him once, briefly, and touched her heels to her horse and went back up the column at a trot.

"What was that?" Ephram asked, from the bench behind.

"Their coach." Rooke had not turned round. He was up beside the wagoner with his hat tipped over his eyes, and Cael had thought him asleep. "She wanted to see the thing she'd been reading about. Now she's seen it."

"She didn't say a word."

"She nodded." Rooke pushed the hat back an inch with one finger. "Between two people in this trade, that's a long conversation."

Cael watched the column draw away up the valley, the matched greys pulling steadily into the rain, until a fold of the land took it. He found, a little to his surprise, that he did not mind the look. A year ago he would have counted it and filed it and lain awake over it. This one had come from somebody who knew exactly what she was looking at, and had looked at it properly, and then gone back to her business without wanting anything from him except to know.

He thought of Hesk's letter from the slate country and its line about the ones who measure and the ones who read. The coast coach had done neither, quite. She had checked the cargo like a harbour master at a hold, and found it was what the papers said, and let it through.

"I've been inspected," he said.

"You've been weighed." Lira, beside him, had her collar up and her hands tucked into her armpits. "Get used to it. There'll be thirteen more of her."

---

Brom's snowdrops gave out on the third evening, at a waystation with a smoking chimney and a landlord who apologized for the smoke before anybody had complained of it.

They had lasted three days in his buttonhole, which was longer than anybody had thought likely. Brom took them out before supper and looked at them for some time in the palm of his big hand, and then laid them on the windowsill beside his bed, where they could see the road. Ephram opened his mouth, and Lira looked at him, and he closed it again.

By then the road had found its shape. They rose before light, ate standing in the yard with their breath smoking, and were moving by the first bell. Rooke rode up front and slept, or let it be thought that he slept. Seln kept his corner of the first wagon's tailboard with the travel file open on his knees, and at every halt he wrote in it, small and neat, and showed it to nobody. Gault sat with one hand resting on the case. The reserves took turns walking beside the wheels to keep their feet warm, and the Shield reserve kept his note in his coat and added to it whenever he believed nobody was watching him.

In the long flat afternoons Cael worked the Log against his knee and charted what there was to chart, which was mostly other people's traffic. The road grew busier every day. There were carters and drovers, coaches with the mail, a tinker's cart hung with pans that rang like a whole steeple of bells over every rut, and once an entire family moving house, with the grandmother on top of the load and a goat tied on behind. And more and more often, coming up out of the side roads and turning north and east onto the main one, there were other academies.

Not all of them were as grand as the coast house. One was a single cart with five fighters and a coach in it, under a banner that had been mended so many times it was more patch than banner. One was three green wagons with a cook wagon trailing behind, from which a smell of onions drifted back along the road for half a mile. Each of them came onto the road and went the same way, and none of them turned back.

"Everything's going downhill," said Brom on the fourth evening. He was standing at the waystation gate watching another column go past in the dusk. "All toward the one place."

"Water does that," said Karis.

"Water doesn't know where it's going."

"Neither do half of them. They know where the road goes." Karis turned back toward the light of the door. "It isn't the same thing at all."

Gault was the only one of them who had been to Norhold before, and he did not talk about it.

He had been once, a long time ago, in two borrowed farm carts with a wheel lost in a ford, and had come fourth in the Copper on the figures, and every one of them knew the story because he had told it at the confluence over a cup of something he was not used to. Since then he had not said another word about the city. On the fifth evening, when the others had gone in, Cael found him sitting on the bench outside the waystation's kitchen door with the case on his knees and his hands folded on its lid, looking at the road.

"Is it very different?" Cael asked. "From what you remember."

Gault thought about it with the care he gave every question, as though it might later have to be certified.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I was very young, and frightened, and I had straw in my boots. I remember it was large. I remember the floors were better than any I had stood on, and that nobody in the whole city looked at us twice." He turned the case a little on his knees, so that the handle faced the road. "I suspect that part will be different."

"Because of me."

"Because of all of you." Gault considered the handle. "But chiefly, yes. I should think chiefly because of you." He said it without any weight at all, as he might have said that the evening was turning cold, and after a moment he stood up and took the case inside with him, and Cael sat on alone in the dusk until the last green wagon had gone by.

---

On the sixth day they lay at a waystation with a walled yard, and Lira asked for the corner game.

It was the best yard they had found on the road. The wall ran all the way round it, higher than a man, with a long stone trough along the north side and an open shed on the east where the carts were drawn in out of the weather. The ground was beaten clay, old and hard and swept that morning. While the horses were watered Cael tried it with his heel, and then with one short burst from the trough to the shed door, and came down out of the landing beat feeling the floor's answer travel up through his knees.

It was harder than pine and softer than slate. Five a day, he thought, or near enough to five that he would not argue with it.

Lira was watching him from the end of the trough.

"I want the corner game," she said. "Against you."

He had known she would ask. She had been quiet since the first waystation, not unhappy but turned in on something, and twice on the road he had seen her draw a square in the air with one finger and frown at it.

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only person on this road who can watch me build it." She pushed herself away from the trough. "Anybody else, I can get into a corner if I'm quick enough. You I can't, because you see me setting it and you're gone before it shuts. You don't leave faster than me; you leave earlier. It comes to the same thing, and it's the nearest I've got to her."

Cael looked round the yard, and then at Rooke, who was sitting on the edge of the trough with his hat on his knee and a cup in his hand. Rooke looked back at him without any expression whatever, which meant yes.

"How many bursts?"

"Your five. Mine as Rooke's rule allows." She was already scoring the clay with the edge of her heel. "A square, forty feet. The corners are a stride in from two lines. If I touch you while you're in one, it's mine. If you get to the middle, it's yours. Brom counts."

Brom put down the bag he had been carrying, sat on the shed step, and took a spoon out of his pocket.

The others drifted to the wall to watch. Ephram had his file book out. Karis had nothing in her hands at all, which meant she intended to remember every second of it. Seln was at the yard gate with the travel file under his arm, on his way to the stable to see about the horses, and he stopped there and did not go on.

Lira took her line on one side of the square, and Cael took the other.

"Go," said Brom, and rang the spoon on the step.

She came hard and wide on the first breath, a burst along the north line that cut him off from the long side of the square, clean, with a quick hard landing. That put him on the north side with the trough at his back and the corner a few strides to his left, and one more burst along the short side would shut it with him inside.

He watched her hips.

He did not have to think about it. The read had been doing it on the oak since long before it had a name. Her weight came down into her right foot and settled there, and her hip turned a quarter of an inch toward the shed. Every Wind fighter he had ever seen did something of the kind before a burst, a drawing-together of the body to throw it, and Lira's was the quickest he knew; but it was still a gathering, and the read was still a fraction ahead of it.

So he left before she went. He took one step and then a burst of his own, low, along the very line she was about to close, and by the time she landed on the short side he was past the place where the corner would have been and walking into the middle of the square.

Brom rang the spoon.

"You saw me," said Lira.

"I saw your hip."

"I know you saw my hip." She walked back to her line, breathing quickly. "I'm asking what else."

"Nothing else. That was enough."

She stood on her line for a moment looking down at the clay. Then she nodded once, and Cael saw her do something to her stance that he could not quite put a name to, a settling lower through the knees.

"Again," she said.

---

The second time, she hid the gather.

He could see her doing it, which was the strange part. She had been working at it since the mill town, since Karis spent her left-hand tell in front of a crowd and she promised to fix it. All through the cold term she had drilled on the far end of the oak, alone, folding the gather for each burst down into the landing of the one before. She did it now, and did it well. She came out of her first burst already set for the second, and there was no moment of settling for the read to find, only a small hard landing and then she was gone again.

She was very good. It was not enough.

The first burst put her down on the north line again, three strides from the trough, and after that the read did not need her hips at all, because where she had landed told him where she meant to go. There was only one corner on that side worth shutting and only one line she could shut it along, and her feet were already turned toward it. The floor she was making had said it for her.

"North-east," said Cael, aloud, and stepped out of it.

He was clear before she had finished her second burst. She came down where he had been standing and stopped, and stood there with her hands on her knees.

On the wall, the Stone reserve laughed, and then stopped at once.

"That's not fair," Lira told the clay.

"It's what she'll do." Cael came back to the middle and stood beside her. "You hid the gather, and I didn't need it. Where you land says where you're going."

"Then I can't win." She straightened. Her face had gone very still, which in Lira was worse than temper. "If I hide the set, you read the floor. If I don't build the corner, there isn't a corner. So I can't win."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't need to." She looked past him at the trough, at the shed, at the four lines in the clay. "Zerin doesn't need a read. Zerin just goes, and she's there before I've finished going. You've got a warning and she's got legs, and it's the same problem from opposite ends." She was talking faster now, more to herself than to him. "So the corner can't be a thing I build, because anything I build, a quick enough person gets out of. It has to be there before I build it."

She stopped.

Cael waited. He had learned long ago that this was the part of Lira's thinking that should never be interrupted. From the outside it looked like nothing, a girl standing in a muddy yard with her hands on her hips, and it was the most important thing she did.

"Brom." She did not turn round. "How many has he spent?"

"Two."

"And I've spent four." She went back to her line. "Again."

This time she did not come at him.

She burst once, short, to a spot on the east side that was nowhere near a corner and nowhere near him. It looked like a mistake. The read gave him her weight and her direction, and both were wrong for any corner he could see, and so he did not move.

Then he looked at where she was standing, and understood that she was standing in his road.

If he went east now, to the middle, he would have to go round her, and going round her would cost him a burst. North, the trough was in his way. The only cheap road out of where he stood ran south-west, along the line toward the gate, and he took it at a walk because it was free.

Lira moved again. It was not a burst this time but three quick steps, and she stood on the south-west line a little ahead of him, and now that road cost a burst as well. The free road had become north-west, back the way he had come and toward the wall.

He saw it then, or the start of it. She was not building him a corner. She was charging him for every road that did not end in one.

He spent a burst to go east round her, because east was still the best road to the middle. She had expected that and was already there, a stride off his landing, so he spent another to get past her, and came down out of the landing beat on the far side looking for the middle of the square. He found the shed in front of him and the east wall at his right shoulder.

He was in the corner. He had bought his own way into it, one fair price at a time, and at no point had he been able to see it being built, because nobody had built it. It had been paid for.

Lira's hand came down flat on his shoulder.

Brom rang the spoon, and then, after a moment, rang it again for no reason anybody could see.

For a little while nobody on the wall said anything. Then Rooke set his cup down on the edge of the trough.

"Early," he said.

That was all. He picked up the cup and went into the waystation, and the door swung shut behind him on its leather hinge.

---

Lira stood with her hand still on Cael's shoulder, breathing hard and staring at the place where Rooke had been. Then she took the hand away and turned it over and looked at it, as though it belonged to somebody else and had done something clever without asking her.

"Early," she repeated. "He means I taxed the roads before you needed them." She turned to Cael. "You couldn't see it."

"No."

"Why not? You see everything."

He thought about it honestly, because she would know if he did not.

"I see weight," he said at last. "Where it's going and how hard it's going there. A corner that's being built is all weight, because you have to put your body where the walls are going to be. That wasn't a wall. You stood in my roads and made them dear, and there's nothing in anybody's hips that says what a road costs." He rubbed the shoulder where her hand had been. "I can read you building. I can't read you pricing."

Lira was quiet for a moment. Then she found a stub of pencil in her coat and wrote something small on the back of her left hand. He did not need to read it.

"It works on you because you pay for your bursts," she said slowly. "You've got five, and you count them, and I can make you spend them. I don't know what she pays." She looked down at the hand. "Nobody has ever made her pay for anything. It's not in the times."

"Then you'll find out."

"In the first exchange. I know. I know." But she was very nearly smiling.

They ran it four more times before the light went, and she won three of them. He ran those on his feet alone, with one burst left that he meant to keep. The one she lost was the last, when Cael stopped trying to reach the middle at all and simply stood still in the open, a little off centre, and let her come. She could not price a road he did not mean to take. She stood ten feet from him for most of a minute with her hands on her hips, and then laughed and called it cheating, and said that Zerin would never do it, because Zerin had never in her life stood still.

"You don't know that," Karis pointed out from the wall.

"I know it from the times. Nobody with those intervals has stood still since she was twelve."

Karis came down off the wall then, which she did not usually do until a thing was finished, and walked out onto the clay to the place where the third game had ended. She stood in the corner by the shed and looked back across the square along the roads Lira had closed, one after another, reading them from the bottom up like lines on a page.

"You looked at his roads before you looked at him," she said. "Every time it worked. Every time it didn't, you looked at him first." She turned round. "I've never seen you do that. You've always fought the person."

"I fought the person all season," said Lira. "It was lovely. It doesn't work on her."

"No." Karis wrote nothing down, but Cael saw her put it away somewhere behind her eyes, in the place where she kept other people's methods, filed and squared. "No, I don't suppose it will."

By then Cael had spent four of his five. The fourth had landed hard, the clay coming up through his heels into the leading hip, and the old line ran from the crest of the hip to the inside of the knee for a few minutes afterward, which was the floor's ordinary bill and nothing more. He let it run and kept the fifth. The right shoulder had not been asked for anything all day. The other thing, the one that stood on no record anywhere, lay quiet where it always lay, costing what it always cost from the moment he woke, whether he spent it or not, and asking nothing further.

At supper Lira ate with her left hand flat on the table beside her plate, and every so often she glanced down at it. Ephram bore this for as long as he could.

"What does it say?"

Lira turned the hand over and showed him. *Early,* in pencil, already smudged across the knuckles.

"Is that a lesson or a complaint?"

"Both. Pass the bread."

Brom passed it. Then he spoke, slowly, in the manner of a man who has been turning a thing over since the yard and has only now reached its underside. "She'll pay it, though. Zerin. Whoever coached her will have taught her to pay for things early. That's what a program does. It teaches you to settle your bills before anybody sends them."

The table went quiet.

"I know." Lira tore her bread in half. "So it'll work for a while, and then she'll learn it, the way I just did, and then I'll need the next thing." She looked round at all of them. "That's all right. I'd rather be beaten by somebody learning than by somebody who already knew."

Cael wrote it that night by the waystation's one good lamp, with the yard dark outside the shutter and the lines in the clay still faintly showing under the stars.

*Sixth day. Lira's corner game in a walled yard. Two fails, and then the version that works: she doesn't build the corner, she prices it. She stands in every road out until every road costs a burst, and lets me buy my own way in. I couldn't see it. The read sees weight; it doesn't see price. Rooke said one word, "Early," and went in to his supper.*

*Four bursts of five on clay. Hip's usual. Nothing else asked for.*

*She thinks it will work on Zerin for a while and then stop working, because Zerin will learn it. She's probably right, and she was glad anyway. I have never once seen her afraid of losing to somebody better. Only of losing to the paper.*

On the ninth day there was a flag.

It stood on a tall pole in the yard of a waystation at a crossroads, where the north road met another coming in from the west. The waystation was the largest they had stopped at, with two courtyards and a smithy, and a board by the gate listing the coaches that called and when. Above all of it, at the top of the pole, a banner turned slowly in a wind that was not quite strong enough to hold it out.

It was silver on grey: a plain silver bar across a grey field, with no device and no lettering, and the grey worn at its edges to something closer to white.

Everybody in both wagons looked up at it at the same moment. Nobody had told them what it was, and nobody needed to.

"That's a week out," said Karis slowly. She had the map open now across both knees, and her finger was on the crossroads. "We're a full week from the city. More."

The keeper came out to see to the horses. He was a cheerful bald man with a smith's forearms, and when Lira asked him about the flag he squinted up at it with great affection, as at an old acquaintance who called once in a long while and always paid.

"The marshals put it up. Every cycle. They come down the roads a month ahead in a cart and put one up at every crossroads a week out, and they take them all down again when it's over." He shaded his eyes. "That's the same one as last time, and the time before. I've had this house four cycles and it's never once been a new one."

"Who pays for it?"

He looked startled that anybody would ask. "The tournament. Who else? It's their road while it's on."

Cael sat on the tailboard and looked at the flag for a long while after the man had gone back to his forge.

He had always thought of the tournament as a place, a city with a floor in it. He had imagined fourteen delegations travelling to it the way they travelled to a meet, from the outside, and arriving at its gate. But here was its colour on a pole a week's drive from its walls, put up by its own people at a crossroads that belonged to nobody in particular, and here was a waystation keeper who thought it the most natural thing in the world that the road itself should belong to the tournament for a month. It did not wait for anybody to arrive. It came out a week down every road to meet them, and took the road over quietly, with a cart and a ladder.

"Its writ runs on the roads," he said.

Karis glanced at him and then up at the bar of silver. "Every road in. They always have." She folded the map along its old creases. "It's an old charter, and old charters don't stop at walls. They stop where somebody stopped writing."

Seln had come round from the stable with the travel file under his arm. He stopped beside the wagon and looked up at the pole just long enough to read it, and then made a small mark in the file, and Cael, who could see the page upside down, watched him write the crossroads, the day and the hour in his narrow upright hand.

"You're logging the flag," said Lira.

"The office logs the road." Seln closed the file. "The road now has a flag on it. It would be an odd record that left it out."

He went on toward the first wagon. Lira watched him go with her head on one side.

"He's never once told me a thing I wanted to know," she said, "and every time he speaks, I find I'm glad he did. I don't understand how he manages it."

"Practice," said Karis, without looking up from her map.

At the afternoon halt Cael took Hesk's travelling clock out of his kit and set it forward an hour, as he did in every tournament week. The tournament was still nine days off, and the clock had been wrong in the ordinary direction since he first owned it, but it seemed to him that the tournament's weeks had begun at that pole.

*Ninth day. Silver on grey on a crossroads pole, a week out from the walls. The keeper has seen the same flag four times and thinks the road belongs to the tournament for a month every cycle, and nobody on the road argues with him. I set Hesk's clock an hour fast this afternoon. It's wrong in the usual direction, and it'll be right by accident at the city, the same as always.*

*I thought we were driving toward a measure. We've been inside it since the crossroads.*

# Chapter 28 — The Prospectus Was Counting People

Withrow caught them up on the twelfth evening, at a waystation on a river bend where the road crossed by a stone bridge of five arches, and she was enjoying herself.

Cael saw it before she was out of the carriage. The hired carriage came over the bridge at a smart trot in the last of the light, with Bracken's face at one window and a pile of document boxes roped on the roof, and it pulled into the yard with a flourish the driver had plainly been saving for a week.

The chancellor got down before anybody could reach the door to open it for her.

She stood in the mud in her dark coat and looked round the yard, at the two plain wagons, the horses being rubbed down, the delegation coming out of the kitchen door with their supper still in their hands, and she smiled.

It was not a large smile. It was the smile of a woman who has been worrying for a very long time, and has finished, and has found that there is nothing left to do now but find out.

"Good evening," she said. "Is there any of that left?"

There was. She ate it at the long table with the rest of them, from the same pot, with Bracken on her left and Rooke on her right, and she asked about the road.

She wanted to know about everything. She asked about the coast house's coach and the green wagons with the onions, and the flag at the crossroads, and the walled yard. When Lira told her about the corner game, she put down her spoon and listened all the way through, and asked two questions at the end that showed she had understood every step of it.

Cael had never seen her away from the bluff.

On the hill she was the house, the way the long wall was the house, or the bell; she moved through it at its own pace and was part of its furniture. Here she seemed smaller and quicker, and a good deal younger. She laughed once at something Ephram said, a short surprised laugh that made Ephram go pink to the ears and stay that way for the rest of the meal.

Bracken ate steadily and said very little. He had the year's last administrative pouch on the bench beside him, and once in a while he put a hand on it to make sure it was still there.

When Brom asked him how he had found the faster road, he thought about it seriously.

"Fast," he said at last. "I slept like a quarry stone. The chancellor read the whole way, by daylight and then by a lamp, and I don't believe she slept at all."

"I didn't need to," said Withrow. "I'd already done all my not sleeping. I did it over the last eighteen years, a little at a time, so as not to have to do it now."

When the pot was empty she stood up, and the table went quiet without being asked.

"I'm not going to make a speech," she said. "I've made enough of them this year to last the house a generation, and most of you have heard every one. I only want to say one thing, and then I'll go to bed, because Bracken is right that I have not slept."

She looked down the table, at each of them in turn. She took the reserves in, and Gault, and Seln at the far end with his file, and Rooke, and then the five.

"Once we're through that city's gate, nobody there will see a house on a hill," she said. "They'll see you. Eight thousand people will sit round that floor, and not one in a hundred of them will ever climb our road or read our charter or eat in our hall. That isn't a weight I'm handing you; it's only how cities are. I've watched you all year. Go on exactly as you are." She picked up her cup. "The tournament will measure the five of you. The continent will measure all of us. Good night."

She went up the stair. Bracken gathered the pouch and followed her, and after a moment Rooke followed him, and the table was left looking at the place where she had stood.

"She means it," said Karis quietly.

"She means all of it," said Lira. "That's what's so alarming about her."

Cael wrote it down later that night, in the room he shared with Brom and Ephram, with Ephram already asleep and Brom pretending to be.

He wrote her sentence first, exactly. Then he sat for some time with the pen over the page, working out what she had meant by it. With Withrow there was always a second thing under the first, and the second was usually the one that mattered.

Fourteen houses would walk onto that floor. Most of them would be measured on the strength of their brackets and their rosters, on deep benches and long programs and champions held in reserve.

Halcenvane had none of those things. It had brought a Wind girl that another house had thrown away, and a big quiet Copper that the paper priced at half his weight.

It had brought a researcher who read charters in her sleep, and a Blade who had become a captain this year without noticing, and a boy the registry could not name. It had brought, in the end, a list of people the paper had got wrong.

And Withrow was proud of that list. She had not chosen it in spite of the paper. She had built a whole house on the idea that the paper was wrong about people, and she had chosen it because of that, and the city was going to be the examination.

*Twelfth day. The chancellor came up by the faster road and she's happy. I've never seen it before. It makes her look about ten years younger and twice as dangerous.*

*She said the tournament will measure the five of us and the continent all of us.*

*It's the second half of it that's hers. Eighteen years of building, and the whole house rests on one claim: that the paper is wrong about people. Every person on our roster is an instance.*

*We are not her argument because we're better than the paper says. We're her argument because we're different from it, in five directions, and she means to put the five directions in front of the largest audience on the continent and let them look.*

*I'm the sharpest instance on the list. I'm also the least representative, and I think she knows that too, and has decided it doesn't matter.*

He blotted it and sat looking at the page.

"You're thinking very loudly," said Brom, from the dark.

"Sorry."

"I didn't say stop." The bed creaked as Brom turned over. "She shook all our hands at the gate. Did you notice who was last?"

"Seln."

"Seln." Brom was quiet a moment. "She didn't have to do that. Nobody would have minded if she'd stopped at Gault." Another creak. "She's building something. I don't know what it is, but I know what building looks like. My grandmother used to stand in a field like that, looking at it, before she'd decided where the barn went."

Cael turned down the lamp.

On the fourteenth day the road began to sort itself.

It happened slowly, over a morning, and for a long time none of them noticed it. The road had been busy for a week. For the last two days it had been crowded, with three lanes of traffic where there was room for one, the waystations full by noon and the late arrivals sleeping in their wagons in the yards.

Then, somewhere in the middle of the fourteenth morning, the crowd changed its shape.

The freight moved over. Nobody told it to. There were no marshals on that stretch of road, no flags, no officers on horseback.

But the long ox-drawn wagons of timber and grain and stone, which had held the crown of the road for a week by sheer weight, began one after another to draw onto the verge side and let the academy columns by.

A carter would look back over his shoulder, see colours coming, and edge his team across without a word. A coach with the mail held back at a narrow bridge to let a delegation's wagons cross first.

By noon the whole road had arranged itself into two streams. The heavy traffic ground along on the left, and the delegations moved freely up the middle past timber and grain and stone and the patient heads of oxen, and it held that way mile after mile, as though somebody had drawn it up in an order and posted it at every waystation.

Ephram stood up on the second wagon's bench to see better. "Who told them?"

"Nobody," said Rooke. "Every cycle. It's been happening on this road since before I ever drove it."

"Why do they do it?"

Rooke shrugged. "Because they did it last time."

Karis had been watching the whole thing from the tailboard, with her map shut on her knee and her eyes going from the carters to the columns and back. Now she put the map away inside her coat.

"The city starts here," she said. "Everything from now on is just the city getting closer together."

Cael looked at the two streams of traffic going north together, the one giving way to the other, and nobody anywhere making them.

A little further on, at a ford, a big timber wagon had gone in too deep and stuck, and the delegation columns stopped behind it without anybody's ordering them to. For the better part of an hour the green wagons' fighters and the coast house's riders stood up to their knees in cold water with their shoulders against the timber, and Brom and both reserves with them. The carter apologized to every one of them in a dialect nobody could follow.

When it came free, the carter drew straight over onto the verge side and waited, hat in hand, for every column to go by him before he went on.

Cael had spent the year among rules. He had watched a registrar certify a list and a charter put him on a roster, and a whole circuit shift around one old sentence that nobody had remembered writing. He had learned how much a written rule could hold.

But here was a rule that nobody had written, and that nobody enforced, holding a hundred carters to the side of a road on a cold bright morning a long way from any office.

It held because everybody on the road expected it to. Somebody had done it once, and somebody else had done it the cycle after, and now it was the road.

He wrote it down at the next halt in four lines, and the last of them said: *Customs don't need a clerk. That's what makes them so hard to argue with.*

---

On the seventeenth afternoon the road topped its last rise, and Norhold was simply there, the whole of it in one moment.

The road had been climbing since the morning through farmland and orchards and small grey towns that ran one into the next without much space between them. The traffic was thick and slow. Cael had been working the Log against his knee and had not looked up for some time.

Then the wagon came over the crest of the hill and stopped.

The wagoner had not been told to stop. He had simply pulled up, and so had the driver of the second wagon behind him, and so had the carter alongside, and a coach behind that.

For the length of a long breath the whole road at the top of the rise stood still, every face on it turned the same way.

The land fell away in front of them to a wide river plain, and the plain was full of city.

Cael had seen cities. He had lived in Ardenmere, which had been the largest place in his life until this moment, and he had walked Ostrand's market and Greyvane's terraces and the confluence's three-deep quays. None of them had prepared him.

This was not a town that had grown large. It was a place where four great roads came down out of the hills and met a river wide enough for sea barges, and something had been building on that meeting for a very long time.

There were three walls. He could see all three from the rise, one inside another, each of a different stone and a different age. The outermost was pale and so long that its far end was not wall any more but simply part of the country. There were gate towers along it with flags hung down their whole height.

Inside it lay a river harbour, with masts showing over the rooftops in a grey thicket and a line of great timber lock gates where the river came in.

And in the middle of everything, rising out of the city's heart in tier on tier, flagged and roofed and catching the late sun on a hundred faces of stone, was the thing on Karis's map that the map had called the Concourse.

"That's not a building," said Ephram.

Nobody answered him. Cael was counting, because counting was what he did when something was too large to take in any other way. He could make out separate halls, joined by roofed galleries and stepped tiers into one structure. There were seven, eight; perhaps eleven.

Two tall towers stood at either side of the largest, east and west, paired like gateposts.

He knew from Rooke's briefing that the main floor alone sat eight thousand. Looking at it from the rise, he believed it, and believed too that the figure was modest.

He tried to fit Ostrand inside the outer wall and found that it went in comfortably, with room left over for its fields. He tried Ardenmere and found it would have made a decent district, with a market and two good streets.

"Four times Ostrand," said Karis slowly. She had her notebook out and was not writing in it. "The prospectus said four times Ostrand." She looked at the city for a long time. "The prospectus was counting people. It wasn't counting that."

Brom had stood up in the wagon to see, and stayed standing, one hand on the canvas hoop. He looked at the city for a long time with his face quite still. Then he said, slowly, "My whole village would go in one of those towers. With the cows."

"With the cows on the upper floors," said Ephram.

"They'd like it," said Brom seriously. "There'd be a view."

Gault had not moved from his place by the case. He was looking past all of them at the Concourse with an expression Cael had never seen on him, part wonder and part something that looked very much like dread.

"It's bigger," he said at last, to nobody. "I'd have sworn it was big enough last time." He put his hand on the case's handle. "I suppose they've added to it. Or I've shrunk."

Lira said nothing at all.

Cael noticed it, because Lira always had something to say about a new place, and usually something rude.

She sat on the tailboard with her hands loose in her lap and watched the city, and her face had gone quiet in a way he had seen only a few times.

Later, at the foot of the rise, when the wagons had started again and the city had dropped behind the nearer roofs, he asked her.

"Fenmark used to talk about it," she said. "The older girls who'd been. They'd tell it at supper, the year before a cycle, how big it was and how the floors rang and how the crowds sounded. Every year they told it, and every year it got bigger." She was looking at the road ahead.

"The year they sent me home, I stopped hearing it. I put it out of my head on purpose, because I thought if I ever got here I'd find out they'd made it all up, and I didn't want to have been the sort of girl who believed them."

"And?"

"It's bigger than they said." She nearly smiled. "Good. I couldn't have stood it if they'd got it right."

They lay that night at a crowded inn under the outer wall, five to a room, and entered by the north gate at midmorning on the eighteenth day.

The gate was a passage through the wall longer than the covered walk at home, with a vaulted roof and lamps burning in it at midday.

There was a queue. Delegations waited their turn in it with their wagons nose to tail, and tournament marshals in grey coats with a silver bar at the collar walked down the line checking papers and giving out lettered tallies.

They worked quickly, without fuss, as people work at a task they have done every three years for longer than any of them has been alive and have long since stopped finding remarkable.

Inside the gate there was a board.

It stood in a square just beyond the passage, under a little roof of its own, and it was painted fresh: fourteen panels, each with a house's name and its colours. Some of the panels already had a chalk tick beside them. Cael read them from the wagon as it came out into the light.

There was Auremont's deep blue and silver, and Rhagen's black and a cold glass grey. There was a russet he did not know until Karis said "Ternhall" beside him, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

There were the coast house's colours, already ticked, and a green he recognized from the onion wagons. There was a lake house with no tick, which Karis said was the one on the recount. And there, eleventh from the top, in letters no larger than anybody else's, was Halcenvane.

A marshal chalked a tick beside it while they watched.

Withrow had come up from the carriage behind to stand at the second wagon's wheel and see it done. She stood with her hands in the pockets of her dark coat and looked at the panel with Halcenvane's name on it, eleventh of fourteen, for longer than a panel of painted wood required. She did not say anything. When the marshal had moved on to the next tally, she reached up and laid one gloved hand flat on the wagon's side for a moment, like a rider laying a hand on a horse that has brought her a long way. Then she went back to her carriage.

Bracken, at her elbow, had his pocket-book out. He wrote something in it, briefly, and closed it, and Cael would have given a good deal to know what a registrar wrote on the day his house was ticked onto the continent's board.

The road from the gate ran straight into the city as a wide processional way, between tall houses with shops on their ground floors.

It was hung all the way along with banners, the fourteen houses' colours strung across the street on lines, and between every house's colours the same silver bar on grey that had flown at the crossroads. Crowds stood along the way.

They were not waiting for anybody in particular. They were simply watching the delegations come in, as people in a harbour town watch the ships, and talking about each one as it passed, knowledgeably, with their arms folded.

A man near the second wagon's wheel had a printed list in one hand and a pie in the other, and he was explaining the delegations to his neighbour as they passed, with his mouth full. He did the coast house, and the green wagons, and a southern house in yellow, and he gave each of them a sentence and a placing.

Then the plain wagons came level with him and he read the colours off his list and stopped chewing.

"That's the clause house," he said. "Halcenvane. The one with the boy."

His neighbour craned. "Which is the boy?"

The man looked along the tailboard, from Lira to Brom to Karis to Cael and back again, and could not decide, and Cael saw him try to fit each of their faces to something he had read and fail. In the end he shrugged and took another bite of the pie.

"Second round for the lot of them," he said, through it. "Bar the Wind girl. And the boy doesn't count, because he's not in anything."

Lira, on the tailboard, smiled at him so sweetly that he dropped his pie.

At the far end of the processional way, high over the roofs, stood the Concourse, and on its nearest face was a gate.

It was a great arch between two square towers, and from the top of it hung a banner.

It was too far off for detail. It hung straight down in the still air, long and heavy, silver on grey, and it was older than the gate it hung from. Cael could see that even from a quarter of a mile away; the grey had the colour of old pewter.

It was the same device as the crossroads flag, and nothing like it at all. The crossroads flag had been a signpost. This was where the signpost led.

Rooke answered before anybody had asked. "That's the champion's gate. It hangs there from the end of the last cycle until somebody earns the right to take it down at the end of this one. The charter says so."

Cael looked at it for a long time as the wagons crawled up the processional way. Somewhere in that building was the other thing with the same name, the reference profile in its sealed case. He thought about the two of them, the cloth outside and the tables within, and how the city had hung the one where everybody could see it.

He wrote it out that night, in a room at the top of a guesting-house, with the noise of the processional way still coming up through the shutters.

*Two things in that building carry the same name. One is a set of tables in a sealed case that nobody may touch for the length of a cycle. The other is a strip of old cloth hung where the whole city can see it, over the gate the last winners walked out of.*

*They've put the honour on the outside and the measure on the inside, and given them one name between them, so that nobody ever has to choose which of the two to love.*

*Every argument I've had this year has been with one piece of this at a time, a clerk here and a clause there. Here the whole of it stands in one place, out in the weather.*

*This city is the system with the roof off.*

---

The delegation quarter lay on the river side of the Concourse, a district of tall narrow guesting-houses with balconies over the street, and Halcenvane had the third floor of one of them.

It shared the house with two smaller academies, a hill house on the floor below and a southern house above. It also shared it with a keeper, who stood at the foot of the stair and counted every guest who went up it aloud, in a soft voice, like a shepherd counting sheep through a gate.

He counted the reserves twice. When Brom went past him he stopped counting for a moment, looked up, and said, "Well," and then went on.

Seln took the delegation's papers to the registration hall within the hour.

He went alone, with the travel file under his arm and the roster in a folder of its own, and he was back before Rooke had finished arguing with the keeper about the beds. He had the credentials for all eleven of them, threaded on a cord. He had the floor schedules and the warm-up allotments for the whole of the first week, and a printed map of the Concourse with the delegation's rooms marked on it in ink.

He laid them on the table in the front room in the order they would be needed, and squared them.

"That was quick," said Rooke.

"The queue was arranged by the names of the houses, in the order of the alphabet," said Seln. "The hall was not. One only had to find the right clerk." He took a pencil out of his coat and corrected something on the map. "The hill house's clerk is still in the queue. I believe he'll be there until the lamps."

He was. At dusk the hill house's clerk came up the stair looking grey and beaten, with half his papers. He stopped on the landing outside Halcenvane's door and looked in at Seln, who was sitting at the end of the table with a cup of tea, writing in the travel file.

Then he went on up the stair without a word, and Cael heard Ephram, in the corner, laugh quietly into his sleeve.

That evening Cael stood at the window of the room at the top of the house and counted the watchers.

He did not decide to. It was a habit, the oldest one he had, older than the read and older than the Log.

In every town for as long as he could remember he had found the coats on his first evening, the patient men on corners and in doorways, and counted them, and known where they stood.

They had stayed behind at Ostrand this time, two and two on the bell. Karis had written as much in her marbled log on the morning of the bridge. But the habit did not know that, and he stood at the window and counted.

There was a man on the balcony opposite with a notebook, watching the courtyard below. Three scouts in a hill house's colours were sitting at a table outside a cookshop, looking at every door in the street in turn.

There was a gallery over a warm-up floor across the way, with paying watchers on its benches, and a boy selling printed sheets at the corner, who knew the colour of every house that went by.

A marshal walked the street with a tally. Behind the shutters of the house next door a woman was writing, and every few lines she raised her head and looked across at the windows of the hill house's floor.

He counted twenty and lost the count, began again and lost it at thirty.

None of them was looking at him. All of them were looking at everybody, all the time. Every delegation in the city was watched from every window, by scouts and printers and gamblers and its own neighbours. The whole city had been watching fighters come in for longer than anybody could count, and it saw no reason to stop now.

Somewhere in it, perhaps, there was a pair of coats sent for him. If there was, they were two drops in a river.

He turned from the window. Karis was sitting on the end of the bed with the marbled log open on her knee. She had written the time.

Under it, in the column for the number, she had written nothing yet, and she was looking at the empty space with her pencil held above it.

"How many?" he said.

She thought about it. Then she wrote, in her square hand, two words, and showed him: *All of them.*

*Eighteenth day,* he wrote, later. *I counted out of habit and the count wouldn't close. Every house in this city is watched by every window in it.*

*For longer than I like to think about, no town I walked into had anybody in it watched harder than me. Tonight, by plain arithmetic, I'm one fighter among a few hundred, under a few thousand eyes, and nobody's in particular.*

*It's the first town of my life where nobody is looking at me. Being watched by everyone is the nearest thing to privacy this city has for sale.*

# Chapter 29 — Pre-Sold

The first of them was a boy of about twelve, and he found Cael in the guesting-house courtyard on the second morning, before breakfast.

The courtyard was a narrow well of stone with a pump in one corner and a fig tree in a tub, and Cael had come down to wash at the pump because the house's one basin was occupied by the hill house's whole delegation at once. He had his sleeves rolled and his face wet when the boy walked in through the street door as if he lived there, stopped two yards away, and looked at him with total frankness.

"Are you the unclassified one?"

There was no point in being anything but honest with that face. "Yes."

The boy nodded, satisfied. He reached inside his jacket and brought out a folded sheet of thick cheap paper, and unfolded it with great care, and held it up.

It was a printed likeness. A woodcut face filled most of the sheet, framed in heavy black lines, under a title in tall letters: *THE UNNAMED OF HALCENVANE.* The face was a young man's, square-jawed and serious, with dark brows drawn together and a stare that looked off past the reader's shoulder at something terrible on the horizon. It resembled Cael about as much as a description in a letter resembles the person it describes. The hair was right. Nothing else was.

"Will you put your mark on it?" said the boy.

Cael looked at the woodcut for a while, and then at the boy, who was holding out a stub of pencil as well, having come prepared.

He had never been asked for his mark on anything except a form. He considered it as he would have considered any new procedure. Was there harm in it? Did it commit him to anything? Would it go into a record somewhere? He could find no harm, and no commitment, and no record that mattered, and the boy was waiting with the pencil held out and his whole face set on the answer.

He dried his hand on his shirt, took the pencil, and wrote *Cael* at the bottom of the sheet, under the title, small and plain.

The boy turned the sheet round and studied the mark for some time, with his lips pursed, as a man studies a coin he has been handed in change.

"That's better than the face," he said at last, and folded the sheet back into his jacket and ran out of the street door.

Cael stood by the pump with water running off his chin.

"You've been collected," said Lira, from the gallery above. She had been leaning on the rail the whole time, eating an apple. "How does it feel?"

"Like a form," said Cael. "With a worse picture."

But he thought about it afterward, drying his face on the gallery stair, longer than a joke deserved. All year he had been something that other people wrote down. Clerks had written him on rosters and judges on slates, compilers in their files, and the registry in a single bracketed word. Every one of them had put him on paper for a purpose of their own, and he had never once been asked whether he would like to add anything. The boy had asked. It was a cheap printed face that looked nothing like him, but at the bottom of it, for the first time, there was a mark in his own hand that said he had been there.

He found that he did not mind that at all.

It went on like that all day.

At the warm-up floor across the street, where the house had its first allotted hour, a knot of fighters from two delegations Cael did not know stopped talking as he came in, and did not start again until he had crossed the floor and gone out of the far door. At midday a Concourse steward at the gallery gate checked his credential against her printed list, and then checked it a second time. She was not doubtful of it. She plainly wanted to be able to say, afterward, that she had checked it herself. In the street a woman selling hot bread looked at him and then at a sheet pinned to the side of her barrow, and then at him again, and gave him a roll for nothing and would not take his copper.

By the evening the delegation had changed its shape around him, and nobody had said a word about it.

Brom walked at his outside shoulder now whenever they were in a crowd, a step behind and a little to the street side, where a big quiet man takes up a great deal of the view. Lira, when anybody stopped them, began talking first and kept talking, cheerfully, about the weather and the floors and the price of bread, until whoever it was had forgotten what they had meant to ask. Karis walked behind with her notebook and wrote down every person who stared, by type and time, and at supper announced that there had been forty-one, and that the commonest kind was a man of middle years with a printed sheet in his hand.

It was the old floor-craft of the five of them, the way they had always fought around each other, worked out again on a new floor made of streets.

Rooke saw it at supper and said nothing about it, which from Rooke was approval. Gault saw it too. He watched Brom drift to Cael's outside shoulder as they came in from the street, and Lira step in front of a man who had half risen from his bench with a sheet in his hand, and he leaned across to Cael while the soup was being put down.

"When I was here," he said, "I could have walked down the processional way in my nightshirt and nobody would have looked up from their pie." He considered that. "I didn't, of course. But I could have." He took up his spoon. "I find I don't envy you as much as I thought I would. Do you mind it?"

Cael thought about the boy at the pump, and the woman with the bread, and the steward checking his credential twice so that she could say she had.

"Not yet," he said. "Ask me in a week."

"I shall," said Gault, who always did exactly what he said he would.

He dealt with the likenesses that evening, because they were a thing he did not understand, and things he did not understand had a way of costing him later.

The sheets were sold all along the processional way. There were four shops on the street with them pinned in the windows, between the bout cards and the ribbons in the fourteen houses' colours, and there were two barrows in the Concourse's outer court with sheets clipped to strings along their sides like washing. Cael went to the busier barrow. It was kept by a stout man with a leather apron and a loud voice, who was crying his wares to the whole court in a long chant that named every fighter on his strings.

Cael stood at the barrow and looked along the sheets for some time. Then he took out his purse.

"One of each," he said. "Every one with a Halcenvane name on it. And that one." He pointed.

The barrow-man began unclipping sheets, still chanting over his shoulder at the court. He got as far as the third, and glanced at his customer to ask about the fourth, and then he stopped chanting.

He looked at Cael for perhaps a breath. Then he looked down at the sheet in his hand, which was the one with the square jaw. Then, without a word, and with an air of tremendous self-command, he went on unclipping.

He said nothing at all while he folded them. He said nothing when he took the money, two coppers a sheet, counted exactly into his palm. When Cael had the sheets under his arm and was turning away, the barrow-man cleared his throat, leaned across the barrow, and said in an altogether different voice, low and confidential: "The sinister one sells better. I'm only telling you."

Then he took up his chant again, louder than before.

There were six of them in all. Cael laid them out on the long table in the front room after supper, in a row, and the delegation came and stood round them.

There were two sheets of him from the same printing house. Both were called *THE UNNAMED OF HALCENVANE*, and the same woodcut of the face did for both. In the first the background was a blaze of sunbeams and the young man was heroic, with his chin up. In the second the background was solid black and the same young man looked out of it like somebody waiting in an alley. There was a third, cruder, on cheaper paper, called *THE BOY THE REGISTRY COULD NOT SORT*, which showed a figure in a fighting stance surrounded by question marks.

There was one of Lira, called *LIRA OF THE BLUFF*. It made her a head taller than she was and very much angrier, with her hair streaming and her fists clenched, against a background of lightning.

There was one of a girl in Auremont's colours, called *DAEVA OF AUREMONT*. There was hardly anything left over for the face, which was a pale oval in the middle of the storm with two dots for eyes. Cael looked at it for a long time, and found that it told him nothing whatever, and put it aside.

And there was the sixth, the newest, the ink still smelling. It was called *THE FIVE FROM THE BLUFF*.

It was the whole delegation, in a group, as the woodcutter had seen them walk past his window. It was not a bad likeness of any of them, taken all together. Ephram was tall and thin and looked as though he was about to explain something. Karis was small, with a notebook. Lira was in front, of course. Brom was at the back, enormous, and the woodcutter had given up on him as a person and carved him as a sort of hill, with a head on top.

And at the edge of the group, inside it and not outside, there was a sixth figure.

It was a lean man in a long coat with a ledger under his arm. The woodcutter had put him at the end of the line, standing with the five and a little apart from them, but plainly one of them. It was the same distance he kept from them at every table. And he was there, on the sheet, among them.

Cael had read the compilers' files on the five of them, all four pages of Brom's and every shop's careful guess at his own. None of them had Seln in it. "That is not my scowl," she said. "That is somebody else's scowl. My scowl is far better organized than that."

"It's the lightning," said Ephram. "Lightning makes everybody look disorganized."

Brom looked at the hill with a head on top for a long while without saying anything. Then he folded his copy of the group sheet twice, very carefully, and went upstairs with it. Cael knew without asking that it had gone into the bottom of his kit, with the Velmere letters.

Karis went through all six sheets twice, and then a third time, with her notebook open.

"I'm in one," she said, with real interest. "One of six. Lira's in two, you're in four, and Brom's in one, but he's in it the size of a barn." She made a note. "So the market pays for faces it can see from a street. I read as staff. That's useful to know. I shall go on reading as staff for as long as I possibly can."

Seln had come to the table last, with the travel file under his arm, and he had not looked at any of the sheets until the others had finished. Now he looked at the group sheet. He looked at the lean figure at the end of the line for what seemed to Cael a very long time. His face did not change at all.

"Inaccurate. The office's ledger is larger."

He returned to his place at the far end and drew the travel file toward him.

There was a short silence. Ephram looked at Lira, and Lira looked at Karis, and Karis looked at Cael with her eyebrows a fraction raised.

"Was that—" said Ephram, very quietly.

"I think so," said Karis.

"I didn't know he could."

"Nobody did." Karis turned to a clean page and wrote the date at the top of it, and a single line underneath, and closed the notebook. "First of the year, as far as I can find. I'm recording it as confirmed."

Seln turned a page. If he had heard any of it, nothing about him said so.

Cael wrote it that night, at the window, with the six sheets weighted under his boot on the floor.

*Six sheets, two coppers each, and the whole delegation's in them somewhere. Two of me from one printer, one with sunbeams and one without, because the market likes to be told what to feel and doesn't much mind which. One of Lira, angrier than life. One of Daeva of Auremont, all storm and no face, which may be the most honest of the six.*

*And one of all of us. It took a man with a knife and a block of pearwood, who has never read a file in his life, to see where the sixth of us stands.*

*Every compiler on the circuit missed him, because the compilers start from the rosters and the rosters put him on the last line, as staff. The woodcutter started from his window. Instruments measure what they were built to measure. A woodcutter only has his eyes, and uses them.*

Ephram said it at the courtyard rail on the third evening, and Cael realized afterward that he had been saving it.

They were standing at the rail of the guesting-house's upper gallery with their cups, looking down into the street. Along the processional way the lamplighters were at work with their poles. A man came out of the cookshop opposite, saw Cael at the rail, stopped dead in the middle of the street with a pie in each hand, and stared up at him with his mouth open until a cart nearly ran him down. It was the ninth or tenth time that evening.

Ephram watched the man jump out of the cart's way, and then turned to Cael with a perfectly straight face.

"At Halcenvane you arrived pre-explained. Here you arrived pre-*sold*. Try to be a disappointing product."

Cael laughed before he could stop himself. It was the first time he had laughed since the gate.

Ephram did not laugh. His voice stayed dry and kind together, the cohort's exactness, which had once been turned so hard on Cael, now turned entirely on his behalf. "Half this city has paid good money for a picture of you, and every picture puts on a better show than you ever will. You're going to let all of them down." He raised his cup a little toward the street. "It's the only privacy they sell here. I'd take it while it's going."

"How long have you had that one?"

"Since the mill town." Ephram looked down at the lit street, where the man with the pies had found his feet and was walking away fast, looking back over his shoulder. "I've been polishing it. I wanted it ready for a city."

They stood at the rail a while longer. Below them the processional way filled up with the evening crowd, and over the roofs the Concourse's upper tiers were lit along their edges, a hundred lamps in a long curve, very far up.

"Can I tell you something?" said Ephram.

"Yes."

"At the beginning of the year I'd have hated this." He turned his cup in his hands. "Not you. This. All of them looking at you and not one of them looking at me. I'd have counted it, every night, and hated it." He was quiet for a moment. "I counted it tonight. I don't. I'd rather be trial caller of a house that walks in with you than top of the Iron column of a house that walks in with nobody." He drank. "I don't know when that changed. I'm writing it down so I don't forget it did."

"Trial caller," said Cael.

"Trial caller." Ephram looked at the cup. "I keep waiting to mind that too. A year ago I'd have wanted to be the one they called for, not the one doing the calling." He shook his head slowly. "It turns out I like seeing the whole floor. I like knowing where Lira's going a breath before she does. I like it better than winning, some days, and I never thought I'd say that about anything."

"Don't say it to Rooke."

"I'd sooner be thrown off the gallery." He finished his tea. "Good night."

He went in. Cael stayed at the rail and watched the lamps along the Concourse, and thought that he had known Ephram for a year and was only now meeting him.

Auremont came on the evening of the fourth day, and Cael watched it arrive with his back to it.

The noise came first, down the processional way from the north gate like water coming down a gutter, and the guesting-houses emptied into the street to meet it. Lira was out of the front door before anybody else had found their coats. "Come on," she said, from the step. "If we're going to be beaten by them, I want to see what they look like on a horse." So they went, all five, with Brom at Cael's shoulder, and found a place on the steps of a cloth-hall halfway along the street, where the crowd stood six deep and the boys had climbed the lamp-posts.

The column came at the hour when the light lay longest down the street, gold on one side and blue on the other, and Cael did not believe for a moment that the hour was an accident.

There were forty riders in Auremont's deep blue and silver. Their horses were matched for height and colour, bays every one, and shod alike, so that forty sets of hooves struck the paving in a single even rattle that the read, without being asked, found perfectly regular. The house's banner went first on a tall staff, carried by a rider who did not move in the saddle at all. Behind it came the fighters. They rode by bracket, Copper at the front and Gold at the back, and inside each bracket by their seeding, so that anybody who owned the continental index could have named every face in the line from its place alone.

That was the thing Cael noticed, standing on the cloth-hall steps. It was not hidden. It had been set out.

A column that rode in its seeding order was a column that wanted the street to read its seeding. It had chosen the light it would be read in and the speed it would be read at, and the order in which the street would come to each name, saving the best for last. It was not an arrival at all. It was a page, and the whole street was reading it aloud.

And the street was reading. The crowd round the steps had begun saying the same name before the banner reached them, and it went on saying it, louder as the column came on, like the chorus of a song everybody in the street had learned before they could read. *The Gold's at the back. She'll be last. That's the one.* A man on the step behind Cael was telling everybody what he could see from up there, whether they wanted to know or not. *Daeva. There. Last of all.*

Cael turned round on the step and put his back to the street.

He did it without much thought, the way he would have stepped away from a feint. He found himself facing the crowd instead of the column: a hundred faces on the steps and in the windows of the cloth-hall, all turned the other way, all lit gold from the west, all watching the end of the line come up the street. He watched them instead. He saw what the column did to them as it passed. He saw them go quiet at the banner and loud at the Silvers, and then, as the end of the line came level, he saw a change go across all of them at once, like wind across a field of barley.

It told him a great deal about the crowd. It told him a good deal about Auremont, which had built a column that could do that to a street. It told him nothing about her, and that was what he had wanted.

"You're facing the wrong way," said Brom. He had watched Cael turn, and had said nothing until the sound had passed on up the street.

"I know."

"She went past. Just now. A long way off, at the back."

"I know. I heard them." Cael turned round again. The tail of the column was going away up the processional way, the blue and silver getting smaller against the lit tiers of the Concourse. "Everything in that line was put where it was for somebody standing where I'm standing. If I'd read her just now, I'd have read her the way they laid her out for me." He watched the last riders turn the corner toward the delegation quarter. "I'll take my first look at the procession, from wherever the marshals put me, on the tournament's own floor. Until then, all of it is Auremont's publishing. I decline to review the publishing."

Brom thought about that for a long moment.

"That's either very sensible," he said, "or the most stubborn thing I've heard you say all year."

"Can't it be both?"

"With you it usually is." Brom looked up the street to where the column had gone. "She'd have liked it, I think. If she knew."

"Knew what?"

"That somebody turned his back." Brom started down the steps. "Must be the only back in the city tonight."

*Auremont came in tonight,* he wrote, *forty riders in seeding order, every one of them placed to be read from the pavement, in the best light, at a walking pace. I turned round and read the crowd instead. It's a fine column. It does exactly what it was built to do to a street.*

*It didn't tell me anything about her, because I didn't let it. Everybody here reads everybody else through somebody's arrangement. I'd like one read in this city, just one, that nobody arranged for me. The procession is the nearest I'll get.*

The Archmarshal Vastin read the Norhold files on a Fourth-day evening, in his room in the Compact's house, a week before the opening.

They had been on the chair beside his table since the cold term, in their two boxes, and he had worked round them every day. He had not forgotten them. He had simply not yet found the hour in which he wished to know what was in them, and he was honest enough with himself to notice that this was not the same as having no hour. On the Fourth-day evening the last bundle of the week was finished before the lamps, and the court below was empty except for the porter and his pigeons, and he lifted the first box onto the table and took off its lid.

He read them as he read everything, in order and in full.

He had read a great many files in forty years, and most of them told him more about the office that had made them than about the thing they described. These were no different. Their courtesies told him which officers liked one another and which did not. Their dates told him that somebody had been in a hurry in the cold term, and then had not. He noted that, as he noted everything, and did not make it carry more than a date can carry.

There were the liaison papers between the observation office and the tournament's adjudication office, courteous and dull. There was the rotation of the observation officers through the three weeks, with their seats and their hours, and the names on it were names he knew. He found Ilsev in the first seat, as he had expected, and Havel in the second. There were the instructions that had gone out under each paper, and the acknowledgments that had come back. He read them all and found nothing in them that a careful office would not have written.

Then he came to the seating of the Compact's row in the Concourse gallery.

It was a plain document, a plan of twelve seats drawn in a clerk's neat hand, with a name-plate entered against each. The six observation seats came first, then the district officers, then a liaison seat, then two left for officials of the host city. He read along them in order. One, two, three. Eleven.

Twelve was held.

There was no plate entered against it. Where the name should have been there was a short instruction in the same clerk's hand. *Seat twelve: held. No plate. Stewards to keep the seat clear for the length of the sitting.* Beneath that, in the column for the grade of the officer for whom a seat was held, the clerk had written one word.

It was his own grade.

Vastin sat back in his chair.

His left hand was resting on the table beside the plan, and it ached, as it did most evenings now. He opened it and closed it once, slowly, and looked at it while he did.

He had not asked for a seat at Norhold. He had not asked to be sent, or told he might be, or consulted about the row. He went to his own register and turned back through it, every outgoing letter for three months, to be certain of that, and he was certain. Nothing had gone from this room about the finals except the order for these two boxes. And somewhere, in some office he had not written to, a clerk had been told to hold a chair at the end of a gallery row, without a name on it, at the grade of the man who now sat reading the instruction.

There was nothing irregular in it. Seats were held at every sitting, for officials whose coming was not yet settled. A held seat was a courtesy. It obliged nobody to sit in it.

He noted that. He took his day book from the drawer and wrote, under the date, one line: *Norhold, Concourse row: seat twelve held at this grade. Not requested by this office.* He read the line back, as he read every line, and it was true.

Then he did a thing he had done perhaps a dozen times in forty years. He noticed a thought beginning, a long one with a shape to it, and he declined to let it go on. It was not that the thought was improper. It was that it had nothing yet to stand on but an empty chair, and he had spent his working life refusing to let a thought carry more weight than the evidence under it.

He closed the day book and put the plan back in its box, in its place, and the lid on the box.

He did not put the box back on the chair. He set it on the corner of his table, where it would be the first thing he saw in the morning.

Then he sat for a little while longer in the lamplight with his hands folded on the table, listening to the pigeons settling in the eaves across the court, and to the porter locking the street door below. He was not thinking about the chair. He was careful about that. He was thinking, as he often did at the end of a week, about the shape of the week, and whether every reading in it had been entered and every entry been true. They had. He turned down the lamp.

# Chapter 30 — Cheaper Than Deciding

The orientation began at the second bell on the fifth morning, at a side door of the Concourse that Cael would not have found in a week, and it was conducted by a marshal who had plainly given it many times before and had decided long ago to enjoy it.

He was a short broad man with a grey beard cut square and a voice that carried without being raised.

He met them at the door with a list, ticked them off one by one, and looked at each of them as he did it, so that by the end he had every face matched to a name.

When he came to Cael he looked a little longer than at the others, and ticked the list, and said nothing about it.

When he had finished he tucked the list away. "Fourteen houses, and every one of them gets this walk, and the draw at dusk. I'll show you every floor you'll fight on and every door you'll need, and I'll hand you the rules at the end, and if you've any sense you'll read them tonight and not on the morning of your first bout. Questions at the end, please. Follow me."

They followed him.

He took them through the Concourse from the inside, along corridors and galleries and up and down stairs, and the building that had looked from the rise like a range of hills turned out, close to, to be a town.

There were warm-up halls with sprung floors, dressing rooms with numbered doors, a kitchen the size of the house's long hall, a surgeon's corridor with clean beds in a row.

Brom looked down the row of beds for some time as they passed, and said, to nobody in particular, that he hoped to see a great deal less of it than of the kitchen.

In one of the warm-up halls a coast house's Copper was running drills on a sprung floor while his coach counted, and he stopped in the middle of a drill to stare at Cael going past the open door.

His coach, without breaking the count, reached out and turned the boy's head back to the floor with one hand, as you would turn a tap. The marshal saw it and did not smile, quite.

There were four lesser floors.

They saw the second of them last, a square hall under a high roof of glass, with a pale hard floor that rang a little under their boots.

Lira stopped in the middle of it and stood there with her eyes half shut while the others went on, and Cael saw her rise very slightly onto the balls of her feet and settle back, testing it.

"Fast." She had seen him waiting for her. "Very fast, and very honest. It won't give you anything." She looked up at the glass. "I like it."

Then the marshal took them through a last pair of doors and out onto the main floor, and for a while nobody said anything at all.

It was a long oval of grey stone, polished by three hundred years of feet.

Round it, on every side, the tiers went up in four great banks, stone benches rising one above another so steeply that the topmost rows seemed to lean over the floor.

They were empty now, except for a few cleaners working their way along with brooms, small as insects, very far up.

At the east and west ends stood the two towers Cael had seen from the rise, and on the face of each, high above the floor, hung a great board, painted black and ruled in white, with nothing written on it yet.

On the south side a long dais held five tall chairs in a row and a clerk's desk below them.

"The panel," said the marshal. "Five judges. You'll know how they mark by now, I expect; every house does."

He pointed east, then west. "The boards are for the figures. The east tower is the timekeepers'. The west tower is the adjudication office."

He paused, and something in his voice changed, very slightly. "That's the Chief Adjudicator's tower. You'll see him at the opening. You won't see much of him otherwise. He doesn't come down unless the mark needs him."

Cael looked up at the west tower.

There were open galleries along its upper floors, and in them, far off, he could make out figures moving about. They were clerks in grey, going between desks with papers, at a pace that did not hurry and did not stop.

In the middle of the main floor the stone had been cut in a circle of square holes.

They were about the size of a man's two fists, sunk deep into the floor, perhaps two dozen of them in a ring, and each was stopped with a plug of dark wood worn smooth and level with the stone.

Ephram pointed. "What are those?"

"Sockets," said the marshal. "For the exhibition ring, when there's call for it. It goes up in the second week if there's been filing, and not before." He glanced at Cael, very briefly, and away again. "There's been a fair bit of talk about that this cycle."

---

The rules were handed out at the end, at the side door, in a printed program bound in grey card, one copy for each house's coach and one for each fighter.

Karis had hers open before she had stepped back into the street.

She read walking, with Lira steering her round the posts by her elbow, and by the time they reached the guesting-house she had found the page she wanted and was reading it aloud, at the long table, before anybody had taken off a coat.

*Bracket bouts at the continental finals are scored by the exchange. A bout is fought over five exchanges. In each exchange every clean touch the flags give is one point, and the exchange ends at the bell or as soon as either fighter has three points in it. The points of all the exchanges are added together. A bout ends before the fifth exchange if the fighter who is behind could not draw level even by taking every point that remains. If the points are level after five exchanges, the figures decide.*

She put the program down.

For a moment nobody spoke. Every one of them had spent a year fighting to two touches inside four exchanges, and every one of them was turning that year over in their heads and finding it did not fit here.

Lira spoke first, slowly. "So it isn't first to two."

"Nothing's first to anything." Karis had taken a pencil out and was writing figures in the margin of the program. "Every exchange is money. You can lose one and buy it back in the next, and the next, and the next, if you've got the legs."

She drew a line under her figures. "Or you can lose one three-nothing in the first breath of the bout and spend the other four buying it back."

"Five exchanges," said Brom.

Everybody looked at him. He was sitting at the end of the table with the program open on his knee at the same page, and he was frowning at it as though it had said something about his family.

"I've never fought a fifth. Not once. Not at any meet. Four's the most anybody ever asked of me." He turned the page back and read the paragraph again. "That's a long time to keep a door shut."

Lira shrugged. "Then shut it for five."

"I intend to." He did not look up from the page. "I'm only saying I've never tried."

Rooke had been standing by the window with his own copy unopened in his hand. Now he came to the table.

"Read it again tonight, all of you. Every word. Then think about the third point in an exchange. A man who's two up in an exchange with the bell coming will stop and hold, because the third point ends it early and he'd like to rest. A man who's two down will throw everything at you, because he's got nothing to save it for. You'll see both, and you'll feel both, in yourselves."

He tapped the program. "Exhibitions keep their own old rules. That's yours." He nodded at Cael. "Five exchanges at most, two touches ends it, a clean throw a touch. Nothing about it changes. The tournament doesn't touch the category. It never has."

"And third place?" said Ephram. He had found something at the foot of the page. "It doesn't say."

"Because there isn't one." It was Gault who answered, from the end of the table, quietly. "There's never been a bout for third at Norhold. The two beaten semifinalists stand in the bracket's standing by their figures, and that's all."

He turned his cup round on the table. "That's how I came to be fourth. On the figures. I never fought for it. I sat in the dressing room and a clerk came and told me."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Lira leaned forward. "Was that worse? Or better?"

Gault considered it with great care.

"Better. I'd have lost the bout."

They sat up late over the program that night, all five of them, at the long table with one lamp.

Karis had worked out every way a bout could end and written them on a single sheet: three-nothing, three-nothing, three-nothing, and done in three; two-one every exchange and a long tired walk to the fifth; a three-nothing lost in the first breath and four exchanges spent buying it back.

She laid the sheet in the middle of the table and they argued over it like a family over a will. Lira wanted to know how fast a three-nothing could happen, and Karis said faster than anybody liked.

Ephram wanted to know whether a fighter two up with the bell coming could simply stand off and run out the time, and Rooke, from the window, said yes, and that anybody who did it in front of the five judges would see what the judges thought of it on the board eleven minutes later.

Brom did not argue.

He sat with his elbows on the table and the program open at the page and read the paragraph once more, slowly, moving his lips a little. Then he closed it and put his big hand flat on the cover.

"Five," he said, to himself. "All right."

Rooke took Cael back to the main floor rail at the end of the afternoon, alone, and stood beside him there for some time without speaking, looking up at the west tower.

The cleaners had gone.

The great oval was empty and quiet, and the light came down through the high windows along the top of the tiers in long slanting bars that lay across the grey stone and across the ring of plugged sockets in the middle of it.

In the west tower's galleries a lamp had been lit, and the grey clerks were still moving between their desks.

"Umber," said Rooke at last.

Cael waited.

"I told the squad about him at the start of the year, at the map table. I'm going to tell you again, here, because you're the one of us most likely to end up across a table from him."

He kept his eyes on the tower. "On the opening day this floor will be full of people on strings. Fourteen houses of them. The house pulls one string and the coach another, and the money and the index and the folks at home pull the rest. I answer to Withrow. Withrow answers to a board and a charter and eighteen years. You answer to the four of them, and to whatever it is you write in that book."

He did not look round. "That man answers to the mark. Nothing else. He doesn't care who wins. He doesn't care who's watching. He cares whether the number on that board is the true one."

"You trust him."

"I trust him more than anybody I've met in this trade, and I've met most of it."

Rooke was quiet a moment. "Everybody else here will want something off you. A story, a figure, a bout, a look at what's under your coat."

He shook his head slowly. "Not him. He can't want anything from you, because there's nothing he wants in the whole world except for the measure to be right."

He pushed himself away from the rail. "If you ever find yourself in front of him, keep that in mind. It'll be the only conversation in this city where nobody's selling."

He went back across the floor toward the side door, his footsteps loud in the empty bowl. Halfway there he stopped and turned.

"And Cael."

"Yes?"

"If that day comes, don't try to sell him anything." Rooke's face did not change. "Not a story, not a figure, not a look. He'll know at once, and he'll think the less of the house for it." Then he went on, and the side door shut behind him.

Cael stayed at the rail.

Rooke had never once asked him what lay under the record, and he had not asked now. But he had come as near as Rooke ever came to saying that he knew there was something there, and that it was Cael's to carry and not his.

Cael stood at the rail and looked up at the lamp in the west tower.

He thought of Vell, in Ardenmere, who kept a circuit ledger and would not write a false line in it for anybody.

He thought of Bracken at his green table, certifying a list without omission because a true record was the only kind there was.

He thought of Hesk in his cold workshop, warming a barometer and waiting for it to come right.

Now there was a grey man in a tower who answered to a mark. They were the people who held a pattern and would not let it slip for anybody's convenience.

*Another for the short list,* he wrote that evening, *of people who'd sooner be right than be on anybody's side. Vell. Bracken. Hesk at his bench. Now a grey man in a tower. Every one of them has done me more good than all the people who ever wanted something from me put together, and not one of them was trying to.*

Before the light went he slipped back into the Concourse by the side door the marshal had shown them, alone. Nobody else on the roster would ever have to know that ring, and he meant to know it before anybody built it.

The bowl was nearly dark.

A few lamps had been lit along the lowest tier for the cleaners, and the long bars of light from the high windows had climbed the tiers and gone. He walked out across the polished stone to the ring of sockets and stood in the middle of it.

Then he paced it.

He walked it twice, from socket to socket straight across, once north to south and once east to west, and wrote both figures in the notebook. It was fifty-odd feet, a little longer one way than the other, and wider than any floor he had fought on all year.

He knelt and put his hand flat on the stone inside the ring, and then on the stone outside it.

They were not the same.

Outside the ring the stone was old and even, worn smooth by centuries of feet and brooms. Inside the ring it had been patched, and patched again, in a dozen different stones of a dozen different shades.

Some of the patches were nearly as old as the floor, and some looked to be from the last cycle or the one before.

The ring had been taken up and put down again more times than anybody had counted, each time to whatever size the bouts of that cycle had asked for, and every time it went up it left a little more of itself behind in the stone.

He stood up and looked round at the plugged holes in the half-dark. Whole cycles must have gone by without anybody fighting on this ground at all.

And still, between every cycle, somebody had come out onto this floor with an oilcan and a rag, and kept every plug sound, and never once needed to ask why.

"Volunteer category, are you?"

The voice came from behind him.

It was a steward on her closing round, a tall woman with a lantern and a great ring of keys at her belt, and she had crossed the floor so quietly that he had not heard a step.

She held the lantern a little higher and looked at his credential, and then at his face, and nodded. It was not really a question. The printed sheets had seen to that.

She did not move him along.

She stood for a moment inside the ring with him and looked round at the plugged sockets, with the patient attention of somebody looking at a piece of furniture that has been in the family a long while.

"My grandmother worked the floor crews," she said. "Forty years. She put this ring up three times in her whole working life, and took it down three times, and the rest of the time she oiled the plugs."

She touched one with the toe of her boot.

"She always said this ring was like an old doorway somebody's walled over with brick. You don't pull the brick out, or you'd have to decide what to do with whatever came through. And you don't knock the frame down either, because one day somebody might want a door there." She shrugged. "So you leave the bricks where they are and keep the frame painted. It's cheaper than deciding."

She nodded to him again and went on up the floor with her lantern and her keys, checking the doors as she went, and he heard each one close and lock behind her.

He stayed in the ring a little while after the last door had shut, until the dark had come right down the tiers and there was nothing left to see but the faint shine of the plugs at his feet.

He found he was not thinking about whoever might file.

He was thinking about the steward's grandmother, oiling plugs for forty years in a floor she had put a ring on three times, and about how many people like her it had taken, one after another, to keep a door open that nobody walked through.

Then he went out by the side door, and found the porter there waiting to lock it behind him, without the least impatience.

He wrote her sentence down whole that night, at the window, under the line he had written in the first week of the year about Bracken's forms, that every phrase in one was a scar from some old fight.

*Cheaper than deciding. Every clause I've ever stood on is a walled-up doorway somebody kept painting.*

The draw was made at dusk that same day, in the Concourse's outer court, by lamplight, in front of everybody.

The court was a great paved square under the Concourse's north face, and by the time the light began to go it was full. All fourteen houses had come, every fighter and coach, and behind them half the city.

On a platform at the court's head stood a long table with a drum on it, a barrel of polished wood on an iron spindle, and behind the table sat three clerks from the west tower with their ledgers open.

It took two hours.

The seeded names were laid in first by their bands, read out one by one from the clerks' ledgers and chalked up on boards behind the table.

Then the drum was turned, and the rest came out of it one slip at a time, each read aloud in a marshal's carrying voice, each chalked into its line.

The marshal who turned the drum had a way of pausing with each slip held up in the lamplight before he read it, just long enough for the whole court to draw breath, and Ephram said afterward that he must have been practising the pause for a month.

Every name that came out of the drum went through the court like a stone into a pond.

Fourteen delegations stood in their knots and worked the brackets in their heads as the lines filled, and the court was full of the sound of it, a dozen accents doing arithmetic aloud at once.

When it was done the clerks carried the ledgers away, and printed boards went up along the court's east wall by torchlight, twelve feet of them, the whole tournament set out in black type.

The crowd moved to the boards. Nobody read the draw alone. That was the custom, Rooke had said: you read it with your house round you, so that whatever it said, you were not by yourself when you found it.

Withrow had come with them, in her dark coat, and Bracken with her, and Seln with the travel file open on his arm, already copying each of the house's lines into it as the boards went up.

At the far end of the court Auremont read its boards in a knot of deep blue, forty strong, very quiet. Cael kept his eyes on his own wall.

Halcenvane read it in the order it had always read things. Karis went fastest, from the top. Cael went slowest and read everything. The others went to their own names first.

Lira found hers inside a minute. She read her pool from the top line down: an Ash Path fighter from a southern house in the first round, a middle seed, and a second round that would want looking at.

Then her eyes went on down, past the quarterfinal, to the line she had worked out on the back of her hand on the bluff.

*ZERIN — AUREMONT — WIND — IRON 9.*

She looked at it for a long time with her hands in her coat pockets. Her face did not change.

"Good." It came at last, quite calmly. "Nobody hiding behind a door, then. Plenty of time to be ready." She did not move away from the board. Her eyes stayed where they were, on a name three lines of type below her own.

Brom's Copper bracket was a long one, and it read, Karis said afterward, like a meeting of the circuit's mistakes.

Half its names were men and women whose ranks had come late or never, and a good many carried a compiler's advisory in the index.

At the top of it, on the first seed's line, stood the name Brom had pinned above his bed for ten weeks.

*MAREK — RHAGEN — GLASS — COPPER 1. FIRST SEED.*

Brom read it twice, and his broad face eased into something very like contentment.

"First seed at Copper One. Somebody in the seeding office reads past the paper."

He looked down the rest of his own line, the rounds stacked under it. "Early rounds this week. Quarters in the second. The final's before the rest day." He nodded to himself. "Good. I'd hate to wait."

Ephram read his pool twice, and then once more with his finger on it, and Cael read it over his shoulder. The first round was the coast house's second entry, the house whose coach had nodded on the road.

There was a likely meeting in the round of sixteen with the lake house's methodical Shield, and nothing of his own house anywhere in his quarter of the draw before the semifinals at the earliest.

"Nothing free," said Ephram. "Nothing unfair." He took his finger off the board. "Correct tournament."

He looked at it a moment more, and then went on in the same flat voice he used for reading out a bill. "The round of sixteen's what I'm paying to get in. Anything after that, I'm trading on."

Karis had gone quiet.

Cael caught the change before he had turned his head. She had been reading fast and talking as she read, telling Lira where the southern houses had landed and Rooke where the lake house's Silvers sat.

Then she had stopped talking, and stopped reading, and stood quite still in front of the Iron board with one finger resting on a line.

It was her own line. *KARIS — HALCENVANE — EMBER — IRON 3.*

Her finger was not on her name. It was two lines below it, on the row for the quarterfinal, where the draw said she would meet whoever came through the other side of her pool.

Cael read the line under her finger. The house was Ternhall. Next to it, in type no larger than any other on the wall, stood *IVENNE*.

"You know her," he said.

Karis did not take her finger away. "We shared a floor. At Ternhall. All the time I was there." She spoke very evenly, and it cost her something to.

"She took the place I left. The top of their column. We learned the lattice side by side, and we kept notebooks on it, and for a long time we traded the notebooks every week."

She was quiet for a moment. "She can read me the way you read me. Better than you do. She knew it while it was forming."

Cael noticed that she had not said the name. She had read it, and touched it, and talked about it for a minute, and not once said it aloud.

Karis took out her notebook and wrote the line in it, house and name and round, though it was printed on a board in front of her and would be printed in every gallery sheet in the city by morning.

Cael understood that the writing was the point. Then the notebook went back into her coat.

"Quarterfinal. Well. Everybody was always going to be measured."

Withrow had seen it too.

She was standing a little behind them, and Cael saw her read Karis's line and the line two below it, and look at Karis's back, and then turn her eyes back to the brackets with a face that had seen nothing in particular.

She did not say a word. She moved, very slightly, so that she stood between Karis and the crowd behind.

Karis went on reading the board where she had left off, at the same speed, as though nothing had happened.

Cael read last, and longest, and he read all of it.

Below the last bracket, almost down at the paving, hung one more frame.

It was older than the boards round it, dark wood gone nearly black, and the card in it was printed in an old plain type that nobody used any more. It was the demonstration category's card.

At the top it set out the category's rules, five short lines that had not changed in living memory, and the size of the ring, and the provision for rating against the Standard.

Under them, where any other frame would have held a bracket, ran a column of ruled lines.

They were blank. The category did not draw. It waited for filings, and nobody had filed.

He stood in front of it a long while.

The crowd moved round him, reading and talking, and after a time he became aware that the people nearest him had stopped reading the brackets and were reading him reading the blank lines.

Lira was at his elbow. So were the others. He had not heard them come.

"Everyone else knows who they're fighting," he said. "I'm about to find out who volunteers."

# Chapter 31 — The Mark

Chief Adjudicator Umber sent his clerks home at the ninth bell on the eve of the opening, and then sat down alone with the case.

They did not want to go. They never did, on this night of all the nights in the cycle, and he had known them long enough to let them make their small protests before he sent them.

The senior clerk wanted to stay and hold the lamp.

The youngest, who had been in the tower only since the last cycle and still walked about it as though the floors might give way, wanted to bring him tea, and then a shawl for his knees, because the galleries were cold after dark.

He accepted the tea and declined the shawl, and thanked them both. Then he told them that the mark had managed three hundred years without a shawl and would very likely manage one more night.

They went down the stair together, talking in low voices, and he heard the outer door close behind them, and then the tower was quiet.

The office of the adjudication occupied the whole of the west tower, five floors of it, and on this one, the fourth, it kept its oldest things.

The long room ran the width of the tower, with tall windows at either end. One looked out over the city and the other down onto the main floor through a gallery screen of carved stone.

Down its length stood the clerks' desks in two rows, each with its lamp and its blotter and its stacks of ruled paper squared to the edge, and at the far end, on a table by itself under the best lamp, stood the case.

It was a plain thing to look at. It was a chest of dark wood about the length of a man's arm, bound at the corners in brass gone brown with handling, with two leather straps round it.

Where the straps crossed on the lid, a cord had been passed through both and knotted, and over the knot three seals had been set in red wax.

Umber drew a chair up to the table, and took off his spectacles and polished them, and put them on again, and took out his glass.

He did not need to do this. He knew that perfectly well.

The case had stood in the strong room on the floor below for three years. Two locked doors had stood between it and the stair, and a clerk at a desk who logged every soul who passed them, and not one entry in that log was out of order.

Tomorrow he would carry it out onto the main floor, and open the seals in front of eight thousand people, and every one of them would watch him do it and not one of them would be close enough to see the wax.

If he set his new seal on old wax that had been tampered with, nobody in the Concourse would ever know.

He would know. That was the whole of the matter, and had been for as long as he had held the office.

He bent over the first seal with the glass.

It was his own. He had set it himself, three years ago, on the last night of the last cycle. That had been after the last bout had been rated and the last figure posted and the tables put back in the case for the long sleep.

The office's mark stood clear in the wax, the bar and the ring, every edge of it sharp. The date of the closing was cut beneath it.

And there, low on the right where a careless eye would take it for a flaw in the casting, was the small scratch he made with the corner of his thumbnail in every seal he set, a private habit forty years old that he had never told anybody about.

The scratch was there. His own thumbnail had made it.

He checked the second seal in the same way, and then the third. The second had been set by the senior clerk and witnessed by him, and he compared its impression against the drawing of the matrix in the seal-book, line by line.

He followed the cord with his fingers from the knot along both straps to the brass rings and back, feeling for a cut or a splice or a place where the twist ran wrong.

He went over the lid with the glass, and then the hinges, and then the lock.

It took most of an hour. The tea went cold beside him.

When he had finished, he sat back in his chair and took his spectacles off again and rubbed his eyes.

The seals were whole. The cord was whole. Nobody had touched the case since he had closed it with his own hands three years before.

He had known they would be. He had never once in all his years found anything wrong on this night, and he did not expect ever to find anything.

That was not why he looked. He looked so that tomorrow, when he stood up in front of the city and said the sentence, he would be saying a thing he knew and not a thing he believed.

---

The calibration ledger lay beside the case, and when he had drunk the cold tea he opened it and read the week's last page again.

Every judge on the cycle's lists had been tested that week, as every judge was tested before every cycle.

They had sat in the long room below in fives, behind the same small boards they would sit behind on the dais, and the clerks had shown them old bouts, bouts that had been rated by earlier panels and whose true figures stood in the office's books.

Each judge marked them blind. The clerks took the slates and compared them with the books, mark by mark, and wrote the differences in the ledger.

Most of the differences were small, and wandered on both sides of the line, as honest differences do. A judge might be a shade hard on execution one morning and a shade soft the next.

That was a human eye, and the panel's five eyes and the striking of the high and low were there to absorb it.

One judge's differences did not wander.

He had marked the Blade bouts generous. It was not much. It was a quarter of a point, on effect, never more.

But it was a quarter of a point on every Blade bout he was shown, two days running, and always on the same side of the line.

The clerks had flagged it on the first evening, and Umber had given him the second day to correct it. On the second day he had marked a quarter generous again, without knowing that he did.

Umber had struck him from the lists that morning, and written the letter himself.

He read the ledger's entry now, in the senior clerk's careful hand, and the line under it in his own, and he was sorry.

The man was a good judge. He had a quick eye and a fair mind, and he had sat on Umber's panels for three cycles without a word of complaint from anybody.

He had fought in the Blade bracket himself, a long time ago. Somewhere in him he still loved it, and the love had come out on his slate a quarter of a point at a time.

That was the trouble with a kind eye. It was the best thing a man could bring to most work, and on a panel it was a fault.

Umber closed the ledger.

He had kept this office through more cycles than any of his clerks had been alive for, and he still remembered his first opening. He remembered the weight of the case in his arms, heavier than he had expected, and the way the noise of the tiers had come down on him when he walked out with it, so that for a moment he had not been able to hear his own feet. He remembered his hands shaking as he cut the cord. The old Chief Adjudicator, his master, had stood at his elbow and said nothing at all. Afterward, in the tower, the old man had poured him a cup of something strong and said only, *You checked them last night. So your hands may shake. The seals did not.*

He had not understood it for years. He understood it now.

He got up, stiffly, and walked the length of the long room to the gallery screen, and looked down through the carved stone at the main floor.

It was dark down there. A single lamp burned on the lowest tier for the night porter, and in its light he could just make out the dais on the south side, the five tall chairs in their row.

Tomorrow at midmorning the tiers would be full to the top rows, every one of the eight thousand places taken. The fourteen houses would walk out onto that floor behind their colours.

The crowd would roar for the banner, as it always did, because a banner was a thing people could love.

Then he would carry the case out, and break three seals, and say a sentence.

He said it now, very quietly, under his breath, standing at the screen in the dark.

He had said it at the opening of every cycle he had kept. He had not written it. It was older than he was, and older than any of the men who had said it before him whose names he knew.

Every Chief Adjudicator before him had said it in his turn, and every one of them, Umber supposed, had sat up the night before with a glass and a cold cup of tea, for the same reason he did.

It was a short sentence, and a plain one. There was no ornament in it at all. It said three things and said each of them once, and it had held up the whole weight of the tournament for longer than anybody could remember.

That was what Umber believed in, if he believed in anything, more than in charters or ceremonies or banners. He believed in a sentence that worked.

A sentence worked when it was true every single time it was said.

The first time it was said and was not true, it would stop working. It would stop forever, even on all the days afterward when it was true again, because nobody hearing it would ever be sure which kind of day it was.

So it had to be true every time. And he was the one who said it, so he was the one who had to make sure.

He went back to the table and lifted the case in both arms, carefully, as he would have lifted a child. He carried it down the stair to the strong room, and logged it in with the clerk at the desk in his own hand.

Then he put on his coat and went home through the quiet streets, an old man walking slowly, and nobody he passed looked at him twice.

Cael had been told, more than once, that he would stand in a tunnel for a long time on the first day of the new month, and he did.

It ran under the north tiers, wide and dim and lit with lamps, and all fourteen houses waited in it in their files, one behind another, with their colours on staves at their heads.

Somewhere beyond its mouth a band was playing. It played a slow, plain, stepping tune that nobody in the tunnel could name and everybody in it knew, and through the stone over their heads came another sound under the music.

Eight thousand people were humming it under their breath, a crowd humming a thing it had heard at every opening it had ever been to.

The houses went out in the order they had qualified. The earliest went first, small houses from far regions blinking at the light, and the great names waited at the back for their turn.

It meant the crowd read the file from its smallest end to its largest, and kept the best of it for last, like a good storyteller.

Halcenvane was eleventh of fourteen.

Withrow stood at its head in a plain formal grey that Cael had seen her wear perhaps twice.

Behind her stood the four in their bracket order, Brom for the Copper and then Ephram and Karis and Lira for the Iron, and behind them, after a gap, Cael.

The gap had taken the marshals most of a quarter of an hour.

Three of them had stood in the tunnel with a list while the early houses filed out, arguing in low voices. Every so often one of them had looked at Cael and then back at the list.

There was no bracket to put him in and no rank to order him by. The procession had a place for everybody who had one or the other and no place at all for anybody who had neither.

At last the eldest of them had taken him by the elbow, quite kindly, and walked him to the very back of Halcenvane's file, a stride behind Lira, and left him.

That was where the list ran out. It suited the marshals' logic. It suited the record, too, though the marshals could not know that.

Then the file in front moved, and they walked out of the dark.

He had stood in front of crowds all year. None of them had been anything like this.

The tiers went up on every side so steeply and so full that they stopped looking like seats at all and became a single sloping wall of faces.

The noise came down off that wall in one long pressure that he felt on his skin and in the bones of his chest, and for the first few strides his feet did not trust the floor under it.

He did what Rooke had taught them to do with weather on a floor. He let himself feel it, and named its price, and walked on.

Halfway across, the noise changed under him.

It did not get louder. It came apart.

A movement ran along the tiers ahead of Halcenvane's file, faster than the file was walking, people leaning to their neighbours and pointing, and inside it the roar broke into separate voices, and some of them came down to him through the stone. *That's the clause house. At the back. The one with no tier. That's him, the last one.*

He heard his whole year said back to him in pieces by strangers, and he kept walking.

The houses wheeled in the middle of the floor and drew up in their blocks along the north side, facing the dais.

Auremont came out of the tunnel last. Forty of them, in their blue and silver, and the order of the procession turned their column round, so that their Gold walked first, at its head.

Behind the last of them, alone, came the banner.

It was carried by the tournament's own colour-guard, four men in grey on either side of a tall staff, at a slow march. No house walked behind it.

The Concourse got to its feet for it, all of it at once, with a sound like a long wave breaking along a beach, and Cael felt the difference in his chest as plainly as a change of floor.

They had cheered the houses. They stood up for the cloth.

It told him more about this place in a few breaths than the charter had in a year. The people on these benches did not love any house. They loved the thing every house was walking toward.

Then he took his eyes off the silver, and looked for her.

He had been saving his eyes for a week.

He had turned his back on her in the street on purpose so that this would be the first look, and he had known since the orientation what the geometry of the morning would give him.

Auremont's block drew up on the far side of the floor. Halcenvane's stood on the near side. Between them lay a hundred yards of polished stone, and no part of the ceremony would bring them any closer.

So he read her at a hundred yards. He gave it everything he had, the read and the compound gaze together, the way he would have read the last exchange of the hardest bout of his life.

The read always asked a body the same four things. Where was the weight? Which way was it going? What did the body do just before it moved? And who was all of it for?

She answered the first two the way any good fighter answered them.

She was tall, and nineteen, and her weight went into the stone and came back out of it cleanly at every step, the honest walk of somebody who had spent her life on floors.

Far behind her, at the tail of the file, the colour-guard kept its slow march, and she kept the same pace, no faster and no slower.

The third question found nothing. He could not find a tell. That was not unheard of at a hundred yards; good fighters kept their tells small, and a hundred yards was a long way.

The fourth question was the strange one.

Every fighter walked out onto a floor with their weight aimed at somebody.

He had read it in a hundred fighters this year. They aimed it at the crowd, or at an opponent, or at a coach in the stand, and some of them aimed it at a mirror they had carried round inside themselves since they were children.

The aim was always there. It was the easiest thing in a body to read, because it was the one thing the body most wanted read.

Hers was aimed at nothing at all.

The noise in that bowl had bent him when he came out of the tunnel. He had seen it bend every fighter in every file that morning, one way or another, into swagger or stiffness or a careful ignoring of it.

It did not bend her. She walked through it at the head of a file that ended in the most loved object on the continent, with eight thousand people on their feet, and her body carried on as though she were crossing an empty yard in the rain.

She was not showing anybody anything, and she was not hiding anything either. There was simply no one it was for.

The music ended. The houses stood in their blocks, and all along the north side the fighters let their shoulders down at last.

Her head turned.

It did not go to the banner, or the dais, or the tiers.

It went straight across the hundred yards to the north side, to the eleventh block, and moved along Halcenvane from one end to the other and back again, unhurried. It stopped nowhere. Then it went away.

He put it in the observation notebook that evening, and then into the Log, the same words in both. It wanted a page of its own.

*Daeva, at a hundred yards. The read asks every body four things: where's the weight, where's it going, what happens just before, and who's it for. She answered the first two like any good fighter.*

*The third I couldn't find at that distance. The fourth has no answer. It isn't for anybody.*

*Either there's nothing there to read or she keeps it further in than a hundred yards can reach, and it comes out the same either way: whatever's printed on her sheet is the least of her.*

*When the houses stood, she looked at us. Along the block and back again. Not curious. She was counting what we'd brought.*

When the floor was still again, the west tower's door opened, and an old man came out of it carrying a box.

Cael had pictured somebody larger. Umber was slight and a little stooped, in the adjudication office's plain grey, and he crossed the great floor at the pace of a man crossing his own kitchen in the morning.

Two clerks walked behind him with a small table between them. They set it down in the middle of the floor, and he set the case on it, and stood back and looked at it for a moment with his hands folded.

Then he took a glass out of his coat, and bent, and went over the seals.

He did it one seal at a time, close, the glass to his eye. He ran his fingers along the cord from the knot to the straps and back.

Nobody in the tiers could have seen the wax from where they sat. Cael, on the near side of the floor, could not see it himself.

Every person in the Concourse watched him do it anyway, in a silence so complete that Cael could hear the lamps along the lowest tier.

When he had finished, he cut the cord and broke the seals, and lifted his head and spoke.

The Concourse had been built so that a voice at the middle of its floor would reach its highest bench, and his reached it. He did not raise it.

It was a dry, careful, unremarkable voice, the voice of somebody reading out an inventory he has checked himself, and it said the sentence once.

*the mark is the mark of the last cycle and every cycle; no hand has adjusted it; against it all are measured alike*

He opened the case. He lifted the first of the bound tables out of it and laid it on the table, and one clerk held the cord and the other the wax.

He pressed the new cycle's seal down onto the cover with both hands and held it there. Then he lifted it, and bent to look at the impression, and nodded once to himself, as a carpenter nods at a joint.

Rooke had come up behind Cael in the block while it was happening. He bent his head and spoke, low, under the sound beginning to come down from the tiers. "They cheer the banner. They *trust* the seal. Listen."

Cael listened.

It was nothing like the noise for the banner. That had been a wave breaking. This was quieter by half and went on three times as long, a low slow sound like a great many people agreeing with something under their breath.

Very few of them could have told par from a pothole, or read a line of the tables in that case. Every one of them knew exactly what the seals were there to keep out.

They did not love that wax. They leaned on it.

*Before he set the new seal he went over the old ones with a glass, in front of all of us,* he wrote that night, under the line about Daeva.

*Nothing in the charter asks him to. Nobody in the tiers could have seen it if he'd left it out. He did it the way other men wash their hands before a meal.*

*Rooke told me Umber has nothing to want from me. Having watched him, I'd say it differently. Of everybody in this whole machine, he's the one whose want I can read from the far side of a floor, and it has nothing to do with me.*

*He wants the number to be right. It was in every move of his hands.*

---

Next morning the brackets began, on four floors at once. By the end of the first hour it was plain that Rooke's sheet in the long upper room had never been a warning. It had been a forecast.

Lira's first bout was on the second floor, the square hall under the high glass, and it ran long.

Her opponent was an Ash Path fighter from one of the southern houses, a middle seed, a compact quiet man a few years older than her with ash-grey wraps on his hands.

His regional file said he was good and no more. On the board at the end of the hall he was a line of figures that would not have kept a semifinalist awake.

The floor was the pale hard floor Lira had stood on at the orientation, true and fast and honest, and it gave neither of them anything but each other.

Cael was at the rail with Rooke and Brom. Ephram had gone to watch the coast house warm up. Karis was on the next floor, through the arch, fighting the same hour.

In the first exchange Lira ran the survey she had run all year, wide and quick, and it came back wrong.

The Ash fighter's screens were the ordinary kind, the kind every regional Ash fighter used: brief clouds of grey breath let out at the hands and thrown into the air between them, a veil that hung for half a beat and then thinned.

Cael had seen a dozen fighters do it on the circuit.

They threw their veils everywhere, all the time, to look busy.

This man was a miser with them.

Every veil went up exactly where it covered something he meant to do. One went up to hide a step to his left, and one to hide a change of hands, and one to hide nothing at all, so that she would learn to wonder.

Lira had built her whole year on seeing a little more of the other fighter than the other fighter wanted to show. For the whole of that exchange she saw precisely as much as he allowed.

He touched her on the hip at the end of a veil she had walked straight into, and the flags went up. Then he touched her again on the shoulder, out of the next one, and the flags went up again.

The bell went with the third veil still hanging in the air between them.

Nought to two.

Lira walked back to her chalk and stood there breathing, looking at the grey haze thinning over the floor. It was the first opening exchange she had lost all year.

"He's not hiding himself," said Brom, beside Cael. "He's hiding what he's about to do."

"I know." Cael was watching Lira's shoulders. "She knows too."

Rooke kept his mouth shut at the interval. He handed her water over the rail, and she drank it, and handed back the cup, and he took it.

In the second exchange she stopped looking.

Cael saw her decide it, in the first three breaths. If the grey was going to tax her eyes, then she would stop spending them.

She came in at a walk, and then at a run, and then at a walk, the way Ephram did, and every time the grey went up she did not try to see through it. She listened.

The floor rang. That was what she had said about it at the orientation: that it was honest and would give her nothing. It gave her one thing, which was that it was honest.

Every step the Ash fighter took struck the hard pale boards with a clean small sound, and no veil he could throw would muffle it.

Behind the grey Lira could not see his left foot go, but she could hear it, and on the third veil she heard it go and burst to where it was going before it arrived.

Her palm landed on his ribs out of the cloud. The flags went up.

It was not clean. Listening cost her something every time, and Cael could see what it was from the rail. She was a beat late on everything, because she had to wait for the sound before she could go, and a fighter who waits for a sound has given away the first half of every beat to whoever makes it. Twice she went to where his foot had landed and found him a stride further on, because the sound had told her where he had been and not where he was going.

The Ash fighter noticed. He was quick about it. Halfway through the exchange he began to land soft, on the outsides of his feet, and once he put a foot down hard on purpose and went the other way.

She heard that one, and went to it, and he touched her from behind the grey. It was a feint of the foot without the weight in it, and it was a clever one.

Lira stood very still for half a breath after the flags went up. Then she nodded, once, to herself, and the next time a foot came down hard she did not go to it. She waited for the second sound, the one that came when the weight arrived, and went to that instead. She touched him once more, cleanly, a breath before the bell.

Two to one. Two to three on the bout.

At the second interval she came to the rail. She did not drink.

She stood with her hands on it and her eyes half shut, the way she had stood on the floor at the orientation, and Cael understood that she was listening to the hall.

"Feet," she said, to nobody. "I'm fighting his feet." She opened her eyes. "It's slow. I'm a beat late on everything, because I'm waiting to hear."

"Then don't wait," said Cael.

She looked at him.

"Price his roads," he said. "The way you did in the yard. You don't need to see him to make the roads dear. You only need to know where they go." He nodded at the floor. "The floor tells you that. It's a square. It's got the same roads with your eyes shut."

Lira looked at the floor for a moment longer. Then she smiled, very slightly, and went back to her chalk.

The third exchange was the corner game.

She did not go at him at all. She burst once, short, to a place on the floor that was nowhere near him, and stood there, and the Ash fighter threw a veil out of pure habit and stepped behind it to his left.

She heard the step. She had already been standing in his left road, so he paid a burst of his own to go round her. She heard that too, and was in the next road before he landed.

He threw a veil there as well, and she walked through it without looking and stood where his feet were about to be.

He had a great many veils. He spent them like a clerk who has found that the ledger no longer balances, faster and faster.

They hung in the air all over that side of the floor, grey on grey. Inside them Lira moved from road to road by the sound of his feet, and made every one of them cost him something.

She touched him three times in that exchange. He touched her once. On the third of hers the flags went up and the exchange closed early, as the rules said it would, and the hall made a noise Cael had not heard it make all morning.

Three to one. Five to four.

At the third interval the Ash fighter's coach leaned over the far rail and said something to him, short and fierce, with one hand making a flat cutting motion. Cael read the gesture without needing the words. *Stop spending. Hold them back. Make her wait.*

The Ash fighter nodded. He stood on his chalk breathing and looked at his own grey hands, and Cael saw him count something on his fingers, very small, against his thigh.

"He's counting what he's got left," said Brom.

"He hasn't got enough," said Cael. "He spent it buying the first exchange. He'll have three, maybe four, and she knows where every one of them will go now."

Lira did not look at the far corner at all. She stood on her chalk with her eyes closed and her head a little on one side, listening to the hall. A broom was going somewhere in a corridor. A man was coughing in the upper gallery. Under all of it, on the far side of the floor, a pair of feet in soft wraps shifted their weight from one to the other, and she was listening to that.

In the fourth exchange there were no more veils. He had spent them. He threw one at the start out of habit, and it came out thin and patchy and hung for less than half a beat. After that he fought her plain, on the hard pale floor under the glass, with nothing to hide behind.

Lira did not need to hear him any more. She could see him. She did not waste the advantage.

Three to nothing, closed early, in a breath under half the bell.

The flags stayed up a long moment after the last touch, and the clerk at the end of the hall called the total. Eight to four.

The fighter behind could not have drawn level with the one exchange remaining, and so the bout was over in four.

The Ash fighter stood on the floor for a moment, looking at his hands. Then he walked across to her and held out one of them, the grey wraps still on it, and she took it.

"You stopped looking," he said. He sounded more interested than anything.

"You made it too dear," said Lira.

He thought about that, still holding her hand, with the grey wraps between them.

"I'll remember it," he said. "Next cycle I'll have something you can't hear." He let go. "You'll have something too, I expect."

"I expect so," said Lira, and meant it, and the two of them walked off the floor in opposite directions like people who would quite like to meet again.

The figures went up on the hall's board eleven minutes later, at the proper interval. Lira's was twenty-six, the Ash fighter's twenty-four.

Karis had won next door in the same hour, and come back through the arch in time to see the fourth exchange from the end of the rail.

She had fought a Rune Path fighter from a river house, a careful, methodical man who laid his marks out on the floor in a slow and beautiful order, like a scribe ruling a page.

Karis had read the order in the first exchange, and in the second she had begun to arrive at each mark half a beat before he finished it. Three and nought; two and one; three and nought.

Eight to one, with two exchanges still to fight and the river fighter unable, even with every point in them, to draw level. The clerk had stopped it after the third.

She came along the rail and stood by Rooke, and he looked at her and nodded, once.

"You two are in different halves," he said. "I'll say it once more, so that nobody has to wonder. Lira's pool and Karis's pool don't meet until the final, and not anywhere before it. So you can stop looking at each other's sheets."

He tapped the program in his hand, open at the two columns. "Solve the bout in front of you."

"I wasn't looking at hers," said Karis.

"You were looking at hers all night," said Lira, coming up the floor to the rail with her wraps still on. "I saw you."

"I was looking at the Ash fighter in her pool. That isn't the same thing."

Lira leaned on the rail beside them and drank half a cup of water at once. Then she turned to the delegation and gave them what she had learned in a single line, as she always did.

"At home they wanted to know if I was good," she said. "Here they want to know if I'm good with my eyes shut."

She put the cup down. "He picked out the one sense I can't do without and sent me the bill for it. In the first round."

Cael wrote her margin that night under the Daeva line and the Umber line, and showed it to her before he closed the book, and she read it and nodded.

*She won by changing, not by being ahead. At the meets she had both. Here there's no room to spare, so the changing has to do all the work. Zerin's figures say Zerin brought her room with her.*

# Chapter 32 — Seat Twelve

Brom's first bout was the shortest of the morning, and the comedy of it was over before most of the hall had found its seats.

His opponent was a Copper from a southern house, a strong, eager young man with a good deal of shoulder. He came out of his corner at the bell already turning to Brom's left, and he went for the old seam on the left side as straight as a dog goes for a dropped sausage.

Cael, at the rail, knew exactly where he had read about it. There was an old edition of Brom's file in circulation, the first one the shops had made, four close pages with the seam laid out on the third. The shops had sold a later one since. This young man had bought the cheap one.

Brom let him come.

He did not turn to meet him. He did not need to. The seam had been mended since the slate country, and the door on his left side was the strongest in the house, and he stood there and let the young man arrive at it like a carter arriving at a toll-gate he had forgotten was there. The funnel took him in. Brom's left forearm came across and closed, and his right came through, and the flags went up.

Three to nothing in the first exchange. Three to nothing in the second. In the third the young man tried something different, and it was not different enough. Three to nothing, and the clerk stopped it, because nine points was more than anybody could have come back from with two exchanges left.

The young man stood in the middle of the floor looking at his hands, much as the Ash fighter had looked at his. Then he looked at Brom.

"It said your left," he said. He sounded hurt.

"It did," said Brom kindly. "It's an old edition."

The second bout, in the early afternoon, was the real news of Brom's day.

His opponent was a Stone Path from one of the hill houses in the far north-west, and Karis knew him before he had taken off his coat. She had his house on her map, pinned in a knot of hill slips with a red thread, and she leaned over to Cael at the rail while the man was still warming up.

"Watch him between exchanges," she said. "He won't stand still. None of them do. They train on stone, and stone's cold, and nobody in those hills has stood still between two exchanges since they could walk."

He was a short, square, quiet man with a Copper rank on his card that nobody on the rail believed for a moment. He had warmed up for an hour before the bout, in a corner of the hall, alone, steadily, until the sweat ran off him. He was the buried kind, Brom said later. Some house had kept him at Copper for its own reasons, and he had taken its purses, and now he was here.

Brom warmed up in the opposite corner the way he always did, briefly, with Rooke's nineteen exercises run through once at half speed and the last of them twice. He did not look at the hill fighter at all. Cael, who knew him, saw that he was taking a good deal of trouble not to.

"He'll keep moving," said Rooke, at the rail, as Brom came past to hand over his coat. "All of it, the whole bout. Don't chase him."

"I don't chase," said Brom.

"You don't. But you've never had anybody run rings round you on purpose." Rooke took the coat. "Let him run. Running costs."

At the bell he did not come in. He went round.

He circled Brom at a walk, and then at a trot, and then at a walk, never still, round and round the fourth floor's grey boards. Brom had to turn to follow him, and every turn was a turn toward a door. Brom turned right, and that was nothing. Then the hill fighter changed direction without warning and Brom turned left after him, and in the middle of the turn, in the space of a breath while the guard went from one hand to the other, the hill fighter came in.

The door held. It was the strongest door in the house, and it held. But to keep it shut Brom had to stop turning and plant, and while he planted, the hill fighter went round him the other way and touched him on the right shoulder, from behind.

Then he did it again.

Brom took one back, a plain heavy drive through the middle when the hill fighter misjudged his circle by half a stride. It was the only time in the exchange that the hill fighter was where Brom could reach him, and Brom did not waste it. He put the whole of his weight behind the drive, and the hill fighter went back two strides and very nearly sat down, and the hall made a low sound of appreciation. Then the bell went at one to two.

"He's not going for the door," said Lira, at the rail. "He's using it. Every time Brom shuts it, he has to stand still to do it."

"Brom knows," said Cael.

Brom did know. In the second exchange he stopped following.

He planted in the middle of the floor on the first breath and stayed there, and let the hill fighter go round him. He turned his head and nothing else. When the hill fighter came in from the right he met him, and when he came in from the left Brom did not turn to the door at all. He let the man come all the way to him, close, and took the touch on his forearm, and drove through it with the other hand.

It was not pretty. It was a big man standing still in the middle of a floor and letting a quick man hit his arms, and twice the gallery laughed, not kindly, when the hill fighter went round him three times in a row and Brom only turned his head. But every time the hill fighter came close enough to touch, he came close enough to be touched, and Brom's hands were heavier than his.

Two to one, Brom's way. Three each, on the bout.

The hill fighter walked his circle in the interval, round and round his own corner, never still, the sweat steaming off him under the lamps. He was looking at Brom the whole time. Cael could see him thinking.

---

He had thought of something by the third exchange, and it was a good thing.

He stopped circling. He came straight at Brom down the middle of the floor, fast, as though he had decided to fight like everybody else after all. Brom, planted, met him, and for a moment it was a plain exchange of weight, Iron Skin against Stone, the heavy dull sound of it carrying all through the hall. Brom's drive landed first, and the flags went up for him. Then the hill fighter stepped out of it to the left, very quick, and Brom, who had been standing still for a whole exchange, was a beat late to move.

The hill fighter touched him on the hip. Then on the shoulder. Then, as Brom turned at last, on the ribs, and the flags went up for the third time, and the exchange closed early.

Three to one. Six to four on the bout, the hill fighter's way, with two exchanges left.

Brom walked back to his corner slowly. He did not look at the rail.

"He's learning faster than Brom is," said Ephram quietly.

"Brom's not finished," said Lira.

Rooke was at the rail, too. He had not said a word all bout. In the interval he leaned over, as Brom came past, and spoke four words, and Cael, two places along, heard them.

"Nobody stands still. Walk."

Brom looked at him. Then he nodded once and began to walk.

He walked a circle of his own in his own corner, slow and heavy, round and round, as Rooke had made them all do in the cold halls of the slate country to keep their feet warm. When the bell went for the fourth exchange he did not plant. He walked out into the middle of the floor and kept walking, round, at his own pace, and the hill fighter, who had been circling all his life, found that there was a second circle on the floor and that it was in his way.

It was not fast. Brom was never fast. But he was moving, and a moving man does not have to start moving, and the beat the hill fighter had stolen in the third exchange was not there to steal any more. When the hill fighter cut in to the left Brom was already turning left with him, the guard changing hands inside the turn so smoothly that the door was never open at all. Brom met him in the middle of it, and drove.

Three to nothing, closed early. Seven to six, Brom's way.

The hall made a sound. Most of the people in it had come to see somebody else.

The fifth exchange was the first fifth exchange of Brom's life. He fought it like a man who had fought a hundred.

Both of them were tired now. The hill fighter's circle had shrunk to a few strides, and Brom's breath was loud in the quiet between touches. They walked their two circles round each other on the grey boards like a pair of millstones, slower and slower. Once the hill fighter went for the left door, fast, with everything he had left. Brom turned left to meet him on purpose, and let him find out exactly how strong it was. That was a point. Then the hill fighter took one back with a fine low sweep that Brom did not see until it had touched him. Then Brom took another, plainly, through the middle, a breath before the bell.

The last of it came with the bell nearly up. The hill fighter tried the left door one final time, low, under the guard, with a twist of the hips that Cael had not seen him use all bout and that he must have been saving for exactly this. It was a good twist. It found the door half a hand open. And it found Brom already turning into it, so that the half hand closed on the hill fighter's wrist instead of on nothing, and Brom's other fist came through over the top, slow and plain and impossible to stop.

Two to one. Nine to seven.

The two of them stood on the floor afterward with their hands on their knees, breathing like bellows. Then the hill fighter straightened, and walked across to Brom, and held out his hand. Brom took it in both of his.

The gallery stood up for the hill fighter as he walked off. He had not won, and every person in that hall knew he had earned it anyway.

The figures went up eleven minutes later: Brom twenty-four, the hill fighter twenty-three.

"Five," said Brom, at the rail, when he could speak. He was holding a cup of water he had not yet drunk. "That's a long time to keep a door shut."

"You didn't keep it shut," said Cael. "You kept it moving."

Brom thought about that.

"So I did," he said. And then, after a moment, with the slow satisfaction of a man finding that a thing is true after all: "Rooke said walk."

The Copper bracket would run on into the second week, its quarterfinals and its semifinals, and its final before the rest day. Brom was in its last sixteen. Above him on the board, three lines up, stood Marek's name, with a chalk tick beside it.

Ephram fought the coast house's second entry in the last bout of the afternoon, on the third floor, and Rooke used it that evening to teach them all something.

The coast house's second entry was a Blade like Ephram, a tall fair young man with long arms and a long, easy, rolling stride, and he moved over the floor as though it had water on it. Karis, at the rail, had her map in her head. Cael saw her lips move as he came out. *Long in the stride. All of them. They don't know they do it.*

The first two exchanges were even. The coast Blade was good, quick and clean, with a fine high guard and a habit of drifting back out of range after every attack. He fought like every coast house, braced for a floor that might go out from under him at any moment, so that everything he did was a little longer and a little looser than it needed to be, and every bit of it had been drilled until it was very fast.

Ephram took the first exchange two to one with the plain entry he had kept all year, and the heel behind it, and the late half-turn of the front foot that had won him the mill town. The coast Blade had read none of it in any file. He met it honestly and lost honestly.

In the second he had read it. He took a point off a feint Ephram had not seen, and gave one back, and the bell went at one each. Three to two after two, Ephram's way, and the coast house's bench had begun to talk among themselves in low voices.

Then, in the third exchange, Ephram did something that made Lira grip the rail.

He let the coast Blade touch him. He did not seem to try very hard not to be touched. He went in at the same angle three times, and three times the coast Blade met him and drifted back out of range, and twice the flags went up for the coast Blade, and once Ephram missed altogether. All the time Ephram's eyes were not on the man's sword arm. They were on his feet.

The bell went at nought to two. Three to four on the bout. The coast house's bench was on its feet.

"What is he doing?" said Lira.

"Paying," said Cael.

He had seen it with the read before Ephram had finished paying. Every time the coast Blade attacked, he reset. He drifted back out of range in the same way, on the same foot, in a long rolling backward stride, and every time the stride was a hand's breadth too long. It was the length a man learns on a wet floor, where a short step slips. He had never been told about it, because everybody he had ever fought had done it too. And in that hand's breadth, at the far end of the stride, there was a moment when his weight was all on his back heel and he could not come forward again.

Ephram had spent two points finding it, and he had found it.

In the fourth exchange he went in at the same angle, and let the coast Blade meet him and drift back, and followed him back. He did not go in after the sword. He went after the stride, so that he arrived at the far end of it at the exact moment the coast Blade's weight came down on his heel. Ephram was in front of him with nothing between them, and the coast Blade could do nothing about it but watch.

Three to nothing, closed early. Six to four.

The coast Blade stood on his chalk afterward and looked down at his own feet, like a man looking at a pair of friends who have let him down. On the coast house's bench their coach had risen. She was the lean woman who had ridden alongside the wagon on the third day of the road, and she was looking at Ephram now as she had looked at Cael then, a long, unhurried look, item by item.

Ephram did not look back at her. He was walking his circle in his own corner, as Rooke had taught them all on the slate, and counting under his breath.

In the fifth the coast Blade tried to shorten his stride, and could not; it was in his legs from his childhood. Ephram took three more. The coast Blade took one, a fine touch high on the shoulder that Ephram did not mind in the least.

Nine to five.

The coast Blade shook his hand at the end with real warmth, and said something to him that made Ephram's ears go red. As they came off the floor, the coast coach passed the end of the Halcenvane rail on her way to her own fighter. She did not stop. She dipped her head to Ephram, once, briefly, without breaking her stride.

Lira saw it. "That's two of us she's nodded at," she said. "She'll run out of nods."

"She won't," said Cael. "That's not the kind of coach she is."

That evening Rooke stood at the end of the long table in the guesting-house with a cup in his hand, and talked about the third exchange for a quarter of an hour.

"He spent two points," he said, "to learn how the man went backward. Two points, in an exchange he knew he'd lose, against a bout he meant to win. And then he took the man apart in the next one with what he'd bought." He looked down the table at Ephram, who had gone pink. "That's the trade. That's the whole trade. Not one of you did it as well as that all year."

Ephram studied his plate.

"Karis told me about the stride," he said to it. "On the road. I only went and looked."

"Then Karis did the reading," said Rooke, "and you did the paying, and between you it worked. Write that down as well." He sat. "Round of sixteen. The lake house's Shield. We'll talk about her tomorrow."

Ephram wrote it down. Cael saw him do it, in the file book, under the table, and saw what he wrote: *Two points for a stride. Karis read it. Cheap.*

The hill house's clerk brought the news up the stair on the third evening, and he brought it as a grievance.

Cael was at the long table with a bowl of soup. He heard the noise before the clerk: a roar from the direction of the outer court, carrying over the roofs, and then another, and then feet pounding on the stair. The clerk stopped in the doorway with a damp printed sheet in his fist. He looked at Cael for a long moment with the face of a man who has queued all week behind a house that never queues, and has now been sent upstairs to carry its good news. Then he held out the sheet at arm's length, and dropped it on the table, and went back down without a word.

It was the exhibition frame from the boards, set in type an hour after it had been chalked. The ruled lines under the category's rules were no longer all empty. The first two had names in them.

The first was a Rhagen name. A Silver of the sixth rank, Shield Path, captain of Rhagen's squad for the team trial. The category asked every filing for its reason, and Rhagen's captain had given two words. *Doctrinal interest.*

The second was from the lake house: a Blade, a duelist of Silver's fourth rank. His reason was two words as well. *The crowd.*

Lira read it over Cael's shoulder, and whistled, softly, which she almost never did.

So nobody with a rank worth having ever filed, and the only volunteers were curious men and quarrymen." She tapped the back of the sheet. "That held while you were something the regions talked about. Here the city's telling a story, and you're in the middle of it. Now the story takes in whoever stands across a ring from you, whether he wins or not. *Again.*

On the fourth afternoon Cael went out into the city with Brom, and found that the city had a tournament of its own.

A broadside seller recognized Brom first. He was a boy of fifteen or so with an armful of damp sheets, and he stopped dead in front of them in the outer court and looked up at Brom, a long way up, and then down at the top sheet in his arms, and back again.

"You're the wall," he said.

Brom considered this.

"Am I?"

The boy showed him. Under the day's Copper results, in small type, somebody had written three lines about the bout that had gone to five against the hill fighter, and signed them with a printer's mark, and called him *the Halcenvane wall*. Brom read it twice, and paid the boy a copper for the sheet, and folded it into his coat beside the sheet with the hill on it.

"The wall," he said, when the boy had gone. He tried it again, quietly, the way he tried every true thing anybody told him. "I suppose a wall's a sort of door that doesn't open."

"Yours opens."

"Only when I want it to." He walked on, and Cael saw that he was pleased, in the slow, careful, half-hidden way Brom was ever pleased about anything said of himself. "My grandmother will cut that out and pin it over the stove, if it ever gets as far as Velmere."

The betting rows ran the length of the court's west side, a long line of chalk boards under awnings, every bracket priced to the half point and men shouting the prices. Cael's name was there after all. It was not on any board with a price beside it. It was in a small frame on its own at the end of the longest row, in neat white paint: *NO BOOK ON THE DEMONSTRATION BOUTS.* The wool town's rule had walked all the way to Norhold.

There were printers selling the day's results damp off the press, and cookshops. A likeness-seller at the end of the row had sold out of the group sheet and chalked *THE FIVE: GONE* on his board, and was taking orders for the next printing. Outside a tavern two men were arguing at the top of their voices about whether the boy with no tier was a fraud got up by a clever house, and a third was trying to sell them both a copy of his face. There were and colour-sellers with the fourteen houses' ribbons on long poles. Two small girls had set up an upturned crate and were running a fierce trade in delegation buttons, two Rhagen black for one Auremont blue and no arguing. And all through it, in every queue and tavern door and printed sheet, the city was telling itself the story of this cycle out of whatever it had to hand. There was the champion house and its Gold. There was the house from the bluff, back after eighteen years. There was the wall. And there was the boy with no tier, who had pulled two Silvers out of a fossil in an evening.

Brom leaned his arms on the court's rail and watched Cael watching. "You've been taking in every face in this court."

"It's reading me. It's only polite to read back." Below them a printed sheet went from one hand to another, and Cael saw his own woodcut face go past upside down. "The coats at home were reading my file. They wanted something out of it. These people only want a good afternoon, and I'm in it. Nobody down there cares how anything works. They want to see what happens next."

"And when the theater and the mechanism are the same thing?"

Brom asked it mildly, without looking round, in the voice he kept for the one question a season that carried the most weight.

Cael opened his mouth and found that he already had an answer, a quick one. He shut his mouth again, because the quick one was not good enough.

"Then I'd better know which of the two I'm standing on," he said at last.

Brom nodded once, and let it stand, and they walked on.

*Brom's question today: what if the show and the machinery turn out to be one thing? I had an answer ready and it was the wrong size, so I gave him a slower one, and I'm still not sure. I keep a list of Brom's questions that I answered faster than they deserved. He has never once asked a small one.*

On the fourth evening Assessor Havel sat in the second seat of the Compact's row, high over the main floor's east rail, and read the seating order again.

He had read it on the coach coming north, as he read every paper he was given, completely, and he had read its twelfth line twice. *Seat twelve: held. No plate. Stewards to keep the seat clear for the length of the sitting.* In the column for grade there was one word.

He knew the grade. Not many men in the Compact held it.

His right hand had begun to ache at the web of the thumb, as it did by every evening now, and he opened and closed it once on his knee, slowly, before he trusted it with the folder again.

He did not let himself go any further than that. He had spent his working life watching for patterns, and he knew the difference between a pattern and a guess wearing a pattern's coat. A held seat was a held seat. It meant somebody's coming was not yet settled. It did not say whose.

Below him the last Iron bout of the day was being fought, and beside him Ilsev's pen moved steadily across her lap-desk. He took the folder out of his kit and read the twelfth line once more. The notebook lay in the kit as well, flat between the second manual and the third, where he had put it on the night he packed. He could feel its edge against the back of his hand.

Ninety pages. Four written on. Eighty-six blank.

He did not take it out.

He thought, instead, of the four entries already in it, as he sometimes did at the end of a long sitting, in order, without opening the cover. Three of them were short and dry and a long while old. The fourth he had written on the bluff, in a working room during a recess, and under it, the one time in all those pages, he had added three lines of what he made of it. He had not liked himself for those three lines. He had not crossed them out either.

Nothing had happened. A chair stood empty at the end of a row because somebody had told the stewards to keep it so, and chairs stood empty for that reason at every sitting he had ever attended. The notebook's rule was that it held what had happened and nothing of what he made of it, and nothing had happened yet.

He put the folder back in the kit, beside the notebook, and squared it, and turned his attention to the floor.

But some while later, when the bout was over and the figures had gone up and Ilsev had begun her note, he found that he had turned his head without meaning to and was looking along the row at the empty chair. He made himself look away again.

Cael saw a steward defend it that same evening.

He had climbed the east stair to the public gallery to look at the Compact's row close to, after four days of charting it from below. The crowd in the public gallery next to the row was thick, and a stout man with a broadside and a meat pie had pushed along the end of the public bench until he was at the rail-cloth with the registry's grey sigil on it, and beyond it, a single empty chair.

He sat down in it.

A steward was at his shoulder before the pie had reached his mouth. She did not raise her voice. She spoke to him low and quick, with a hand on the back of the chair, and the stout man got up, red to the ears, and went back along the public bench. The steward brushed the seat with her sleeve when he had gone. Then she went back to her post at the head of the stair and stood there with her hands behind her back, looking at nothing, as though it had never happened.

It was the third time Cael had seen it done.

He looked along the row. He had known it for four days by face and by seat. Ilsev had the first seat, the senior evaluation seat, with her files squared on her lap-desk at an angle that had not shifted by a hair since the opening. He remembered that exact, unhurried care from a hearing room and from the bluff, and in every act of hers that he knew of she had been perfectly correct. Havel had the second. He was the quiet records officer from the bluff, older than Cael remembered him. Cael had hardly exchanged a word with him, and yet he had turned up at the edge of Cael's life at Ardenmere and at Greyvane and on the bluff, watching and writing. Round them sat the row's ordinary people: a district officer, two clerks, a liaison man, two officials of the host city who came and went.

Every one of their chairs had a brass plate on its back with a name cut into it. He had read the plates from the floor with a borrowed glass on the first day.

Twelve had none.

He had the borrowed glass again now, and he put it to his eye. The chair was the same as the others, dark wood with a cane seat, and the registry's grey rail-cloth ran on in front of it without a break. There was a clean pale square on its back where a plate would have been screwed, and the screw holes were there, empty, from some earlier sitting. Somebody had sat in it once. Somebody would again. Just now the stewards had been told to keep it for a person whose name they had not been given.

He took the row plate by plate in his head, as he would have taken a delegation's roster, looking for the person the empty chair was waiting for. Everybody whose work was in that row already had a plate. Everybody whose work was somewhere else in the Concourse sat somewhere else. Whoever the chair was for, then, was either more senior than every name on every plate, or nobody at all. And nobody at all did not get a steward standing at the head of the stair to keep his seat.

He could not find the name. He wrote that down plainly, because the oldest rule of the notebook was that a failure went into it as clearly as a finding.

*Compact row, seat twelve. Held, unplated, empty, and guarded. Three times now a steward has cleared it, the last time of a man with a pie.*

*A brass plate is a name screwed onto a chair before the person arrives. You only do it when you're sure of them. Twelve has a steward and a brushed seat and an instruction, and no name. So somebody is sure there will be somebody, and isn't saying who. Or hasn't been told.*

*I keep a book of the people who watch me. Every one of them has a page with a name at the top. This one has a chair.*

*Blank pages are the ones that get written last.*

# Chapter 28 — The Prospectus Was Counting People

Withrow caught them up on the twelfth evening, at a waystation on a river bend where the road crossed by a stone bridge of five arches, and she was enjoying herself.

Cael saw it before she was out of the carriage. The hired carriage came over the bridge at a smart trot in the last of the light, with Bracken's face at one window and a pile of document boxes roped on the roof, and it pulled into the yard with a flourish the driver had plainly been saving for a week.

The chancellor got down before anybody could reach the door to open it for her.

She stood in the mud in her dark coat and looked round the yard, at the two plain wagons, the horses being rubbed down, the delegation coming out of the kitchen door with their supper still in their hands, and she smiled.

It was not a large smile. It was the smile of a woman who has been worrying for a very long time, and has finished, and has found that there is nothing left to do now but find out.

"Good evening," she said. "Is there any of that left?"

There was. She ate it at the long table with the rest of them, from the same pot, with Bracken on her left and Rooke on her right, and she asked about the road.

She wanted to know about everything. She asked about the coast house's coach and the green wagons with the onions, and the flag at the crossroads, and the walled yard. When Lira told her about the corner game, she put down her spoon and listened all the way through, and asked two questions at the end that showed she had understood every step of it.

Cael had never seen her away from the bluff.

On the hill she was the house, the way the long wall was the house, or the bell; she moved through it at its own pace and was part of its furniture. Here she seemed smaller and quicker, and a good deal younger. She laughed once at something Ephram said, a short surprised laugh that made Ephram go pink to the ears and stay that way for the rest of the meal.

Bracken ate steadily and said very little. He had the year's last administrative pouch on the bench beside him, and once in a while he put a hand on it to make sure it was still there.

When Brom asked him how he had found the faster road, he thought about it seriously.

"Fast," he said at last. "I slept like a quarry stone. The chancellor read the whole way, by daylight and then by a lamp, and I don't believe she slept at all."

"I didn't need to," said Withrow. "I'd already done all my not sleeping. I did it over the last eighteen years, a little at a time, so as not to have to do it now."

When the pot was empty she stood up, and the table went quiet without being asked.

"I'm not going to make a speech," she said. "I've made enough of them this year to last the house a generation, and most of you have heard every one. I only want to say one thing, and then I'll go to bed, because Bracken is right that I have not slept."

She looked down the table, at each of them in turn. She took the reserves in, and Gault, and Seln at the far end with his file, and Rooke, and then the five.

"Once we're through that city's gate, nobody there will see a house on a hill," she said. "They'll see you. Eight thousand people will sit round that floor, and not one in a hundred of them will ever climb our road or read our charter or eat in our hall. That isn't a weight I'm handing you; it's only how cities are. I've watched you all year. Go on exactly as you are." She picked up her cup. "The tournament will measure the five of you. The continent will measure all of us. Good night."

She went up the stair. Bracken gathered the pouch and followed her, and after a moment Rooke followed him, and the table was left looking at the place where she had stood.

"She means it," said Karis quietly.

"She means all of it," said Lira. "That's what's so alarming about her."

Cael wrote it down later that night, in the room he shared with Brom and Ephram, with Ephram already asleep and Brom pretending to be.

He wrote her sentence first, exactly. Then he sat for some time with the pen over the page, working out what she had meant by it. With Withrow there was always a second thing under the first, and the second was usually the one that mattered.

Fourteen houses would walk onto that floor. Most of them would be measured on the strength of their brackets and their rosters, on deep benches and long programs and champions held in reserve.

Halcenvane had none of those things. It had brought a Wind girl that another house had thrown away, and a big quiet Copper that the paper priced at half his weight.

It had brought a researcher who read charters in her sleep, and a Blade who had become a captain this year without noticing, and a boy the registry could not name. It had brought, in the end, a list of people the paper had got wrong.

And Withrow was proud of that list. She had not chosen it in spite of the paper. She had built a whole house on the idea that the paper was wrong about people, and she had chosen it because of that, and the city was going to be the examination.

*Twelfth day. The chancellor came up by the faster road and she's happy. I've never seen it before. It makes her look about ten years younger and twice as dangerous.*

*She said the tournament will measure the five of us and the continent all of us.*

*It's the second half of it that's hers. Eighteen years of building, and the whole house rests on one claim: that the paper is wrong about people. Every person on our roster is an instance.*

*We are not her argument because we're better than the paper says. We're her argument because we're different from it, in five directions, and she means to put the five directions in front of the largest audience on the continent and let them look.*

*I'm the sharpest instance on the list. I'm also the least representative, and I think she knows that too, and has decided it doesn't matter.*

He blotted it and sat looking at the page.

"You're thinking very loudly," said Brom, from the dark.

"Sorry."

"I didn't say stop." The bed creaked as Brom turned over. "She shook all our hands at the gate. Did you notice who was last?"

"Seln."

"Seln." Brom was quiet a moment. "She didn't have to do that. Nobody would have minded if she'd stopped at Gault." Another creak. "She's building something. I don't know what it is, but I know what building looks like. My grandmother used to stand in a field like that, looking at it, before she'd decided where the barn went."

Cael turned down the lamp.

On the fourteenth day the road began to sort itself.

It happened slowly, over a morning, and for a long time none of them noticed it. The road had been busy for a week. For the last two days it had been crowded, with three lanes of traffic where there was room for one, the waystations full by noon and the late arrivals sleeping in their wagons in the yards.

Then, somewhere in the middle of the fourteenth morning, the crowd changed its shape.

The freight moved over. Nobody told it to. There were no marshals on that stretch of road, no flags, no officers on horseback.

But the long ox-drawn wagons of timber and grain and stone, which had held the crown of the road for a week by sheer weight, began one after another to draw onto the verge side and let the academy columns by.

A carter would look back over his shoulder, see colours coming, and edge his team across without a word. A coach with the mail held back at a narrow bridge to let a delegation's wagons cross first.

By noon the whole road had arranged itself into two streams. The heavy traffic ground along on the left, and the delegations moved freely up the middle past timber and grain and stone and the patient heads of oxen, and it held that way mile after mile, as though somebody had drawn it up in an order and posted it at every waystation.

Ephram stood up on the second wagon's bench to see better. "Who told them?"

"Nobody," said Rooke. "Every cycle. It's been happening on this road since before I ever drove it."

"Why do they do it?"

Rooke shrugged. "Because they did it last time."

Karis had been watching the whole thing from the tailboard, with her map shut on her knee and her eyes going from the carters to the columns and back. Now she put the map away inside her coat.

"The city starts here," she said. "Everything from now on is just the city getting closer together."

Cael looked at the two streams of traffic going north together, the one giving way to the other, and nobody anywhere making them.

A little further on, at a ford, a big timber wagon had gone in too deep and stuck, and the delegation columns stopped behind it without anybody's ordering them to. For the better part of an hour the green wagons' fighters and the coast house's riders stood up to their knees in cold water with their shoulders against the timber, and Brom and both reserves with them. The carter apologized to every one of them in a dialect nobody could follow.

When it came free, the carter drew straight over onto the verge side and waited, hat in hand, for every column to go by him before he went on.

Cael had spent the year among rules. He had watched a registrar certify a list and a charter put him on a roster, and a whole circuit shift around one old sentence that nobody had remembered writing. He had learned how much a written rule could hold.

But here was a rule that nobody had written, and that nobody enforced, holding a hundred carters to the side of a road on a cold bright morning a long way from any office.

It held because everybody on the road expected it to. Somebody had done it once, and somebody else had done it the cycle after, and now it was the road.

He wrote it down at the next halt in four lines, and the last of them said: *Customs don't need a clerk. That's what makes them so hard to argue with.*

On the seventeenth afternoon the road topped its last rise, and Norhold was simply there, the whole of it in one moment.

The road had been climbing since the morning through farmland and orchards and small grey towns that ran one into the next without much space between them. The traffic was thick and slow. Cael had been working the Log against his knee and had not looked up for some time.

Then the wagon came over the crest of the hill and stopped.

The wagoner had not been told to stop. He had simply pulled up, and so had the driver of the second wagon behind him, and so had the carter alongside, and a coach behind that.

For the length of a long breath the whole road at the top of the rise stood still, every face on it turned the same way.

The land fell away in front of them to a wide river plain, and the plain was full of city.

Cael had seen cities. He had lived in Ardenmere, which had been the largest place in his life until this moment, and he had walked Ostrand's market and Greyvane's terraces and the confluence's three-deep quays. None of them had prepared him.

This was not a town that had grown large. It was a place where four great roads came down out of the hills and met a river wide enough for sea barges, and something had been building on that meeting for a very long time.

There were three walls. He could see all three from the rise, one inside another, each of a different stone and a different age. The outermost was pale and so long that its far end was not wall any more but simply part of the country. There were gate towers along it with flags hung down their whole height.

Inside it lay a river harbour, with masts showing over the rooftops in a grey thicket and a line of great timber lock gates where the river came in.

And in the middle of everything, rising out of the city's heart in tier on tier, flagged and roofed and catching the late sun on a hundred faces of stone, was the thing on Karis's map that the map had called the Concourse.

"That's not a building," said Ephram.

Nobody answered him. Cael was counting, because counting was what he did when something was too large to take in any other way. He could make out separate halls, joined by roofed galleries and stepped tiers into one structure. There were seven, eight; perhaps eleven.

Two tall towers stood at either side of the largest, east and west, paired like gateposts.

He knew from Rooke's briefing that the main floor alone sat eight thousand. Looking at it from the rise, he believed it, and believed too that the figure was modest.

He tried to fit Ostrand inside the outer wall and found that it went in comfortably, with room left over for its fields. He tried Ardenmere and found it would have made a decent district, with a market and two good streets.

"Four times Ostrand," said Karis slowly. She had her notebook out and was not writing in it. "The prospectus said four times Ostrand." She looked at the city for a long time. "The prospectus was counting people. It wasn't counting that."

Brom had stood up in the wagon to see, and stayed standing, one hand on the canvas hoop. He looked at the city for a long time with his face quite still. Then he said, slowly, "My whole village would go in one of those towers. With the cows."

"With the cows on the upper floors," said Ephram.

"They'd like it," said Brom seriously. "There'd be a view."

Gault had not moved from his place by the case. He was looking past all of them at the Concourse with an expression Cael had never seen on him, part wonder and part something that looked very much like dread.

"It's bigger," he said at last, to nobody. "I'd have sworn it was big enough last time." He put his hand on the case's handle. "I suppose they've added to it. Or I've shrunk."

Lira said nothing at all.

Cael noticed it, because Lira always had something to say about a new place, and usually something rude.

She sat on the tailboard with her hands loose in her lap and watched the city, and her face had gone quiet in a way he had seen only a few times.

Later, at the foot of the rise, when the wagons had started again and the city had dropped behind the nearer roofs, he asked her.

"Fenmark used to talk about it," she said. "The older girls who'd been. They'd tell it at supper, the year before a cycle, how big it was and how the floors rang and how the crowds sounded. Every year they told it, and every year it got bigger." She was looking at the road ahead.

"The year they sent me home, I stopped hearing it. I put it out of my head on purpose, because I thought if I ever got here I'd find out they'd made it all up, and I didn't want to have been the sort of girl who believed them."

"And?"

"It's bigger than they said." She nearly smiled. "Good. I couldn't have stood it if they'd got it right."

They lay that night at a crowded inn under the outer wall, five to a room, and entered by the north gate at midmorning on the eighteenth day.

The gate was a passage through the wall longer than the covered walk at home, with a vaulted roof and lamps burning in it at midday.

There was a queue. Delegations waited their turn in it with their wagons nose to tail, and tournament marshals in grey coats with a silver bar at the collar walked down the line checking papers and giving out lettered tallies.

They worked quickly, without fuss, as people work at a task they have done every three years for longer than any of them has been alive and have long since stopped finding remarkable.

Inside the gate there was a board.

It stood in a square just beyond the passage, under a little roof of its own, and it was painted fresh: fourteen panels, each with a house's name and its colours. Some of the panels already had a chalk tick beside them. Cael read them from the wagon as it came out into the light.

There was Auremont's deep blue and silver, and Rhagen's black and a cold glass grey. There was a russet he did not know until Karis said "Ternhall" beside him, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

There were the coast house's colours, already ticked, and a green he recognized from the onion wagons. There was a lake house with no tick, which Karis said was the one on the recount. And there, eleventh from the top, in letters no larger than anybody else's, was Halcenvane.

A marshal chalked a tick beside it while they watched.

Withrow had come up from the carriage behind to stand at the second wagon's wheel and see it done. She stood with her hands in the pockets of her dark coat and looked at the panel with Halcenvane's name on it, eleventh of fourteen, for longer than a panel of painted wood required. She did not say anything. When the marshal had moved on to the next tally, she reached up and laid one gloved hand flat on the wagon's side for a moment, like a rider laying a hand on a horse that has brought her a long way. Then she went back to her carriage.

Bracken, at her elbow, had his pocket-book out. He wrote something in it, briefly, and closed it, and Cael would have given a good deal to know what a registrar wrote on the day his house was ticked onto the continent's board.

The road from the gate ran straight into the city as a wide processional way, between tall houses with shops on their ground floors.

It was hung all the way along with banners, the fourteen houses' colours strung across the street on lines, and between every house's colours the same silver bar on grey that had flown at the crossroads. Crowds stood along the way.

They were not waiting for anybody in particular. They were simply watching the delegations come in, as people in a harbour town watch the ships, and talking about each one as it passed, knowledgeably, with their arms folded.

A man near the second wagon's wheel had a printed list in one hand and a pie in the other, and he was explaining the delegations to his neighbour as they passed, with his mouth full. He did the coast house, and the green wagons, and a southern house in yellow, and he gave each of them a sentence and a placing.

Then the plain wagons came level with him and he read the colours off his list and stopped chewing.

"That's the clause house," he said. "Halcenvane. The one with the boy."

His neighbour craned. "Which is the boy?"

The man looked along the tailboard, from Lira to Brom to Karis to Cael and back again, and could not decide, and Cael saw him try to fit each of their faces to something he had read and fail. In the end he shrugged and took another bite of the pie.

"Second round for the lot of them," he said, through it. "Bar the Wind girl. And the boy doesn't count, because he's not in anything."

Lira, on the tailboard, smiled at him so sweetly that he dropped his pie.

At the far end of the processional way, high over the roofs, stood the Concourse, and on its nearest face was a gate.

It was a great arch between two square towers, and from the top of it hung a banner.

It was too far off for detail. It hung straight down in the still air, long and heavy, silver on grey, and it was older than the gate it hung from. Cael could see that even from a quarter of a mile away; the grey had the colour of old pewter.

It was the same device as the crossroads flag, and nothing like it at all. The crossroads flag had been a signpost. This was where the signpost led.

Rooke answered before anybody had asked. "That's the champion's gate. It hangs there from the end of the last cycle until somebody earns the right to take it down at the end of this one. The charter says so."

Cael looked at it for a long time as the wagons crawled up the processional way. Somewhere in that building was the other thing with the same name, the reference profile in its sealed case. He thought about the two of them, the cloth outside and the tables within, and how the city had hung the one where everybody could see it.

He wrote it out that night, in a room at the top of a guesting-house, with the noise of the processional way still coming up through the shutters.

*Two things in that building carry the same name. One is a set of tables in a sealed case that nobody may touch for the length of a cycle. The other is a strip of old cloth hung where the whole city can see it, over the gate the last winners walked out of.*

*They've put the honour on the outside and the measure on the inside, and given them one name between them, so that nobody ever has to choose which of the two to love.*

*Every argument I've had this year has been with one piece of this at a time, a clerk here and a clause there. Here the whole of it stands in one place, out in the weather.*

*This city is the system with the roof off.*

---

The delegation quarter lay on the river side of the Concourse, a district of tall narrow guesting-houses with balconies over the street, and Halcenvane had the third floor of one of them.

It shared the house with two smaller academies, a hill house on the floor below and a southern house above. It also shared it with a keeper, who stood at the foot of the stair and counted every guest who went up it aloud, in a soft voice, like a shepherd counting sheep through a gate.

He counted the reserves twice. When Brom went past him he stopped counting for a moment, looked up, and said, "Well," and then went on.

Seln took the delegation's papers to the registration hall within the hour.

He went alone, with the travel file under his arm and the roster in a folder of its own, and he was back before Rooke had finished arguing with the keeper about the beds. He had the credentials for all eleven of them, threaded on a cord. He had the floor schedules and the warm-up allotments for the whole of the first week, and a printed map of the Concourse with the delegation's rooms marked on it in ink.

He laid them on the table in the front room in the order they would be needed, and squared them.

"That was quick," said Rooke.

"The queue was arranged by the names of the houses, in the order of the alphabet," said Seln. "The hall was not. One only had to find the right clerk." He took a pencil out of his coat and corrected something on the map. "The hill house's clerk is still in the queue. I believe he'll be there until the lamps."

He was. At dusk the hill house's clerk came up the stair looking grey and beaten, with half his papers. He stopped on the landing outside Halcenvane's door and looked in at Seln, who was sitting at the end of the table with a cup of tea, writing in the travel file.

Then he went on up the stair without a word, and Cael heard Ephram, in the corner, laugh quietly into his sleeve.

That evening Cael stood at the window of the room at the top of the house and counted the watchers.

He did not decide to. It was a habit, the oldest one he had, older than the read and older than the Log.

In every town for as long as he could remember he had found the coats on his first evening, the patient men on corners and in doorways, and counted them, and known where they stood.

They had stayed behind at Ostrand this time, two and two on the bell. Karis had written as much in her marbled log on the morning of the bridge. But the habit did not know that, and he stood at the window and counted.

There was a man on the balcony opposite with a notebook, watching the courtyard below. Three scouts in a hill house's colours were sitting at a table outside a cookshop, looking at every door in the street in turn.

There was a gallery over a warm-up floor across the way, with paying watchers on its benches, and a boy selling printed sheets at the corner, who knew the colour of every house that went by.

A marshal walked the street with a tally. Behind the shutters of the house next door a woman was writing, and every few lines she raised her head and looked across at the windows of the hill house's floor.

He counted twenty and lost the count, began again and lost it at thirty.

None of them was looking at him. All of them were looking at everybody, all the time. Every delegation in the city was watched from every window, by scouts and printers and gamblers and its own neighbours. The whole city had been watching fighters come in for longer than anybody could count, and it saw no reason to stop now.

Somewhere in it, perhaps, there was a pair of coats sent for him. If there was, they were two drops in a river.

He turned from the window. Karis was sitting on the end of the bed with the marbled log open on her knee. She had written the time.

Under it, in the column for the number, she had written nothing yet, and she was looking at the empty space with her pencil held above it.

"How many?" he said.

She thought about it. Then she wrote, in her square hand, two words, and showed him: *All of them.*

*Eighteenth day,* he wrote, later. *I counted out of habit and the count wouldn't close. Every house in this city is watched by every window in it.*

*For longer than I like to think about, no town I walked into had anybody in it watched harder than me. Tonight, by plain arithmetic, I'm one fighter among a few hundred, under a few thousand eyes, and nobody's in particular.*

*It's the first town of my life where nobody is looking at me. Being watched by everyone is the nearest thing to privacy this city has for sale.*

# Chapter 2 — Without Omission

His registry name was on the fifth line, and it took him a moment to know it.

*Caelen Hesk-ward, 41-7843-V, enrolled practitioner; see demonstration provision, enrollment basis, filed.*

The entry instrument lay open on the records-hall counter with its four companions stacked square beside it, and Bracken had turned it round so that Cael could read it right way up, which he did not need. It was the fourth day of term, a Fifth-day, in the hour after the morning's session let out, and the stone hall was empty except for the three of them and the clerk at the far desk, who was copying something with his tongue between his teeth. Bracken had sent a boy to the residence with the first bell, carrying a half-sheet that said *Records hall, the hour after session. Bring nothing.* Nobody else had been asked. Withrow had declined to come down, and had said so to Bracken in Cael's hearing in the covered walk the afternoon before, in five words: "It's a filing. File it."

So it was a filing, and there were three people at it.

"Read your line," said Bracken. "The charter gives an enrolled practitioner the right to inspect his own entry before it leaves the house. Nobody ever uses it. You're going to."

Cael read his line.

He had seen the name before, of course. It was on the registry's own extract in the enrollment file, and on the Greyvane transcript, and on the summons that had brought him up the bluff road in the first place. But it was not a name anybody had ever called him. It was the name a clerk at Denvash had built out of a boy and a workshop on the morning of his Kindling. The given name had been made formal, the household he came from had been turned into a word with a hyphen in it, and a number stood after it that meant nothing to anyone but the registry. Seeing it here, in a list of five, under Lira and Brom and Karis and Ephram, was like hearing his own voice played back from the far side of a valley.

"It's correct," he said.

"Good." Bracken took the instrument back. "The schedule office will cut it down to something it can print. They always do. That's their business, and none of mine."

Under the instrument lay the fee note, with the house's seal on it. Under that lay the acknowledgment of the meet calendar, initialed. And under all of them, thicker than the other four together and bound in a grey card cover with a tape round it, lay the document the charter really required: a certified copy of Halcenvane Academy's enrollment record, every name on it, current to the first of the month.

Karis stood at the end of the counter with her hands behind her back, as if she did not trust them near the paper.

Bracken opened the copy at its last leaf. The leaf was ruled, and near its foot, above a blank space and a line for his name, ran a single printed sentence that every registrar on the continent signed many times in a year.

*I certify this record complete and true, containing every practitioner enrolled at this institution on the date given, without omission.*

He read it through. His lips did not move, but Cael watched his eyes go along it twice, at the pace of a man walking a fence he has walked every day and has suddenly been told might be a boundary.

"Twelve years," said Bracken, "I've signed that sentence. More times than I could count, and I can count most things." He uncapped his pen. "In twelve years I never once read the last two words as anything but the end of a page. *Without omission.* Somebody wrote those in, a long time ago, because somebody else had left a name out of something, and it had cost a person dear. Every phrase in a form is a scar from some old fight. You forget that. You sign over the scar and think it's only printing."

He signed. Then he shook sand over the wet signature from the little box he kept by his elbow. It was a habit from the days before the house bought blotting paper, which nobody had been able to break him of in twelve years either. He tipped the sand back into the box with the edge of his hand.

"There." He tied the tape round the cover. "Now listen, because I'll say this once and you are a person who writes things down."

Cael waited.

"This morning I have decided nothing about you." Bracken said it to the tape, knotting it. "I wasn't asked to. Your name is on that record because you're enrolled, on a lawful basis, adjudicated in public, and renewed by a panel in front of the Compact's own chairs. The charter says the record goes, so the record has gone. A registrar who decides things is a registrar who will one day be asked, under oath, why he decided them. In twelve years nobody has asked me that question, and nobody is going to begin with this file." He looked up then, over the counter, and there was something under the dryness of his face that Cael had seen there only once before, on the step of a grain factor's counting house with his hand flat against a door. "When somebody comes to look, and somebody will, they'll find a clerk's routine with nobody's fingerprints on it but the form's."

"Thank you," said Cael.

"Don't." Bracken slid the bundle into its leather wallet and turned the flap down over the house's seal. "If you must thank somebody, thank whoever drafted that form. He's been dead for sixty years, and nobody has ever thanked him for anything."

He pressed the wallet's catch shut and set it on the counter's end, squared to the edge, for the courier.

"Noon," he said.

---

Karis wanted to see it leave the hill.

"Once it's in the courier's bag it belongs to somebody else," she said on the records-hall steps. "I've been minding it for twelve weeks. I'd like to watch it go." She said it the way she said anything about her own feelings, as a finding she had checked and was now reporting, and Cael went down the bluff road with her as far as the first bend. A stone bench stood there under a thorn the wind had bent half over, and the whole valley lay open below it, river and bridge and town and the long brown fields going away to the hills.

The noon courier came down past them at a working trot, a lean man on a grey, with the house's wallet strapped flat inside a case against the saddlebag. He touched his hat to Karis without slowing. They watched him take the switchbacks one after another, a rider and then a small rider and then a speck with a grey under it. At the road's foot he went by the two men in road-grey coats just as the other two came up the river street to meet them, unhurried, on the bell. None of the four so much as turned a head. Nobody ever looks at a courier; that is what couriers are for.

Down on the Ost the ferry lay in at the near bank. The grey went aboard with a farmer's cart and two women carrying baskets of eggs, the ferryman leaned on his pole, and the whole cargo went out across the brown water together, the house's entry for the Continental riding between a cart of turnips and the eggs.

"Well," said Karis. "That's done."

She did not get up off the bench. She had the grey notebook open on her knee and she was not writing in it, and Cael, who had seen her sit over an open page without writing perhaps three times in two years, stood where he was and waited.

"Be the regional registrar," she said.

"What?"

"You're him. You've just broken the seal. You've read down to the fifth line and you don't like it, and you've got fourteen days and a clerk and a copy of the charter." She closed the notebook on her finger. "Find me the step. Find me the one place in this morning where somebody did something you can object to. Go on. You're better at hunting gaps than anybody I know."

He thought about it. It was a fair request, and she would not thank him for being kind.

"The enrollment," he said. "I object to the enrollment. The boy should never have been let in."

"Adjudicated at Greyvane in a public hearing. Renewed here, in front of an Archmarshal, by a panel of record. Two houses, two sets of seals. Next."

"The record, then. The house could have sent a shorter one."

"Then the registrar signs a certificate that says *without omission* over a record with an omission in it." She looked at him. "Do you want to be the one to write down that you'd have preferred Bracken to lie? Over his name? In ink?"

"No."

"Nobody will. Next."

"The clause." He had kept it for last, because it was the strongest. "It was written for something else. For a house that hid two good fighters to please a richer one. It was never meant for this."

"It was never meant for this," Karis agreed. "It says what it says all the same, and it's their charter, and their own board wrote it in a temper forty years ago, and nobody has touched a word of it since. If they want it to mean something narrower, they have to say so, in print, with a date on it, and then explain to every house on the circuit why the rule against hiding fighters now allows a little hiding." She opened the notebook again. "There's your hunt. Three roads. Every one of them ends at the same door, and behind the door somebody has to sit down and write out, in a clear hand, which true thing they wish had been false."

"Nobody's going to write that."

"No." Below them the ferry was most of the way across, the pole going in and out of the water in its slow rhythm. "That's the whole design."

She wrote one line then, quickly, and when she spoke again it was in a different voice, flatter, the voice she used for things she meant to be quoted on.

"Everyone's going to say we did something this morning. In a fortnight half the circuit will be telling the other half that Halcenvane played a trick at the registry, and they'll be very pleased with themselves for spotting it." She did not look up from the page. "We didn't put you on the roster. We just declined to commit fraud by leaving you off. The clause does the rest."

Cael stood with the valley in front of him and turned the sentence over, the way he turned over a guard somebody had shown him on the oak, looking for the place where it would give.

It did not give anywhere. That was the strange part. There was not one word in it that reached for anything, or decorated anything, or claimed a single thing it could not carry. It was built the way Brom built a guard: no part of it worth attacking, and the whole of it not worth the trouble. And the thing it described was not his doing at all. A clerk's record was true; a dead board's rule was binding; and everything that followed would follow out of other people's words, honestly applied, with his name at the bottom of a list like a weight at the bottom of a plumb line.

He found he wanted to thank her, and could not think of any way to do it that she would not hand straight back across the bench, so he said the only part of it that was a plain fact.

"Twelve weeks."

"Twelve of the best weeks I've had since Prynn's index." She stood, and brushed the thorn's dry leaves off her coat. "Don't make it a debt. I'd have to keep the account, and I haven't the time."

They walked back up together. Behind them the ferry grounded on the far bank, and a speck on a grey went up the road beyond the river toward the hills, and the morning's work was out of every hand on the bluff for good.

---

There was a new form in the wing's rack, and Cael saw it before he saw anything else.

It sat in the sixth pigeonhole, where the sixth pigeonhole had stood empty all last year. Buff paper, sharp-edged from the press, headed in the plain type the wing used for everything: *Tournament travel: kit, equipment and floor declarations.* Below the heading ran three columns and a set of ruled boxes for the meet hosts to countersign. It had not existed a week ago. Somebody in this wing had spent the empty weeks of the recess building the season's paperwork before anyone had told the house there would be a season, so that on the first morning of term a nervous third-year would find a form to fill in instead of a question to ask.

Cael knew who had built it. He looked at it exactly as long as a boy looks at a new form, and no longer.

He had come down in the afternoon with the term's floor-allocation sheet, because any enrollee asking for floor time tied to his evaluations had to lodge it at the counter, and the counter was the counter. The season had made floor hours into money overnight. Half the house had remembered at breakfast that it needed supervised time on the oak. Four people stood ahead of him: a Mire third-year with a bandaged thumb, two Current girls quarrelling in whispers about a bout neither of them had fought, and a Stone first-year who had plainly never been in the wing before and was holding his form in both hands like a hymn sheet.

Behind the counter the desk clerk sat at his ledger by the window, adding a column under his breath and losing it whenever the Current girls' whispering rose. Gault's door was shut, with a voice behind it. And at the copying table, rising each time the queue moved to take the next sheet, the teaching assistant did the counter's work.

He did it the same way for everybody. He took the form. He read it, every line, at an even pace, from the name to the signature. He stamped the received line, tore off the copy-slip along its rule, and gave the slip back. The Stone first-year got what the Mire third-year got, and the Mire third-year what the first-year got, down to the second it took to read the reason for the hours. Cael had worked out in his second month on the bluff what that was for. A man who read every form from every hand was a man nobody remembered reading anything in particular.

The Current girls went off with their slips, still quarrelling. Cael stepped up and laid his sheet on the counter.

Seln read it. Name. Path line left blank, by rule. Evaluator of record. The hours asked. The reason. The stamp came down on the received line with its small flat sound. The slip tore cleanly. Then, with two fingers, Seln set the sheet on the stack at his left hand and squared it to the counter's edge, and squared the stack to the sheet.

"Magister Gault signs evaluation hours on Fifth-days," he said. "The schedule goes up the morning after."

"Thank you for the form."

"Mm."

That was always all of it. Cael had his fingers on the copy-slip.

Seln said it to the stack. "Pack for the third one. The halls at the third one are cold."

He had already turned back toward the copying table. By the time Cael had folded the slip into his coat the pen was in Seln's hand again and his head was down over a ledger, and a Blade second-year with ink on his cuff had stepped up behind and was laying his own form on the counter.

Cael went out through the wing doors and along the covered walk at the pace of someone on his way to the library, and he did not allow himself the sentence until he was past the north arch. Then he allowed it once.

The third meet. By the board, it was a quarry town ten days' drive to the north-west, a name he had never heard and a date in the deep part of the season. There had been nothing on the board about halls. There had been nothing about anything, except a date and a name. Twelve empty weeks behind a counter had given the man the season, read down to the temperature of its rooms, and he had spent one sentence of it, in front of a queue, in the voice he used for the hours.

He had written a rule about that man, last year, in the common room with the door shut and the other three listening. *Gratitude is the leak.* Nothing in the recess had loosened it by a thread. So he would pack for the third one, warmly; and next Fifth-day he would say thank you for the form, and the one after that; and in no room anywhere, to anybody, would he ever say a word more.

He put it where he put everything that came across that counter, in the ledger he kept in his head and nowhere else.

*Third meet: watch it. He can't tell me why. I can't ask.*

---

Gault countersigned at the fourth bell, at his own desk, with his door open and the new calendar pinned flat on the wall behind him.

"Sit," he said, without looking up. "This is a signature, and then it's news, and the news is better than the signature."

Cael sat. Gault signed the floor form in his square careful hand, blotted it, and set it on the tray for the desk clerk. Then he leaned back in his chair until it complained, and laced his fingers on his waistcoat, and regarded Cael with the satisfied air of a man who has been looking forward to saying something for several days.

"The panel travels," he said.

Cael waited.

"Your next evaluation. The provision's second." Gault tipped his head at the calendar behind him, where a line in his own hand marked the half-month and read *Panel of record: Gault, with two. Measured against: baseline (entry), and the note of the twentieth of Reaping.* "It falls inside the qualifying season. Under the old reading of the provision you'd have had to come home for it, wherever the season had taken you, and sit it here, in this wing, on this frame. The chancellor didn't care for that. She wrote to the registry's district office. The district office, to the astonishment of everybody who has ever written to a district office, agreed by return without a hearing." He unlaced his fingers to count on them. "The enrollment basis goes where the enrollee goes. The evaluation goes where the basis goes. So the evaluation goes where you go. If the delegation is in a mill town on the day, then the panel sits in the mill town."

"With the same instruments?"

"With instruments to the same standard, checked by me on the day, the way I check these on Fourth-days." Gault's face did something that in another man would have been a grin. "I have been issued a case. For the seals and the record books and the calibration weights. It is a very solid case." He paused. "It has a handle."

Cael looked at him.

"I have never in my life been issued anything with a handle," said Gault. "Everything I own in this wing is a ledger or a drawer. It is ridiculous how much I like the handle. The porter tells me I carried it across the quadrangle twice this morning for no reason. That is not true. It was three times."

Cael found he was smiling, and let it stand, because the door was open but the wing was empty.

"And an exhibition?" Cael asked. "There's no tier to validate."

Gault took his spectacles off and looked at them, as he did when a question had gone somewhere he had not expected.

"No," he said. "There isn't." He put them back on. "So the seat will sign that it watched. The card will say *exhibition*, and the figure, and the officer's initials, and it will go into the validation book under a heading that I should think nobody has written in for forty years. Somebody will have to go and find out what the heading is called." He seemed to find this agreeable. "It will be very good for them."

"Will there be observers at the meets?" Cael asked. "From the Compact?"

"At every meet." Gault sobered, a little. "The tournament charter has the registry validate every tier result. A Copper win goes onto a Copper fighter's record only once a Compact officer has watched it and signed that the tier was what the paper said. So there will be a validation seat at every floor, every meet, a man or a woman with a badge and a book." He reached for the next form on his tray, and then did not pick it up. "Nobody will have to come up this bluff road to look at you this year, enrollee. You are going to them."

Cael carried the sentence down the wing stair like something hot and stood at the Crown yard's rail until it had cooled enough to look at.

Since Ardenmere the arrangement had run in one direction, and he had never once seen it run the other. He stayed where he was, and the apparatus travelled to him. Wardens had come to a market town. Assessors had come to a circuit hall. A delegation with an Archmarshal at its head had come up this very road in three carriages and stayed eleven days. And all that time two coats stood at the ferry landing and two at the road's foot, because the bluff was where he was, and somebody paid by the year to have it watched.

Now, in five weeks, the arrow turned round. He would climb into a wagon behind a wagoner and be driven, at a walk, into the middle of every eye the Compact owned.

He took out the observation notebook and wrote Gault's sentence under Gault's name, with the date, as he wrote everything. Then, because an honest ledger ran two columns, he wrote the other side under it.

*And for one season, every officer who comes to look at me will be seated, badged, and printed in a meet program. For three years I've counted watchers out of windows. This year they'll come with seat numbers.*

---

He told the others about the handle at supper, and it was a mistake, because Brom asked to see it.

"It's Gault's case," said Cael. "In Gault's office."

"I'd just like to see it." Brom broke his bread with great seriousness. "A man is pleased about a handle. I want to know what kind of handle. Brass. Leather. Iron with a turned grip."

"You want to know whether you'd be pleased about it."

"I'd like to know whether a reasonable man would be." Brom ate some bread. "I've never been issued anything at all. Rooke issued me a sheet, but it's a sheet."

"You were issued a cohort placing," said Lira. "Third. It's on paper strangers read. Bracken signed it at a green table."

"That's not a handle."

Karis, who had been writing, said without looking up that she would ask Gault on Fourth-day what the handle was made of, because it was the only way the subject was ever going to be closed. Brom thanked her gravely. Lira put her head down on her folded arms on the table and laughed into them until a Mire first-year at the next table turned round to see what was wrong with her.

Ephram, passing their table on his way out with his bowl, stopped behind Brom's chair.

"I've heard about the handle," he said. "The whole Blade hall has heard about the handle. Gault showed it to the porter, and the porter showed it to the kitchen." He considered Brom's upturned face. "You want to see it."

"I'd like to see it."

"Everybody would like to see it," said Ephram, and went on toward the door with his bowl, and said over his shoulder, without breaking step, "It's leather. Double-stitched. It's a very good handle," which was, Lira said afterward, the longest speech about an object that anybody had ever heard him make.

They told the rest of it after that, quietly, with the bread gone. Bracken's sand. The courier and the ferry. Karis's sentence on the bench, which Karis would not say again, and which Cael said for her, word for word, so that Lira and Brom could have it too. Lira listened to it with her chin on her arms.

"The clause does the rest," she said. "I like that. It's the first time in my life the rest has been done by somebody else's paper."

Cael said nothing about the counter, and nobody asked him about the counter, and that was the arrangement working.

Before he wrote the day up, he wrote to Hesk.

He kept it short. Hesk did not like long letters; he said a long letter was a man who had not yet decided what he meant. So Cael wrote what had happened, in the order it had happened, and stopped. The roster had been filed at noon by the registrar, with every name on the enrollment record, his own among them in the registry's spelling. The courier had gone over on the ferry with a cart of turnips. Gault had been issued a case with a handle and was unreasonably pleased about it. The first meet was five weeks out in a wool town two days to the south.

He did not write about the counter. That belonged to nobody but the four of them, and possibly not even to them.

He sat for a while with the pen lifted over the last line, because there was one more thing that was true and he did not know whether Hesk would want it. In the end he wrote it anyway, in the same hand as the rest, because Hesk had always liked a thing set down plainly whether or not it was comfortable.

*I've been measured by a great many people who wanted a particular answer. This year I'm going to be measured by people who don't care which answer they get. I didn't know until today how much I'd wanted that.*

He sealed it for the morning post and set it on the sill, and then he turned back to the Log.

He wrote the day up at the window that night. The fountain was going in the court again under a house that was full now, with a dozen lamps lit along the residence front and the sound of somebody on the floor above dropping a boot and swearing at it in a perfectly clean word. He kept it short. There would be a great deal of season, and he wanted room for it.

*Roster filed at noon. Eleven names on the travel sheet. Five on the entry. Mine the fifth, in the registry's spelling.*

*Season's open. The tournament rules say an academy fields what it enrolls. I'm enrolled. Somewhere a committee is about to have a very bad week, and for once, none of it will be my argument. It's just their own text, arriving.*

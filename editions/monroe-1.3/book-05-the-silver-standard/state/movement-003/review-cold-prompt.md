# Chapter 14 — The Bill

The circular came up the bluff road on the morning courier, two days after the wagons got home, and Rooke chalked it onto the cohort board in the covered walk before the second bell.

He did it without ceremony and without comment, as he did everything on that board.

He wiped the old figures off with the flat of his hand, wrote the new ones in their place, and stood back a pace to see that the columns were straight before he went in to the floor.

Cael read it on his way past with Brom.

The circuit's six houses were set out in a column by the points they had gathered across two meets. The river academy stood first, carried there by nine Irons and a deep draw, and Halcenvane stood second. The gap between the two of them was smaller than the gap between Halcenvane and the third house, and Rooke had written it in figures under the column, very small, as if to keep it from getting ideas.

Under that, in the cohort's own corner, were the season's lines for the five. They were plain lines of the kind Rooke wrote, with titles and placings and nothing else. Lira's said *Iron. Two titles. No bout lost.* Ephram's said *Iron, upper draw. One title.* Karis's said *Placed, both meets.*

Brom's said *Copper. Bouts unbeaten, none lost.*

Brom stopped in front of it with his hands in his coat pockets and looked at it for some time. Then he took one hand out and tapped the line once with a knuckle.

He did the same to the card on his door every night, on his way in, to remind it he was there.

"He's copied it out of the records," said Cael.

"I know." Brom put the hand back. "I've never had a line like that on a board in my life. Not in Velmere, not anywhere." He considered it. "It's a dull line. *None lost.* It sounds like a man counting spoons."

"It's the best line on the board."

"That's why it's dull," said Brom, and went in.

There was no line for Cael. He had known there would not be one, and he looked for it anyway. The exhibition category added nothing to a house's standing; the charter said so, in the provisions Karis had found on the second night of drafting. A demonstration performance was rated for the figure only, and the figure went into the meet record and stopped there. Six bouts, six wins and six figures in a tidy band, and not one point of it had carried Halcenvane a single place up Rooke's column.

It did not trouble him. It was the arrangement, and he had known its terms since the card was printed. Still, he stood there a moment longer than he needed to, reading four lines that were not his, and found that he was proud of every one of them in a way a line of his own would never have let him be.

Lira caught them up at the refectory door with her hair still wet from the wash-house. She had read the board on her way past as well, because Lira read every board she passed and could no more help it than she could help breathing.

"Second," she said.

"Second on the circuit," said Cael. "First at the mill town."

"I know what it says." She took a bowl and stood looking at the porridge without taking any. "I'm only saying it out loud so I can hear what it sounds like. Second on the circuit. Two titles each for Brom and me, and one for the champion of the upper Iron, who's going to be unbearable until the next meet. And nobody's sent the bill."

She took her porridge at last and turned toward the table. "I keep checking the post. I keep looking at people's hands to see if they're carrying it."

"You said that by the stove," said Brom.

"I'm saying it again in daylight. It's different in daylight." She sat. "By a stove it sounds wise. In daylight it sounds like a superstition, and I'd like to know which one it is."

Karis was already at the table with the grey notebook open beside her bowl. She did not lift her eyes from the page, but she turned it.

"It's not a superstition," she said. "It's a prior. You've been charged for every good thing since you were fourteen, so you expect to be charged. That isn't a feeling, it's a record, and a record counts as evidence until something better comes along."

She turned another page. "The bill's real. It just isn't addressed to you."

Lira looked at her for a moment, and then across the table at Cael, who had a spoon halfway to his mouth. He watched her work it out.

"Oh," said Lira. "You're writing it yourself."

"Two points against a grappler," said Cael. "Three on a soft floor, and a conceded point when the light's behind me. It's all costed. I'm paying it on purpose, so that nobody else gets to send it."

She ate a spoonful of porridge and thought about that, and her thinking showed in her jaw, as it always did.

"That's worse," she said at last. "You know that's worse."

"It's tidier."

"It's worse *and* tidier." She pointed the spoon at him. "That's your whole trouble in three words."

Brom laughed, and so did Cael. Ephram came in late with his new file book under his arm and wanted to know what was funny, and was told, and did not think it was funny at all, which made Brom laugh again.

But on the way to the floor afterward Cael found that he had been glad of the laugh, and that his own share of it had come a fraction later than it should have.

All through the road home he had thought of the program as a defence, a fence he was building along a boundary. Lira had turned it round in one sentence and shown him the other side of it. The bill she had been waiting for since the stove had a shape now, and the shape was his own handwriting.

---

Rooke had taken the training sheet on the evening they got home and given it back the next morning with nothing written on it, which was his way of saying he meant to think about it.

It was a single page of the kind every fighter in the cohort handed in at the start of a block. Cael had written it with care. He had not written the whole program on it, because the program lived in the Log and nowhere else, but he had written the parts that needed a floor and somebody to work against. At the top of the list, in his plainest hand, was the first item.

*Slow first exchange against a grappler or leverage fighter. Concede the first touch. Win from behind.*

Rooke found him at the edge of the sprung oak on the morning of the Fourth-day, with the sheet in his hand.

"Slow how?" he said.

Cael had expected the question, though not so soon. "Slow enough to be caught."

"That's not an answer, that's a result." Rooke folded the sheet in half along its crease and held it up between two fingers. "A fighter can be slow three ways. His feet can be late, or his eyes can be late, or he can see the thing in good time and decide late. Which of those three are you buying?"

"The feet."

"Only the feet?"

"The eyes stay where they are."

Rooke looked at him a moment longer. Cael had the feeling he often had with Rooke, that a whole conversation had just taken place on the far side of the man's face and that he would never be shown the minutes.

"Then that's the drill," said Rooke. "Early eyes and late feet. It's harder than it sounds. Your feet have been leaving early since before you came up the bridge, and they won't like being told to wait." He turned his head. "Stone. Here."

The Stone reserve came across from the bags. He was a third-year, broad and steady, and he had a Stone fighter's way of standing as if somebody had planted him and expected a crop.

He was on the delegation's roster as a reserve and on the cohort board as a man who would be very good in two years, and in all the time Cael had known him he had never once said a sentence that was not about the floor.

"You're a grappler today," Rooke told him. "Close, hold, and put him down if he lets you. He's going to let you, the first time. Don't be polite about it."

The Stone reserve looked at Cael, and at the sheet, and at Rooke. "He's going to let me on purpose?"

"On purpose. It's on the sheet."

"Right," said the Stone reserve, and went to his mark.

It was harder than it sounded, and it sounded hard.

The first time, Cael's feet left early regardless. The read gave him the reserve's weight a fraction before it moved, as it always did, and before he could stop them his feet had carried him a stride to the left and out of reach, so that the reserve closed on nothing but oak. Rooke said "Again," without any inflection at all.

The second time he held his feet, and held them too long. The reserve came in low and wide and took him round the waist like a man lifting a sack of meal, and Cael went down onto the oak hard enough to hear it, with no plan whatever about what was meant to happen next.

"That's not slow," said Rooke. "That's absent. Again."

It took the rest of the morning.

By the time the light from the high windows had walked halfway across the boards, he could do it about one time in two: the read early and clear and telling him exactly where the reserve was going, while his feet stayed where they were for one beat past comfort and then went, and went too late, so that the reserve's hands found his shoulder or his ribs and the touch was given cleanly to a man who had earned it.

Then he came back from behind, and that was the easy part, which was the strange thing about it. Once the touch had been given, his feet were his own again. The reserve was a Stone who rooted and held and was very hard to move, and not at all hard to step round.

"How does it feel?" said Rooke, at the end of it.

Cael considered the question honestly. "Like lying."

"To him?"

"To my feet."

Rooke made a small noise that might have been the beginning of a laugh, in a man who began them. He unfolded the sheet and wrote on it standing up, with the stub of chalk he kept for the boards, and handed it back.

Under the first item, in Rooke's square capitals, it said: *Early eyes, late feet. One in two. He holds like a gentleman. Find somebody who doesn't.*

Brom found him that afternoon at the bags, reading the line for the third time.

"I'll do it," said Brom.

"You're not a grappler."

"I'm not a gentleman, either." Brom took off his coat and folded it over the bench. "The Stone holds you the way Rooke told him to. He thinks about it first, and you can feel him thinking. A man who really wants a fight on the ground doesn't think about it at all. He just wants it, and you can feel the wanting through his hands."

He began to strap his right wrist out of habit, though it had been healed for a month. "My grandmother's yard was full of men who unloaded wagons all day and wrestled each other after supper for the bench nearest the fire. You learn what a hold is, growing up next to that. Come on."

So they went out onto the oak again, and Brom held him properly.

It was nothing like the first grip. The Stone reserve had taken hold of him firmly, from the outside, with some thought about where he meant to put him down, the way a careful man takes hold of a load.

Brom took hold of him to keep him. His hands came in under Cael's arms and met at the small of his back, and there was no pause in them anywhere, no beat in which they stopped to ask what came next. They closed, and went on closing.

The read had told Cael precisely where Brom would be. It had not told him what it would feel like when Brom got there.

He broke the hold with a step. It was a sharp turn of the hips away from the join of Brom's hands, and a drop, and a push off the boards. The oak gave under his heel and breathed and gave the push back, and the spring of it carried him out under Brom's arm and away. It took him two tries. The first time Brom simply followed him down and sat on him.

"That's the one," said Brom, from on top of him. "The one where you think your feet are going to come back in time."

"They came back."

"On this floor." Brom got off him and held out a hand. "This floor helps you. Everybody on the circuit knows what our oak does. I'd not want to try that on stone."

Cael took the hand and let himself be pulled up. He noted the sentence as he stood, because Brom did not say things twice and it was worth having the first time.

They ran the hold eight more times, and Cael broke it six. He wrote the costing that evening at the window of their room, with the oak's afternoon still warm in his legs.

*Item one, costed. A slow first exchange against a holder. Early eyes, late feet. Price: the first touch, given cleanly; the ground of one exchange; two points off the figure at the panel, by design. Recovery: from behind, on foot. Brom's hold is real. The Stone's is polite. On our oak I break Brom's six times in eight with a step and the floor's spring, and the two I lose are the ones where I trust my feet to come back in time.*

*Brom: "I'd not want to try that on stone." Noted. The quarry town's floor is stone.*

He looked at that last line for a while, and then added another under it, smaller.

*I've costed the touch, the ground and the figure. I haven't costed the floor.*

He meant to come back to it. There would be ten days of road in which to come back to it, and a hundred hours on a tailboard with nothing to do but think, and he was sure that somewhere in all that time he would find the place where a step that worked on sprung oak would stop working on slate.

The counter in the assessment wing was open on the Fifth-day, as it was every Fifth-day, and the queue was as long as it always was in the week after a meet. Half the cohort had coursework overdue, and the other half had travel forms they had been told to fill in a fortnight ago.

Cael stood in it with his coursework under his arm and read the notices on the wall, as he always did. There was a new one about lamp oil, and an old one about the wash-house that had been up so long its corners had gone brown. Under the window a porter was sweeping the same three yards of floor with great care, and in the courtyard outside the bell for the third hour began and finished.

Seln was behind the counter at the copying table. He looked as he had always looked: a mild, tidy man in a grey coat, with a pen that never seemed to need dipping and a face that nobody in the wing ever managed to describe afterward.

He took each folder as it was handed across, opened it, checked the cover sheet against a list, closed it, and laid it on a stack, and he did it at the same pace for every folder in the queue.

When Cael reached the counter, Seln took his folder, opened it, checked it and closed it.

"Thank you for the form," said Cael.

"Mm," said Seln, and laid it on the stack.

That was all, as it was every week, and as it had been for most of a year.

Cael went out through the wing's side door into the cold with the queue shuffling forward behind him, and did not look back, because he never did.

He was halfway across the courtyard before he realized that he had been listening at the counter for another sentence. *Pack for the third one.* He had heard it there in the first week of term, in front of a queue, said in the voice of a clerk reading out a fee table.

In two days the delegation would leave for the third one. He had packed wool. And he found, at the back of his mind, that he had half expected the counter to say something more now that the third one was so close. It had said *Mm.* That was the whole of its correspondence, and so far it had never once been wrong.

Bracken was in the records hall when Cael went to collect the meet records for the travel file. He stood behind the long counter in his black coat with his sleeves held back by bands, sanding a page, and he finished sanding it before he looked up.

Bracken never in his life let a page wait for a person.

"The quarry town," he said. "Ten days by the hill roads, and the post up there is a matter of faith." He laid the page on its pile. "So I'll send a pouch after you. The records office keeps one for the hill coach, which goes up on the Second-day and the Sixth-day, and it will find you at the quarry town if the hill coach finds the quarry town, which it generally does."

He took a canvas pouch from under the counter and laid it on the wood. It was grey and worn soft, with the academy's seal stamped on the flap and a brass ring for the lock. "Certified copies of anything the meet's clerks want from us. The chancellor's letters. And yours, if anybody writes to you."

"Mine?"

"Letters." Bracken looked at him over the counter with an expression of great patience. "Your grandfather writes, I believe, at a considerable rate. A letter sent to the town post at the quarry town sits in a box on a counter with forty others, where anybody who likes can read the outside of it. The pouch is locked here and unlocked by Seln at the far end, and what's inside it stays sealed until it reaches the hand it's addressed to."

He put the pouch back under the counter. "I don't suppose anybody on the circuit cares who writes to you. I simply prefer that they shouldn't have the chance to find out."

"Thank you," said Cael.

"It's a pouch," said Bracken, and went back to his sanding.

The grey notebook had a page near the back that Karis called the open lines. It was where she kept things that had not finished, questions without an answer yet and debts without a date, each under its own heading in her square hand with a line ruled beneath it. When a thing was finished she drew a single line through its heading, very straight, and left it there to be read through, because she did not believe in pretending a thing had never been open.

Most of the headings on that page had lines through them. One that did not was nearly the oldest thing in the book.

*Records hall, last term. The reader. Client unidentified.*

She had the page open on the common-room table that evening when he came in with the meet records for her to check against the travel file.

She was not writing under the heading. She was looking at it, as she looked at a sentence in the charter when she suspected it of something.

"Half a year," she said, without lifting her eyes. "Half a year ago, last term, a man who reads papers for money came up our stair three nights running and copied the plans of the door you came in by. Bracken caught him, and counsel has his book. He never knew who paid him, and I believe him, because the money came through a letter box by the river that somebody had taken for a month and given up."

She laid her pen across the page. "The wing wrote two lines in the incident book that night. *Pattern is consistent with private interest. Not Compact pattern.* I've thought about those two lines more than I've thought about most whole books."

"Because they were good," said Cael.

"Because they were *careful*." She looked up. "A careful note isn't the same as a closed one. Private interest is a very large country. And I don't like a door that somebody went to the trouble of having drawn, and then never came back to use."

He sat down across from her. "There was a man at the coaching inn on the road home," he said. "By the fire. He bought both meets' records out of the courier's bundle without asking the price. I watched his finger stop at the exhibitions on the wool town's leaf."

Karis wrote that down at once under the heading, small, and sat back.

"There's a trade in it," she said. "Not the reading of papers in records halls. The other end of it. Men who buy meet records and keep them in columns."

She tapped the pen on the line she had just written. "I've known that in a general way since the wool town. I hadn't thought very hard about what it would mean to be the thing in the columns."

"Does it change the line?"

"It might shorten it. It might lengthen it." She turned to a fresh page further back, wrote a heading, and ruled the line under it. *The trade in records.* "If the man at the inn and the man who took the letter box are in the same trade, then the door was drawn for a shop, and the shop is open, and the reader's client was never really a person at all."

She looked from one heading to the other. "If they aren't, I've got two open lines instead of one, and the old one's worse."

"Which do you think?"

"I don't think yet. I collect." She closed the book on her pen. "Ask me at the quarry town. Things are always clearer in the cold."

He went up to bed with the line in his head. He had carried it most of a year without looking at it much, at the bottom of his mind among the other old coins, and now Karis had taken it out and put it on the table and it looked heavier than he remembered.

Halfway up the stair he decided that he would not let it be heavy. It was an open line, not a weight.

He had a meet to fight and a program to begin, and a floor of stone he had not yet costed, and Karis would hold the line as she held everything, in a book that did not forget. He would carry the meet.

He made the decision on the stair, between one step and the next, and he noticed while he made it that a year ago he could not have done it at all.

The wagons went down to the ferry at first light on the Seventh-day, and the two coats at the landing watched them go, as two coats had watched everything go from that landing for longer than anybody on the bluff liked to count.

They were on the bell, two and two. Karis wrote the time in the marbled log without being asked, and nobody else looked at them.

Then the river was behind them, and the road ran north and west, and for ten days it did not stop climbing.

The first three days were farm country, rolling and wet, with sheep in the hollows and woodsmoke lying flat along the valleys in the mornings. On the fourth day the hedges gave out and the walls began, grey drystone walls that ran up the hillsides and over the tops, ruler-straight. On the fifth the farms got smaller and further apart and the inns got colder, and on the sixth morning there was ice in the horse trough at the waystation for the first time.

Brom broke it with his fist and declared it a fair fight.

Cael rode the tailboard, as he always did, and spent the long climbing hours on the line he had added under the costing. He tried the escape from Brom's hold in his head on every floor he had ever fought on: the wool town's pine, the mill hall's hard boards, the Ardenmere circuit's packed earth, the wash-house stone at Greyvane where he had once fallen badly and been laughed at for a week. On every floor that gave, the step worked. On every floor that did not, he could not make it work, however he turned it, and on the eighth morning he stopped turning it and wrote in the Log only *the step needs the floor's help; slate won't give it; find another answer before the bout or bring the feet back sooner.* It was not a solution, and he knew it was not.

On the eighth day the roofs changed. Until then they had been thatch and tile. Now they were slate, dark blue-grey and heavy, laid in rows that caught the light like water when the sun broke through, and the walls were slate too, and the gateposts, and the steps up to the doors.

One village had a slate trough and a slate bench by the well, and a slate step at the inn door worn into a shallow dip by a hundred years of boots.

On the ninth day the road forked at a slate milestone, and Lira read the milestone as the wagon went by. Cael watched her read it, and saw what was cut into the face that pointed north, and saw her look away from it.

The name was on the milestone. It was the first time it had been on anything they passed.

Nobody said it. Lira did not say it, and so nobody did.

That evening at the waystation the talk at supper was about the horses and the ice, and whether the hill coach would truly find the quarry town, and whether Ephram's new file book would survive the damp. It was cheerful talk and a little louder than usual, and Cael listened to it through the whole meal and did not hear the name once.

On the second day of it he wrote it down, sitting on the tailboard with the observation notebook on his knee while the wagon climbed a long grey hill at a walk.

*The whole company is keeping a silence. Nobody agreed to it, and nobody's said a word about it, which is how I know it's being kept. Ten people who can't stay quiet about a meat pie for an hour have gone two days without one name, and they're doing it so well I'd not know if I weren't counting.*

*Lira knows. She read the milestone yesterday and looked away, and every one of us saw her do it, and every one of us decided on our own to say nothing. She hasn't thanked anybody. She hasn't asked anybody to stop. She's let it stand.*

*I don't know what's under it, and I'm not going to ask. Whatever she's carrying up this hill is her kit, and the rest of us have been told, without a word, to keep our hands off the straps.*

He closed the notebook. Ahead of the wagon, near the top of the hill, Lira was walking beside the horses with her hands in her pockets, as she had walked beside them for most of the way since the milestone.

She walked well and quickly and paid the hill no attention at all, and she did not look back.

Seln was sitting in the second wagon with the travel file open on his knees, as he had sat for ten days, copying something from one sheet to another in a hand too small to read from the tailboard. He did not look up at the hill or at Lira. Once, when the wagon jolted, he lifted the pen clear of the page and waited for the wheel to find the road again, and then went on.

The wagon crested the rise. Below it, very far off and much lower down, a long thin lake lay in a fold of the hills like a blade laid on a grey cloth, and at its far end, where the hills closed in, something smoked under the low sky.

"There," said Rooke, from the front wagon. It was the first word anybody had spoken in some time.

Nobody asked him what. They could all see it.

# Chapter 15 — Cold Halls

The quarry town stood at the head of the lake where the hills came down to meet it, and it was made of the hills.

They came into it at dusk on the tenth day, down a long road cut in steps into the hillside. The first thing Cael understood about the place was that there was no other stone in it.

The houses were slate. So were the roofs, and the walls, and the walls round the gardens. The long quay where the barges lay was slate too, laid in great flags that the weather had worn smooth and dark.

Above the town the hillside itself had been cut away in terraces, ledge on ledge, each one a quarry face, so that the whole mountain looked like a stair that a giant had begun and given up.

Smoke came out of every chimney and lay along the water. The air smelt of wet stone and coal and fish.

The inn the clerks had booked for them stood on the quay, with its back to the hill and its front to the lake.

It was old and low and deep, with a common room that ran the whole length of the ground floor and a fire at either end of it.

The landlady met them at the door with a lamp and looked at the delegation for a long breath without speaking. People in that country, Cael would learn, looked at most things for a long breath first. Then she said they would want soup.

They did want soup. They had it at the long table by the east fire, the whole company together, with their coats still on because the room had not yet decided to be warm.

Brom ate three bowls.

Ephram wrote the inn's name in his file book under the date and looked at it with satisfaction. Gault set his case on the bench beside him and kept one hand on its handle through the whole meal, as if the hill might try to take it.

Lira ate quickly and went up before anybody else. She said she was tired, which was true, and nobody said anything about the milestone, which made two days of nobody saying anything about it.

Seln did not stay for the end of supper either. He finished his soup and folded his napkin square and told Rooke, to the table in general, that he would see to the inn's account with the landlord before the man went to bed, so that the travel file would be straight in the morning.

Then he went out through the door to the kitchen passage, with the file under his arm.

A little later, from the window by the east fire, Cael saw a grey coat go along the quay in the dark toward the barges and the lamps of the alehouses at the water's edge, walking at a clerk's pace, neither fast nor slow.

He thought nothing of it.

Inns kept their ledgers in odd places, and the landlord was very likely in one of the alehouses with his friends, and Seln went where ledgers were.

It was the kind of thing Seln did all the time, and it was the kind of thing nobody ever noticed him doing, and Cael noticed it now only because he happened to be at the window.

He went up to bed. Brom was already asleep, flat on his back, with his boots set side by side at the foot of the bed as neatly as a valet would have set them.

The window was thick with frost on the inside.

Cael scraped a hole in it with his thumbnail and looked out at the lake, black and still, and at the terraced hillside above the town, where here and there a lamp burned on a ledge. Somebody, it seemed, lived up there in the face of the mountain.

He thought about slate, and fell asleep thinking about it.

The meet was held in the slate exchange.

It was the biggest building in the town, a long stone hall at the end of the quay where the quarry companies had once sold their stone in lots.

The lots had long since gone to a newer yard up the valley, and the hall had been left standing for the guild's meetings and the town's fairs and, once a season, the circuit.

Its roof was so high that the rafters were lost in the dark above the lamps.

Its floor was slate, laid in great true flags the size of tabletops, and the walls were slate, and the light came in through tall narrow windows along the south side in long pale bars.

At either end of the hall stood an iron stove. They were big stoves, as tall as a man, with chimneys that went up into the dark and doors that glowed when the stokers opened them. They were burning as hard as they could.

The hall did not notice.

Cael stood just inside the great door on the morning of the Third-day, with the rest of the company behind him, and felt the cold come up through the soles of his boots.

It was not the cold of the road, which was a cold of wind and wet. This was a still cold, an old one, a cold that had been sitting in the stone since the stone came out of the hill and had no intention of going anywhere.

His breath stood in the air in front of him. Across the hall a steward was writing on a board in fingerless gloves, and two fighters from some other house were warming up in their coats.

*Pack for the third one. The halls at the third one are cold.*

It had been said to him across a counter, with a queue shuffling behind him, by a man who never lifted his eyes from a folder. He had packed wool on the strength of it, and so had everybody else, because he had told them.

Now he stood in the hall it had been about and felt it arrive, on time and in full, like a bill a careful merchant had sent ahead of the goods.

Rooke had stopped beside him in the doorway. He looked at the hall for perhaps the space of ten breaths.

He looked at the stoves, and at the roof, and at the two fighters warming up in their coats, and at the steward's gloves, and at the flags under his own feet.

Then he turned round to the company.

"Everybody listen," he said. "We're changing how we warm up. All of it, from today."

They listened.

"Nobody takes a coat off until the steward calls their name. Not one minute before. You warm up in your coat and you fight in your shirt, and the coat goes back on the moment you're off the floor. Gault will hold them."

Gault, behind him, looked startled and then resigned.

"You warm up twice," Rooke went on. "Once an hour before your call, properly, to a sweat. Then you put your coat on and you sit, and a quarter of an hour before your call you warm up again, short, to wake it up. If you only do the first one, you'll be cold by the time you're called. If you only do the second, you'll be cold all the way through and only warm on the outside."

He held up a finger. "And nobody stands still. Between exchanges you walk. You don't stand at your mark with your hands on your hips getting your breath. You walk a circle, slowly, like a horse after a race. A body that stands still on this stone gives its heat to the stone, and the stone keeps it."

"Do we drink?" said Ephram.

"Warm water. Not cold. Never cold." Rooke looked at the stoves. "Hands. Oil your hands before you wrap, and keep them in your armpits when they're not working. A cold hand can't feel a touch land, and a hand that can't feel a touch can't take one cleanly."

He looked round at them all. "That's the doctrine for this meet. It costs nothing but patience. Most of the houses in this hall won't bother."

Most of the houses in the hall did not bother.

Cael watched them all that day and the next, from the delegation's corner, while Rooke's doctrine went into the company's habits one rule at a time.

The other houses warmed up once, as they always had, in the hour before the first call. They took their coats off to do it, because that was what you did, and then stood about at the ropes in their shirts and talked until they were called.

By the time they were called they were cold again, and they did not know it, because the cold of that hall did not announce itself. It simply took a little off everything: a fraction off a burst, a quarter-beat off a guard, a finger's width off a reach.

He wrote it down that night.

*Seln's sentence arrived this morning at the door of the slate exchange, on its schedule and in full. Thirteen words, spoken to a folder, with a queue waiting. Rooke read the hall in ten breaths and turned the warning into a doctrine, and the doctrine is worth, by my reckoning, two or three bouts' worth of margin in a hall where nobody else has one.*

*I've opened a page for that man's sentences. On the left, what he said and when. On the right, what it turned out to be worth. It's a short page. Every line on it has paid.*

The floor's price had to be learned before he could spend it, so on the Fourth-day, in the hour the stewards gave the visiting houses for practice, Cael went out onto the slate to find out what it was.

He went with Lira, who needed to know as badly as he did and did not want to say so.

The flags were beautiful. They had been laid by masons who knew stone, with joints so fine that a knife would not go into them, and they were dead flat from rope to rope.

The slate had a faint sheen on it where a hundred years of feet had polished it, and in the bars of light from the south windows it looked almost blue.

It was the truest floor he had ever stood on. It was also the hardest. It did not give, and it would not give anything back.

He burst once from a standing start along the line of the rope, to see.

The Wind took him twenty feet, as it always did, and set him down, and the landing beat locked him where he landed.

On the sprung oak at home that beat was a stillness, a held breath with the floor breathing under it. On the mill hall's boards it had been a jolt.

Here it was a blow. The slate sent the whole of his landing straight back up through his heels and into his knees and his hips, and there was nowhere for it to go, so it went everywhere at once.

He stood in the landing beat until it released him and waited for his hips to stop telling him about it.

"Well?" said Lira.

"Four," said Cael. "Four free on this, in a day. Maybe three, if the cold gets into the hip." He had had six on the oak and five on hard boards; stone had always been fewer, and now he knew by how much. "The fifth would be billed to the morning."

"And the landings?"

"Worse than the bursts." He rolled his hip, carefully. "The burst's the same everywhere. It's the floor that sends the bill."

She nodded. "Three a bout for me, same as always. But the landings." She burst along the opposite rope, low and fast, and landed, and he saw her face. "That's a stone that hates you."

"It doesn't hate you. It just doesn't care."

"That's worse," said Lira, and burst back.

She landed beside him, and stood in her landing for a moment with her mouth set, and then shook it out of her legs.

"Rooke's rule," she said. "One extra burst for a wall is a fair price. Two in a bout is a habit." She looked down at the slate. "On this, I think one extra might be a habit."

"Then don't take it."

"I wasn't going to." She bent and put her palm flat on the flags to feel how cold they were. "I just like to know what a thing costs before somebody tries to sell it to me."

They worked the floor for an hour. He learned that the slate was fast underfoot, faster than boards, so that a step went further than he meant and a turn came round quicker.

He learned that the polish was worst near the middle of the floor and least near the ropes, where fewer feet had gone.

He learned that the read worked better here than anywhere he had fought, because a body standing on a floor that gave nothing had nowhere to hide its weight. Every shift went straight down into the stone and showed.

And he learned what Brom had told him on the oak.

He asked the Stone reserve to hold him, the way Brom had held him, and the reserve did it as well as a polite man could.

Cael turned his hips and dropped and pushed off the slate to get out under the arm, as he had done six times in eight at home.

Nothing pushed back. The slate took his push and gave nothing, and his feet, which were fast on that floor, went out from under him faster than he had meant, and he sat down hard with the reserve on top of him.

He tried it again, and again, more slowly each time. It would not come.

On oak the step worked because the floor did half the work. On slate the floor did none, and his legs alone could not make up the difference before a holder's hands closed.

"You need another answer," said the Stone reserve, getting off him. It was the longest sentence he had addressed to Cael all year.

"I know."

"Or you need your feet to come back sooner."

"I know that too." Cael got up and stood on the slate, feeling its cold come through his boots. "I'll find one."

He did not find one. He thought about it for the rest of the day, and in the evening he wrote in the Log only *on slate the escape has no answer; bring the feet back sooner, or don't let him close.*

He looked at the second half of that sentence for a long while. It was the whole of the problem, and it was the opposite of item one on his own program.

The draw went up on the hall board that evening, chalked in the steward's square capitals with a cold hand.

Lira's first bout in the Iron bracket was a quarterfinal, the draw being small, against the second entry of a house whose name nobody in the company had said aloud for four days.

Ephram read it, and turned away from the board, and went to look at something else. Karis read it, and wrote it in her notebook, and said nothing.

Brom read it and stood in front of it with his hands in his pockets, as he had stood in front of his own line at home, and then went and sat by the fire.

Cael read it.

Lira came last. She read it with her coat buttoned to the chin, and stood in front of it for perhaps the space of a breath.

"Good," she said, to nobody, and went up to bed.

It was the fourth day of the silence. Nobody broke it.

Brom broke it, in a way, an hour later, in the dark of the room he and Cael shared under the roof, with the frost thick on the inside of the window and the lake making small sounds against the quay below.

"Do we say anything?" he said.

Cael had thought he was asleep. "To her?"

"To her. Tomorrow. Before she goes out." Brom's voice came out of the dark, slow and considered. "I keep thinking somebody ought to. And then I think that if I were her, the last thing I'd want before a bout is ten people being kind to me in a row, like a queue at a counter."

"Then we don't."

"No." There was a long pause. "I'll not say anything very loudly, then," said Brom. "I'll stand near her and not say it. She'll know."

"She'll know," Cael agreed.

Brom turned over, which in that narrow bed was a considerable undertaking, and was asleep almost at once, as he always was once a thing had been settled.

Cael lay awake for a while longer, listening to the lake, and thought that Brom had just described, in four sentences in the dark, the whole of what the company had been doing for four days.

Nobody had needed to agree to it. Everybody had simply decided to stand near her and not say it, and trusted her to know.

---

Ephram was in the loading yard behind the exchange at first light on the Fifth-day, alone, in his coat, working his entry.

Cael saw him from the inn's back door, where he had gone to look at the weather.

The yard was a long open court where the quarry carts had once unloaded, with slate flags and a slate trough and a wall on three sides, and Ephram had chalked himself a mark on the flags and was coming off it, again and again, into the space where an opponent would stand.

It was the late half-turn of the front foot after the heel, the thing he had found at the mill town and won a title with. He ran it slowly, and then at speed, and then slowly again, his breath going up white in the still air.

Karis was standing at the inn's back door beside Cael.

She had a cup of something hot in both hands and her notebook under her arm, and she was not watching Ephram. She was watching the other end of the yard.

At the other end of the yard, where the wall had a gate in it to the street, a steward from the exchange stood with his shoulder against the gatepost and his hands in his armpits, watching Ephram work.

He watched him for some time.

Then he went out through the gate into the street, not hurrying.

Karis wrote something in the notebook, small, and went back inside without saying anything.

The houses warmed up in the long covered bay at the hall's east end, where the stone-carts had once backed in out of the rain. The stewards had roped it into lanes, and each house had a lane and a corner by the draw.

Halcenvane's corner was by the east wall, with a coat-rack the stewards had brought in for them, an old iron thing on four legs that wobbled.

Lira had one wrist wrapped and was halfway through the other, standing by the coat-rack, when a house came in and took the lane two over.

There were eight of them, fighters in blue and slate grey, with a coach Cael did not know and an older woman carrying a box of tape and kit on her hip.

Cael was at the delegation's rack two strides from Lira, running his own warm-up in his coat as Rooke had ordered, and he saw Lira see the colours. He read it as he would have read an opponent.

Her weight went back a fraction, and her shoulders came up a fraction, and she went still in a particular way he had seen once or twice before and never forgotten.

It was the stillness of somebody bracing for weather.

The weather did not come.

The house in blue and grey warmed up. The fighters were young, seventeen and eighteen, and nervous and cold.

They talked too loudly and laughed at things that were not funny, and one of them dropped a wrap and chased it across the lane.

The coach walked up and down the lane with his hands behind his back and said things to them that they did not listen to. The woman with the tape set her box down on a bench and began to sort it.

Partway through the sorting she looked up and across the two lanes at Halcenvane's corner, at the coat-rack. She saw Lira. Cael saw her see her: a small start, and her hands going still in the box.

Then the woman nodded. It was not much of a nod.

It was a little awkward, and a little sorry, and entirely without ceremony.

It was the nod of somebody who had once liked a girl and had not been able to stop a thing from happening to her, and knew it, and was not going to pretend otherwise across two lanes of a cold yard.

Then she looked down and went back to her tape.

Lira did not move. One wrist was still unfinished.

Cael watched her. He had placed himself within reach of her without seeming to, which was a thing he had learned to do a long time ago and no longer had to think about.

He saw her find, standing there, that the thing she had braced against was not there.

There were eight cold children in a lane. There was a tired coach, and a woman sorting tape. That was all of it, all that had been waiting at the top of the hill.

She finished her wraps. She pulled the last turn tight with her teeth, the way she always did, and spat out the end of it, and looked across the two lanes once more.

"Huh," said Lira. "It's small."

She said it to nobody, or to the coat-rack. Cael heard it anyway.

He heard in it the whole of the five days on the road, and the milestone, and the years before the milestone, all of it concluding in three words at a coat-rack in a cold yard.

He had heard her say something like it once before, on another cold morning, outside another hall she had been afraid of. He gave it back to her.

"No," he said. "You mean it's small."

Lira looked round at him. For a moment her face did not know what to do. Then she made a noise that was mostly a laugh, and shook her head, and took her kit to the lane to finish warming up.

The silence had lasted five days. It was over before her bout was called.

The bracket bouts began at the second bell, the Copper first, and Cael spent the morning in the gallery watching the hall instead of the floor.

Nobody else in the building was looking at the hall itself. To Cael it was the best thing in it.

The gallery ran along the north wall of the exchange on a timber platform the guild put up for fairs, three rows of benches stepped one above another, with a rail at the front.

From the top row you could see the whole floor and the stewards' table and the panel's trestle and most of the gallery as well.

Cael sat in the top row with the observation notebook on his knee and his coat buttoned to the chin, and charted the people who were watching.

He had seen some of them before without seeing them.

At the wool town and the mill town there had been men and women in the galleries who were not partisans or bettors or families, and he had noticed them without ever asking where they came from.

Here there were more of them. The quarry town was the last meet before the threshold, and the last before the finals' field began to take its shape, and anybody who wanted to know anything about this season's fighters had come to know it here.

There were three kinds of them, by the end of the morning. He drew them in three columns.

The first kind was official, and made no secret of itself.

They were scouts from academies, with their house's colours on the covers of their notebooks and seats in the gallery's best rows, listed in the meet's printed program among the other guests. Most were from houses in the region.

Two were not. They sat together near the middle of the front row, a man and a woman in good dark coats, with a device on their notebooks that Cael knew from the head of one of Rooke's continental sheets. They were from Auremont.

Auremont was the house every coach on the circuit named first whenever the finals at Norhold came up.

It had sent two people to sit in a cold hall in the hill country and watch a regional meet, because a house that means to carry the banner home does not let itself be surprised if it can help it.

The second kind worked for money and did not mind who knew it. They were the handicappers who made the prices on the chalk board in the exchange's yard.

They sat where the light from the south windows was best and wrote all the time, without looking up, at a speed that meant they were not writing words.

Cael watched one of them for a full bout and saw him make a mark at every exchange and two at every touch, and look at the board in the yard through the open door between bouts, and make another mark.

He had read that board at the wool town and the mill town. He had found it, both times, more right about who would win than half the coaching notes he had seen.

The third kind did not sit anywhere for long, and it was the third kind that took him the whole morning.

He brought the chart to Karis at the midday break.

She was on the delegation's bench by the east stove with her notebook, between the interviews she had arranged with the presiding adjudicator's clerk, and she took the chart before she took his question, holding it to the light from the window and reading the three columns through before she looked at him.

"The third column," she said at last. "Yes. They're compilers."

"Of what?"

"Of people." She handed the chart back. "They buy what other people see. A steward's notes. A regular's opinion. Anybody with a seat that has a view, they'll buy the view off him for a cup of something. Then they put it all together into a file on a fighter, and they sell the file to anybody who pays the fee. Houses buy them to get ready for a bout. The betting houses buy them to set a price. Families buy them so they can feel they know something."

She turned a page of her notebook, and Cael saw that she had already headed it, in her square hand, *the trade in records*. "The circuit behaves as though they aren't there. Nobody issues them a licence, so officially nobody sees them. Think about who that suits."

"The compilers."

"No. The compilers are easy to see. You saw them in a morning." Karis closed the notebook on her pen. "It's the clients who get to be nobody."

He went back to the gallery after the break to test it, because a thing Karis said was a thing worth testing, and he wanted to see if he could find one of the third kind sitting still.

It took him most of an hour.

He found her in the end in the third row, near the hall end, a woman of middle years in a coat of plain grey wool, the kind of coat a farmer's wife might wear to market, with a small notebook on her knee.

There was nothing about her at all. That was the first thing he noticed, once he had noticed her: that there was nothing about her to notice, and that it had been arranged.

The second thing he noticed was how she watched.

Everybody in a gallery watches something in particular. A family watches its own fighter and nobody else. A bettor keeps one eye on the chalk. A coach watches a technique and goes blank during the bowing and the calling.

This woman watched everything, evenly, on a round.

Her eyes went from the fighter on the left to the fighter on the right, to the stewards, to the panel's trestle, to the corners, to the gallery, and back to the fighter on the left, at the same steady pace, without stopping longer on one thing than another.

They went round the hall like the beam of a lighthouse goes round a bay, and she wrote as they went, in a small quick hand that did not seem to need her to look at it.

He watched her for three bouts.

During the third, without any change in her pace at all, her round brought her eyes up the gallery to the top row and to him.

They stopped there. They stopped for exactly as long as her round allowed for anything worth a second look, which was perhaps two heartbeats.

She wrote something. Then the beam went on round the hall, to the stewards and the trestle and the corners, and did not come back.

Cael sat very still with the observation notebook on his knee.

He wrote it down that evening.

*Third row, hall end. Grey wool, middle years, nothing to look at, which is the point. Her eyes go round the floor on a fixed beat and never stop longer for one thing than another.*

*I watched her for three bouts. In the third she looked up and saw me watching her, and wrote it down, and went on. No alarm, and no pretence of not having seen. She entered a fact.*

*Her next file on me will say I watch the watchers. It'll be right. Whoever pays for that page will have one more fact about me than they had before, and it's a good fact, and I gave it to her free.*

*Everything that has ever watched me has watched slower than I do. The Compact watches me the way a tide watches a shore; the wing watches on its calendar; even the panels only look for the length of a bout.*

*This woman keeps my pace. She logged me logging her. Neither of us was surprised by anything except being noticed.*

*So the thing that reads me at my own pace isn't the Compact, or the wing, or a panel. It's a market, selling copies. Noted. I'm not comfortable about it, and I'm writing that down too.*

# Chapter 16 — Page Three

Brom had been given the pages before the first bell, and he did not open them until noon.

The man who gave them to him was the coach of a house from the south, a broad, courteous man of about fifty with a soft voice and a coat that had been good ten years ago.

Cael knew him slightly from Rooke's profiles. His house was small and poor and well run, and it had lost regional finals to richer houses for a long time without ever seeming to hold it against anybody.

His Copper entry stood in the other half of the draw from Brom's, which meant that if both of them won through the morning, they would meet in the final.

He had crossed the warm-up bay to Halcenvane's corner while Brom was strapping his hands, and held out four folded sheets of paper.

"We paid for this," he said. "My house did, a month back. Everybody pays for them now, and I'll not pretend we're above it." He waited until Brom had taken the pages. "If my lad gets through the morning, he'll be standing across from you with page three in his head. Page three will get him hurt. I'd rather he met you with nothing in his head at all."

Then he nodded once and went back across the bay to his own corner, with his hands behind his back.

He had handed them across like a man returning a dropped glove in the street.

Brom stood holding them for a moment. Then he folded them once more, along the old crease, and put them in the inside pocket of his kit bag, and finished strapping his hands.

He won his first bout of the morning in two exchanges, without the pages, against a Copper from a hill house who had not bought anything at all.

At the midday break he brought the bag to the delegation's bench by the east stove, took the pages out, and laid them on the bench, still folded.

Then he opened them.

Brom did not read papers about himself alone. He never had, as far as Cael knew, except the letters from Velmere, which he read by himself and folded small and never spoke of. Everything else, he brought to the table.

He had never said why. Cael had once supposed it was a kind of modesty, and later decided it was the opposite: that Brom did not trust his own face to read a thing about Brom, and wanted other faces there to check it against.

So they read it together, the five of them, on a bench by a stove in a cold hall, with Ephram reading over Brom's shoulder because there was no room on the bench.

It was a good file. That was the first thing, and nobody enjoyed it.

It ran to four close-written pages, set out in sections like a clerk's report. It had Brom's bouts going back to the Ardenmere circuit, every one of them, with the results right. It described the Iron Skin Path in plain working terms, and described them correctly.

It priced his patience, and his habit of letting the other fighter spend first, and it gave an example of a trap he had run on strikers the season before last, with the steps in order.

Then there was page three.

Page three was the old seam. It described, in loving detail, the gap Brom had once had under the reset on his left side: the moment after he took a blow on the struck side and turned the force away, when he finished the turning before he drove, and for half a breath was neither hard nor moving.

It was right. It had been right a year ago. It came with a recommended sequence for drawing the gap open and stepping into it, in four numbered steps, like a recipe for bread.

The seam had been closed since last term. Rooke had closed it with nineteen exercises, four a week, until Brom drove while he was still hard on the struck side and the gap was not there to step into.

And Brom had shown it closed in public, in the Copper final at the end of that term, in front of a full hall, when he had taken Lira to the fourth exchange and lost by a single beat on a seam that held.

Whoever had written page three had not been in that hall. Or had been, and had not understood what they saw.

"But this is a year old," said Ephram, who was reading it upside down. "So it's no use to anybody. Isn't that good?"

"It's better than good." Brom did not look up. He was reading page three again, slowly, with one thick finger under each line. "Everybody who's bought this is going to come at my left side, at the reset, looking for a door that isn't there any more. Rooke and I spent the whole of last term bricking that door up."

He laid page three flat on the bench with the numbered steps uppermost, for the others. "They're going to queue up at it."

Karis held out her hand for the file. Brom gave it to her.

She read all four pages through without speaking, as she read anything she meant to rely on.

Then she turned back to the first page and read the line at the bottom of it, in small print, that said where the compiler's material had come from.

"Gallery observation and meet records," she said. "That's all. There's nothing about the nineteen exercises, nothing from the training floor, nothing a person could only know by standing in our hall." She folded the pages and handed them back. "Not one line of this was bought inside our gate."

"So the walls hold," said Lira.

"Against this shop." Karis opened her notebook. "It says the walls hold against the people who wrote this. It says nothing at all about the shops we haven't met."

The gallery began to fill an hour before Lira's bout, and it filled in a way Cael had not seen at a meet before.

It was not the usual gallery of a regional's second session, families and coaches and a few townspeople in out of the cold.

These people had come for a particular bout, and they came early to be sure of a place, and they talked to each other while they waited in the low, pleased voices of people who expect to see something. They were quarry people mostly, carters and masons and their wives, in heavy coats.

They filled the three rows of benches and then stood along the back wall, and then along the rail at the floor's west end, until the stewards had to ask them to keep the aisles clear.

Cael had heard the story three times since they arrived, without asking for it once.

The landlady had told it to Gault on the first night, over the soup, while Gault nodded and did not know who she meant. A carter at the trough in the loading yard had told it to another carter on the second morning.

And on the gallery stair an hour ago two girls of about fourteen had told it to each other, in the hushed voices of girls telling a story they like.

It was always the same story, and it was always a little bigger than the last time. A girl had been taken into the academy up the valley, and the academy had thrown her out, and now she had come home in another house's colours with a title in each hand, to fight the academy's own fighter on her own country's stone.

In the first telling she had been thrown out for cheek. By the third, she had been thrown out for being better than the academy's favourite, and the academy had known it all along, and been ashamed.

None of the tellings had the circuit in it, or the station hall at Ostrand, or the years of boards and warehouses that had come between that academy and this floor. None of them had anything in it that had cost her anything.

Cael stood at the rail at the floor's north side with Brom and watched the gallery fill, and wrote one line in the observation notebook.

*They want a reckoning. I hope they don't get one.*

He was not sure, when he read it back, which of two things he meant by it. Then he decided he meant both.

The bracket floor was in the middle of the hall, the coldest place in the building, as far from both stoves as the masons who laid it could have managed. It was a roped square of slate flags about forty feet on a side, polished dark in the middle where the most feet had gone.

A steward stood at each corner with a flag, and the panel sat at a trestle on the south side with their backs to the windows, five people in coats with slates on the table in front of them.

The Fenmark boy was on the floor first. He was tall and narrow and careful, with a long reach and a face that was trying hard to show nothing. He was a Wind Path, like Lira, because Wind was what that academy was famous for; it was why a Wind girl of twelve had been sent there at all.

Rooke's sheet had given him four words: *Good. Schooled. Expects respect.*

He had warmed up an hour ago with his coat off, as his house did, and then stood at the rope in his shirt while his coach talked to him for a long time with one hand on his shoulder. The talk had not seemed to help.

Lira came out of the corner in her coat. She took it off when the steward called her name and not one moment before, and handed it to Gault, who stood holding it like a man holding somebody else's baby.

Under it she was warm. Cael could see it from the rail. She moved loosely, and her hands were open, and her breath did not show as much as the boy's did.

The steward said, "Exchange."

The boy opened with the first form.

Cael knew it from Lira's descriptions, though he had never seen it run. It was the Fenmark way of taking a centre: a measured walk forward and then a burst at a fixed distance, then a reset and a second burst at the same distance, so that the fighter arrived in the middle of the floor having spent two bursts and owning the space between.

It was a good form. It was meant to make the other fighter give ground or meet you, and either way you had decided where the bout would be.

The boy burst, and landed a hand short.

It was not much. Cael saw it only because he was watching the landing and not the burst. The boy's Wind had taken him a fraction less far than his eyes had planned for, and when his feet came down on the slate his knees took the jar stiffly, a beat late, as cold knees do.

He reset, and walked, and burst again, and landed short again.

Lira did not burst at all. She walked.

She walked a slow circle round the outside of his centre, staying a stride beyond where his bursts were meant to reach. When he took the centre she let him have it, and kept walking. He had to turn to keep her in front of him, and turning on that floor, with its polish in the middle, he had to keep his weight low and careful.

So he turned, and she walked, and the glass ran.

Halfway through it he burst a third time, to close on her, and landed short a third time, and she was not where his landing had expected her to be. She was where his landing actually was, a stride to his left, already moving.

She did not take the touch. She could have. Cael saw it from the rail and so, he thought, did Rooke. She let the boy's landing beat go by with her hand open at her side, and walked on.

"Why not?" said Brom, very low, beside him.

"She's making him spend," said Cael.

The glass ran out with no touch on either side. The steward said, "Even."

They went back to their marks, and Cael watched them go. The boy walked back stiffly, with his breath going up white in front of him, and planted himself at his mark, fists on his hips, to get his breath.

Lira walked a slow circle round her mark, as Rooke had told her to, like a horse after a race, and never once stood still.

The boy had spent three bursts. Lira had spent none.

The boy had been on his feet in the cold for the whole glass, standing where he had taken the centre and turning on the polish to follow her, and she had been walking. Her body had kept its heat, and his body had given it to the stone.

"That's the exchange," said Brom.

"That's the exchange," Cael agreed.

He watched the boy's coach at the rope, talking urgently. He watched the boy nod, and set his feet, and roll his shoulders, and breathe out.

The boy had understood that something had gone wrong, and had decided it was his bursts, and was going to put more into them. He had not understood that it was the floor.

---

In the second exchange the boy ran the doctrine.

Cael understood what he was watching about four steps in, because Lira had once drawn it for him on the back of a feed bill at Ardenmere, late at night, when she could not sleep.

It was the Fenmark sequence, the four forms run one into the next: the first to take the centre, the second to press, the third to turn the opponent, the fourth to finish.

It was the thing Fenmark taught its Wind fighters across their whole time there, one form for every year a student stayed, and a fighter who could run all four cleanly was a fighter Fenmark would put its name to.

The boy ran it well. He had been taught by people who knew it and he had worked at it every day, and it showed. The first form took the centre again, and this time his bursts did not land short, because he had put more into them.

The second form pressed: short bursts, one after another, straight at Lira, each one pushing her back a stride toward the rope.

She went back. She went back exactly as far as each burst asked her to, no more, giving him ground at the rate he was buying it.

Then he went into the third form.

The third form was the turn. It was the moment in the sequence where the pressing fighter, having pushed his opponent back toward a rope, swung round her on a short arc to come at her from the side and drive her along the rope instead of into it.

The whole sequence hinged on it. On the third beat of the turn, the doctrine planted the fighter's weight on his left foot to swing.

Lira had been taught that turn at Fenmark at fourteen. She had told them, at fourteen, that it was wrong. The right foot was the one to plant, she had said; on the left, there was half a beat in the middle of the turn when the fighter was neither pressing nor guarding, when his weight was all on one leg and that leg was the wrong one.

She had said it to the wrong person, in the wrong tone, at the wrong age, and somebody had written it down in a few words that meant she could not be taught, and she had gone home.

The boy came round on the third beat with his weight on his left foot, as he had been taught.

Lira went under it.

She went from inside the turn, from the side he was swinging toward, low and fast, with one burst that carried her in beneath the line of his arm and across the front of his planted foot. For half a beat she was in the place the doctrine did not guard, because the doctrine did not know it was there.

Then her palm was flat against his ribs, under the arm, and she was out again on the far side of him before his weight came off the left foot.

"Touch. Halcenvane."

The gallery made a noise, a long exhaling noise like a crowd that has seen the first blow of a reckoning.

It was not a reckoning. Cael watched the boy's face as he turned. The boy was not angry and not ashamed. He was puzzled.

He looked down at his own left foot, which had done just what it was taught, and then at Lira, already walking her slow circle back to her mark.

Nobody had ever come at him from inside his own teaching before.

The boy's coach talked to him between the exchanges for a long time, with both hands on his shoulders now, and the boy nodded through all of it.

When he came back to his mark his face had changed. He looked frightened and brave at once, which is the face of a young fighter about to try something he is not ready for.

He went straight into the fourth form.

The fourth form was the finish, and it was the hardest thing in the Fenmark book. It was a third-year piece at the earliest, and most fighters did not run it cleanly until they were leaving.

It was a chain of three bursts in a rising rhythm, each one shorter than the last, ending in a strike that came from above and behind the opponent's guard.

Run well, it could not be met, only avoided. Run a little early, before a fighter was ready, it opened him like a door from the first burst to the last.

The boy was two years from ready, and he ran it anyway.

The first burst was good. The second was a fraction rushed, and the third came before the second had finished, so that for most of a breath he was in the air and coming down with nothing under him and nothing between Lira and the whole of his body.

Cael saw two endings and knew that Lira saw them too.

She could take his legs as he landed: a sweep at the moment his feet touched the polish, and the boy would go down on the slate from the height of a burst, hard, in front of his whole region. It would be a clean touch, legal, and very fast.

Or she could meet him: step into his landing with her shoulder, before the landing beat had let him go, and put him back a stride and onto the floor that way. That would hurt, too.

She did neither. She stepped half a stride to her right, so that he came down where she had been standing a moment before. She put her palm flat on his breastbone as he landed, gently, almost as a hand is put on a door to stop it swinging.

He did not fall. He stood there, on the slate, on his own two feet, with her hand on his chest.

"Touch. Halcenvane. Two to nothing. Bout to Halcenvane."

The gallery did not know what to do. Cael heard it not know.

It had come for a reckoning, and it had been given a quarterfinal, won in three, by a fighter who had made the result certain in the first exchange and then declined to make it hurt.

Lira took her hand away. The boy stood a moment longer where she had stopped him, getting his breath. Then he walked back to his rope upright, in front of his own hills, and his coach put the coat round his shoulders.

Cael's pencil was moving before the corner flags came down.

*She won it in the first exchange by being warmer than him, and she won it in the second because she knows the doctrine from inside and the doctrine doesn't know it has a gap. Without his teaching, a Wind boy from that house is a pair of long legs on cold slate with nobody to tell him where to stand.*

*The last exchange is the one I'll keep. He came down out of a form he wasn't ready for with his whole body open, and she had two ways to put him on the stone. I watched her see both. She chose a hand on his chest, and let him stand.*

*The registry's instrument said she was Iron. That last touch said something the instrument hasn't got a word for.*

They shook hands at the centre of the floor while the panel's slates went face down. The boy said something. Lira answered him. Then the boy laughed, a short surprised laugh, and they let go.

The figure went up on the board in the steward's square chalk at its proper interval: Lira twenty-six, the boy twenty-one. The gallery read it and began to drift out toward the chalk board in the yard, faintly cheated, talking about the next bout.

One woman did not drift. The woman with the tape box from the blue-and-grey house stood by the rope at the floor's east corner with a roll of tape still in her hands, and watched Lira walk back to Halcenvane's corner, and did not move until she had gone.

Brom's second bout of the day was the first of the page-three buyers, and he was not hard to find.

He was a wiry quarry-country Copper of about twenty with real craft in him, the kind of fighter who had learned on rough floors from rough men and kept what worked.

Cael watched him warm up and liked the way he moved. He watched him come out of his corner in the first exchange, and saw where he went.

He went to Brom's left side.

He went there with everything he had. He feinted high to the right to make Brom turn, and turned him, and as Brom took the feint on his right forearm and turned the force away, the wiry man was already moving round to the left, into the place where the reset would open.

He had his whole weight committed to the step. He meant to arrive at the seam the instant it opened, with a strike already on its way.

It did not open. Brom finished turning the force while he was still driving, hard on the struck side, and there was no half-breath of nothing in the middle. There was only Brom, coming round with his left shoulder, into a man who had committed everything to being somewhere that was not there.

"Touch. Halcenvane."

The wiry man went back to his mark shaking his head. In the second exchange he tried it again, more carefully, with two feints instead of one.

It was the same door, and it was still bricked up, and when he had spent his feints and his position and his breath knocking on it Brom simply stepped in and touched him.

"Two to nothing. Bout to Halcenvane."

The wiry man came to the rope afterward with his chest still heaving. He had the look of a traveller whose map has walked him into a bog.

"Where did it go?" he said. "The left. It was there. It was written down."

Brom unwound his strapping, one turn at a time.

"That gap went last year," he said, not unkindly. "You paid for another man's notes on me, and they'd gone stale before you bought them." He finished the strapping and rolled it. "Do your own next season."

The wiry man stood there a moment longer. Then he nodded, slowly, and went away, and Cael thought it was very likely the best advice anybody had given him all year.

Karis's quarterfinal was on the west floor in the middle of the afternoon, against the home house's Stone.

He was a heavy, patient young man from the quarry town's own academy, who had grown up on that slate and fought on it every week of his life.

Karis fought him the way she fought everybody, as a measurement she happened to be standing inside.

In the first exchange she found out how he rooted: all at once, with his weight going down into the stone like a post into a hole. She took her touch there, at the moment he settled, with a flat palm on his forearm and a small hard spark of heat at the contact that made him take the arm back and look at it.

"Touch. Halcenvane."

In the second exchange he took his touch back, near the west rope, with a heavy step off the flags by the stove that she saw coming and could not quite get clear of.

In the third he tried twice to draw her back toward the stove end, giving ground slowly in that direction and waiting for her to follow, and twice she did not follow, because she did not know why he wanted her there and she disliked being asked to do things she could not account for.

The fourth went to the glass with nobody touched. Level after four, the figures decided it, and the panel gave it to the Stone, twenty-three to twenty-two.

Karis came off the floor with her notebook already open.

"He likes the stove end," she said, writing. "His touch came there, and twice he tried to take me there. I don't know why yet." She frowned at the page. "I'll have it by morning."

"You lost," said Brom, mildly.

"I lost a bout," said Karis, still writing. "I've gained a question. On the whole I think I came out ahead."

Ephram won his quarterfinal in three, against a Blade from a valley house, and came off puzzled.

"He didn't bite," he said. "My entry. The half-turn after the heel. I threw it twice and he didn't touch it either time. He just stood off it, both times, as if somebody had told him it was coming." He shook his head. "I won anyway. I just don't know why he knew."

Karis looked up from her notebook at him. Then she looked down again and wrote something, small, and did not answer.

Cael saw her do it, and remembered the steward at the gate of the loading yard at first light, with his hands in his armpits, watching Ephram work.

---

"What did he say?" Brom asked at supper.

The inn's common room was warm by then, at last, with both fires lit and the whole company at the long table and the landlady going up and down with a pot.

Lira had a bowl of stew in front of her and was eating it steadily, without looking up.

"He said there's a drill in their second-year book with my name on it," she said. "Footwork for the third turn. They named it after me before I left, when I was still the girl who was going to be good. They've never taken the name off."

Brom put down his spoon.

"And he said it's on the left foot," said Lira. "My drill. With my name on it. On the left foot." She ate a mouthful of stew. "So I told him, next time he runs it and nobody's watching, try it off the right. And he said that's not in the book." She shrugged. "And I said, no. It wouldn't be."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Ephram began to laugh. He tried not to, and could not stop, and put his face in his hands, and Brom laughed too, the great slow laugh he did not use often.

Karis wrote something in the grey notebook and underlined it. Lira went on eating her stew.

But Cael, across the table, saw the corner of her mouth, and knew that she had been waiting all afternoon for somebody to ask.

"They kept your name," said Brom, when he could, "and threw out your foot."

"That's Fenmark," said Lira. "That's it exactly. You could put that over their door." She finished her stew and pushed the bowl away. "The boy was all right, though. He laughed when I told him. He didn't have to laugh. He had a whole hall of his own people watching him lose to the girl they threw out, and he laughed." She considered it. "I'd have liked him at fourteen, I think. I hope somebody up there is kind to him."

Gault, at the far end of the table, looked up from a sheet of figures he was checking. "If he runs it off the right foot next year," he said, "and somebody asks him who taught him, I should very much like to be in the room."

"So should I," said Lira, and for the first time that evening she laughed properly herself.

When supper was over and the plates were being cleared, the door from the kitchen passage opened and Seln came in from the quay. He had his coat on and the travel file under his arm, and his face was the same as it always was.

He hung the coat on the peg by the door, and sat down at the end of the long table, in the place nobody else wanted, by the draught.

"When the plates are gone," he said, to the table in general, "the office has a matter to put to the table. It won't take long."

# Chapter 17 — The Service Side

Seln settled the inn's account first, because it needed settling.

He found the landlord where the landlady said he would be, in the alehouse at the far end of the quay, at a table by the fire with two bargemen and a game of dominoes.

He waited until the hand was finished. Then he laid the delegation's tally on the table and went through it line by line in the light of the fire: beds for the whole delegation, five nights, supper and breakfast, the stabling, the wagon yard, a charge for coal that was too high and was brought down without unpleasantness.

The landlord signed. The figure went into the travel file in Seln's small, square hand.

He did it first because it was true, and because a man who has come to a place to do one thing does well to be seen doing another thing completely.

Then he bought the bargemen a drink, because they had lost their hand while he waited, and sat down.

He had worked rooms like this for fifteen years, in towns he no longer remembered the names of, and every one of them had a second floor that was not on any plan.

There was the town that the stewards saw, and the town that the fighters saw.

And under both of them, in alehouses and back rooms and the kitchens of inns, there was the town that served the other two: the carters and runners and clerks and stable boys who carried things from one place to another and were never asked what they had seen on the way.

A clerk with a tally in his hand was invisible in that town. The stewards took him for a man owed money or owing it, and the runners did not see him at all.

He had spent fifteen years making sure of that, and he was good at it. It was, if he was honest, the thing he was best at in the world.

So he sat by the fire with the bargemen, and listened.

"Slate's down," said the older of the two. "Down a penny a ton since the valley yard opened."

"Coal's up," said Seln.

"Coal's always up."

"It is," Seln agreed.

That was very nearly the whole of his share in the talk for an hour. The bargemen did not mind. Men who work on water like a listener, and they had not had a good one in some time.

They told him about the slate and the coal and the lake, and about a barge that had gone down off the north shore the year before last with a load of roofing and a dog, and how the dog had swum home.

Seln asked what the dog was called. It was the right question. After that they would have told him anything, and in the course of the evening they told him a good deal that they did not know was worth telling.

He had known, since the mill town, that the market would be here.

He had copied the boy's figures off the board at the mill town with his own pencil and laid them beside the wool town's. Six figures, inside three points. He had said so across a document table to the boy, in the office's voice, and the boy had heard it.

And Seln had known while he said it that he was not the only man on the circuit with a pencil. A column of figures that tidy was not only a warning. It was also a product. Somebody would be selling it.

Somebody was. It took him the first evening to learn that much.

The handicappers' runners drank at this alehouse after the boards closed. They were boys and young men who carried slips between the chalk board in the exchange's yard and the betting houses in the town, and they knew every other runner on the quay by his coat.

The compilers' readers drank here too, though they did not call themselves anything. They sat with the stewards and bought the stewards' evenings for the price of a jug, and went home early.

By the time the alehouse put its lamps out, Seln knew that there was a file in the town on the provision boy from Halcenvane. He knew that it was the current edition, made since the mill town, and that a commercial house that sold such files across half the continent had made it.

He knew roughly where the one copy in the quarry town was kept, and what kind of person kept it.

What he did over the next three evenings, and with whom, and at what price, nobody would ever be able to write down. Nothing of it was ever written anywhere. He had arranged it that way, as he arranged most things, so that there would be nothing to find.

---

On the third night he read it.

The room under the inn's roof had a table the width of a tray, a bolt on the door, and one candle. He used all three.

The window was thick with frost on the inside. Below it the lake was black, and the quay was empty, and somewhere along it a dog barked twice and gave up.

He read it the way he had been taught to read another officer's product, long ago, at a long table under an old man whose name nobody in the office used any more. The first time through, he read it for what it said. The second time, he read it for how it had been made.

What it said, he kept to himself. It was good work. It was better work than most of what crossed his own desk from his own office, and he noted that without surprise, because he knew what the office paid and what the market paid, and they were not the same sums.

It was careful. It was dry. It did not guess where it could count. It knew a great deal about the boy that was true, and it knew nothing about him that mattered.

He admired the paper before he admired anything else, because paper was where a house showed what it thought of itself. It was good rag stock, cut square and sewn at the spine with linen thread, the kind of paper a firm buys when it expects its product to be kept and consulted and not thrown on a fire after one reading.

The hand was a clerk's hand, small and level and patient, and it did not change from the first page to the last, which meant one person had written the whole of it at a desk, from notes, without hurrying.

There were no blots. Where a figure had been corrected, the old figure had been struck through once and left legible, so that a reader could see what had been changed and judge for himself whether the change was honest.

Seln approved of that very much. He did the same thing himself.

In the margin, on the page where the compiler had set out the season's figures in a column, there was a note in a small hand. It was one word, and a question mark after it.

*Managed?*

Seln sat and looked at the word for some time.

The compiler had come to it from the outside, from the figures, as he had come to it himself at the mill town with a pencil. Six bouts in a band too narrow to be weather. Any patient person with a column could see it. The compiler was a patient person with a column.

It had taken the market one season of watching from cold galleries to find the word, and it had written the word with a question mark, because it was honest and it could not yet prove it.

He did not need the question mark.

He had had the word since last year, and he had not got it from figures.

He had been standing in the east hall at Halcenvane last year, with the dial sheets held against his chest, when the post locked. He had seen what happened at the bay in less time than it took to see it.

He had seen two things inside one breath that the boy's record said he did not own: a move forward on the Wind, into the face of a live danger, when every line of the record said the Wind only ever took him away; and a blow taken on a forearm that went into the forearm and did not come out.

Seln had filed nothing about either of them. His brief had a category for exactly that sight, and he had not used it.

Then there had been the third-year, Jask, telling the refectory queue for a week what the boy had told him at the foot of the lecture stair. *I'm quick off a read when I've slept.* Seln had heard it told six ways, and had let every one of them stand.

Then there had been the file that came home from the guest floor with nothing written on it. He had looked at that file for an evening, and had written one line in his own hand on his own page, the kind of line he let himself write perhaps twice a year. *unmarked file. one of three. probably the third.*

And there was one slip of wing paper, cut small with the wing's own knife, in a hand that was nobody's. He had written it once, and squared it, and sent it the only way it could go.

Those were his reasons for knowing the word. He had never written them down together. He did not write them down now. He only laid them side by side in his head, beside the compiler's question mark, and looked at the difference between the two roads to the same place.

He did not know what the boy was managing. He had never tried to know. He had made it a rule, over fifteen years, never to wonder past what was in front of him, and the rule had kept him alive and employed in rooms where wondering was not safe.

He knew that something was being held. He knew it was being held well. That was all he knew, and he did not reach for any more.

He turned back to the first page and began again, for how it had been made.

A file is made the way a house is built. You cannot see the order of the work when it is finished, unless you know where to look. Seln knew where to look.

Every commercial file he had ever read on a fighter began with the fighter's bouts, because bouts were what the buyers paid for. This one did not. This one began with the door.

It began with the enrollment basis: the clause, the citations under it, the long chain of verification by which a registrar on a bluff had shown that a boy with no Path on his papers could stand on a floor at all.

Only after the door had been set out, carefully, did the file come to the boy's record. And only after the record did it come to the boy himself, and after the boy, to the people round him.

Door, record, man, company. It was a strange order for a fight file. It was an order a buyer would never ask for.

It was the order of a house that had started building its stock before it had a single customer.

Seln knew that order. He had seen it once before, half a year ago, last term, at the top of a records-hall stair in the middle of the night.

A man who read papers for money had come up that stair three nights running and copied the plans of the door, and nothing else. Not the boy's floor hours. Not his baseline. Not a line about what the boy could do.

Only the door, the clause and its citations and the registrar's verification, letter by letter and seal by seal. Somebody had wanted the door drawn before they wanted anything else.

The man had been caught, and his little book had gone to counsel. He had never known who paid him. The job had come on paper, unsigned, paid in advance, through a letter box near the river that some factor had taken for a single month and then let go.

The wing had written two lines in the incident book that night. Seln knew the two lines well, because he had written them. *Pattern is consistent with private interest. Not Compact pattern.* He had written what he could prove, and put the pen down.

On the first evening by the alehouse fire, among a great deal about the price of coal, one of the runners had told him how this particular house paid the people who read for it.

It paid in advance. It paid on paper, unsigned. It paid through boxes near water, let by the month and given up, so that no box was ever used twice and no reader ever had a name to sell.

The runner had told him this because it was a grievance among the readers, who would have liked to be paid by somebody they could shout at.

He held the stair and the alehouse fire in his head together, and let them sit until they agreed.

They agreed. The Compact had never sent the reader up the stair. When the Compact wants a paper, it sends a clerk with a warrant to read it at the counter in daylight, and makes the registrar fetch the chair.

The reader had been the house's buyer. Half a year ago, before the boy had fought a single bout on the circuit, the market had smelt a new product coming, and had sent a reader up a stair to fetch the first part of its stock.

The reader had been caught. The house had shrugged and built the door from the public hearing instead, as any shop does when a supplier fails. And now the product was on the shelf, in every meet town on the circuit, and anybody with the fee could read the boy's door before they read his bouts.

Who had commissioned that first job, Seln could not say, and would never be able to say. The answer lay behind more than one pair of hands, in a trade built out of hands passing things along. He found that it did not matter. The real buyer had been the trade itself, filling its shelves ahead of the customers. That was only good shopkeeping.

Private interest. He had written it half a year ago, carefully, as an inference. It was not an inference now.

In the morning, while the delegation was at the exchange for the first bracket session, the copy went back where it had come from.

It went by the same removes it had come by, in the same order, and by noon it was where it had been on the first evening, and no hand that had touched it had seen his.

Seln did not keep it. He had not copied a line of it. A file that stays bought is a file somebody can trace, and a man who copies what he has bought has made two things to be found instead of one.

He did not burn anything, because there was nothing to burn. He did not write anything down, because there was nothing he would forget.

He did not ask himself why he had gone to so much trouble over a matter the wing had already set down as private. He seldom asked himself anything that could not be answered out of a record.

The question was there, somewhere, the way a step is there in a dark stair. He went down past it without slowing, and did not put his weight on it.

He walked back along the quay at a clerk's pace in the thin morning light, with the travel file under his arm, and closed the matter in the only ledger he had never had to lock.

It was half a year old. It had been open the whole time, on Karis Dellenmoor's page at the back of her grey notebook and on his own page in his own head, and he had known it was open on both.

He found he was looking forward to watching her draw the line through it. She drew a very straight line.

The rest was residue, and the residue was permanent.

Now the file sat on shelves for anybody's money. It would go on being sold, edition after edition, for as long as the boy fought anywhere a gallery could see him. Any fighter who mattered would walk out to meet him having met him already, on paper.

Nothing anybody did would ever take that back. It was not a danger in the way the stair had been a danger. It was weather, and the boy would have to fight in it.

Seln thought the boy would manage. He had watched him take the arithmetic at the mill town without a flicker and walk away with it.

Whatever the boy did with a sentence once it had been said to him was the boy's affair. The office had said what the office could see. That was the whole of the office's part, and it was finished.

He spent the rest of the morning at the exchange, sorting the travel file, and in the afternoon he copied the bracket figures off the board as they went up, in a neat column, without any appearance of interest.

When the supper bell rang at the inn he was still on the quay. He walked back slowly, in the dark, and worked out how he would say it.

He would say it to the table as a file. He would use the office's voice: plain and dry, with nothing in it that a hostile clerk could hold up afterward.

He would give Karis Dellenmoor what she would ask for, which would be a reason to believe it, and he already knew which reason he would give her. He would give the boy nothing at all, because the boy did not need anything from him, and would not have asked.

He opened the door from the kitchen passage, hung his coat on the peg, and sat down at the end of the table by the draught.

Cael watched him do it, and watched him wait until the plates were gone.

Rooke and Gault had gone to the coaches' supper at the exchange. The reserves had gone up.

The landlady had taken the last of the bowls and wiped the table and gone back to her kitchen, and there were six of them left at the long table by the east fire: the five, and Seln at the far end by the door.

Seln did not move up the table. He spoke from where he was, to the teapot, more or less.

"The office has closed last term's records-hall matter," he said. "The man on the stair. His job was bought by a commercial house that makes files on fighters and sells them up and down the continent. It was bought their way. What they were buying it for is on sale in this town tonight: a file on the enrollee, made since the mill town." He poured himself tea. "The office's finding: private interest, confirmed — the market, not the Compact. Who first paid for the job can't be found from here. In the office's view, it no longer matters."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Lira put her cup down. "Half a year," she said. "Half a year we've had that line open. A man comes up our stair in the night to draw the door, and we build a whole term of being careful on it. And it was a *shop*?"

Seln reached for the milk. "Most threats are commerce wearing a cloak." He poured. "Pull the cloak off, and nine times in ten there's a shopkeeper underneath."

Karis had the grey notebook open at the back, at the page of open lines. She had not written anything yet.

"How sure is the office?" she said.

"The office would put its name to it before any panel you care to choose." He answered in his counting voice, flat as a column. "The house builds its files in a particular order, and the job on the stair was the first piece of that order. It pays its readers in a particular way, and the job on the stair was paid that way. Every buyer has a hand, as every clerk has one. This house has written its orders in the same hand since before any of us were born. It doesn't change its hand for one stair."

He drank some tea.

"And there is an absence that confirms it," he said. "Suppose the client had been an office. The Compact, or the registry, or any body with a seal and a standing. An office that wants a thing and fails to get it asks again, louder, on better paper, with a name at the bottom. Nobody asked again. Instead, the house went round to the public hearing and built the door from that, more cheaply, and sold the file anyway." He set the cup down. "Offices insist. Shops make do. This one made do."

Karis looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at her page.

"Accepted," she said.

She drew a line through the heading. It was a very straight line.

Cael watched her draw it, and felt something he had carried at the bottom of his mind for half a year turn over and become a different thing. For half a year it had been a draught under a door. Now it was a paid bill in a drawer.

"One thing left," said Karis, with her pen still on the page. "If there's a file on Cael in every meet town, then everybody at Norhold has read him before they meet him. All of them. And Auremont's had scouts at this meet. They won't be buying their file from a shop."

"No," said Seln. "Theirs will be better."

"Good," said Cael.

The table looked at him.

At the far end, by the draught, Seln looked at him as well.

The look lasted one second past the place where Seln's looks ended. Cael had watched that man not look at him across a counter every week for most of a year, and he had never once seen a look of his run over. From Seln it was as loud as a question.

Cael answered it.

"They'll prepare for the file," he said. "The file is the record. I've lived inside the record since Ardenmere. Everything in it is true, and everything in it is what I chose to show." He set his cup down. "So Auremont is going to spend the whole cycle drilling its fighters against a thing I made to be drilled against. I'd rather fight people who prepared for the wrong me than people who prepared for nothing." He looked up. "A man who's prepared has committed. And I can read a man who's committed."

Brom laughed, the great slow laugh. "That's page three," he said. "You're doing page three on the whole continent."

Everybody laughed then, even Karis. The line that had stood open for half a year went into the ledger where it belonged, among the weather.

Karis closed the notebook. Lira refilled the teapot. Ephram, who had followed perhaps half of it and admired all of it, said he was going to bed before anybody explained the rest.

Seln stood, and took his coat from the peg, and went up. He did not say goodnight. He never did.

But Cael had seen his face in that one long second, and he wrote it down that night at the frosted window, by his own candle.

*He looked at me too long. Everybody else at the table heard a fighter talking tactics. He heard what I actually said: that I am content to be permanently unknown, and that I have arranged it so. Of the six of us at that table, he's the only other one who chose it. Two professionals of the same strange trade, and a teapot between us.*

*Neither of us said so. In our trade you don't.*

By noon on the first day the handicappers' chalk in the exchange's yard had put a price on every bracket bout. There was a column for the exhibitions as well. On the first day the column was blank.

On the second morning, when the company came across the yard to the exchange, it was not blank. Across the top of it, in a handicapper's quick chalk, somebody had written a single line, and a little crowd had gathered in front of it to read it and laugh.

*No book on the demonstration bouts.*

Ephram read it twice and looked offended on Cael's behalf. "No book?" he said. "I'd put money on you."

"Then you'd be the only one," said Karis, "and they'd have to give you terrible odds, and you'd win a copper for every crown you laid."

"That's the point, isn't it," said Brom. "Nobody'll take the other side."

Lira stopped in front of it with her kit on her shoulder.

"I'm going to find out why," she said.

"Now?" said Karis.

"Now. Before my semifinal. If I wait till after, I'll be too tired to enjoy it." She put her kit down by Cael's feet. "Mind that."

She went across the yard to the betting house's stall, where the board-man stood under an awning with his chalk and his slate and his hands in his coat. Cael watched her go up to him. He watched the board-man look at her, and recognise her colours, and grin, and begin to talk.

Lira could walk up to anybody in the world and have them talking to her inside a minute, and she knew it, and so did everybody else.

He picked up her kit and went in to watch Brom's semifinal.

Brom's semifinal opponent had read page three, and had read it carefully, and had built his whole bout on it.

Cael kept the notebook open on the rope and wrote the bout down as it came.

The man was a solid Copper from a house in the valley, square and well-schooled, with good hands. He came out in the first exchange with a plan, and the plan was the four numbered steps on page three.

He feinted high to draw Brom's right forearm. He stepped round to the left as Brom took the feint and turned it. He set his feet at the place where the seam would open, and waited for it, with a strike loaded.

The seam did not open. Brom drove through his turn, hard on the struck side, and came round, and the strike the man had loaded had nowhere to go.

The man reset. He tried it again with the steps in a different order. Then he tried it slower, and then faster. He spent his feints and his footwork and his breath, all of it, on one door, because page three had told him the door was there and he had nothing else.

That was the thing. He had not brought anything else. He had paid for a plan, and the plan was all he had.

By the third exchange the man's purse was empty, and he was still standing at the same wall. Brom let him knock. Then Brom stepped in, twice, and the flags came up twice.

"Two to nothing. Bout to Halcenvane."

There were two other Coppers in the gallery's front row whom Cael had marked that morning, from other houses, with their own bouts still to come. He had seen them in the first session with folded pages in their laps. Through Brom's semifinal he watched their faces as well as the floor.

Both of them watched the man step round to the left. Both of them leaned forward when he set his feet at the seam. Both of them waited for the gap to open, with the exact expectation on their faces that the page had given them.

And when it did not open, neither of them changed anything. They sat back and waited for the next try, as if the page could not be wrong and the floor must be.

The flags were still up when Cael wrote it.

*A stale page doesn't just fail the man who paid for it. It herds him. It herds everybody who's read it into the same doorway, and they all stand there waiting for a door that isn't there, and none of them looks for another way in. Stale paper is a funnel. Brom is standing at the narrow end charging admission.*

He looked up from the page. Brom was at the rope, unwinding his strapping, turn by turn, with the satisfied face of a man who has been paid twice for the same work.

Then Cael looked along the gallery to the third row, at the hall end, where a woman in plain grey wool was writing in a small notebook without looking at it. Her round came up the gallery and found him, and stopped for its two heartbeats, and moved on.

He wondered what page three of his own file said, and who was going to come and stand at it, and whether he would know the door when they did.

# Chapter 18 — Freight

The Iron semifinals were fought on the west floor, and the west floor was nearer the stove.

It was the hall's second bracket square, a roped space of slate about the size of the main floor, laid along the west wall where the guild had once weighed its lots. The great iron stove stood a dozen strides beyond its far rope, roaring, with its chimney going up into the dark. On a cold morning you could feel it on your face from the floor's west edge, and from the east edge you could not feel it at all.

Ephram drew the home house's Stone.

Cael had watched the Stone beat Karis the afternoon before. He was a big, heavy-framed young man from the quarry town's own academy, with thick shoulders and a slow, patient way of standing, and he had fought on this slate every week of his life. He did not look clever. Karis had said, coming off the floor, that he was cleverer than he looked and that she did not yet know how.

She had not had it by morning, after all. She had told Ephram at breakfast what she did have, which was that the Stone's touch against her had come near the stove end, that he had twice tried to take her there, and that he seemed to like it. Ephram had nodded and written it in his file book: *Stay away from the stove end.* It was a sensible note, and it was the wrong one.

"Exchange."

Ephram came off his mark with the entry he had built at the mill town. It was the second-year entry with its two weights, and then, after the heel, the late half-turn of the front foot that had won him a title. Cael had watched him work it in the loading yard at first light the day before, again and again, until it was as smooth as anything Ephram owned.

The Stone did not touch it.

He did not step into the half-turn, or meet it, or bite on the first weight. He stood off it, a stride back and a little to the side, where the half-turn could not reach him, and let it go by. Ephram reset and threw it again, and the Stone stood off it again in the same place, as calmly as a man stepping round a puddle he has known about for a week.

Ephram's face changed. Cael saw him think it, *he knew*, and then saw him put the thought away for later and go on fighting, which was the right thing to do and not an easy one.

The glass ran out. "Even."

In the second exchange the Stone took the stove end.

He did it without any hurry. He walked to the floor's west edge, where the warm air off the stove came over the rope, set his feet on the flags there, and waited. He did not come out. He let Ephram come to him.

Ephram did not come. He had Karis's note in his head, and he stayed away from the stove end as the note told him, working the floor's east side, the cold side, and coming at the Stone from angles to try to draw him out.

The Stone would not be drawn. He stood his ground on the warm slate and turned to keep Ephram in front of him, and every time Ephram came at an angle the Stone gave him half a step, never more, always toward the north. Slowly, without ever seeming to go anywhere, he moved Ephram where he wanted him: north and east, away from the stove, onto the flags by the north windows.

Cael did not see what was wrong with those flags until Ephram's foot told him.

The north side of the hall was the coldest place in the building. The breath of the gallery and the steam off the fighters rose and drifted along the roof, and met the cold glass of the tall windows, and settled again as damp. By mid-morning a thin skin of it lay on the slate along the north rope, too thin to see. It made the flags there faster than the rest of the floor, so that a step travelled further on them than a fighter had meant it to.

Ephram stepped on the north flags, and his step went a hand further than he had meant.

It was not much, and he was a good fighter and caught himself at once. But for a quarter-beat he was a hand closer than he had planned to be, with his weight a fraction ahead of where he wanted it, and the Stone had been waiting the whole exchange for that quarter-beat. He came off his warm dry flags in one heavy stride and put a flat hand on Ephram's shoulder.

"Touch. Quarry house."

The home crowd in the gallery made a deep, satisfied noise.

Ephram went back to his mark and did not walk a circle, as Rooke had told them all to. He stood at his mark, fists on his hips, and looked at the north flags, and then at the stove end, and then at the Stone, standing easy on his warm slate with the stove's heat on his back.

Rooke, at the north rope, said one word, not loudly. "Walk."

Ephram walked.

In the third exchange Ephram worked it out, or half of it.

He stopped trying to draw the Stone out of the stove end, and went to him instead. He went straight across the floor toward the west rope and the warm flags, and as he went Cael saw him see the thing Karis had not been able to see from the floor the day before.

The air above the stove end moved.

The heat from the great stove came over the rope and rose off the warm slate, and the air above those flags shimmered very slightly, like the air over a road on a hot day. A man standing in it was a little blurred, and his shoulders were a little blurred, and a fighter who read weight from the shoulders, as Ephram had been taught to and nearly everybody was, saw the Stone's weight move a fraction late through the wavering air every single time.

The Stone was not hiding his weight. He was standing where the room hid it for him.

Ephram came in low. He dropped his eyes from the Stone's shoulders to his feet, below the shimmer, where the air was clear. Feet do not lie about weight, and on that floor nothing could hide them. He read the Stone's next step in his ankles a fraction before the shoulders showed it, went inside it, and touched him on the hip.

"Touch. Halcenvane. One to one."

Cael let out a breath he had not known he was holding. Beside him Karis was writing fast.

"Feet," she said, without looking up. "Below the air. I couldn't see it from the floor. He can."

"He's got it," said Brom.

"He's got half of it," said Cael.

The fourth exchange was the half Ephram had not got.

The Stone had seen the low entry once, and he was a patient man who did not need to see a thing twice. When Ephram came across the floor again, low, with his eyes on the Stone's feet, the Stone did not stay on the warm flags to wait for him. He gave ground slowly, a step and then another, north and east, back across the floor toward the windows, and Ephram followed him low and reading his feet, so that the two of them went together onto the cold side of the floor.

Ephram did not look at the flags. He was looking at the Stone's feet, which was what the third exchange had taught him to do, and the Stone had known that it would.

On the third step Ephram's own foot came down on the damp north slate and went a hand further than he had meant, again. He was low and committed, and this time he could not catch it. He arrived a half-beat early and too close, with his weight already spent, in front of a man who had walked him there on purpose.

The Stone touched him on the chest.

"Touch. Quarry house. Two to one. Bout to the quarry house."

Ephram stood still for a moment on the north flags. Then he looked down at them, put out his foot, and drew the sole of his boot slowly across the slate, and felt it slide. Cael watched him understand all of it, one exchange too late.

He shook the Stone's hand at the centre of the floor and meant it. Then he walked back to the delegation's rope, took his file book out of his coat pocket before he had put the coat on, and stood at the rope in the cold, writing, while the panel's slates went face down and came up again.

The figures went up a few minutes later. The Stone had twenty-four, and Ephram twenty-three.

Ephram did not look at them. He was still writing.

Lira's semifinal asked her nothing she had not already answered. It was a Blade from a valley house who fought just as Rooke's sheet said he would, and she beat him in two exchanges on the main floor before the gallery had properly settled.

Her final was in the afternoon, against the Stone.

Ephram found her at the coat-rack an hour before it. He had a page in his hand, torn out of the file book, and he held it out to her without a word, as stiffly as a clerk handing over a bill he is not proud of.

Lira took it and read it. It was three items, in Ephram's careful hand, under the heading *Quarry house, Stone, semifinal: account*. She read it twice.

"Can I keep it?"

"That's what it's for," said Ephram.

She won the final in four exchanges, two touches to one, and she fought it off Ephram's page. She never once read the Stone's shoulders near the west rope; she read his feet, below the moving air, from the first exchange. The Stone took the first touch anyway, because he was good, with a heavy quick step off the warm flags that she saw and could not quite get clear of. Lira took the second exchange's touch from below, as Ephram had done. In the third the Stone began, slowly, to give ground toward the north windows, a step and then another, inviting her after him onto the wet slate.

She did not follow. She stopped in the middle of the floor on dry flags, and stood there, and let him go.

The Stone stood by the north rope for some time, waiting for her. The gallery began to murmur. At last he came back out to the middle, because the glass was running and a fighter who will not come to his opponent cannot touch her. As he came off the damp slate onto the dry, his feet changed speed, and she was waiting for exactly that change. She took the touch there, and the fourth exchange's after it.

The figure was twenty-five. It was her third title of the season, and she had not lost a bout.

She came off the floor with Ephram's page still folded inside her wrap, where she had tucked it before the first exchange.

"That was your bout," she told him at the rope. "I only fought it."

Ephram said nothing at all. But he went and stood by the stove for some time afterward, by himself, with his hands held out to it, and Cael noticed that he was not cold.

Brom's final came last of all, as the light went from the south windows.

His opponent was the southern coach's boy, whose coach had handed the pages across the warm-up bay that first morning. He was a slight, serious Copper of about seventeen with a quick left hand, and he came out to his mark looking very young. He had read nothing about Brom, because his coach had made sure that he would not.

So he fought Brom the only way a boy can fight a man he has no map of, which is from scratch.

Cael found it the best bout of the meet to watch, and nobody else in the gallery seemed to know it. The boy came in behind a high probing line, slowly, testing, finding out what was there. He did not go near Brom's left side, because nobody had told him to. He went at Brom's right instead, the side the file had called the strong one, because he had looked at it for himself and thought it might not be as strong as all that. He was wrong about that, but he was wrong honestly, with his own eyes, and he learned something from every touch he did not land.

In the second exchange he took a touch off Brom. It was a real one, a quick left hand that came round under Brom's right forearm while Brom was turning a feint, and Brom felt it land and nodded to him across the slate. Then Brom priced him, patiently, for an exchange and a half, and took him twice.

"Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

At the rope the boy stood in front of Brom with his chest heaving and his coach a pace behind him.

"I didn't have anything on you," the boy said. "I didn't know anything."

"I know." Brom held out his hand. "You fought me. Not the paper." He shook the boy's hand once, firmly. "Hardly anybody's doing that this season. Remember you did."

The boy went off under his coach's arm. Cael guessed those two sentences would still be with him at the next meet, and the one after that.

The southern coach waited until the boy had gone to the bench. Then he came back to the rope by himself and stood in front of Brom with his hands behind his back.

"He'll be better for that," he said. "Better than if he'd won off a page." He looked at Brom for a moment longer, and then, briefly, at the rest of them along the rope, one face after another, the way a man counts his change. "Good season to you," he said, and went.

Karis watched him all the way back across the floor and wrote one line in her notebook without looking down at it.

Brom's figure was twenty-three and the boy's twenty-one. It was Brom's third title of the season, and the cohort board at home would need its line rewritten, and Rooke would rewrite it in the same words, because the words were still true.

At supper Lira gave them the board-man.

She gave him to them whole, with his voice and his hands. She had saved him all day, through a semifinal and a final, and now she gave him to the long table by the east fire with her stew going cold in front of her, standing up for the best parts.

"No book," she said, in the board-man's voice, which was slow and broad and full of chalk. "No book on your lad, miss, because a book wants two ways for a thing to go, and there's only the one. There's not a soul in this town who'll lay so much as a copper on the other fellow, and you can't make a price on a thing nobody will take the other side of."

She wiped her hands on an invisible apron.

"So I asked him about the margin, because I'd heard the mill town tried it." She went back into the voice. "Aye, we tried it. Not *will* he win, but *by how much*: two touches to one in three, two to nothing in four, all of that. A tidy market. We closed it inside a day." She leaned on the table with both hands, as the board-man had leaned on his slate. "And then the slips came in, and every slip said the same thing. Narrow win, three exchanges, nothing to look at. You'd have thought somebody was going round the town door to door with the answer."

"They had," said Karis. "They'd all read the same file."

"That's what I said!" Lira pointed at her. "And he said, aye, and then he leaned right over his slate, like this, and told me the part he said he'd never once seen in all his days at the chalk." She dropped her voice. "*They were right.* Every one of them, narrow and three exchanges and nothing to look at, to the touch. I can't chalk a price on a sunrise, miss. Uncertainty is the whole product. Take it away and I'm a man standing in a yard with a piece of chalk." She straightened up. "Then he pointed his chalk across at the floor. You were on the bench by the rope doing your wrist up, looking like a clerk's apprentice waiting for his dinner. And he said—"

She stopped, and looked at Cael, and gave it to him in the board-man's voice, slowly.

"He's the most classified fighter on this circuit. Some clerk ought to write and let the registry know."

The table laughed. Brom laughed until he had to wipe his eyes, and Ephram laughed and then laughed harder because Brom was laughing, and even Karis laughed once, sharply, and wrote it down. Cael laughed with them, properly, because it was funny and because Lira had done the board-man's hands perfectly.

Then Ephram gave them his loss, because it was his turn and because he wanted to.

He did it standing, as Lira had, with his file book open, and he read it out item by item in the flat voice of a carter reading a manifest at a toll gate.

"Item. Heat-shadow. The air over the stove end moves, and if you read shoulders you read them late. He knew that, and I didn't. Cost: the second exchange." He turned a page. "Item. Floor speed. The north flags carry a skin of damp off the windows, and they're a hand faster than the rest of the floor. He walked me onto them twice. Cost: the second exchange and the fourth." He turned another. "Item. In the fourth I committed early because my foot travelled. That one's mine. I'd seen the flags by then and I still didn't look at them, because I was too busy looking at his feet. Cost: the bout."

He closed the book.

"That's the account," he said. "I'd have it otherwise. But it balances."

Nobody laughed this time. Rooke had come back from the exchange while Lira was doing the board-man and had been sitting at the far end of the table by the fire with his coat still on. He did not say anything. But he took his own file out of his coat and wrote one line in it, and Cael, who could read Rooke's hand upside down at twenty feet, read it.

*Takes a loss like freight. Ready.*

Karis had a notebook open beside her bowl. It was not the grey one. It was a long thin book with a stiff cover that she had bought at the wool town, and it was nearly full.

"While we're doing accounts," she said. "Eleven pages."

"Of what?" said Brom.

"The clerk." She held the book open toward them so they could see how close the writing ran. "The presiding adjudicator's clerk. He's sat at the end of continental panels for longer than any of us has been alive, and he'll talk to anybody who asks him a proper question, so I asked him two afternoons' worth." She turned the pages as she spoke. "How cold changes flag timing, which it does: the stewards' hands are slow in a hall like this one, and a touch at the very end of a glass gets called a quarter-beat late. How five judges work in practice, as against how the manual says they ought to. Which of the manual's rules are law, and which are only habits that everybody has forgotten are habits." She closed the book. "When we get to Norhold I won't need to ask anybody which is which. I'll see it from the gallery."

"You lost a quarterfinal to do that," said Lira.

"I lost a quarterfinal to a man who knew where the stove was," said Karis. "I'd have lost it anyway. This way I lost it with a full book."

Later, at the window of his room, he wrote the board-man down another way.

*A betting house has checked Seln's arithmetic on its own account and come to the same answer. The file on me calls my margin to the exchange, and the town bet on it, and the town was right. A man whose whole trade is not knowing has looked at me and found nothing he doesn't know. He calls me the most classified fighter on the circuit, as a joke, and it is the most accurate thing anybody has said about me all season.*

*So far I've thought of the scatter program as manners, a polite answer to Seln's sum. It isn't manners any more. From tomorrow it's cover, and it starts in the morning.*

Karis did not wait for the road home. She held the audit that same night.

She asked Rooke for the whole company, and Rooke, who did not ask her why, gave it to her. They met in the inn's back parlour after the common room had emptied: the five, and Rooke, and Gault with his case, and the two reserves, and Seln, who sat on a hard chair just inside the door, as he always did. The wagoner was asleep in the stable loft. Karis said that was just as well, because the wagoner talked to horses and horses talked to nobody, and she had no concerns about him at all.

She stood at the parlour table with her notebook and went through it like a lesson.

"This is what the market has on each of us," she said, and read it out, a few lines a head, item by item. On Brom it had page three and his bouts. On Lira it had her bouts and her titles and the story the town had told about her in three versions. On Cael it had what every record had: the public suite at its documented rates, the burst's distance and the landing beat, the read that looked like timing, the six figures in their band. "Here's where every line of it came from. Meet records, bought. The gallery, watched. Hearing transcripts, paid for at the copying rate." She looked up. "All of it from outside. There's nothing from our training floor, nothing about the program, nothing about who trains when or with whom, and nothing anybody said inside our gate or inside these inns. The walls hold."

She turned a page.

"Here's how they keep holding. Nothing tactical in a taproom, and nothing in a warm-up yard. Nothing said within thirty feet of a rail. The people who buy what a seat can see will buy what it can hear as well, and they'll get it cheaper. Corrections get made in a closed room, or they wait until there is one."

Gault, who had sat on more meet floors than all the rest of them together, nodded slowly.

"Letters," said Karis. "Bracken's pouch, both ways, and never the town post. The pouch is sealed at both ends. The town post is a box on a counter." She turned another page, and looked at Ephram.

Ephram looked back at her.

"Yesterday morning at first light," said Karis, "you ran your entry in the loading yard behind the exchange, in the open, twice through, slowly and at speed. From the inn's back door I could see you. From the gate, so could a steward. Your quarterfinal opponent declined that exact entry twice that afternoon, and you said at the rope it was as if somebody had told him. This morning the Stone declined it too." She closed the notebook on her finger. "Somebody did tell them. It was sold inside a day."

The parlour was quiet. Ephram's ears went slowly red, all the way round.

Then he nodded. He took it as the cohort took a correction from Rooke, straight and without argument, and Cael liked him for it more than he had liked him all season.

"Closed floors," said Ephram. "From now on. What else?"

"One more." Karis looked round the room. "Yesterday a coach from the south walked across the warm-up bay and gave Brom his file, so that his own lad would lose honestly. That was decent. It was also a piece of business. In doing it he let this table know his house is a customer of the same shop, and he put us in his debt for a season he hasn't fought yet. And while we read page three, he was across the bay with his hands behind his back, reading us." She put the notebook down. "Decent and instrumental at once. Nearly everything in this trade is both. "Two to nothing. Bout to Halcenvane."

The Blade shook his hand and said, with a kind of puzzled pleasure, that that had been quicker than the record. Cael said the slate was a good floor, which was true.

Cael could not tell whether Rooke had seen the three points. He thought it very likely that Rooke had seen them and decided, as he decided most things about Cael, to put the correction on the sheet and leave the cause where he had found it.

He had not looked at the gallery during the bout. He looked at it afterward, walking his circle at the rope as Rooke had told them to, and found what he had expected to find. The two from Auremont were in the front row in their good dark coats, with the device on their notebooks.

The older one, the man, never wrote during an exchange. He watched with his hands still on his knees, and when the exchange was over he wrote for a long time, a whole page, without looking up. So he held whole exchanges in his head from start to finish, and trusted them to keep. A man who could do that had fought once, and fought well.

The younger one, the woman, wrote constantly. But as Cael watched her pen through the rest of the morning's bouts, he saw that she did not write during the exchanges either. She wrote between them, and only, as far as he could make out, between his. She wrote while he walked back to his mark, and while he stood at it, and in the landing beat after his burst, and in the moment after a touch when he turned to go back. She was writing in the gaps.

He did not know what she was writing.

He thought about it through the rest of the morning, while he rested in his coat before the afternoon, and he could not make it come out. He knew what his fights showed, because he had built them to show it. He did not know what his resets showed. It had never occurred to him that they could be read, and somebody from the best house on the continent had sat in a cold gallery and read them all morning.

The figure would post in the morning, and he would find out then what three points up looked like on paper. He found, to his surprise, that he was not thinking about the figure at all.

His second challenger of the day had filed late the evening before, and was waiting for him at the west floor after the noon bell. He was a quarry foreman, and he was a grappler.

# Chapter 19 — First Installment

The foreman was waiting at the west floor's rope with his coat already off.

He was the biggest man Cael had fought all season, and not the tallest. He was perhaps a head shorter than Brom and half again as wide, with a short thick neck and forearms like the posts of a gate.

He ran a lifting gang on the third terrace of the quarry above the town, Rooke's sheet said, and he had run it for twelve years.

As a young man he had gone to the Bronze trials twice and come home both times, and after the second time he had stopped going and started lifting stone. He was a Stone Path, of the rooting kind. He fought as close to the other man as he could get, and then closer.

He had filed the challenge late the evening before, in a big careful hand, and in the space for a reason he had written *my lads asked me to*.

Cael had read that line twice, and liked him for it, and gone to bed thinking about item one.

*Against grapplers and leverage men: a slow first exchange. Let him have the first touch. Win from behind. Two points off.*

It was the right man for it, on the wrong floor. Cael knew both things as he stood at his mark in his coat with the cold coming up through his boots.

He had known the second since the Fourth-day, when the slate had taken his push and given nothing back. He had looked for an answer for four days and not found one. *Bring the feet back sooner, or don't let him close.*

He had decided, in the end, that he would bring the feet back sooner. He had decided it on the tailboard and again at the window and again that morning, and it had seemed a reasonable thing to decide every time.

The steward called his name. He gave his coat to Gault and walked out onto the slate.

Under the exhibition rules, a fighter thrown cleanly onto his back had been touched, as surely as by a flat palm. The foreman knew that rule very well. Cael could see it in the way he stood.

"Exchange."

Cael gave him the first exchange, as he had planned to.

He kept his eyes early and his feet late. The read gave him the foreman's weight a fraction before it moved, as it always did, and he let the weight come.

The foreman came straight in, without hurry, low and wide, with his hands open in front of him like a man walking up to a nervous horse.

Cael gave ground a beat late. He gave it a beat late again. He stood on the cold slate and let the big man close, one step and then another, and did not leave.

He had expected the foreman to take the touch the cheap way. The Stone reserve had taken it that way at home, a flat hand on the shoulder and done, because the drill had told him to.

The foreman did not want the cheap touch. He wanted Cael.

His hands came in at the collar and the left sleeve, both at once, and closed, and the closing did not stop. It was Brom's hold on the oak, the hold of a man who meant to keep what he had taken.

Cael felt the foreman's hips come in under his own. Then the slate went away from under his feet, and the hall turned over once, and he came down on his back on stone that had never in a hundred years given anybody anything.

"Touch. Quarry."

The breath went out of him all at once. He lay on the slate for a heartbeat looking up into the dark under the roof, with his whole back ringing, and knew that he had been given what he had asked for, to the letter, and that it had cost more than he had priced it at.

He got up. The foreman had already let go and stepped back, as the rules required, and was walking back to his mark.

The gallery along the north wall made a noise that was half cheer and half groan.

The foreman's own people, the quarry lads up from the terraces in their dusty coats, were cheering. Everybody else had come to see the provision boy and was disappointed.

That was the first exchange, and it had gone as designed. A touch given, and given cleanly, to a man who had earned it.

But it had been slow, and slowness on that floor had a price he had not written down. He had stood still in the cold for most of a glass, on purpose, letting a man come to him.

He had not walked. He had not kept moving, as Rooke had told them all to on the first morning. His legs had stood on the slate for the whole of that exchange, and the slate had been taking the heat out of them the entire time. Slate took the heat out of everything.

He walked his circle now, in the interval. It was not enough. He could feel it in his calves, a faint stiffness, like the first hour of a cold morning before the blood has properly come.

The second exchange was where the design ran out.

He meant to come back at full pace now. That was the plan: the touch given, the first exchange spent, and from here the feet were his own again, as they had been on the oak every time. *Win from behind.*

He stood at his mark and told his feet that the waiting was over.

The foreman had learned something too.

He had been given the first exchange, and he did not know he had been given it. As far as he could tell he had met a boy who was slow off the mark on a cold floor, and he came across the slate to meet that boy again, sooner and harder.

He did not walk this time. He came in two long strides, low, with his arms out, closing on the place where a slow boy would be standing.

Cael's eyes were early. His feet were not.

They had been told for a whole exchange to wait, and they had been standing in the cold while they waited, and now that he wanted them back they came back a fraction late. It was not much. It was perhaps a quarter of a beat.

On the oak at home it would not have mattered, because on the oak a late step could be bought back with the floor's spring. On the slate there was no spring to buy anything with.

He turned to go, and the foreman's right hand found his left wrist.

It was one hand. It was enough. The grip closed on the wrist and did not stay there.

It walked. It went up his forearm to the elbow, hand over hand, in two short heavy movements, faster than Cael would have believed a hand that size could move.

Then it was above the elbow, on the upper arm, and the foreman's hips were turning in, and the arm was being drawn across the big man's body like a rope across a capstan.

Cael knew what came next. It was a lever. The arm was the bar and the shoulder was the hinge, and in a moment the foreman would lean, and either the shoulder would give or Cael would go down onto the slate again to save it.

It was a clean hold, legal, and very good.

And at the contact, under the foreman's hand, something in Cael was ready.

He felt it the way you feel a latch under your thumb. It had been there at every contact since Ardenmere.

Reydan's gift, the thing that took a blow into the forearm and down through the body and out through the feet, so that what arrived had nowhere to stay.

It would take this. He knew it would take this. It would take the whole of the foreman's lean into his arm and send it down into the slate, and his shoulder would never feel it, and the big man would find himself leaning on something that was not there.

It was on no record anywhere at Halcenvane. Nobody official had ever seen it.

It had not been shown on a scored floor or in front of a crowd since the bout at Ardenmere, and there were five judges on the trestle at the south side of this floor with slates in front of them, and two people from Auremont in the front row of the gallery.

He kept his thumb off the latch.

The foreman leaned.

The force came into the arm and had nowhere to go but the shoulder, and it went into the shoulder. It went into the front of the left shoulder, into the seam where the arm meets the body, and something there pulled.

It was not a crack. It was worse than a bruise and less than a break: a hot tearing pull deep in the joint, as if a thick cord had been drawn too tight and some of its threads had let go.

The hall went white at the edges.

He did the only thing there was to do. He went with it.

You cannot fight a lever. You can only go the way it is going, faster than it expects.

He turned toward the pain instead of away, down and under the foreman's arm, and dropped to his knees on the slate, and twisted, and the foreman's grip, which had been made for a man standing up, slid off the turning arm.

For half a breath Cael was on his knees beside the big man's legs with nothing holding him.

He went down the times to the bottom of the block, and then went back to the top and went down them again.

He knew Wind times. He had watched Lira's for years and logged them, and knew her burst intervals and her closing distances better than he knew his own.

These were Wind times, and they were not Iron times. The gap between one burst and the next was shorter than anything in Rooke's file. The figures for a long sequenced run belonged further up the page by two whole tiers.

And beside the closing speed, in a different ink, stood a small mark and a set of initials, which meant that some examiner had looked at that number, refused to believe it, measured it again himself, and signed his name to it.

"There," said Lira. "You went back to the top. Everybody goes back to the top. I've watched three people do it tonight."

She took the page back from him and held it against her knee again without looking at it. She did not need to look at it.

"Do you know what I see in it?" she said. "I see a door. Over and over. Somebody found her at twelve and let her in, and every year after that there was another door, and somebody opened it and held it for her. Every one I stood outside of and knocked on, the whole time I was growing up."

She turned the page over in her fingers. "And she went through every single one of them properly. That's the part. She didn't waste one. There isn't a trick in it anywhere. It's just doors, and a girl walking through them well."

She put the page in her coat and picked up her mug.

"Now the coat-rack," she said.

He waited.

"For years I had a picture in my head." She looked out at the lake. "I walk into their hall in somebody else's colours, and they see me, and they know what they let go. I had it worked out to the last breath. I used to run it on the walk home from Vell's when I was too tired to sleep."

She drank. "And this morning there they were, two lanes over, and I went to find the picture, and there was nothing there to find. I stood at that coat-rack with one wrist wrapped, looking for it."

"I saw."

"I know you did. You were standing where you always stand." She turned the mug in her hands. "So all day I've been asking where it went. And I think I know."

She looked at him. "The moment was three years of work, and I already had it. Vell's floor. Greyvane. The crown. That counting room at the station, and the frame, and the four seconds. Every one of those was the moment. I just kept waiting for them to be in the room for it. And somewhere on a wagon I stopped needing them in the room at all, and I never noticed I'd stopped."

Cael said nothing for a while. She had earned a while. Then, because she had brought him the account to be looked at properly, he looked at the one line she had left off it.

"The woman with the tape box," he said.

"She—" Lira stopped. The name was right there; he heard it in her breath. She did not say it. She let it go back to wherever she kept it, and he watched her choose to, and put the choice away somewhere he kept things he respected.

"She's the one who brought my things out to me," Lira said. "At the end. They'd shut the wing to me by then. She'd folded everything and put it in my bag, and carried it down to the gate herself, and she didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything anybody was allowed to say."

She drank the last of her mug. "So she nodded this morning, and I nodded back. One each. That's the whole estate. Institution's bankrupt; the person gets paid."

She set the empty mug on the step between them. Then she put her hand on her coat, over the folded page, and was quiet for a while.

"She's not faster than me," she said.

The lake did not move. The lamp up on the mountain did not move. Lira sat on the cold slate with her hand on the page and finished it properly, as she had learned to finish things.

"She's faster than me *right now*."

She stood up and took both mugs.

"If the draws at Norhold go the way they ought to, she's in my semifinal," she said, already on the steps. "Put down that I'm glad."

He put it down.

Rooke gave the rest of them the coaches' supper in the common room when the fiddler had at last given up, standing at the end of the long table with the folio under his arm.

"We came up here to qualify," he said. "That's what this year has been. Four meets, and get over a line before the year turns. Three meets are done. I think we're close enough to see the line. I'll believe we're across it when it comes up the road on a circular, and I'll not believe it a day before."

He put the folio on the table. "From tonight, though, the work is for Norhold. And Norhold is no bigger version of this."

He laid the extracts round the table face down, one in front of each of them.

"Nobody fights at Norhold who didn't cross a threshold to get there. So there is nobody there at the bottom of a tier. You won't meet one fighter the whole tournament who's at the floor of their rank. Whoever you draw has climbed to the last rung their tier owns." He put the last extract down in front of Ephram. "It's a bracket of ceilings. Read what you've been given. Then go to bed."

Brom turned his over by the fire, where he read everything, with Cael standing at his shoulder, because Brom did not read papers about other people by himself either.

The Copper pages were thin. Nobody spent much money on Copper files at the continental level, Karis said from the other side of the table, and you could see where they had saved it.

Brom went down the page one entry at a time, slowly, his finger under each line. Halfway down he stopped. Cael had already stopped there.

*MAREK — Rhagen Institute — Glass — Copper, Rank 1 — notations attached.*

The notations were short and dry and frightening. There were no figures for strength worth reading and none for speed. There were first exchanges, one after another, and in nearly every one the other fighter had been taken apart before he had properly begun.

Every touch was marked against a place on the opponent where one thing joined another: a shoulder line, the turn of a reset, the gap in a guard as it changed hands.

Beside every touch stood the same small mark. Karis leaned across the table to look at it, and said what the compilers meant by it: the same join, found once and then worked again and again until the flags came up.

"Copper Rank One?" said Ephram, who had come round the table to see. "With that?"

"That's a number somebody wrote down," said Brom. "Rhagen teaches by doctrine. They're holding him at One for reasons of their own."

He read the notations again from the top, slowly, all the way through. He read them as he ate, completely, and his face went still and heavy as it did for very few things. Cael had first seen it do that at Ardenmere, before either of them had a house or a rank, and had learned since then what it was for. It was the face Brom kept for a problem he respected.

Brom put the page down.

"This one's been mispriced too. The dangerous direction."

The meet record went up on the exchange's board at the first bell the next morning, while the wagons were being loaded on the quay.

Seln copied it, as he copied everything, in a neat column on a fresh sheet, with the travel file under his arm and the cold coming in through the great door behind him. Cael stood beside him with his arm in Gault's scarf and read the exhibition leaf for himself.

There were two figures by his name this time: twenty-six and twenty.

Beside them, smaller, in the clerk's square chalk, stood the unrounded composites. Twenty-six exactly. Twenty and a third.

He looked at them, and then at the column he carried in his head, the six figures from the wool town and the mill town that had kept time like a metronome. Twenty-three, twenty-two, twenty-four, twenty-two, twenty-one, twenty-three. And now twenty-six and twenty, the highest figure he had ever been given beside the lowest, posted the same morning from the same hall.

The band had come apart at both ends. It breathed. It looked like a fighter who had good days and bad ones, on floors that suited him and floors that did not.

It had cost a shoulder to make it look like that, and he had paid for every point.

Halcenvane stood first of six on the meet. Lira had taken the Iron and Brom the Copper. Ephram had a semifinal, and Karis a quarterfinal and a notebook full of the clerk.

On the season's count the house was still second, by less than before, with one meet left on the card and the threshold close enough to see. Rooke read the board from the bottom up, folded his copy into his coat, and said, "Wagons."

Nobody moved at once. Lira stood in front of the Iron leaf with her hands in her pockets and read her own name at the top of it, and the figure beside it, and the word under it that the clerk had chalked in square capitals: *Title.* She read it the way she had read the milestone on the ninth day of the road, all at once and without stopping. Then she did not look away from it. She looked at it for a good while, and Cael, standing behind her with his arm in a scarf, understood that this was the difference, and did not say anything.

Brom read the Copper leaf with his head on one side and said, "Same line again," with deep satisfaction. Ephram copied the whole record into his file book, both brackets and the exhibitions and the house totals, standing up, in his careful hand, and when Karis asked him why, when Seln was already copying it, he said that a champion of the upper Iron kept his own files and that he did not intend to stop being one just because he had lost a semifinal. Gault picked up his case by its handle and held it a little higher than he needed to, so that the registry clerk at the next table could see the stitching.

In the third row of the empty gallery, a woman in plain grey wool sat with a small notebook on her knee. Her round came to the exhibition leaf and stopped for its two heartbeats, and she wrote something, and went on.

The next edition of somebody's file, Cael supposed, would think him a little less tidy than the last one had.

Good. That was the product working.

Seln had written the quarterly at first light, at the document table in the empty common room, before the board went up and while the wagons were still standing in the yard.

It took him a quarter of an hour, in the office's standard form. He wrote every word slowly and read every word back, as he would have read a stranger's work, hunting for the fault.

*Subject competed within the basis of his enrollment. Exhibitions conducted under charter provision; figures recorded, variation within the range expected. No undisclosed capability observed. Schedule continues. Routine.*

Each line held its load, and not a hair more. The variation was within the range expected; by noon it would be on a public board in chalk to prove it. Nothing undisclosed had been observed under conditions that allowed it to be confirmed. The schedule did continue. It was routine.

He sealed it and handed it to the courier who carried the region's sealed mail north. He watched the man ride out along the quay toward the hill road until the road took him. Then he went back inside, picked up the travel file, and went to stand in front of the board with his pencil.

True. Complete in form. Empty.

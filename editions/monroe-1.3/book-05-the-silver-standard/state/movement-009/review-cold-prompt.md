# Chapter 54 — Terms

The first thing he did was put the clock back.

He woke before the light, which he had known he would, and lay for a while with his eyes shut, listening to the harbour turn over under the window. Then he sat up and reached for Hesk's travelling clock on the chair, and turned the hands back by an hour, until they agreed with the bell that was just then beginning on the far side of the basin. One bell, then a second answering it from the timekeepers' tower, then the little cracked one at the fish steps, late as always. He set the clock down. It ticked. For once it was right, and he had made it right himself, and that was one thing in the day he could be sure of.

There was a dull hold behind his eyes from the night before. It was the price of going back down into the trial at a tenth of its speed, and he had known when he paid it that it would still be there in the morning. It was not pain. It was more like the feeling of a room in which somebody has recently been talking loudly. He let it be.

The light came up grey and then white off the water. Down on the quays the first carts were already moving, more of them than any morning yet, and all going the same way.

He dressed slowly. There was nothing to hurry for. Everything that could be done had been done, and written down, and burned, and the rest was out there on a floor that had been bolted into the ground for one afternoon and would be taken up again in the night.

The keeper was on the stair when they came down, as he was every morning, and he counted them past him under his breath as he always did. He got to five. Then he did a thing he had not done in three weeks of mornings. He stood aside against the wall, and took his cap off, and held it against his chest until the last of them had gone by.

Nobody said anything about it. Ephram, at the bottom, looked back up at him and nodded, and the keeper nodded too, as if they had settled something between them.

Brom had found food. Nobody had asked him to and nobody had seen him go out for it, but there was a loaf on the long table that had not been there at midnight, still warm in its cloth, and a crock of butter, and a paper of salt fish from the quay, and he was cutting the loaf into slabs with his bandaged forearms held well out of the way. Cael ate two. He did not taste them. He ate them because Rooke would ask.

Lira did his wraps at the end of the table, the left hand and then the right, frowning at the tension as she had frowned at it on every floor since the sprung oak. Karis sat across from them with her notebook shut on the board in front of her and both pink palms laid flat on its cover. She did not open it once. Ephram talked, in a voice that had been sanded to a whisper by four exchanges of calling across a hill, about the lock-keeper's hand-bell, and the price of salt fish, and how nobody in the southern house could sing; and nobody listened to a word, and everybody was glad of every one.

When they went out the street was full.

It was full the way a river is full after rain, all of it going one way, and as the five of them went down it people came out of their doors and fell in behind them without a word, as if they had only been waiting at the windows to see which way to go. Hawkers walked along beside the crowd without calling anything. On the bridge over the inner basin a woman selling hot chestnuts had given up selling them and simply stood holding her pan, watching the plain colours go by.

At the north gate of the Concourse the queue for the standing places went back along the harbour wall farther than Cael could see. A steward told Rooke it had begun forming at the second bell, in the dark, and that people had stood in it all night in the cold with blankets over their heads and not one of them had complained. He said it in the tone of a man who had stood in that gate for every sitting the city had held in his working life and had never once heard of such a thing.

---

The marshals posted it before the brackets had even begun. Absolute capacity. Every seat in the four banks of the tiers was sold, every standing place on the gangways, every corner of every gallery. That was eight thousand. Beyond them the stewards had stopped counting. People stood on the tower stairs and along the crews' walkways under the roof, and the stewards walked past them with their faces carefully blank, as if they were not there at all.

The betting rows had done something new. The office had let it be known the day before that it did not care for it. For the whole sitting the rows had kept their old frame on the wall, *NO BOOK ON THE DEMONSTRATION BOUTS*, and nobody had touched it. This morning there was a second board beside it. It did not offer odds on who would win. Everybody already knew who would win. It offered odds on the figure. The bands of the Standard were chalked down it in a column, from Iron at the bottom to Gold at the top, cut into half points, with a price against every one. Men were queueing to buy them. Cael read the board through once on his way past, from top to bottom, and saw that there was no line on it anywhere for no figure at all. It had not occurred to anybody to offer one.

The broadsides had spent a week deciding what to call the afternoon. There were four of them in the city now, and they agreed about nothing. But somewhere in the last two days each of them, on its own, had arrived at the same word. Every front sheet in every hand at the gate called it *the exhibition*, with no other word in front of it, as if the category had only ever held one bout and this was it.

The morning card ran first, and Cael did not see any of it.

The warm-up hall was under the east tiers, low and long and lit by lamps, and Halcenvane had it to itself by some arrangement nobody mentioned. Through its stone ceiling the card came down to them as weather. They heard the Iron final as a long roar and then a short one. The Silver was mostly silence, with one great shout in the middle of it. The Gold went on for a long time. Then came the trial final, Auremont against the river academy. It was a sound Cael knew in his own legs from three days ago, rising and falling with every exchange, and at the end of it a long level roar that went on and on and told him, without anybody needing to say it, whose flag had gone up.

Ephram, hoarse, put his ear against the wall for the end of it. "Auremont," he said. "Without her. She wasn't on the sheet. They kept her in a box all morning." He took his ear away. "I'd have kept her in a box too."

Between the bracket finals Gault came down from the north gate, where he had been since the second bell with the two healers of the north station, and stood in the doorway and nodded at Cael once, as a man nods at a builder whose wall he has just walked along, and went back up.

The Compact's row was full. Seln brought word of it with the protocols, and later Cael saw it for himself.

At the end of the row was the twelfth chair. It had been held, unplated, for thirteen days before anybody sat in it, and had been empty again since the day of the duelist. There was a man in it now. He wore the plain grey of an inspecting officer and nothing on his collar. Seln said he had come in by the east stair an hour before the brackets began, alone, with no case and no clerk, and had sat down, and had not moved.

---

A little before the call Cael went up the tunnel to the mouth of the north break and looked at the floor, because he wanted to see it once with the bowl full round it.

Nine chairs stood at the long table on the west side, where five had stood for the Silvers. The senior exhibition panel had its five, and four reserves had been brought up to sit with them, every one of them with a chart. Behind them, at a table of their own, sat three men with ruled sheets in front of them and nothing to do yet. They were the recording scribes the office had asked for. Two of them were ruling extra lines. The third had his hands folded and was looking at the masts.

And Umber was on the floor.

He had come down out of the west tower by himself, in the plain grey of his office, and he was walking the ring. He went round it close to the barrier, at the pace of a man walking round his own house at night to see that the doors are shut. He stopped at each of the four masts and looked up at its iron crown, and down at the cable going into the trench. At the oak he stopped longest. He stood on the old dark wood for a moment, quite still. Then he stepped off it onto the new stone at its rim and put his weight on his heel, and then on the ball of his foot, and then moved a stride and did it again.

Cael had read the crews' certificate the night before, pinned in the tunnel with the foreman's name and Auremont's risk officer's beneath it. The floor was true to the specification. Every bolt had been driven and every peg checked. It was written down and signed. Umber had signed it too, as the office required. And here he was in front of eight thousand people, testing it again with his own foot, as if the signatures were the beginning of knowing a thing and not the end of it.

Cael watched him all the way round, and something in his chest that had been held tight since he woke let go a little. Whatever this man's instrument said this afternoon, it would be what the instrument said.

Back in the warm-up hall Rooke took the last five minutes, as he always took them.

He did not gather them in. He stood by the door of the warm-up hall with his coaching sheet folded in his inside pocket next to card four, and waited for the others to move away of their own accord, which they did, because they knew. Then he came and stood in front of Cael, a little closer than he usually stood.

"Five minutes," he said. "I'll use three. You'll want the other two."

"Yes."

"There's no figure to fight for today and no flag to count. Forget both. You've got one job out there that's worth anything, and it's the one you've done since you were a boy in a yard. Chart her." He took Cael's right wrist and turned it over, and looked at Lira's wrap, and turned it back. Lira, at the wall, did not say anything about him checking her work, which on any other day she would have. "Chart her the whole way through, whatever she does to you. And when you feel yourself start to be frightened, which you will, because I would be, don't spend the chart on it. Frightened is expensive and it isn't information."

He let go of the wrist.

"That's three," he said, and stepped back.

Brom did not come away from the wall, because he did not need to, and he said it from where he stood, in the same flat voice he had used in the back room two nights before, with his bandaged arms folded and his left shoulder held a little lower than his right.

"Survive the first one."

That was all. Everybody else that week had given him advice. Brom had given him a job, with a beginning and an end, and it could be done or not done.

At the half-bell Seln appeared in the doorway with the folder for the day.

He laid it open on the end of the bench and took Cael through it as he would have taken him through a coursework form at the counter: the protocols countersigned for the delegation; the credential confirmed; the healers' forms for the north station, each with its line for a signature and a line for a witness. Cael signed where the pen pointed. Seln blotted each, and turned the page. At the end of the folder there was nothing. There was no extra sheet and no strip of grey paper between the leaves. The folder ended where the forms ended, because whatever there had been to say had been said already, and burned.

Seln squared the papers and closed the folder.

"The office confirms the schedule," he said.

"Thank you for the forms."

Seln tucked the folder under his arm and went up the stair to the gallery, at his counter pace, and did not look back.

---

A grey-haired official with a stand and a lamp, though the day was bright, had been set beside the referee at the south end of the floor. When he lifted his sheet the bowl hushed for him, tier by tier, from the floor upward.

First the category, by its old name. Then the article of the charter it was heard under, and the filing, with its date, and the word *Accepted.* that had been painted on the board below the tunnel. Then the rider's number, for the underwriters' clerks. Then the names.

"Daeva. Auremont Academy. Storm Path. Gold, Rank Three."

He stopped. He turned his page, though there was nothing on the next page that he needed, and then he read the other name, and the house, and then he came to the place on his sheet where a Path and a rating went.

"Unclassified."

Nobody in the bowl made a sound, either time.

Cael had heard that silence once before, very small, in a circuit hall at Ardenmere, where Vell's ledger had first kept the form for him. A clerk had read his name there in the same flat voice he used for everybody else, and stopped where a rating went, and said the word, and gone on. Nobody there had made a sound either. It was the oldest courtesy a floor had. They had given it to him at Ardenmere in a room of two hundred. They gave it to him here in a room of eight thousand. It was exactly the same size.

Then the protocols were read, and then they were read again. Every bout above Silver had them read twice, Seln had said, so that nobody could claim afterward not to have heard. The referee and the panel each held the power to stop the bout, either without the other, and nobody could appeal it. There were two healers at each fighters' gate. If fire went where nobody meant it to go, the masts would take it, and the cables would carry it down into the trench, and the trench would carry it off toward the east break, out of the bowl. On the second reading Cael listened for the one thing he had marked on the first. Every line of it was measured in tiers. Every threshold in it, for when a healer should stand and when a bout should stop, was set against a fighter's tier. There was one fighter in the bout to whom none of it could be set.

When the reading was done each party was allowed one walk round the ring. Cael took his.

He went round it the way he had gone round every floor of his life, with his feet. At the north, the east, the south and the west he set a heel down hard and listened up through the bone of his leg. Every time the stone told him the same flat thing. It would not give under him, and it would not forgive him. He felt the half inch under his soles as he came down off the oak toward the south. It was so slight that he would never have known it without the man with the level, and now that he knew, he could not stop feeling it. He passed the east mast and heard nothing from it. It would not hum until there was something in the air to hum at. He stepped across the cap seam at the east break, and felt it through his boot, once, the way she had said.

She did not walk the ring. She stood at her mark by the south break with her hands at her sides, looking at the middle distance. She had walked it already, two evenings ago, with him.

When he came back to the north flag she looked up from the air in front of her, where her eyes had rested all morning through the reading and the protocols and his walk round the ring, and for the first time that day she looked at him. She spoke at the pitch two people use on a crowded stair.

"Terms hold?"

"Terms hold," said Cael.

The referee stood at the oak in his grey with the silver bar at his collar, and said the rules of the category in the voice of a man who had said them a thousand times, and looked from one of them to the other, and lifted his arm, and brought it down.

---

She did not go to the oak.

He had built his whole first minute on her going to the oak. He had said it to her face, two evenings before, and she had told him she would have changed it by today, and he had believed her and planned for the oak anyway, because a habit of six years does not change in two days. It had changed in two days. She went the other way. She went out from the south flag toward the west barrier, unhurried, and put her shoulder almost to the boards, and stood at the wall as he had seen her stand at nothing else, looking along it.

Then the air along the west barrier went heavy.

He was late. He knew it as it happened. He was reading her, because she was the thing in front of him, and she gave him nothing to read. Her weight did not gather. Her hips did not set. There was no small turn of the head before a commitment, nothing that every other fighter he had ever met had been obliged to show him. The read asked its questions of her body and her body answered the first two like any good fighter's, and the third not at all. Then she was down the barrier at him, along the foot of it, and he went sideways out of her road on a burst, the first, and felt the stone take his landing hard, and turned.

The wash came after her like a wake behind a boat.

It struck him across the right shoulder while he was still in the landing beat. It should have put him over. It was a Gold's wash at the head of a Gold's lane, the kind he had watched go over Ephram on the trial floor and turn him half round. It went into him instead, the way a hammer goes into a bell, and he hummed with it, and the hum ran down through his legs into the stone and was gone. He did not rock.

He heard the bowl see it, all eight thousand of them at once, up every bank of the tiers to the crews' walkways under the roof where the stewards had stopped counting, and it was not a roar. It was a kind of shift, the sound of eight thousand people moving in their seats at once to look again at something they could not have seen. On the west side, at the long table, one of the reserves leaned to the panelist beside him and said something, and the panelist did not answer, and went on writing.

That was Reydan's give, on a tournament floor, for the first time. It was on a floor in front of a crowd for the first time since the bout at Ardenmere that was its only paper. Nobody official had ever seen it. Now nine judges and three scribes and a full row of the Compact had seen it, and it was in the book.

He tried Karis's card then, because it was the only thing he had brought out with him that he had not yet spent.

*Air first. Not her.*

He could not do it. In the shut hall with Lira he had put her down like a parcel and listened past her, and there had been nothing past her but boards and quiet. Here there was a bowl of eight thousand people breathing. There was the referee's silver bar flashing at the edge of his eye, and a woman at the west barrier who was the most important thing he had ever stood in front of. He reached for the air and every one of them came with it. The whole bowl was loud in his head, and she was the loudest thing in it, and he could no more put her down than he could have put down the floor.

So he read her body, because that was what he had, and it was late, and he knew it would be.

And as he read her he understood what she was doing. Her lanes were not full. He had seen her full lane on the trial floor and these were not that. They ran a little short, and a little shallow, and each one came at a slightly different speed from the one before, as if she were turning a dial. She was not trying to finish him. She was finding out where he stopped. It was what Gault did with a plate before he measured anything against it: take the baseline first, honestly, so that every reading after it would mean something. She was taking his. And then she would fight a little above it, so as not to waste either of them.

There was no contempt in it at all. It was the most careful thing anybody had ever done to him on a floor.

She came again.

It was the same, and it was worse, because she was trying something. The air went heavy along a line between him and the west mast, the only open ground on that side, and closed it. He saw it close. He saw the whole thing a half-beat after it began, which meant he saw it too late to do anything but pay. He went back toward the barrier. He put his right palm flat on the smooth boards an inch below the cap, at a hand's width, and pushed. It was the push he had taken from the Bronze at Ardenmere, the shove that moves a man off his feet from no distance at all, and he had never in his life used it to move himself. It moved him. It threw him four feet along the wall, sideways, out of the mouth of her road. The right shoulder took the bill at once, the old dull bill. One blow of the four.

He spent his second burst and his third trying to buy ground instead of time, which was what the house had done all year and what had beaten two Silvers. The idea was simple. The oak was the top of the floor. If he could stand somewhere between her and it, anything she laid at him would be climbing. So he went for the slope above her, off the wall, and got a long stride up it, and then another. Then the air behind him went heavy, along the line he had just left, which she had laid while he was still deciding to leave it. It closed. The stride became a pocket. He came out of the pocket on the third burst and landed at the barrier again with nothing bought at all.

She went down the west barrier one more time, fast, and at the west break her leading foot met the cap seam and caught. It was very small. Nobody in the tiers could have seen it. A lane's worth of Gold went through the seam with a hitch in it, a quarter-inch of trip, and she came out of it and was clean again at once.

He saw her look down at the seam as she passed it.

She did not cross a seam again for the whole of the afternoon.

Then the bell, and neither of them touched.

He stood with his back to the north-east barrier and breathed. She walked back to the south flag as if she had come in off the quays from a stroll. In the time it takes to boil an egg he had put every public thing he owned on that stone: the Wind, at a rate nobody had ever known was his; the read; the give that no official had seen; the push that nobody had seen carry a load since the circuit. Every one of them had cost him ground. None of them had bought any.

He looked up at the north rail, because he could not help it.

Karis was writing. Lira had both hands flat on the rail. Ephram's mouth was open and no sound was coming out of it. And Brom, at the end, with his bad shoulder held low, lifted his right hand where only the floor could see it and showed him one finger.

One. Survived.

# Chapter 55 — Half a Beat

Gault was at the north break with a cup of water before Cael reached it.

The rules gave a fighter the space between exchanges at his own gate, and one person of his house inside the break, and no more. Rooke had given the place to Gault without discussing it with anybody. Gault held the cup out and Cael drank half and gave it back. Then Gault put the flat of his hand on the right shoulder through the shirt, where the push had paid its bill, and pressed with his thumb, and took the hand away.

"One of four?"

"One."

"It'll take three more." He said it as he had said things about Cael's hip and calf and shoulder all year, as a plain fact about a piece of equipment. "Not more than that, if you can help it. You'll be wanting it on the road."

Above them, at the rail, nobody spoke, and Rooke stood with his sheet folded in his hand and his eyes on the south flag as if there were something written on it that he had not yet finished reading. Karis had finished her line and was waiting with the pen lifted. Lira leaned out over the cap of the barrier as far as the steward at the break would let her, which was not far.

"Put her down," she said.

That was all she said. She said it the way she had said *Early* on the waystation clay, the one word she had found for a thing after a whole day of failing to say it in a hundred. Then she stepped back from the cap and folded her arms, and her left arm went in close to her side the way it always did now, from habit, and she did not look at him again.

He walked back to the north flag.

He knew what she meant, and he knew what it would cost. In the hall it had cost him Brom: Brom walking the whole length of a shut room and standing at his shoulder for four minutes, unheard. Here it would cost him the woman. If he took his attention off her body and gave all of it to the air in front of her, then he would not see her. He would not see her weight, or her hands, or anything she did with them. If the air lied to him, or if he heard it wrong, the thing that ended the bout would come from somewhere he was not looking.

He stood at the flag and turned it over once, as he would have turned over a line in the minute. It was a bad bargain. It was the only one on the table.

He took it.

---

The bell went, and he let her go.

He did it as he had done it on the fourth burst in the hall. He did not shut his eyes; that would have told the whole bowl. He let them go soft instead, as a man does when he reads a page from across a room, and he took his read off her the way you take your hand off a rail. She was still there. He knew she was there, the way you know a wall is there in the dark. But she stopped being loud. And when she stopped, the bowl stopped too, all of it, the eight thousand and the referee's bar and the dais, all of it at once. It was like stepping out of a hall full of people into a yard at night.

What was left was the air.

It was the fullest thing in the ring. He had known that from the hall, and from the squall on the harbour wall with the old masts humming. It pressed on the floor and the barrier and on him, evenly, from every side. And there, ahead of her, between her and him, down the long fall of the floor from the oak toward the east, part of it was heavier than the rest.

It was like a fold in a sheet. Something had pinched it from above and drawn it into a crease.

He moved.

He was off the line of it before the lane opened. He did not burst. He did not need to. He simply walked two quick strides off to the side, on his own feet, and turned, and was standing in still air when the lane came down the crease where he had been and went past him. The wash pulled at his sleeve. It did not touch him.

He heard the bowl then, for a moment. It made the sound a crowd makes when a juggler drops nothing.

The second fold came slanting, low, down the south-east fall toward the barrier, to take the ground he had gone to. He heard it sooner this time, because now he knew what to listen for, and he was two strides further along the barrier before the crease had finished closing. Again on his feet. Again in still air. The lane went down to the barrier's foot where nobody was and spent itself on the trench grating with a short flat crack, and he smelled struck flint, and the east mast gave one low note, like a bell touched with a finger.

Karis's card had a line on it for this. He had read it at the stove until he had it, and watched it curl.

*The leave runs out where the lane does.*

It was the one place in the whole ring where he knew for certain she would be. Fold, then lane, then her: she arrived third. She did not choose where her lane ended. The lane chose. Wherever the crease ran out, she would come out of it, at that spot, at that instant, because the air had let her by that far and no farther. She had been doing it since she was thirteen, and nobody had ever been there first to meet her.

The third fold was short and straight, straight at him, down from the oak, the shortest lane she had laid all day. She was out of patience with him. He heard it begin. He heard exactly where it would end, a stride and a half short of the east barrier, where the floor ran out of fall.

He did not go away from it. He went toward the end of it.

It was his fourth burst. It took him along the barrier's foot to the place where the crease ran out, half a beat before anything else got there. He stood there in the open, in the one spot on the floor where the bowl would have said nobody could stand, and turned, and put up his right hand. The lane closed down on him, and the air went flat and hard in his ears, and at its end, exactly where it had to be, she came out of it.

His palm met her left shoulder.

He let the spark go at the contact. It was Karis's fire, by her leave, out of the heel of his hand, and it was the smallest thing on that floor, and it was clean. The lane's own fire came down at the same instant, as it always did, riding the far end. It cracked beside his hand like a whip. He felt it go along the outside of his right forearm from the wrist halfway to the elbow. Then he was out the other side of it on his feet, and she was a stride past him with her hand at her shoulder.

The flags went up on the dais.

One, then three together, then two more, then one. Seven of nine.

"Touch," said the referee. "One to nought."

The bowl did not roar.

It drew in its breath. All of it at once, in every tier, eight thousand people breathing in through their teeth as one person does when he sees a man step off a roof. The sound of it went round the oval and came back. Then there was no sound at all.

Everybody in the city knew the figure. The broadsides printed it under her name every morning, the way they printed the tide. Nobody had scored on Daeva in four years.

She had stopped two strides past him with her back to the east barrier and her right hand flat on her left shoulder, over the place where his palm had been, like somebody who has felt a raindrop and is waiting to see whether there will be another. She took it away and looked at it, as if she expected to find something written on the palm. Then she looked at him.

He had seen her face at a hundred yards in the procession, and at a stride's length on the trial floor, and at four feet by the west mast two evenings ago. It had never done this. Something went across it, quick and bright, and was gone before he could say what it was. It looked like nothing so much as the face of a child who has been given a present she did not know she had asked for. Then it was gone, and her face was only her face again, thoughtful and grave.

She walked back to the south flag. On the way she rolled her left shoulder once in its socket, and looked down at the stone, and he saw her lips move as if she were counting.

When she reached her mark she turned round, and she was different.

He could not have said how. She stood as she always stood. But the air round her had changed its temper, the way a room changes when somebody in it stops being polite. The courtesy was over. She had measured him and found the baseline, and the baseline had just put a hand on her. Whatever she had been holding back, out of care, she was not going to hold back now.

He counted the damage on the walk back to his flag. He kept his eyes up the whole way. A man who looks at his own arm in a ring is telling the other fighter which arm.

The forearm burned from the wrist up, the skin tight and hot and already starting to shine. It was a burn from his own spark and her fire arriving at the same place. The fingers worked. The wrist worked. His feet rang. The stone at the end of her lane had taken the fire two paces from him, and it had come up through his soles into his shins like a struck bar. It would fade. He let it ring.

Four bursts gone. The stone sold four a day. Everything from here on he would be paying for in the morning.

---

The third exchange was the real one.

She laid two folds at once. He had never seen anybody do it, not even her. Two creases came down from the oak on two different falls, the north-east and the east, as if two people had pinched the same sheet in two places, and the lanes came down them a beat apart, crossing. He heard both. He went between them, sideways and back, and they closed behind him like a pair of doors.

Then there were three.

He stopped trying to get anywhere. There was nowhere to get. He stood in the north-east quarter of the ring with the air of the whole floor held in his head, every heavy place in it, every crease starting, every lane running, and he kept it the way Karis kept a ledger that had to balance at every line. He did nothing else. He had no time to do anything else. Every fold she laid he heard and paid for, a stride here, a burst there, a step back. He did not go toward her once. The west half of the ring went, and then the south-west. His ground came in round him like a tide coming in round a rock, until all he had was a wedge of floor between the north and east masts and the barrier at his back.

Then she laid one that bent.

It came along the curve of the barrier from the south-east, close under the cap, and it did not run straight. It followed the wall round. He had no word for it. Nobody on the bluff had a word for it, because Karis had drawn every lane on her wall as a straight line, and every lane he had ever seen had been one. This one curved like a river round a meadow, and he heard it curve, and only just got off the end of it before it reached him. He came out of it with his heart going and his back against the north mast's cable, and he thought, quite clearly, *She has never shown that to anybody.*

And then he thought, *Where is she?*

He did not know.

He had the air. He had every crease in the ring and every lane in it. He had the trench and the masts and the half inch of fall from the oak, the way he had the floor of his own room in the dark. But she was not in any of it. He had put her down at the bell of the second exchange, and he had not picked her up again, and he could not. When he reached for her, the air went thin in his head, and the creases blurred. When he went back to the air, she was gone.

It was one or the other. At this weight, against this woman, it would not do both. He could have the woman or the air. He could not have them both.

He was still standing in that understanding when the next fold came.

It was short and clean, straight down the east fall from the oak, and it ran out a stride and a half from the barrier near the east mast. It was exactly the shape of the one that had scored. He heard the end of it. He knew where the leave ran out. He had been there first once and it had paid him a touch, and his body knew the way before he told it. He went for the end of the lane on his fifth burst, to be there first.

She was not in it.

She had laid it and sent nothing down it but the fire. She had stayed where she was, wherever that was, and let the lane run empty. The only thing that came out of the far end of it was what always came out of the far end, riding the place where the air had been pinched hardest. It was waiting there for him when he arrived. She had learned him in one exchange. She had learned that he went to the ends of her lanes, and she had stopped being at the ends of them.

It took him along the left side, from the shoulder to the hip.

He felt the give take it. Reydan's give opened under the blow and drank, and drank, and the hum went down through him into the stone. Most of it went. Not all of it. What was left stayed in the left side and lit it up, the whole length of it, a deep dull flare like a coal blown on, and his left arm went heavy from the shoulder down as if somebody had hung a sack on it. He stayed on his feet. He did not know how. He found that he was standing, and that he had not moved his feet, and that the north-east quarter of the ring was very quiet.

The flags went up on the dais. Two. Then two more, slowly. Then they stopped.

The referee turned and looked along the long table and counted. Four of nine. The others sat with their hands on their charts and their flags down, and he saw that two of them were looking not at him but at each other. They had seen a Gold's fire land on a man and the man not move. Nobody on that dais had a rule for it.

"No touch," said the referee.

He came a half step in from the oak, toward Cael, and stopped there. "Your hand," he said.

Cael lifted the left hand and closed it, and opened it, and closed it.

The referee looked at the hand for a long moment, as a man looks at a lock he has been told is sound and would like to see for himself, and then he looked up from it at Cael's face. At the north break the two healers of the north station had come up off their stool together, the way men stand up in church, and were standing with their bags in their hands. The referee looked at them, and back at Cael, and at the hand again.

Then he stepped back to the oak, and the healers sat down.

Above the break, at the rail, Lira was saying something. He could see her mouth. He could see her hand, white on the cap of the barrier, and Brom's bandaged arm across her in front, not holding her, only there. He could not hear a word of it. He could not hear Rooke either, though Rooke's lips were moving too. The air had all of him that was left over from the left side, and it would not give any of it back for voices. He watched her mouth for a moment, and found he did not need to hear it. Whatever she was saying, he could guess the shape of it.

Then Rooke put his hand out in front of the three of them, palm down, at the height of the cap, as he had put it out at rails all year. Lira's mouth closed. She stayed where she was.

It went on.

The arm answered, but it answered late. Cael tested it inside the next breath, because testing it later would have been the same as not knowing. The grip was there. The elbow came when he called it. The shoulder came too, but it came behind its order, a little, like a dog that has heard you and is finishing something first. He put the interval at a fifth of a beat, and knew he would pay it on every movement from now until the end. Very well. The left side was not lost. It was only dearer. He would use it at its new price and pretend to the floor it had no price at all, and he kept the arm up and moving every second after that, so that she should never once see it hang.

The rest of the exchange was arithmetic, done on a damaged instrument.

She braided him down the east side, mast to mast. He paid for every step of it in coin he did not have. The sixth burst went, and the seventh, and the seventh was past six and his hip told him about it at once, a hot line from the crest to the inside of the knee; *the bill comes in the morning*, the minute said, and here it was, arriving early. He put his right palm to the barrier twice more and pushed, and the push threw him along the wall each time, and the right shoulder kept the count. Two. Three. Twice he got near enough to her to touch. She had come in close, the second time, to see what the left side would do. Both times she turned her forearm into his reach and took the contact on it. Both times he let the spark go anyway, into the forearm, small. It was past the two contacts the circle allowed in public. It was the minute's to spend. It bought him a step each time: she drew the arm back from the heat, and the lane she was laying drew back with it by a hand's breadth, and he went through the hand's breadth.

He was still reading, and the creases were still clear in his head, every one of them, coming to him half a beat before their lanes as they had since the second bell, however the rest of him was faring. It was the one thing in the whole ring that had not cost him anything yet. But the body had to carry what the read told it, and the body was slower every time.

The bell found him on his feet at the east barrier, with the east mast humming over his head.

---

The space between the third exchange and the fourth was as long as it took to drink a cup of water, and no longer.

Gault put his hand on the left side through the shirt and held it there. He did not press. He held it as a man holds his palm near a stove to see how hot it is. His face did not change.

"Arm."

Cael closed the left hand round the cup and lifted it and drank.

"It'll go to the bell," said Gault. He took the hand away. He did not say anything about after the bell. It was not his business.

Cael stood in the break with the cup and did what he had always done at the end of a hard day, at a window, with a pen. He had no window and no pen. He did it anyway, in the same voice, as if the volume were open on his knee.

*Wind: seven gone. The stone sells four. Past six since the middle of the third, and the hip already sending the morning's bill tonight. Two left that will land where I send them. After that I'd be guessing.*

*The push: three of four. The right shoulder keeping count. One left under the rule, and then the minute.*

*Reydan's give: whole. But it's full. Every blow it ever took went down into the floor and was gone. This one didn't all go. Some of it is still in there, sitting in my chest like a stone in a shoe.*

*The spark: three contacts. Past the two. The right forearm shining from wrist to halfway. It needs her within reach, and she's stopped coming within reach. It buys a step now. That's all.*

*The read: true. Every fold, half a beat early, all three exchanges. But the left side makes it pay a fifth of a beat on everything it buys, and I can have her or the air and not both.*

*The left side: still mine. At a discount.*

*The floor: true. Three exchanges of Gold on it and the half inch is exactly where the man with the level set his pegs.*

Of everything in the account, that was the one entry in his favour. He held on to it. A floor that does what it says is worth something, when nothing else does.

And under the account, while he did it, something else was going on.

He had not asked it to. It never waited to be asked. It had been going on, without a word from him, since the bell of the first exchange. For three exchanges he had stood closer to a Gold's Storm than anybody but the people it was used on had ever stood. And it had been used on him, in earnest, with everything he had on the floor and everything she had on it too. He had heard it from inside, every crease and every lane. He had stood at the end of it and been burned by it. He had carried it down his own left side. All that time the other thing in him had been watching, and keeping what it saw. It had not stopped once.

He felt a thought begin to form about it. He knew what the thought was going to say, and he took his attention off it before it could say it, the way you take your hand off a door you are not ready to open.

*Not now.*

The steward at the break touched his arm. Across the ring Daeva rolled her left shoulder once and settled at the south flag, and this time she did not look at the air in front of her. She looked at the oak.

The referee lifted his arm. Somewhere high in the east tiers a child asked a question in a clear voice, and was hushed, and the hush went round the bowl and did not stop.

# Chapter 56 — Storm-adjacent

She went to the oak and stood on it, and did not leave it again until the end.

In three exchanges she had not stood still once, not for a breath, not even when he touched her; now she walked up the half inch of fall from the south flag to the round of old dark wood at the top of the floor, and put both feet on it, and settled. She looked like a woman standing in her own kitchen. Then she began to fill the ring.

She did not come down her lanes any more. That was the first thing he understood, and the worst. She laid them from the oak, one after another, down every fall of the floor in turn, as a woman sowing a field throws seed in a wide even arc. North-east, east, south-east, south. She did not walk any of them. She sent the fire down them instead, riding the far ends, and let the air close up again behind, and laid the next before the last had finished. Each new crease began before the last had died, so that the heavy places overlapped, and the lanes crossed, and there was never a moment when the floor was quiet.

He heard every one, and the read, which he had carried onto every floor since Brom first stood across from him, did not fail him once. Every fold came to him half a beat before its lane, as it had all through the second exchange and the third, clean and early and true. And every one of them told him the same thing, which was where he could no longer stand.

He lost the Wind first. It happened at the east mast.

He spent the eighth burst getting out of a lane's mouth there, and it was a good burst, true as any he had ever thrown, and it carried him out of the lane and into the edge of the next one, which she had laid a beat before across the place he was going to. He came out of that one with his hair standing up off his neck and the east mast singing over his head. He spent the ninth to clear it. It cleared him a quarter of the way. Then he reached for a tenth, and the hip simply said no. It was not pain. It was a door that had been open all his life, quietly shut.

The give went next, at the south-east.

A wash came down off the oak at him that he would have taken without a thought at the start of the day. He took it on the left side because the left side was where it came, and the give opened under it and tried to drink, and could not. It was already full. The wash went into him and stayed, and threw him a stride sideways into the barrier, and he hit the boards with his bad shoulder and felt the whole left side light up again from the hip. He came off the barrier and kept his feet. Up in the tiers somebody cried out, one voice, and stopped.

The push went third, at the south barrier, and it went in the strangest way of all.

He put his right palm to the boards and pushed, the fourth push, the last the rule allowed. It worked perfectly. It threw him eight feet along the wall, fast and flat, exactly as hard as he had asked. And the eight feet ended in a fold she had laid there before he moved. She had not known he would push. She had not needed to know. She had laid every piece of floor he could reach, and so there was nowhere the push could put him that she had not already been. He pushed again, the fifth, past the four and into the minute, and that worked too, and that too put him into a place she had already filled. The push had not failed. It had been outlived. It could still carry him anywhere he liked, and there was nowhere left to be carried.

Karis's spark he lost last of all, and losing it cost him least. It needed her within reach, and she was on the oak, twenty feet away and more, and nothing she sent down to him had a body in it to touch.

So she drove him north.

The longest road on the floor ran from the oak to the north barrier, straight down the long axis. Every lane she laid on it ran farthest and fastest. She herded him onto it the way Brom had once herded a Copper into the one gap he had left open, a little at a time, so that each step looked like his own idea. The west side of the floor went. The east went. He came down the long fall step by step toward his own gate, with the creases closing behind him and on both hands, until there was barrier at his back.

He was a few strides west of the north break. The north mast stood at his left shoulder, close enough to touch, with its cable going down past him into the trench. Above him, over the cap, was the north rail. He could see Karis's pen, and Ephram's open mouth, and Lira's white hand on the cap, and Brom's arm. He could not hear any of them.

In front of him the whole floor was hers. Every fall of it was laid with something, from the oak to the barrier's foot, and the air in his head was so full of creases that it was like looking at a sheet of glass somebody had struck with a hammer.

He was out of floor, out of answers, and very nearly out of left side. By any count anybody could make, his own included, he was one lane from the end.

---

And there, with nothing left, he saw the whole of the other thing.

It had been there in the break, and he had taken his attention off it before it could finish. It finished now, without asking him. He knew the shape of it as he knew the cost of his own Wind. Every one of the things he carried had come to him the same way. He had been close to it, for a long time, while it was used on him in earnest by somebody who meant it, with everything on the floor for both of them. Lira in a sparring yard, when neither of them knew what she was doing to him. Brom on a hundred floors. A Bronze at Ardenmere, and Reydan, and Karis's hand on the consent. A man on a stair in the third hour of a bad night.

None of them had been anything like this. Four exchanges at the closest range there was. A Gold's whole Path, laid on him from every side, read from the inside, half a beat early, for as long as he had been able to stand up. He had stood at the ends of it. It had burned him. He carried the shape of it down his own left side.

It was all there. It had never once been so complete.

He did not reach. Not yet. There was a line, and the line was older than this floor, and he went to it first.

A year before, near enough, at a long table at Halcenvane, four of them had sat down and written it in a minute in Karis's hand, and numbered it, and signed it. He knew it by heart. He went down it now, one, two, three, in the time it takes to draw a breath, the way he would have gone down a form at Seln's counter.

*One. The conditions are not to be made.*

He had not made them: he had not filed this bout, or asked for it, or gone looking for it, or put himself one step into her way at any time in the whole three weeks of the sitting. She had filed it herself, alone, at the first bell, over her own house's objection, with one word on the form for a reason.

*Two. A reach only inside a fight the other begins, in earnest, awake, and on purpose.*

She had begun it. Nobody on any floor had ever been more in earnest. And he was awake, wider awake than he had ever been in his life.

*Three. The whole cost on paper first.*

It was on paper. Every reach he had ever made was in the old volume, with what it had cost him afterward: days of quiet while the rest of him moved over to make room, the bill paid in a shut room with nobody watching. He had written every one of them down. He had never written down what it would cost at this weight, in the middle of a bout, in front of a city. Nobody could have. There was no page anywhere that held it.

He looked at that for a moment. Then he did it anyway. He did it with his eyes open, knowing which line it was, and that it had held.

Somewhere at the back of him, in its sealed place, the quiet thing from the stair stirred as it always did when he was cornered, and offered, without a word, to hide him in this corner from every eye in the bowl, and he did not so much as turn his head toward it. It had been sealed two nights ago for a debt, and it stayed sealed.

---

Karis said afterwards that it was the worst moment of her life.

She said it at the long table that night, flatly, with her hands round a cup she did not drink from. She had been watching his shoulders, as she had watched them across a hundred floors with a chart in her lap, and she had seen them go still. Not slack. Still. Everybody else in the bowl saw a boy who had run out of road stop running, and put his hands down, and stand there, waiting for the end. Karis saw a boy who had stopped running because he had gone somewhere inside to attend to something. She had known the difference for a long time and never had a column to put it in.

Rooke had no idea what he was seeing. All the same, his hand went out over the cap, palm down, and stayed there.

Lira did not breathe.

It took half a second. It was half a second he did not have, in front of a woman who could cross the floor in less. He stood in it with his weight settled and his hands down and gave her the whole of it, and she did not take it, because she did not yet know what it was for.

Inside, where nobody could see, he turned everything he had left toward what he had been watching, and held still.

That was all it ever was. It was not taking. It was nothing like closing a hand on something that was somebody else's. It was holding still in front of a thing, all of him, open, as you hold still in front of a bird so that it will stay. He held still in front of four exchanges of storm. And the four exchanges came together. The creases he had heard from the inside, the lanes he had stood at the ends of, the fire he had carried down his own left side, which he understood in that half-second was the part he had paid most for and knew best. All of it came together at once, without any hurry. It went into place like a cut stone dropped into the gap in a wall that has been waiting for it, and then it was simply there.

Her last lane came down the long road from the oak, and this one she walked herself.

She had seen him stand still. She did not know why, and she had decided not to wait to find out. She laid the crease from the oak straight down the long fall to the north barrier, to the place where he stood with the mast at his shoulder, and came down it, with the fire at its head. It was clean and honest and final, and every line out of that corner ran into something she had already laid.

He did not try to get out of it.

He laid one of his own.

It was small, and rough, and he did it badly. He did it the only way he knew, the way he had heard her do it from the inside for four exchanges. He pinched the air at his feet into a crease, crosswise, across her line instead of along it, west along the foot of the barrier. It was fifteen feet long, perhaps. It was a third as deep as hers. It was nothing a Storm instructor would have let a first-year walk. But the air took the crease. It went heavy, and then it went flat, and the lane opened along it, and he stepped into it.

There was nothing in front of his foot.

Not less than usual. Nothing. He had pushed against air every day of his life without knowing he was doing it, as a man does not know he is breathing. For one step it was not there to push against. The air had stood aside, the way Karis had said it did for her, the way a crowd stands back for a steward. He gave the step a stride's worth of effort and it carried him two. He went along the foot of the barrier, out of her road, through the place where the air had been, and came out at the far end of his own lane level with her lane's west flank, a lane's width away from it. Nothing he owned could have put him there from that corner. Nothing on any sheet of any Path on the continent said a fighter with no tier could arrive there at that speed.

At the far end of his lane, where the air had been pinched hardest, something cracked.

It was the smallest fire anybody had seen on that floor all day. It was a short bright snap in the air at his elbow, like a flint struck once, and a smell of struck flint, and a flicker that was gone before most of the bowl had seen it. It touched nobody. It did not need to. It only needed to be what it was.

Behind him her lane ran on into the corner where he had been, and found nobody, and spent itself on the barrier. Its fire went where the risk officer's sheet had said it would. The iron crown on the north mast flared white, once, and the cable sang down its whole length like a struck wire, and a crackle ran along the trench grating at the barrier's foot from the north break toward the east and died there. The mast had done exactly what it was rated to do.

---

Nobody made a sound.

Ephram told him afterwards how it had gone outside, because Ephram had made it his business that night to find out, and had gone round the cookshops and the quay steps with his ruined voice asking, and come back with it in order. The quiet had not come all at once. It had gone out from the ring in rings of its own, like the circles from a stone thrown in a pond, and each ring had broken differently.

The farthest broke first. In the outer court the people who could not get in had been following the bout from a man on a barrel at the mouth of the east tunnel, who could see a slice of the floor and called it out to them, exchange by exchange, as a crier calls a race. He had called the lane. He had got as far as *and the boy —* and then stopped, with the whole court looking up at him, for eleven seconds by a carter's count. When they shouted at him to go on he said he was sorry, he had run out of sentence.

Nearer in, the betting rows broke next, and they broke without a word. The men under the awnings had a price chalked for every half point of the Standard, from the bottom of Iron to the top of Gold. Not one of them had a price for this. One by one they shut their ledgers and turned them over on the boards, face down, and stood with their hands on them. Ephram said it looked like men at a graveside.

On the tower stairs it broke loudest. A woman near the top asked the man in front of her what had happened, and forty people on the steps below answered her at once, in four different accents, with the same three words in four different orders. Every version came out meaning *he hasn't got that.*

Inside, it did not break at all for a long while.

On the dais nobody moved. Nine slates lay under nine hands. Behind them, at their own table, one of the three scribes had stopped and was looking at the end of his pen, as if it had done something without asking him. The other two were writing as fast as their hands would go. High over the east rail, in the Compact's row, the quiet records officer from the bluff had stood up, and did not seem to know that he had.

And she had not moved either.

She stood at the foot of her own lane, by the north mast, where the fire had gone into the iron. In all her public bouts nobody had ever seen her stand still in the middle of an exchange except once on a trial floor, for a second. She looked first at the barrier where he had been. Then along the foot of it, to the scorch his little fire had left at the end of his lane. Then at him.

He had expected surprise. It was not surprise. He had watched surprise go across her face in the second exchange, quick and bright. This came slowly, from further in. It was the look of somebody a long way from home who hears, in a crowded street in a foreign city, a voice speaking the words she learned first.

---

He had a breath to see it. Then the cost came due.

He had always paid it later. After every one of the others there had been a quiet room and a day or two, and in that time everything in him had shifted a little, unhurried, to make room. This one would not wait for a room. It came now, on the stone, with a Gold still in the exchange, and it came all at once. The new thing was heavier than anything he had ever carried, and the moment it was in him everything else in him turned toward it, the way every head in a hall turns when a door bangs.

He went to step toward her. The exchange was not over.

He called for the landing beat, and the wrong thing answered.

It was like calling a dog by its name in a yard full of dogs. He had called the Wind as he had called it every day for years, without thinking, and something else lifted its head instead. It was the new thing, raw and enormous and not yet sure where it lived, answering to a name that was not its own. The Wind did not come at all. The foot came down on the stone with nothing behind it. Nothing was wrong with the stone. The man with the level had set it true, and it was true still. It was the leg that had been given the wrong instruction, by the wrong part of him.

It did not hurt. That was the frightening part. It was a question of ownership. For years every one of the things he carried had come when he called it, with no gap at all between the calling and the coming, as a hand comes. Now he did not know which of them was his to call. He stood for an instant in the middle of himself like a man in his own house who has put his hand out for the door in the dark and found a wall.

He stumbled.

The bowl saw it. They had seen him go through the air and come out the other side of it, and now, half a breath later, they saw him fail to take an ordinary step on an ordinary floor. He heard them see it. It was a low sound, like a crowd on a quay watching a man go into the water.

It frightened him, and he let it, because there was no time to do anything else. And then the thing Brom had been putting into him all year, a little at a time, every time he went down on the boards, came up of its own accord. *Chin in. Arms in. Loose. Nothing left lying out.* He went down the way he had been taught to go down. He had not known he had learned it.

She was a stride away, and he was falling at her feet with the left side open and nothing left in his legs.

He saw the choice reach her. He saw it as plainly as he had ever seen a weight move in a fighter's hips. A touch on him now would level the flags. A fire into him now would finish the bout in a way the woodcutters on the quays could have sold for the rest of their lives: the Gold over the fallen boy, with the storm round her head. It was lying on the floor for her. It would have cost her nothing at all.

She did not lay a lane. She did not lift a hand.

The healer in Auremont's warm-up room found the mark on the third look.

It had been hers. She had known it the moment it opened, before her mind had caught up, the way you know your own name called across a street before you have turned round. Nobody else on the continent laid a lane like that. She had watched the coast Storms, and the old border Golds, and the one man from the eastern islands who came every cycle and lost in the second round. Their lanes were theirs, each one, the way a voice is somebody's. This one had been hers. It had been small and rough and laid crosswise out of a corner, where she would never have laid it, by somebody who had plainly never laid one before. But it had her grain in it. It had the little hitch at its far end where she always pinched the air hardest. It was like hearing a bar of a tune she had made up as a child, whistled back to her by a stranger in the street. It was in the wrong key, and too short, but hers.

She did not try to work out how he had done it.

She noticed that she did not try, and she was glad, as she had been glad two days ago on the floor of the boarded room. People had spent her whole life working out how she did things. They had built rooms and programs and floors for it, and written it all down, and every word they wrote had been true, and none of them had ever once got near her. She was not going to do that to him. She would not have known where to start anyway. She only knew that it was hers, and that it had come out of him, and that she had never in her life been so happy about anything she did not understand.

Then she thought about the end.

He had been going down at her feet with his side open and his legs gone, and she had seen the finish lying there, and she had not taken it. She had not decided not to, exactly. She had done what her hands knew. She had let the air under him go slack, so that it would not hold him up and would not let him fall either, but take him down slow, like a hand lowering a cup to a table.

It was the thing the big quiet man had done to her on the coast when she was fifteen. She had spent a month of mornings learning never to let anybody do it to her again, and she had kept the exercise sheet ever since because it was the only honest record of a thing that had been done to her. She had always thought of it as the thing that had beaten her once.

It had not occurred to her before this afternoon that a man might do it to catch somebody.

That kept her on the bench until the lamp needed trimming. She thought perhaps he had not meant it kindly, the big quiet man, all those years ago. Perhaps he had. It did not much matter now. What mattered was that there were two ways to use it, and she had only ever been shown one.

That night, in the marbled notebook in the lining of her case, she wrote one line under all the others. She wrote it slowly, in her best hand, and read it twice, and put the book away.

*Today somebody asked me something.*

*Six guesses from a city that has argued about fighters for three hundred years. Every one of them reaches for a shape the world already owns: a secret register, a hidden Path, a cheat, a slip. Not one comes near. What happened doesn't have a shape in anybody's head yet, and you can't guess at a thing you can't picture. Nobody guesses digestion. I'd thought what kept it hidden was us, the four of us and this book. It isn't. It's that nobody can imagine it.*

# Chapter 58 — The Weather

The file reached Ilsev's desk a little before noon, in the observation office's own case, with the west tower's seal on its cord.

She had known it would come. The entry's last word was *Referred*, and the charter gave only one address for a thing shown on a floor outside classification, which was the Compact's observation seat at the tournament. That was her. So she had come up to the office early and cleared the desk and laid out a fresh sheet of the office's paper, and when the case came she cut the cord and took the papers out in order and laid them in a row: the match record; the nine charts, in copy; the scribes' three accounts; the healers' report from the north station; and on top of all of them, by itself, the entry.

She read the entry first, once, as she read everything. She read it a second time for the words alone, as she had been taught to do, long ago, by a woman who read a form the way a jeweller looks at a stone. *Under*, not *by*. She noted that. Somebody had been careful, at the end of a long night.

Then she began on the routing, which was her work and had been her work since before most of the clerks in the building were born: a minute on the front sheet to say what the file was and whom it went to next, and why, and under what article. She knew the article without looking. She wrote it in her small upright hand and blotted it, and reached into the case for the dispatch slip that would send it on up the line.

There was a second packet under the dispatch slip.

It had come in the same case, by the same courier, from a different office altogether. Her own name was on it in a clerk's hand. She knew the shape of it before she opened it, because she had sent it out herself, months ago, in the cold term, properly, through every channel there was. It was her referral on the designation in the enrollee's file. In all her years she had never before filed twice on one matter. She had filed the first on the strength of what she found at Halcenvane the year before. That one had gone up the stair and not come down. So she had written it again, fuller, with every citation she could find, and sent it up again, because a question nobody answered was a question to be asked again through the proper door.

It had come down.

She slit the packet and took out the return. It was one line, in a registry clerk's round hand, with a stamp she did not know.

*Referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized.*

She sat and looked at it.

Every reader of records learns early that the registry can say no in a hundred ways. *Not of record. Not found. No file.* The registry was full of honest holes, places where something had once been written down and later lost, or never written down at all. She had filed hundreds of those holes in her time without losing a night's sleep over any of them. A hole was only a hole. You marked it, and went round it.

This was not a hole. A hole said, *there is nothing here.* This said, *there is something here, and you may not look at it.* It did not say who had decided that. There was no name under the stamp. There was no office she could write to and ask. It was a drawer somebody had nailed shut and then taken the hammer away.

And it had come to her desk in the same case as a file from the west tower asking the Compact to look closely at the same boy.

She laid the two sheets side by side on the desk. The entry, which asked her to inquire. The return, which forbade it. She looked from one to the other for some time.

Then she did a thing she had not done once in her working life.

She opened the bottom drawer of the desk, where she kept her own things, and took out a sheet of paper that was not the office's. It was a sheet she had bought for herself at a stationer's on the coopers' row on her second day in the city, for letters home, and never used. She laid it on top of everything else, over the office's paper, and she wrote on it. She did not write in her office hand. She wrote in the hand she had used as a girl at her mother's table, before anybody had taught her to make her letters stand up straight.

What she wrote, nobody ever read, because it was not for any file and not addressed to anybody, and it was the first thing she had written in all her years of service that was not meant to be read by somebody else. She wrote it, and read it through once, and folded it twice, and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where she had never in her life carried anything that belonged to the Compact.

Then she finished the routing minute on the front sheet, correctly, under the right article, and sent the file on up the line as the charter required.

Havel brought the second round of the dispatch case in at the half after noon, and saw her do it.

He did not see what she wrote. He did not try to. A junior did not look at a senior's private sheet, and he had never wanted to. But he saw the paper. It was not office stock; he knew office stock as he knew his own boots. And he saw her fold it twice and put it inside her coat, against her chest, and then pick up her office pen and go back to the routing as if nothing had happened.

He knew what that was. He had four sheets like it of his own.

He had carried them for a long while now. Through three postings they had lived in a locked drawer, and since the coach north they had lived in his kit between the second and third of his three manuals, where nobody would ever look, because nobody ever looked in manuals. He had written the first of them years ago, at another posting, on the night he first read the designation on the enrollee's sheet and found that the line for its authorizing office was blank. Each sheet since had been the same question asked again, a little more carefully, by a man who did not know whom he was asking.

That night, in the narrow room the Compact had taken for him at an inn behind the east tower, he took the four sheets out from between the manuals and laid them on the bed. He took a fifth from the bottom of his kit. He wrote the date at its head. Under it he copied out the line from the return, word for word, exactly as the registry clerk had written it. He had seen it upside down on Ilsev's desk for perhaps three seconds, and he found that he had it entire.

*Referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized.*

Under that he added a line of his own. It was short, and he let it stand.

He put the five sheets back between the second and third manuals and buckled the kit. Then he sat on the edge of the bed in the dark for a while, listening to the chandler below him closing his shutters. He thought of the woman two doors along the passage, in the senior's room, with a folded sheet inside her coat. She did not know about his. He would never tell her. He thought that two people now, in one city, were keeping papers that their office had not asked for. Neither of them knew any more than they had in the morning about who had nailed the drawer shut.

Seln wrote his report at the document table in the guesting-house's front room, with the five buff folders lying in their row along the table in front of him.

It was the first report of the sitting he had not had to build.

He noticed that before he had written a word, and it was strange enough that he put the pen down and sat for a moment with his hands on the blotter. Every report he had sent north in a year had been built: true in every line, complete in every box the form asked for, and arranged with great care so that nothing in it led anywhere. He had built them as other men build walls, a stone at a time, checking each. This one did not need building. There was nothing to keep out of it. Eight thousand people had watched the thing. Three scribes had written it down at a speed no clerk could match. The west tower had put it in eleven words and hung them in a frame. Whatever he wrote, the world already held more of it than his form had room for.

He took up the pen again.

*Exhibition, finals day, against the filed Gold challenger of Auremont. Subject fought four exchanges. Capabilities exercised: as in the public record attached, entire. Result and entry: as attached. Observation in this instance is complete, the event having been public.*

He read it through. Every word was true, and every word was all of the truth that was his to write. That had never happened to him before in this work. He found that his hand wanted to go on and write the last line of the form, the line he had written at the foot of every report for a year, the one word that closed it.

He held the pen over the space and did not write the word.

He looked at the space for some time. Then he blotted the sheet as it was, and folded it, and sealed it, and laid it on the corner of the table for Bracken's pouch.

Something in him was lighter, as a man is lighter who has set down a case he has carried so long he had forgotten it was in his hand, and he did not look at it closely. He had a long habit of not looking closely at things in himself, and he kept to it now. He sat with his hands folded on the empty blotter, as he sat at his counter in the wing at the end of a day, and let the lightness be there without asking what it was.

Then, because habit is habit, he took the small book from his inside pocket and opened it and wrote in it too, a few lines, in a hand nobody at the wing had ever seen him use. He put it away.

The locked case stood under the table by his chair, where it had stood every evening of the sitting. He had not opened it. He did not open it now.

Withrow brought the note herself.

She came up the stair to the back room a little after noon, where the five of them were eating bread and cold meat because nobody had felt like going down to the long table. She knocked, which she did not need to do, and came in, and held out a folded sheet to Cael.

"It came to my office by a tournament page," she said. "It's addressed to you, care of me, which is correct. I've read the outside of it, which is my right as your chancellor. I have not read the inside, which is not." She put it in his hand. "I'll be downstairs."

She went. They heard her go all the way down.

Cael unfolded it. It was a half sheet in a spare upright hand he did not know, though he could have guessed whose it was. There was no seal and no heading.

*The Archmarshal Vastin asks the enrollee for one hour, before the houses leave the city. Nothing will be written by either party. It would be better if nothing were brought to write with. The timekeepers' office in the east tower has lent a room for the purpose; it belongs to neither the adjudication office nor the Compact. He is free to say no; it will count against him nowhere, with no one, at any time; and that sentence is not a formality.*

He read it aloud, because they would have asked him to. Then he laid it on the rocking table, and they all looked at it.

Karis spoke first. She always did when a paper was on the table.

"Nothing written," she said. "Think about what that means for us. This whole house runs on one rule, and it's kept you alive since the bluff. If it's on paper, we can survive it. Every word of the hearing was written down, and that's why we could fight it. The provision's on paper. The roster was on paper and that's the only reason you were on a floor at all." She touched the note with one finger. "He's asking you to walk out of the one place we've ever been safe. Whatever's said up there, afterwards there's his memory of it and yours, and his comes with a grey coat and a seal."

"And friendly isn't a safe word from them," said Lira. "I've been asked into a friendly room by an institution. At Fenmark, the morning they told me I was finished there. Everybody was very kind." She folded her arms. "It was the kindest room I've ever been in, and I walked out of it with nothing."

Brom said one thing.

"You'd be on your own."

Ephram had been sitting on the end of the bed with his bread. He was not of the circle and knew it, and had been asked to stay, and had stayed, and said nothing all through lunch. He said it now, in what was left of his voice. "Look at what you'd get and what it costs you. What it costs is easy. You'd be alone in a room with a senior man of the Compact and no witness. What you'd get is whatever he chooses to say, which you can't check. If it's true it'll still be true in front of a witness. If he won't say it in front of one, you have to ask why."

Cael heard them all out, every one, as the house's custom was. Then he said no to all of them.

"I asked for a witness once," he said, "with a warden across a table from me. I was right to. That man was a piece of the machine, and you don't sit down with a piece of the machine without somebody to say afterward what was said." He looked at the note. "This one's different, and I've had a year to learn how. Eleven questions in a room on the bluff. Three sentences of advisory. A chair held for him that he never asked for, two notes, and a walk out before the figure." He turned the note over. "Every move he's ever made toward me he's made in ink, and signed with his whole name. This is the first thing he's ever asked for that he can't sign. A man like that doesn't give up his ink to set a trap. He'd use the ink." He folded it. "I want to know what's worth that much to him."

Nobody argued further. They did not need to; they had been heard. Karis nodded once, as she did when a figure came out right. Lira let her arms go.

Seln was at the door. Nobody had heard him come up. He had the pouch under his arm and he had evidently been there for some of it, and he did not apologise for that, or come in.

"He has kept every rule of a forty-year career," he said, in his counter voice. "If he means to break one for you, the breaking has been paid for somewhere you will never be shown the bill." He shifted the pouch. "Listen accordingly."

Then he went down the stair to Bracken.

The timekeepers' tower stood at the east end of the Concourse, across the outer court from the west tower where the adjudication office lived. Cael had passed under it every day of the sitting and never been inside.

Brom walked with him across the court, although nobody had asked him to and he had lost the argument about the room an hour before and had not said another word about it since. They went through the people still waiting in the copy line, who turned to look, and under the arch at the tower's foot, and there Brom sat down on the step.

He did it carefully, with the bad shoulder away from the stone, and put his forearms on his knees.

Cael looked at him.

"You said no to the room," said Brom. "So I'm not in the room. Nobody said anything about the step." He looked out across the court at the copy line. "It's a good step. I can see the clock from here."

There was nothing to say to that, and Brom would not have wanted anything said. Cael went in.

The stair went up round the inside of the tower, and the room was at the top of it. A timekeeper's boy was waiting at the door. He had the key already turned. He opened the door and stood aside and went off down the stair without a word, so that nobody would have to let anybody in.

The room was full of clocks.

They stood on shelves round all four walls, and on the long table, and two of them hung over the door. Some were brass and some were wood, and a few were in pieces, with their works laid out on cloths. Each had a card tied to it with a date and a hand. And every one of them was ticking, and no two of them said quite the same time. The noise in the room was like rain on a roof. It was the timekeepers' office for the whole Concourse, where every clock the sitting used was brought to be set against the tower's great one and sent out true, and it belonged to nobody in the sitting at all.


Hesk had written to him in the cold term that in a big city every clock disagrees with every other, and every one of them is certain. Cael had never been in a room that proved a letter so thoroughly.

Vastin was sitting at the long table with his back to the window. He got up when Cael came in.

He was in the plain grey, with nothing at the collar. He had no case with him and no papers, not a sheet, not a pencil. Cael had seen this man in a records hall, in a held chair, across a table of questions, and every time there had been a document somewhere within reach of his hand. There was nothing within reach of it now. The hands hung at his sides. The left one was not quite straight at the fingers.

His eyes went over Cael once. They did it as they had on the bluff, without any hurry and without seeming to do it at all. They rested a moment where the strapping showed under the shirt, and a moment on the wrapped forearm, and on the care with which Cael lowered himself into the chair across the table. Vastin said nothing about any of it. Cael saw him take each thing and put it away, and understood that this was a kind of good manners. In his trade you did not comment on what you found. You kept it.

"Thank you for coming," said Vastin, and sat.

Then he frowned slightly, as a man frowns at a figure he has written down wrong.

"No. Thanks are the wrong instrument. You owe me nothing, and I didn't ask you here for anything of yours. I asked you here for something of mine." He laid his hands on the table, the right flat, the left as flat as it would go. "I would rather we began straight."

"Then begin," said Cael.

Vastin looked round the room before he did, at the shelves of clocks.

"The keeper of this room," he said, "can tell you how wrong each of these is. To the half minute. He doesn't throw them out for it. He knows each one's error, and he corrects for it, and so between them they tell him the time very well." He brought his eyes back. "That has been my work for forty years. Not finding the instrument that is right. Learning exactly how each one is wrong."

He did not say what that had to do with anything. He let Cael carry it.

"I closed my evaluation of you last night," he said. "On a stool by a door, at about the fourth bell. I'll not tell you what it found. You were in the ring when it was found."

A clock on the wall behind him struck the half hour. Another took it up a breath later, and a third, much later, as if it had been thinking about it.

"I'm going to give you eight words," said Vastin. "Four, and then four. I have watched them come across my table this year in letters about your file, the first four in the early part of it, the second four since. I want you to hear them in the order they came." He said the first four slowly, each one by itself.

"*Anomaly. Irregularity. Basis. Review.*"

He waited, and then said the others.

"*Posture. Handling. Exposure.*" And then, after a longer space, and more quietly: "*containment.* I have seen that one twice. Both times in minutes that reached me as a kindness, with nobody expecting me to reply."

Cael put the eight in a row in his head.

"The first four are words you use about a fault," said Vastin. "A thing gone wrong in the record, which a careful office would like to find and mend. You find a fault so that you can take it out." He moved his left hand an inch on the wood. "The second four are words you use about a thing you mean to keep in a box. You will not find one of them in the Compact's charter. I've looked. You can't write *handling* about a question. You can only write it about an answer you've already got."

"Who sends you the minutes?"

"I can't name him. I'm not certain there is a him. The better question took me a long while to find. Who decides which men are sent minutes, and which men are left off the list?" He said it flatly, as he would have read a line off a form. "I asked that once, in writing, through the door it ought to go through. The reply came back within the week. It was very civil and there was nothing in it. I have not asked again." He looked at his left hand. "I've noticed, lately, that I have stopped meaning to. I don't care for that. I tell you so that you'll know it about me."

He was quiet for a while then.

The clocks went round. Down in the court somebody was calling out numbers along the copy line, and the numbers came up faintly through the window, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three. Cael waited. He did not know what was coming, but he could see that it was coming, in the way the man sat, and that it had been carried a long way.

Then Vastin lifted his eyes to Cael's and kept them there.

"You should stop competing publicly."

Cael let the clocks have a moment of it. Then he asked why.

"Because the people who want this to stop are not going to use official channels much longer."

---

It went into the room and stayed there.

Neither of them hurried it. The numbers came up from the court, forty, forty-one, and stopped. A clock on the long table ran down with a small sigh and was still, and the others went on without it. Cael sat and read the man in front of him, because that was what he had. The read said there was more, and that it would be given at its own rate. Each sentence was being paid for out of something, and the man was paying.

"Who are they?"

"If I gave you names I would be guessing, and I don't guess." He did not say it to be clever. He said it as Gault said a plate would not read past its last notch. "I can tell you what my year has looked like from my chair. A year ago I was asked what you are. This year I am asked what can be done about you. I answer every one of those letters by saying, again, what you are. Each answer goes up the stair. Each one is entered and filed. I am fairly sure none of them is read." He turned his left hand over, and back. "My office still works. It works very well. It has simply stopped mattering. When the questions stop being questions, an evaluator knows the decision has been taken in some room he was not shown."

"There's talk of a sitting."

"There will be one, after the houses have gone home. I'm called to it, and I'll go." The ghost of something went across his face. "I can't tell you what's on its paper. You won't need me to. You were there yesterday when it was written."

Cael asked the next thing slowly. He knew which question it was.

"The designation in my file. The old one, with no office against it. Is that part of what's coming?"

Vastin did not answer.

He did not look away either. He sat with his hands on the table and his eyes on Cael, and the clocks counted for him, and Cael counted with them. One. Two. Three. Four. In the whole hour it was the only silence that pressed on anything. Cael felt it the way he felt the air before a squall, heavy, all along one side.

"I won't answer that one," said Vastin.

That was all. But it was a great deal. He had not said *I don't know*, which would have been true and easy. He had looked for whoever set that line in the file, as Ilsev had, and found nobody; and it troubled him. It troubled him more than anything else in the hour, more than the eight words, more than the warning. He had just said a thing in this room that could end forty years if anybody heard it, and he would not say this. Cael understood that it was not the record he was wary of. It was the blank in it. A man who has spent his life learning exactly how each instrument is wrong does not like an error that belongs to no instrument at all.

"If I stopped," said Cael. "If I went home and never fought where anybody could see me again. Would it change anything?"

"No." Vastin did not soften it. "That is the other half of why I asked for you. What you do now makes no difference to it. A year ago it would have. A thing you do is an answer, and answers are for questions, and they have stopped asking. Nothing answers a conclusion."

---

The clocks went round. Cael let them, for a while. Then he set down the question he had brought up the stair with him, because he did not think the hour would have a better place for it.

"How did you evaluate me?"

Vastin did not ask what he meant. He looked, for a moment, very slightly pleased.

"With the wrong instruments, for three days," he said. "I came up to the bluff with a file and a plate and a set of questions, and all three told me nothing. A man in my trade has one great temptation, and I gave in to it. I thought the nothing was something being hidden from me." He moved the left hand. "It is the oldest mistake there is. When an instrument won't read, you'd sooner believe it's broken than believe you've pointed it at a thing it was never made to measure."

"And then?"

"Then you answered my fourth question. In full, truthfully, every word of it. And I learned nothing whatever from the answer." Something that was nearly amusement moved in his face. "I have done that to other men for forty years. Nobody had ever done it to me. From that question on I stopped measuring what you could do and started watching how you went about it." He looked at Cael steadily. "The method is not a boy's. That is my whole opinion of you. It's in no file. There is no line on any form where it would go."

He glanced down at his own left hand on the table, and then up again.

"You're counting that," he said. "The hand."

"Yes."

"Good. Count it right. It's worse in the evenings now as well as the mornings. Put that down where you put things."

He did not offer protection. He said, in so many words, that he had none to give, because it would have been dishonest to let the boy wonder. Whatever reach his office had, it had through the very channels he had just told Cael would be stepped round. And a warning from a man who could also shelter you, he said, would have been a smaller thing than this, because you could never be sure which of the two he was giving you.

"Do you think I'll stop?" said Cael.

For a moment the grey face did something Cael would spend half that night failing to name in Hesk's book.

"No. I evaluated you." He let it rest a moment. "But you'll know when the channel changes, now. Knowing is worth something. It's the only thing I had to give."

He stood up, and said a word of thanks to the room. He said it to the shelves and the table and the clocks, the way a man pays a small account on his way out of an inn. Then he went to the door, and out, and Cael heard his steps going down round the inside of the tower at the same even pace all the way to the bottom. He was going back down into a machine he knew to the last screw and could no longer account for.

Cael stayed where he was.

He gave it four minutes by the clock over the door. It may have been five by the one beside it. If he got up at once, the hour would have decided when he left, and he meant to decide that himself.

Then he went round the room with his eyes before he went, as he did every room.

The table bare. Not a paper, not a cup, nothing on the wood that had not been there before. The other chair, empty. Forty clocks, near enough, every one of them sure. And on the sill by the window, apart from the rest and ticking a little behind them, a small brass travelling clock not unlike Hesk's. It had a card tied to its handle in a timekeeper's hand. *Slow. Owner informed.*

Nothing to file. No copy, no witness, no line in any book on the continent. The whole hour was in two heads now and nowhere else. One of those heads belonged to a man who had spent forty years making very sure nothing of the kind would ever happen to him.

He went down the stair.

Brom was sitting on the step under the arch with his forearms on his knees, looking at the copy line. He got up when Cael came out into the light, slowly, the bad shoulder first, and stood.

"An hour," said Cael.

Brom tipped his head back and looked up at the face of the tower, at the great clock every other clock in the Concourse was set by.

"Fifty-one minutes."

They went back across the court together. The copy line had moved perhaps twenty yards. Brom did not ask anything, all the way across, and Cael did not offer anything. At the guesting-house door Brom stopped with his hand on the latch and looked at him once, the way he looked at a floor before he stood on it.

"Good," he said, and opened the door. That was the last either of them ever said about the step.

# Chapter 59 — Same Build

Vastin went down the tower stair at his ordinary pace and out under the arch.

There was a big young man sitting on the step there with bandaged forearms, watching the court. He looked up as Vastin passed. Vastin knew him from the row: the continental Copper champion, the bluff's wall. He nodded to him, as one nods to a man keeping a door. The young man nodded back, gravely, and went on watching the court.

He crossed it slowly. The copy line still ran along the colonnade, and he walked down the length of it and nobody in it knew him. A woman with a basket of eels. Three boys from some hill house arguing about the fourth exchange with their hands. A grey delegation head in Auremont's blue, standing in his place with his hands folded behind him, who did know him, and inclined his head an inch, and was answered by an inch. At the end of the colonnade, by the north tunnel, there was a frame on a nail with a sheet in it. He stopped in front of it and read the eleven words again, although he had heard them read aloud at dawn and could have said them in his sleep.

He had written a note like that himself, once, on the road home from the bluff. Fewer words, and in a worse light, and signed with his whole name. He had thought at the time that it was the most careful thing he had ever written. He saw now that the west tower had done it better. They had not tried to say what the boy was. They had said only what their instrument could and could not do, and stopped.

He walked on toward the inn under the outer wall.

The summons was in the inside pocket of his coat, where it had been since the post came up the road two days ago. A sitting at the seat, when the houses had dispersed. He was to attend and give evidence as the officer of record. It did not say on what. It did not need to. He would take the post road in the morning. He had already told the inn.

He knew the machine he was going back to. He knew it better than any man alive, he sometimes thought, and without any pride in the thought. He knew which door every letter went through and which clerk's desk it lay on and for how long, and which seal sent it upstairs and which sent it down. He could have drawn the whole house from memory on the back of a form, stair by stair, with the names on every door. And for the first time in forty years he could not have told anybody which way it was going to move. He had drawn it all in his head and there was a room in the drawing he had never seen, and something in that room was deciding things.

He had told the boy that he had stopped meaning to ask who chose which men saw the minutes. That was true. He had not told him why. It was not fear. He had examined it closely on the road down, the way he examined everything, and it was not fear. It was nearer to tact. A guest does not open a door in somebody else's house, however long he has lived there.

That troubled him more than the eight words.

His left hand ached. It was the evening. He put it in his coat pocket, against the summons, and walked on.

He did not yet know what he would say at the sitting. He would know when he was standing in it. That was the only way he had ever known anything worth saying. He knew one thing about it already, and it was enough to be going on with. Whatever he said, he would sign every word of it with his whole name.

Cael went down to the rail over the floor at nightfall, alone.

The stewards at the north tunnel let him through without a word, as they had let him through every evening since somewhere in the second week, when they had decided that the boy who walked the empty floors at night was part of the building. He went up into the dark of the lower tiers and along to the rail above the north break, where the five of them had stood the day before. He put both arms on it and looked down.

The ring was still there. It would come up tonight; the crews were already gathering at the tunnel's mouth with their lamps and barrows and long bars, and somebody was laughing. But for the moment it stood as it had stood all day, in the last of the light from the high windows. There was the barrier with its smooth cap, and the four masts with their iron crowns, and the trench grating at the foot of the barrier all round. In the middle was the oak.

He found the mark without looking for it.

It lay along the foot of the barrier, west of the north break, a few strides long. Somebody's broom had been over it and had not been able to lift it. It was a pale line on the grey stone, like the mark a pan leaves on a table, and at its far end there was a scorch the size of a hand where his little fire had cracked. It was the only thing on the floor to show what the city had argued about all day in seven ways and been wrong in all of them. The crews would have it up with the stone before morning. Nobody would think to keep it.

He stood and looked at it until the light went off it.

Under the strapping the left side kept up a low steady complaint, like a dog that has decided to go on grumbling all night. The rest of him was not settled at all. All day he had felt the things he carried moving about in him, slowly, the way men move furniture in a house where a great heavy chest has just been carried in. Every one of them leaned a little toward the new weight, and none of them had found its place. Twice on the walk down he had reached for the Wind and got something else. He thought it would be a long while before he could take a step without thinking about it. He had written that in the old volume already, and the healers' account of it, and his own, which was more honest.

He took out Hesk's book and opened it on the rail. There was just enough light. He wrote standing up.

*They didn't score me low. They didn't score me high. They couldn't score me. I have spent three years telling myself I can beat anyone at my tier. Tonight the fairest instrument ever built agreed with the Arbiter, the brackets, and the registry: I am not at any tier.*

He looked at it for a while. The crews had come out onto the floor below him with the lamps, and one of them knelt at the foot of the north mast and began to loosen its cable.

*I keep waiting for that sentence to feel like a wound. It feels like the truth arriving.*

The long table had all of them that night. Gault, and Rooke, and Bracken at the end with the pouch, and Withrow at the head with her cup. The two reserves were wide-eyed at the bottom of it. Seln sat at the far corner, on the margin, where he had sat all sitting. Cael told them the hour.

He gave it to them in two piles, as Karis had taught him to give anything that mattered. First what had been said, word for word, as near as he could get it. Then, separately, and saying so, what he had made of it. The telling ran longer than the hour. Nobody interrupted. Karis wrote it all down without looking up once.

When he had finished, Lira was first, because somebody had to argue against and she could do it better than anybody.

"Or," she said, "it's the cleverest thing they've done all year." She put her elbows on the table. "They can't order you off a floor. There's no rule for it. Karis would tear it to pieces in a week. So they send up the one honest man they've got, and he sits you down in a room full of clocks and asks you very kindly to walk off the floor yourself. That's what they want. They can't make you. He can ask." She looked round the table. "Honest men get sent to do that sort of thing. That's what honest men are for, to people like that."

"As a report it's thin," said Ephram. He had almost no voice left, and he used what there was carefully. "One source. No way to check him. And his own house gains if you do what he says." He shrugged. "If I'd brought that to Rooke before a trial, he'd have sent me back out for another."

"So don't grade it as a report," said Brom.

They looked at him. Brom argued perhaps twice in a sitting, and they had all learned to listen when he did.

"Grade the man." He turned his cup round in his bandaged hands. "He came all that way himself, by the post road, with one bag. He sat in that row the whole afternoon. "Then grade it both ways," she said, "and keep both. As a report it's poor. As a man he's good. One doesn't cancel the other. "Wardens threaten. Politicians hint. That was neither." The pouch's strap went through its buckle under his fingers. "That was an honest man spending career capital to tell you the truth a few months early. Treat the warning as real, because it is."

Nobody argued with that. At that table, nobody arguing was not the same as agreeing because they had to. It meant there was nothing left to say.

Lira let out a breath.

"All right. Strike mine," she said. "I might still be right. It doesn't matter." She took the pot and began to pour, Brom first, as always, and then round to the left. "Say he's honest and on his own. What do we do? We build. Say he's honest and somebody's sent him on an errand. What do we do then?" She filled Brom's cup. "Same build."

Lira filled his cup last.

"Fifty-one minutes," she said, so that only he heard. "That's what mine took. At the station, with the Arbiter." She put the pot down. "Make of that what you like."

Withrow went last. She stood at the door for a moment looking back at the table, as if she were counting something, and then she said goodnight to all of them in one word and was gone.

Then there were four, in the back room, with the door shut and the window open on the courtyard pump.

They had one thing left to do, and Karis had brought the paper for it.

"The minute from the bluff held," she said. "I want that said first, before anything else, because it's true. Nothing was made. She began it. You were awake and you knew what you were doing." She laid the sheet on the rocking table. "And the third line held too, as far as it went. The cost was on paper. Every cost we had ever seen was on paper." She looked at him. "The trouble is we had never seen one at that weight. We had it written down at the weight of a stair, and a yard, and a copper pot. "It could have gone worse," said Lira quietly. "If she'd been anybody else."

"She wasn't," said Brom.

"No," said Karis. "A rule that only holds when the fighter across from you is kind isn't a rule. It's luck." She took up the pen. "So we put a line in. Not a new rule. The old one, with one more word in it."

She wrote it while they watched, slowly, in the hand she kept for papers that strangers would read long afterwards, and she wrote it twice, a draft and then a fair copy, with nothing crossed out on the second. Then she turned the fair copy round and pushed it across the table to him, and he copied it into Hesk's book under the minute from two nights before, in his best hand.

*The conditions may complete themselves anywhere; the deciding may not. The line holds: directed, open-eyed — and from tonight, costed. Never again in the middle of a fight unless what follows has been priced first, at the weight it will come at. He blotted it and held the book out.

Karis initialled it first, small, at the right of the last line. Brom put his under hers, large and careful. Lira signed hers with a flourish she had certainly never used on any official paper. Cael put his last. Four sets of initials on a page of a book bound by an instrument-maker, under a rule that no registry would ever see.

"There," said Karis. "Now it's the house's."

They went up one at a time, Karis with her papers and Brom with his cup, and Lira last, and Lira stopped on the stair, and he thought for a moment that she would say something, and she did not. She only knocked twice on the doorframe as she passed, as she had knocked on his head on the night of the party, and went on up.

He sat on alone with the window open, and wrote the night's last entry, slowly, while the city put itself to bed below.

*First honest warning I've ever gotten from inside the machine. He built no case, made no ask, took no note. He just told me the weather. The Compact's best evaluator thinks the next thing that comes for me won't wear a uniform or file a challenge. Write it down. Believe it. The crews had worked all night, and the stone of the main floor lay grey and plain from barrier to barrier with the oak in the middle of it, as if nothing had ever stood there. Gault had taken the flat box out of his inside pocket, the one with the old blue ribbon in it, and was holding it against his chest with both hands without opening it. Rooke did not look at the standard at all; he was watching the bowl, the way he watched a crowd at a rail, as if to see who had come. Seln stood at the very end, a step behind Cael, looking at nothing, with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting at a counter. And Withrow did not look up either. She looked along the line of them from the near end to the far, one face at a time, and when she came to Cael she stopped, and stayed a moment, and went back the way she had come.

"Eighteen years they didn't come. Somebody write to Fenmark and tell them what the scrapyard placed."

Karis, beside her, did not write it down. She reached across without looking and took hold of Lira's sleeve, and kept hold of it.

He lifted it again, quite gently, and it did not stop. He looked at the presiding official, and the official, after a moment, looked down at his lectern and turned a page that did not need turning, and waited.

Then the banner.

It came down from the champion's gate for the last time in the cycle. It was carried on its staff by the tournament's own colour-guard, two of them, one at either side, as it had been carried in alone on the first day with no house behind it. Everyone in the bowl stood up. They brought it to the oak at the middle of the floor and stood there with it, level, neither raised nor dipped.

The transfer was the oldest procedure in the charter. Cael watched every step of it, because it was an instrument being handed from one keeper to the next, and he had never seen it done.

The presiding official came down from his lectern. Four ties of grey cord held the banner's sleeve to the staff. The charter named the order they were to be loosed in. Bracken had told them at supper that a herald had once been fined for doing it the other way round. He undid the two middle ties first, the upper and then the lower. Then the tie at the head. Then the tie at the foot, last, so that the cloth came away from the staff all at once into the colour-guards' hands and did not touch the floor. The two of them laid it in four, corner to corner, without a crease out of true. It went into a case of plain pine that was very much newer than what was put in it.

Then two registrars sat down at a small table that had been carried out onto the stone, and signed the transfer, one after the other, in ink. Eight thousand people stood on their feet in silence and watched two clerks write their names.

Cael thought he had never seen anything more like the bluff in his life.

At Auremont's place on the floor the pine case was unfastened a second time. Auremont's own people sleeved the banner to Auremont's staff, with the same four ties in the opposite order, and raised it. Then the house's procession took it round the floor once, close under the barrier, slowly, so that every person in the bowl could see it go by at the height of their eyes. Silver on grey. The mark's own flag.

Daeva walked at the head of her house's file, two strides behind the banner, where the marshals had put her.

She had walked behind it on the first day, from the tunnel to the gate, and she had felt nothing in particular about it then. It was a flag. Her house had won it in other cycles before she was born and would win it in others after. Her name was on the reason it had come back this cycle, people said, and she supposed that was true. She did not feel it as hers.

She watched the plain colours come up on her left as the procession turned at the north end of the floor.

They were standing in the tunnel mouth under their honour staff, all of them in a line. The big one with the ribbon. The Ember girl with her notebook under her arm. The Wind girl, with her arm held close at her side and her chin up. And at the end of the line the boy, with his left shoulder held a little stiff under his coat, watching the banner go by as she had seen him watch everything since the procession, as though he were reading it.

She had not planned anything. She noticed, afterwards, that she had not planned anything at all. That was the second time in her life.

She stepped out of the file.

It was four steps to the barrier where they stood. The colour-guard's sergeant saw her go and opened his mouth. The banner went on without her. The near tiers saw and made a sound like a wave turning over. She crossed the four steps and stopped in front of the boy, with the barrier's cap between them, and the shadow of the banner sliding away along the stone behind her.

"Rematch," she said.

It was not a challenge. A challenge was a form you filed. It was more like what two craftsmen say to each other at the end of a fair, when one of them names a town and a season and the other nods: an appointment, said once, to be kept. He looked at her and nodded.

She was back in the file before the sergeant had decided whether anything had happened. Four seconds. One of the scribes timed it, from habit. She walked on behind the banner all the way round to the champion's gate, and saw it go up, and did not once look back.

The procession marshal's office wrote her up for it that afternoon. The citation came to her lead instructor in the evening, folded and sealed, with the office's ribbon on it, for the program's file. He brought it up to her room himself and held it out, looking at it rather than at her, with his head on one side.

"You'll want to read it," he said. "For the form."

"Thank you."

She took it. When he had gone she turned it over once in her hands. Then she opened the lining of her travelling case and put it in, still sealed, beside the old exercise sheet from the coast and the marbled notebook, and closed the lining over it. She never did open it. She found, years afterward, that she had forgotten it was there.

# Chapter 60 — Seven Things

He closed the year that evening in Hesk's book, at the table under the window, with Hesk's steel pen.

It was not the end of a year by anybody's calendar but his own. But it was near enough a year since he had sat at another window, on the last night of the old term, and written down what was in the till. A thing that has been opened ought to be closed, and the ledger had been open all that time. He turned past the inventory at seventeen, and the trial, and the ring, and the minute, and the rule, and the warning, to the next clean leaf. He ruled the margin with the edge of the closing ceremony's printed card, because he had no rule with him. Then he sat a moment before he wrote, with the pen lifted. He did that perhaps once a year and had never written it down.

*The year, closed.*

*Seven now, and the one I can't account for.*

*Lira's burst. Six free on the sprung oak; four a day on this city's stone. On finals day I spent nine, two of them past six, and the hip sent both bills the next morning with interest. The landing beat: four years of trying to trim it, and it is no shorter. Since yesterday afternoon it doesn't always come when I call it. Sometimes something else comes.*

*The push I took from the Bronze at Ardenmere. Five blows in one sitting, on a scored floor, in front of eight thousand, the first load it has carried in public since the circuit. The right shoulder is still counting the fifth.*

*Brom's read. It never failed. Not once, in four exchanges. This year it learned to read the air as well as a body, and at Gold it will do one or the other and not both. I learned that in the ring, which is a bad place to learn things.*

*Reydan's give. Nobody official had ever seen it. On finals day nine judges, three scribes and a full row of the Compact watched it drink a Gold's wash and then most of a Gold's fire. That's over. It's in the book for good.*

*Karis's spark, by her leave. Three contacts past the two. One of them was the touch. The right forearm will shine for a week.*

*The quiet thing from the stair. Sealed through the most watched afternoon of my life, and in its last corner, when it offered. Never used, anywhere, by anybody's count. Rent from waking, spent or not. The second year of "nowhere yet" closes tonight, and it is still nowhere. The debt it was sealed for is still owed and still unspoken.*

*Daeva's storm. Taken in the middle of a fight she began, awake, by the old minute's three lines, with the cost on paper as far as paper went. Fifteen feet of it, a third deep. The first notice that ever said Gold. The first that ever warned me what it gave might not hold, and that part stands. Everything else I carry is still shifting to make room for it, slower than after any of the others. Gold is heavy. Hesk taught me to write down what a thing costs the instrument as well as what it does. So: written.*

*And at the foot of the list, the still place from session nine. Looked at again tonight. Nothing to add.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He stopped there, and then went on, because there was one more account owed, to a man who would hear about the match from everybody on the continent but him.

*Reydan will read the accounts. It still isn't the answer. Not yet.*

He dusted it with sand, Bracken's way, and gave it a full minute by Hesk's clock before he turned over.

Somewhere to the south and west, at about that time, in a room no window had ever been cut into, a question was put.

What is known of that sitting comes from a few lines of its minute and from one participant's account, given late and to one person. It is not much. The officials who sat in it were senior and, by the custom of such rooms, without names; that was the point of them. A clerk read the question into the record. He had been chosen to read it because his voice had nothing in it.

*Whether the classification [SHATTERED] shall be designated an active threat category.*

The tournament's record was laid before the room. Beside it lay the west tower's entry from Norhold, the same eleven words, which had come the whole way down the post road for this. Then the officer of record was called. He had come in person. He gave his evidence standing, and when it had been written down he signed every line of it with his whole name.

"You are proposing to classify a boy as a threat category because your instruments cannot classify him as anything else. I have evaluated the instruments. The finding is theirs, not his."

They thanked him. His evidence went into the minute. Nobody replied to it. The chair moved to the next item.

Further down the table somebody asked that the file's standing designation be read in. Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4. By itself, the speaker said, it gave all the warrant the room needed to take the matter out of ordinary hands. The minute then asked, for completeness, which office had authorized that designation.

Nobody in the room could say.

It was carried in a sub-layer of the registry itself. No instrument authorizing it was of record. A junior of the secretariat said that a trace had been attempted, and closed. The room made the small pause that rooms of that height make, and a line went into the minute, and the sitting went on. The designation was useful, and at that height useful things are not asked where they came from. It had sat in the file since the first minutes of his Kindling, and it sat there still: a flag no living hand had authorized.

Nobody called for a vote. The question was *entered for formal proceedings*. In such rooms the words mean the answer has been settled and only the day has not. The officials went back to offices whose names are not written down. The clerk gathered the minute. Somebody put out the lamps.

Then the room was empty, and the file lay where the clerk had squared it, thicker by one entry with no number in it. Nothing had been decided. Everything had been entered.

---

The road took the tournament off them a little at a time, the way a long walk takes a fever out of a man.

For the first days it was still all round them. Waystations had silver-on-grey hung at their gates, and the innkeepers came to the doors to look at the plain colours go by. Twice a rider overtook the wagons with a fresh broadside from the city, and Ephram bought both and read them out on the tailboard in what was left of his voice. The guesses had bred. There were eleven of them now. The one word was in none.

Cael read the road by its doors. Every waystation had a board beside the door, and at the first few the board still had the finals card chalked on it, every result, as if the innkeeper could not bear to rub it off. A few days on, the boards were selling hay and oats by the bushel. A few days after that a board had nothing on it but nail-holes, and the woman who kept the place asked Gault, quite kindly, what had brought two wagons so far north this early. Gault told her. She thought about it, and said she had heard there had been something on, at the coast, and wasn't that nice for them, and went to see about the soup.

The old volume ran out a leaf at a time as the road did. He gave it a few lines a night, small, and most nights they said the same things. *The Wind at three on yard clay. Came when called, twice of three. The left side gone yellow at the edges. The flag stands.* Each night the things he carried had shifted a little further toward the new weight, and each night they had not finished. The healers had said a month. His own reckoning said more. The notice's warning sat at the back of his eyes where he had first read it, and did not move.

On the ninth day they reached the district seat.

The column stopped in the square outside the registry while the reserves watered the horses, and Brom got down and went in by himself. He was inside a long while. When he came out he had a folded paper in his hand. He did not look at any of them. He sat down on the wagon's tailboard beside Karis and turned it over in his fingers, the plain side up, and then he said it to the cobbles in front of his boots, all at once, before anybody could ask.

"Mandatory advancement review. Any continental title brings one. A panel, in that building, before ninety days are out. They'll look me over for Iron."

Karis's pen came up. She did not write anything. She waited.

"It isn't a promotion," said Brom. "It's a hearing about whether I get offered one." Then he slid the paper inside his coat, into the pocket where the letter from Velmere was, and pressed the coat flat over both. "I know Copper. Every seam of it. I can walk it in the dark. Iron I've only ever watched from the side."

"Ninety days," said Lira, from the wagon bed. "You could map a lot of floor in ninety days."

"I'd have ninety days to decide whether I want one drawn."

Nobody pushed him. Nobody on that wagon had ever needed to push Brom toward anything; he got there, or he didn't, by his own road. Karis capped her pen without having written a word, which Cael had never once seen her do. The paper rode the rest of the way home in his coat beside the letter, and he did not take it out again, and that was all the year had to say on the matter.

On the eleventh day, at a waystation where the coast road came in from the south, the coach from the coast pulled into the yard with its horses steaming while they were eating, and stood there blowing while the coachman got down. The coachman put his head in at the door and asked if there was anybody here from the bluff, and held up a letter. Cael knew the hand from across the room.

It was dated four days before. Hesk had not had his six pages. He could not have; they were still somewhere on the hill road, in Bracken's pouch. He had written off the broadsides, which reached the coast faster than any pouch. Then he had worked out which road the wagons would take, and which crossroads they would come to on which day, and sent the letter to meet them there. He had been right about all of it. Hesk's answers had always set out before the questions reached him.

Cael read it at the board with his stew going cold.

*Third on the continent, and the judges wrote "no number goes here." Your grandmother would have had that framed. Seventeen, unmeasured, and true. Come home when the road allows. — H.*

He read it again. Then he slid it in behind the old volume's front board, with the other letters that lived there, and ate the stew cold, and did not mind.

On the twelfth day the watchers came back.

There were two of them, at the far end of a bridge in a market town, standing by a hired horse where the road came off the bridge into the square. Grey coats and good boots. Cael knew both faces. He had counted them for two years from the bluff's walls and windows and the ferry steps. He counted them now from the wagon's bench, and was surprised to find that he was very nearly glad of them. After a city of eight thousand who had all been looking at him at once, two men and a horse were almost homely.

He wrote it that night next to the warning, because that was where it belonged.

*Two of them, at a bridge. The old arrangement, two and a horse. After Norhold it looks small, and I find I'm fond of it. But Vastin spent an hour telling me the old way is finishing. So: noted, and kept beside his hour. They may be the last watchers I get whose names I could learn.*

On the fourteenth day Karis closed the year's papers.

She did it at a waystation table after supper, and the whole ceremony of it was that she would not have one. She took out everything the year had made, from the cases it had ridden in: the clause and the four pages, the qualifying letters, the circular, the rulings, the trial rules, the nine charts on the backs of old seating plans, the entry. She sorted it all into two piles on the board. She tied each with tape and wrote a contents leaf for each and put the leaf on top, and the bundles went back into the case.

"Two hundred and six sheets," she said, and shut the lid. "Nine of them matter. I've no idea yet which nine. I won't have for a good while. So they all go home."

Ephram spent the last three days working out how to tell it on the bluff.

He tried his versions on the wagon, on the reserves and the horses and anybody who would sit within reach. There was a long one with the trial in it. There was a short one with only the match. There was one that began with the roster and was still in the qualifying year when they reached the next waystation. He threw them all away. Somewhere past the second-to-last ferry he said one more out loud, and stopped, and said it again, and kept it.

"We went down there and they counted us, and the count's on a staff in the middle of the continent where anyone can read it. And one of us they couldn't count." He considered it. "Anyone who wants it longer can carry my kit up the bluff while I tell it."

---

The last ferry took them over on the seventeenth day.

On the ferry he wrote in the old volume for the last time, at the foot of its last leaf, until the line ran into the binding and there was no room for a word more. He shut it and put it inside his coat with Hesk's letter. Everything he wrote from now on would go into Hesk's book, and he thought it would be a long while before Hesk's book was full.

Ostrand came up out of the valley the way it always came: brown and slate above the wide slow river, with the bluff behind it and its one long roofline catching the last sun. The wagons went up the climb with nobody saying much, because it was the last mile of a long year, and every one of them had found that it was enough just to be riding it, together, with the light going off the water behind.

Halfway up, Cael spoke, to nobody in particular. He had not planned to. It came the way a line comes sometimes at the end of a page, when the page has been working toward it all along without your knowing.

"I have seven things that aren't a Path," he said. He let the wagon roll on a little way. "A match record no instrument can hold, and five people who watched them fail to score me."

He let that sit.

"And somewhere above all of it, a room full of careful people deciding what I am. Again."

"They're behind schedule," said Lira. "You already decided."

"Years ago," said Brom.

Karis, writing: "Noted."

Seln was on the tailboard with the pouch across his knees. He looked up the road toward the gate and said nothing. Cael had learned that this was how he agreed.

*The standard was silver. He wasn't anything the silver could measure. The road home ran through a continent that now knew it — and somewhere behind them, in a room with no windows, the machine began deciding what to do about what it couldn't measure.*

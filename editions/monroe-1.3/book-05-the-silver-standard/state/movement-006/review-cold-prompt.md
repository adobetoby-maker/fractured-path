# Chapter 33 — Eleven Minutes

Cael timed Lira's quarterfinal on Hesk's travelling clock, because it was the only clock in the delegation that nobody else wanted.

It was an hour fast. He had set it that way at the crossroads, as he always did in a tournament week, and it lost a little more of the hour every day besides, so that by now it was wrong about the time in two directions at once. But it was honest about minutes. A minute on Hesk's clock lasted exactly as long as a minute anywhere else, and that was all he needed from it this morning.

He held it open in his palm at the rail of the second floor and watched the long hand.

Lira's second round had gone by on the morning of the fourth day, before the city walk, and nobody had needed to say much about it afterward. This was the quarterfinal, the first bout on the morning's card, and the hall under the high glass was already half full when the two fighters came out to their chalk.

Her opponent was a Force fighter from one of the inland houses, a broad, unhurried man with a heavy jaw and thick wrists. His regional file called him a counterpuncher. Karis had put it more plainly at breakfast, over the bread.

"He does nothing first. He stands in the middle and waits for you to come to him, and when you come, he pushes, and you go backward a long way. Thirteen bouts this year, and he's never once been the one to move first in any of them."

"Then she'll make him," Brom had said, and gone on buttering.

The bell went. The inland fighter walked to the middle of the pale floor and stood there with his feet planted and his hands low.

Lira walked once round him, the whole square, at an easy pace, with her hands loose and her eyes on his feet. She did not go in. She let him wait for her, and he waited with the calm of a man who had been waited at all his fighting life and had always won the waiting.

Then she went in, fast and straight, and he pushed.

It was a big push and an honest one, laid from his planted feet into the air in front of him, and Cael felt it from the rail as a shove in the chest of the whole hall. Lira was not in it when it arrived. She had burst out of her own entry a stride short and to the left, so that the push went past her into empty floor, and she touched him on the shoulder while he was still finishing it.

One to nothing. Then he touched her twice, both times on the hip, both times when she came in close on purpose to learn what he did there. The bell went at one to two.

She walked back to her chalk and drank the water Rooke held out to her over the rail.

"Learning his reach," said Rooke, to nobody in particular.

"Buying it," said Karis.

In the second exchange Lira did not go in at all. She walked her square, and every time she passed one of his roads out of the middle she paid a little for it, a step here and a feint there and once a half-burst that went nowhere, so that each road was hers before he could need it. Cael watched the inland fighter begin to feel it. He had planted in the middle in order to wait. Now the middle was shrinking round him, and the floor he had meant to push her across had all been sold to somebody else while he stood on it.

He moved first. He had no choice left, and it was the first time he had done it in fourteen bouts.

She was already standing where he moved to. Three to nothing, closed early.

The third went much the same way, except that he took one back, a fine low push that caught her as she landed and sent her skidding a yard on the hard pale boards. In the fourth he had nothing left but the middle of the floor, and she owned all the rest of it. Three to nothing again, closed early, and the clerk at the end of the hall called the totals. Ten to three. The inland fighter could not have drawn level even with every point of the last exchange, and so the bout was over.

Cael shut the clock.

"Eleven minutes," he said.

Ephram leaned over to look at it. "That clock's an hour wrong."

"It's wrong about the hour. It's right about the minutes."

Lira came off the floor unwrapping her hands and was barely out of breath. The inland fighter had shaken her hand with a sort of puzzled courtesy, and now he stood on his chalk looking at the middle of the floor, like somebody who had set his cup down on a table and come back to find the table gone.

At the far rail an older man in Auremont's blue was writing in a small book. He had not written a word during the bout. He had watched every exchange with his hands folded in front of him, and only afterward, steadily, from memory, had he begun to write. Cael had charted him at the quarry town, the scout who wrote after and never during, and he knew whose sheet the writing was for.

Lira saw him too. She took her coat from Gault and put it on, and did up the buttons one at a time, and looked past the scout at the board on the end wall, where the next line of her pool stood in white chalk over the inland fighter's name.

"Right," she said. "Now the real one."

Seln brought the notice up the guesting-house stair at noon, squared at the corners, and laid it on the long table in front of Rooke.

It was a single sheet of the west tower's grey paper, with the adjudication office's mark at the head. Rooke read it, and frowned, and read it again, and then pushed it across the table to Cael without a word.

*The demonstration-exhibition category,* it said, *requires the main floor and the senior exhibition panel. Both are required this week by the bracket semifinals and the Copper final. The demonstration ring will therefore be raised when the brackets have cleared the main floor, and the bouts filed in the category will be heard in the order of their filing as soon thereafter as the floor allows. Houses concerned are notified accordingly.*

Under it, in a clerk's small slanting hand, somebody had written the names of the two filing houses and one word after each: *deferred*.

"Four days," said Brom, who had read it upside down from the other side of the table. "You said four days, Rooke. On the third night. I heard you."

"I did say it. The tower said it too." Rooke took his pipe out of his pocket, looked at it, and put it back unlit. "Now the tower says otherwise."

"The tower has changed its opinion," said Seln.

Nobody laughed, exactly. But Brom put his head down on his folded arms for a moment, and Lira looked hard at the ceiling, and Ephram made a small noise into his cup. Seln's face did not change at all. He took the notice back, made a note in the travel file, and fitted the sheet under its clip with one finger, and gave no sign at all of having spoken.

Cael found that he was not disappointed.

He sat there with his hands flat on the table and looked at the feeling, as he looked at anything that arrived without being asked for, and found that it was relief. What surprised him was its size.

All year he had been the thing in the middle of every floor. He had been charted and priced, protested and filed, sold in woodcut on street corners and argued over in taverns, and every hall he had walked into since the wool town had turned its head to watch him come in. He had grown so used to the weight of it that he had stopped noticing he was carrying anything at all. Now a clerk in the west tower had set it down for him, for a week, for reasons that had nothing to do with him, and he felt his shoulders come down a whole pack's weight.

"I can watch."

They all looked at him.

"Every bout you've got this week, I can be at the rail for. All of them. Nobody's going to want anything from me until the ring goes up." He heard how glad he sounded and did not try to hide it. "I can chart Zerin properly. I can chart whoever comes through Karis's pool. I can chart Marek's next two bouts and the man after that. I've got nothing else to do."

Rooke looked at him for a long moment over the table.

"You've been the best scouting I've had all year," he said at last. "I haven't said so, because you had bouts of your own to think about, and I don't ask a man to carry two sacks up one hill." He nodded at the travel file, where the notice had gone. "Seems you've only the one this week. Carry it."

Lira leaned across the table on her elbows.

"Then carry it to me first," she said. "Tonight. The back room. Bring everything you've got on her."

*The west tower has put the ring back,* he wrote that afternoon, sitting on the end of his bed with the observation notebook on his knee. *Nobody will look at me for a week. I didn't know how heavy the looking had got until somebody took it off for a few days, and now I keep wanting to stretch, like a man who's been carrying a sack so long he's forgotten it was there.*

*For one week I'm an instrument and not a specimen. I was an instrument long before I was anything else. I'd like to find out whether I'm still any good at it.*

---

The back room had been a store for the guesting-house's linen once, and it still smelled faintly of lavender and starch. It had one small window over the courtyard, a table that rocked unless somebody folded paper under its short leg, two chairs, and a door that shut properly, which was the reason Lira had taken it.

She had claimed it on their first night in the city by a delegation custom that Cael suspected she had invented on the stair. Nobody had argued. Now she shut the door, folded a scrap of paper under the table leg, and sat down across from him with Zerin's extract in front of her and the lamp between them.

This was the oldest thing they did together, older than the oak at Halcenvane and older than the circle. Neither of them had ever given it a name. She would put down on the table everything her body had learned about a fighter, from inside a bout or from the floor beside one, and he would put down what his eyes had kept, and they would argue across the table until the two halves fitted, like a pair of hands finding each other in the dark.

It had never yet taken them more than one evening.

"Three bouts," he said, opening the notebook at the page he had built that afternoon. "Her first round, her second, and her quarterfinal, which she fought this afternoon on the far floor while you were eating. Eleven exchanges, and she hasn't lost one of them. She's given up four points in all, and two of those were in the same exchange, to a man who more or less threw himself at her."

"I've got her times." Lira put her hand flat on the extract. "I've had them since the quarry town, and I know them better than I know my own. I know how fast she is. What I want is how."

"Then ask me where she starts."

"Where does she start?"

"That's the trouble." He turned the notebook round so that she could read it. The page was covered in his small charting hand, every exchange broken down into its beats, and under every burst was a line where he had written what came just before it. Nearly all of those lines were empty. "I can't find where."

Lira read the page. Then she read it again, more slowly, with her finger going down the empty lines one by one.

Every Wind fighter Cael had ever read had a moment before a burst. It was a small moment, but it was always there: a breath drawn and held, the hips setting, the weight sinking into the driving foot, the whole body drawing itself together for what it was about to do. Lira's was the quickest he knew, and on the oak he could still call it a fraction early, every single time. It was how the read had first learned her.

He had looked for Zerin's through three bouts at the full gaze, and it was not there.

"She doesn't gather," he said. "I've watched every burst she's made since the opening, and she never once gets ready to make one. There's no gather because there's no burst, Lira. Not the way you mean a burst. She doesn't stop and then go. Going is just how she walks about. The speed doesn't switch on anywhere. It's on already."

Lira sat back in her chair. She looked at the ceiling for a while, and the table rocked under her elbow, and she moved her elbow somewhere else.

"So there's nothing to catch."

"The read catches a body getting ready to do something. She's never getting ready. She's always already doing it."

He had known it since the afternoon, and it still sat badly with him. The read asked a body what it was about to do: where the weight was, and which way it was going, a fraction before it went. It had worked on every fighter he had ever stood near, because every fighter he had ever stood near had needed to get ready, and getting ready was a thing the skin could feel at a pace and a half. It had never once occurred to him that somebody might simply not need to.

"It's like listening for a clock that doesn't tick."

"Nothing to take hold of." Lira said it slowly, without any bitterness, rather as a carpenter might run his thumb along a joint he knew he could not have cut. "No loose board anywhere."

"Not yet."

"You always say *not yet*."

"I always mean it."

She very nearly smiled. Then she got up, because Lira could only think about a problem for so long sitting down, and stood in the narrow space between the table and the wall and tried it. She tried to go without getting ready, to burst from standing the way Zerin walked, with no breath and no setting of the hips. Her body would not let her. Every time, a fraction before she went, it gathered, as it had been gathering since she was small, and on the fourth try she went anyway, half gathered, and caught her hip on the corner of the table and sat down on the floor.

The lamp rocked. Cael caught it.

"Well," said Lira from the floor, rubbing her hip. "That's why she started at twelve."

She got up again and sat down in her chair. Then she pulled the extract back toward her, turned it over to the blank side, and took the pencil from behind her ear.

"Again," she said. "Her quarterfinal, the second exchange, from the bell. Tell it to me as though I'm standing on the floor with her and can't see a thing."

He told it to her. He took it a beat at a time, as he had taught himself to do over the years for exactly this, so that the fight came out of his mouth in the order her body would have met it. She sat with her eyes shut and her hands moving a little on the table, and every so often she said *stop* and asked him where the left foot was, and he told her, and she went on.

They stopped when the lamp began to smoke.

---

Ephram's round of sixteen was the third floor's last bout on the afternoon of the sixth day, and the lake house's Shield did exactly what Rooke had said she would.

Rooke had talked about her for half an hour on the third evening, at the end of the long table, with his cup going cold beside him. She was a woman of about thirty, a careful, quiet fighter who had come up through her house's ranks a season at a time and had never been in a hurry about any of it. She did not fight like a showman, as her house's Silver duelist did. She fought like a mason. She put up every pane in the same order, and she kept them fresh on a count as steady as a mill wheel.

"You'll find her clock," Rooke had told Ephram. "Anybody can find her clock. That's not the question. The question's what you do with it once you've found it, because she's known it was there for fifteen years and she's still winning."

Cael had found her clock in the warm-up hall, before Ephram had his coat off.

He had watched her put up her panes against a sparring partner and counted under his breath. Breath, plant, pane. Every pane went up on a breath and a planted foot, a sheet of coverage as clear as cold water, and from the moment it was made it began to thin. She renewed them oldest first. Five beats, five panes, round and round. It was the plainest clock he had seen since the salt road.

He had a thought he had first had on the oak in the cold term, and he let himself have it again, quietly, at the back of the warm-up hall. *A touch of fire on the pane as it formed, and the whole wheel would slip a beat.*

He had no fire to spend this week, and Ephram had never had any. Ephram was a Blade.

The bell went, and Ephram went to work.

He did not try to cut through her, because a Blade's edge on a fresh pane did nothing at all; the pane took it and the flags stayed down. Instead he walked round her and watched, as he had learned to do on the slate, and let her show him the wheel. It cost him. She touched him once from behind a fresh pane while he was still counting, and again when he tried a quick entry to the left to see what she did there, and a third time on the shoulder from a pane she had renewed a breath before he expected it. He touched her once, a fine low cut that found her between two panes at the very end.

One to three, closed early. Ephram walked back to his chalk with his lips moving.

"He's counting," said Lira.

"He's paying for the count," said Karis.

In the second he had it. He went in on the fourth beat of her wheel, every time, at the pane that was oldest and thinnest, and twice his edge went through it as a knife goes through a sheet of wet paper. The gallery woke up. The lake Shield gave nothing away on her face, but she touched him twice in return, both times as he came out through the old pane, and the bell went at two each.

Three to five on the bout.

"He's got it," said Brom.

"She's got him having it," said Rooke.

In the third, she shortened her count.

Cael saw it on the first beat. The breath came sooner and the foot went down sooner, and the panes went up on four beats instead of five, so that none of them was ever old enough to be thin. Ephram went in on the fourth beat and found a fresh pane where an old one should have been. His edge skidded off it, and she touched him on the wrist as he recovered.

She paid for it too, and Cael could see what she paid. A shorter count was a dearer count. Her feet had less time for anything except planting, and her counters came a little later and a little lighter than before, because every spare beat now went into keeping the wheel turning faster. There was slack in her that had not been there in the first exchange, and there was more of it with every pane.

But Ephram had nothing that could reach into slack. He had a Blade's edge and a good heel and a late half-turn of the front foot, and none of those could strike a pane before it was made. He found the slack once, with a long low entry that slipped under her guard. She took the other three.

One to three. Four to eight.

In the fourth he fought like a man who knew to the last copper how much he still owed. He tried nothing new. He went to the beat where her shorter wheel ran thinnest and took two touches there, cleanly, one on the hip and one on the ribs, and the gallery stood up for the second of them. She took three, steadily, without hurrying, the last from a pane that had been up for less than a breath.

Two to three. Six to eleven. Ephram could not have drawn level with every point of the last exchange, and the clerk called it over in four.

He shook her hand. She said two or three words to him that nobody at the rail could hear, and he nodded. Then, before he let go, he said something back, longer, and Cael saw the lake Shield's careful face change a little, as faces do when somebody tells them a thing they had not known about their own work. Ephram told them later what it was. He had told her that her fourth beat ran thin on the left side after she shortened the count, and that a Blade with a slower heel than his would never find it, and that she should know. "She'll fix it," he said. "She'd have found it herself by the next cycle. I thought she might as well have it now." Then he walked off the floor and up the steps to where the five of them stood, and before any of them could open their mouths, he spoke.

"That was the price of admission," he said. "I said so at the draw. The round of sixteen was what I'd pay to get in." He started to unwrap his hands. "Paid in full. I'd like a receipt."

Lira laughed, and so did Brom. Rooke did not laugh, but he put his hand on Ephram's shoulder once, briefly, which from Rooke was a good deal more.

"She shortened the count," said Ephram. He was still breathing hard, and his face was red, but his voice was perfectly steady. "I found her wheel in one exchange, and she changed it in the next. Fifteen years of doing it one way, and she changed it in a breath because a boy from the bluff had found it." He looked down at his hands, half unwrapped. "I couldn't hit a pane before it was there. Nobody could. Not with an edge."

Cael said nothing. He was thinking of a copper pot on the twentieth of Reaping, and four instants of fire, and a pane cracking as it came up.

*Ephram lost to a clock today,* he wrote that night. *He found it in one exchange and she changed it in the next, and he had nothing that could change back. Then he walked up to the rail and priced his own loss out loud before anybody could price it for him, or price it kinder than it deserved. I've never heard a loss paid so plainly by the man who owed it.*

*He'll be at the rail for the rest of the week now. He told me once he likes calling better than winning, some days. Tonight I think he meant it, and I think he also minded, and I don't think he'd thank me for writing down the second part.*

The second evening in the back room went worse than the first, and ended better.

Lira had spent the day turning Zerin over in her head, between the warm-up hall and Ephram's bout and supper, and by the time she shut the door behind them she had a list. She had written it in ink on the back of her left hand, the way she did when she did not trust a pocket to keep a paper.

"She doesn't gather," she said. "Fine. Then I don't read her start. I read something else. Her ends. Where does she stop?"

"She doesn't stop much either."

"Her turns, then. Nobody turns at full speed."

He showed her the page. Zerin turned at full speed. She went round a turn the way a stream goes round a stone, without slowing at all, and came out of it already going wherever she had meant to go before she reached it.

"Her landings."

"Clean. Every one."

"Her left side. Everybody's got a bad side."

"She's got a less good side. It's still better than most people's good one."

Lira looked at the back of her hand for a while. Then she licked her thumb and rubbed the whole list out, line by line, until her knuckles were blue with it.

They sat for some time with the lamp hissing between them. Below the window somebody was drawing water at the pump in the courtyard, and the handle squeaked on every stroke.

Then Cael turned the notebook back to the first page, to her first-round bout, and began to go through it again from the bell. He was not looking for anything this time. He had spent two days hunting gaps in her and had come up with an empty net, so now he simply read, as he might have looked out over a field from the top of a wall, letting the whole shape of the thing come up to meet him.

He saw it in the third bout. He went back and found it in the first, and then in the second, where it had been all along.

"She's never behind."

Lira looked up.

"Look." He laid the three pages side by side under the lamp. "Any exchange you like. She's ahead from the first beat. She's always the one who gets there first, and everything she does afterward is built on top of that. All her angles take it for granted that the start belongs to her. When she defends, she isn't answering anybody. She's only getting to the place before you do." He put his finger on the margin. "Nobody at Norhold has put her second. Not for one beat in eleven exchanges. So there isn't a line on any of these pages about what she does when she is."

"Maybe she does it beautifully."

"Maybe she does. Nobody knows, and that's the point. Nobody's been there, so nobody's written it down. Not the scouts, not her coaches. Not even her."

Lira had gone quite still.

"Corner her."

"Corner somebody who walks faster than you can run." He did not say it to stop her, and she did not hear it that way. She heard the problem in it, plainly stated, and her answer to a true problem had always been the same, which was to reach for a pencil.

She wrote one word on the back of her hand, where the list had been. *Behind.*

"What does her file say about before? About where she came from?"

"Nothing I've seen. It's all times."

"Then I want the rest of it." She stood up, and the table rocked. "Rooke's got the long one, the whole commercial file. I'll ask him for it in the morning." She looked down at him, and he saw that she was not discouraged in the least. She was the other thing, the thing she turned into when a problem finally showed her its face. "You found a country nobody's mapped. I'll go and live in it. I've lived there before."

She went out and left the door open. Cael sat on in the little room for a while with the three pages in front of him and the pump squeaking below.

It came to him slowly, sitting there, that what he had found was not really about Zerin at all. A file was made out of what a fighter had been asked to do. Every page of Zerin's was full, because she had been asked for everything a program could think of and had given all of it, and the one blank country on her map was only the one place nobody had ever thought to send her.

He wondered what would be blank on his, if anybody ever got hold of the whole of it.

He wrote that down, and then crossed it out, and then wrote it down again underneath, smaller, where only he would ever look for it.

# Chapter 34 — The Other Road

Karis was still at the long table when Cael came down from the back room, and she had not lit a second lamp.

Everybody else had gone up.

The guesting-house had settled round her for the night, with the hill house's boots going to and fro on the boards overhead and somebody on the southern house's floor below singing the same three lines of a song over and over, never getting to the fourth.

Brom sat at the far end of the room in the window seat with Marek's notations open on his knees, reading them for what must have been the hundredth time, and he lifted one hand to Cael without looking up from the page.

Karis had laid out her work as she always did. Her notebook lay open at a fresh leaf headed *QUARTERFINAL* and ruled into her four columns. Beside it lay her own chart of the two bouts Ivenne had fought at Norhold, taken from the gallery. Beside that was a thin packet of four pages in a russet paper cover, bought that afternoon from a shop in the delegation quarter. Every house's file on every other house could be had somewhere in this city for the price of a dinner, and Ternhall's was no different.

The four columns were empty.

From the way the pen lay, square to the edge of the page and dry at the nib, Cael guessed she had been sitting in front of them for an hour.

He took the chair opposite. "Shall I run it? The way we always do."

"Run it."

So he began at the beginning, with the first plain question they always began with, and asked her for Ivenne's tells.

Karis did not answer at once. She looked at him across the lamp, and then she stood up and stepped back from the table into the clear floor between the benches, and set her feet.

"Watch my weight," she said.

He watched.

She brought her right hand up as though to make a contact, and in the half-beat before it went, her weight slid back onto her rear foot and settled there, quite small, like a cup set down on a saucer.

"That's hers," said Karis. "She does it before every ignition. I trained half of them into her." She dropped the hand. "That one we drilled together in our second year, on the long floor at Ternhall, until neither of us could stop doing it. I could tell you which week it set in."

She showed him two more without being asked. A glance at the floor before a planted foot, to a place she did not need to look at; a breath let out through the mouth on the last touch of a run. Both of them were Ivenne's. Both of them, Karis said, had been hers first, or perhaps the other way round, and after so long neither of them could have sworn which.

Ternhall taught Ember in a shape it called a lattice, and Cael had learned the shape from Karis over two years of watching her work. An Ember fighter's fire came at a single point, only on contact, through the hand. The lattice was what Ternhall built round that one point: three anchors, three planted footholds in a triangle on the floor, laid down in a set order, so that any contact the hand made from inside them had the floor under it. It was a frame and a drain at once, because the heat of an ignition ran down through the planted feet into the boards instead of back up the caster's arm.

And Ternhall paired its students in their first year and taught them to grow their lattices against each other, so that by the end each knew the other's footing better than her own.

Karis had grown hers against Ivenne's.

She came back to the table and sat. "Now ask the other question. The one that's actually useful."

He thought for a moment. "And the other way about? What of yours came from her?"

"Every one." Karis picked up the russet packet, held it a moment in both hands, and gave it to him. "Third page. The last paragraph. Read it to me aloud, would you? I've read it to myself. I want to hear how it sounds in somebody else's mouth."

He found the paragraph and read it.

*Subject's method is her signature. Nothing reaches the floor with her that has not been on paper first, and whoever has read her paper has read the bout before it begins. Pressed for time, she goes back to shapes she has already written down. She does not invent well, knows it, and declines to try.*

He put the packet down. The handwriting under the print was small and slanting and very clear.

"That's Ivenne's hand," said Karis. "I'd know it in the dark. That's the truest thing anybody has ever written about me, Cael, and I've written a great deal about myself."

"Standardization is legibility," he said quietly.

"My line, from Greyvane, said about the registry, in triumph." Her mouth moved, not quite into a smile. "Here it is again, about me, in a shop on the river, for two marks."

At the end of the room Brom had stopped turning pages.

Karis looked down at her ruled leaf for a long while, and Cael watched her follow the thing through to its end, carefully, as she followed everything, even when the end was plainly going to be somewhere she did not want to arrive.

"Everything I have ever written about an opponent," she said at last, "is worth something only because I know a thing the opponent doesn't know I know. That's all study is. It's a gap between two people's knowledge, and you live in the gap. And there's no gap here. Whatever I could put in these columns, she put in hers two years ago. We each hold the other's key." She stopped. "Except that isn't true either. Hers is the one that's rusted."

"Why?"

"Because she stayed. She's still on the long floor with somebody new at the other end of her lattice, doing what we did, the way we did it. Staying keeps a person in plain sight." She touched the russet cover with one finger. "Ternhall's book on me stops on the day I left. Everything since is a blank page to them." She looked up at him. "And the reason it's blank—"

"Say it," said Cael. "For the notebook."

"The reason it's out of date is you." She said it to his face, and then she turned and said it again down the length of the room to Brom, and then she looked at the ceiling, toward the floor where Lira lay asleep.

"All of you. At Ternhall my method and I were one thing. That's what a doctrine is for. Then I came and sat down at your table, and I've spent two years since writing down people who fight past whatever's written about them." She was quiet for a moment. "I don't know whether any of it went in. I can't see it from in here. Ivenne has never sat at this table. If I'm honest, neither have I, not from the outside."

"Then she'll find out on the floor," said Brom, from the window. "So will you."

For a while longer she sat over the four bare columns. Then she picked up the pen and wrote one word across the tops of all four, in her small square hand. *Unknown.*

*Karis's study died tonight,* Cael wrote before he slept. *She sat in front of four ruled columns and found that whatever she could put in them, the other woman put in hers long ago. I've seen her lose arguments. I've never seen her method lose one.*

*It made me ask who could do that to me, sit across a table holding my key. Three people, and all of them are upstairs asleep, and none of them ever would.*

---

Rooke ran Lira's preparation himself the next day, in a hired practice hall behind the Concourse's east tower, with the door bolted, in two closed sessions, one before noon and one after.

He did not correct her once in either of them. Cael noticed it before the first hour was out. All year Rooke had worked his fighters with a steady trickle of small corrections, a heel here and a hip there and *again*, *wider*, *no*, as a miller keeps adjusting his stones.

This morning there was none of that.

The corrections were finished. They had been finished for a while, Cael supposed, and what was left was quieter, closer to the way Hesk would set one clock beside another and correct neither, only note down the difference between them.

The two reserves stood at chalk marks as gateposts, as they had on the oak in the cold term. And Ephram ran Zerin.

He had come to the hall at first light with Cael's charts of her three bouts copied out in his own hand, which he must have done by lamplight in the room he shared with Brom. He ran them against Lira on the floor a line at a time. He went where Zerin had gone and turned where she had turned, entered off the left as she entered off the left, came out of every turn already moving, as near to it as his legs would take him.

He could not come near her speed.

Nobody in that hall could have, and he knew it better than anybody. But he ran her shape with a fierce, exact patience that Cael had not seen in him before, and whenever he got a line wrong he stopped dead, walked back to his mark without a word, and ran it again before anyone had to ask.

Rooke stood at the side with his arms folded and watched it for most of the morning. Then, between two runs, he spoke to Lira.

"Speed's the one thing no hall can lend you," he said. "I can't give it you in here and neither can he. What you'll get from him, and from this one's pages—" he tipped his head at Cael without turning it, "—is her shape. You'll get her shape. The real thing you'll get once, on the day, with the gallery full." He paused. "So count the first exchange as fees. Pay them. Expect to lose it, and learn everything you can while you do."

Lira nodded and wrote it on the inside of her wrist.

Cael stood at the edge of the floor with the notebook open and did not write anything for a while. A coach was telling his fighter to give away the opening of the biggest bout of her life, and to use it for taking notes. He turned it over and could not find a single coach he had ever met who would have said it, and he thought that no opponent had ever been paid a higher compliment by a man she had never spoken to.

In the afternoon Lira ran her corner work against Ephram's Zerin, first at a walk, then at speed, then at speed with every burst counted and the bill added up after each run. Ephram's lungs gave out long before her ideas did. At the end he sat down on the floor with his back to the wall and his head between his knees, and Lira brought him a cup of water and stood over him while he drank it.

"You were a good Zerin," she said.

"I was a slow one."

"A slow good one. That's exactly what I needed."

He looked up at her, scarlet to the roots of his hair. "I owe you a final from the confluence," he said. "I don't mind paying it off a little at a time."

She laughed and pulled him up by the wrist, and the two of them walked the floor together once more at a stroll, while she told him which of his Zerins had been nearest the real one and he argued with her about the third.

Cael closed the notebook and watched them go round. He had seen Lira prepare for fights all year, and he knew the shape of it in her, the restlessness, the extra hour on the oak, the way she ate standing up.

None of that was here today.

She had done her worrying on the quay steps and in the back room, and what was left in her now was a sort of working calm, the steady attention of a carpenter on the last day before a roof goes up, when every beam has been cut and the only question left is whether it fits.

---

In the hour after the second session Brom said he wanted to see Rhagen warm up, and Cael went with him.

The tournament kept one floor open for warming up to anybody who cared to stand at its rail. It was the only place in Norhold where every house could watch every other house for nothing, and most of them, knowing it, sent their fighters there to do as little as possible in front of strangers. Rhagen did not. Rhagen came as a body, eight fighters and two coaches in black and glass-grey, and worked the open floor for a full hour exactly as they would have worked their own hall at home with the doors locked.

They talked the whole time, and that was the first thing Cael noticed and the thing he went on noticing. Every drill on Rhagen's half of the floor had a voice laid over it. A fighter would move and a coach would name the move aloud. The other fighter would answer it and name the answer, while a third stood to one side and wrote it down.

Angles had names and half-beats were counted out loud. A gap in somebody's guard would be spotted, named, argued over by three people at once, and closed, and then the closing would be named too.

Marek worked at the far end of the floor against a tall fighter with a captain's knot on his sleeve.

Cael knew who the captain was.

Rhagen's Shield, Silver of the sixth rank, whose name stood first on the demonstration frame with *Doctrinal interest* written after it. He looked at the man once, long enough to know him again in any crowd, and then he took his eyes away and kept them away.

He had settled it with himself on the stair that morning. Whatever Rhagen's captain did on an open floor, he did there because he had chosen to be seen doing it, and Cael did not mean to carry the man's choice into the ring as if it were a finding.

So he watched Marek.

Marek was a head shorter than Brom and spare, built close to the bone, and he moved the way his notations read, with nothing in it for show. The captain was building coverage against him slowly, a layer at a time, the way a teacher works a sum on a slate for a slow pupil.

Marek was taking it to pieces.

He found a place in it and named it, and the captain closed it, and Marek found the new place the closing had made and named that. Neither of them went above half speed. The coach at their elbow wrote without once lifting his pencil.

Twice the whole Rhagen floor stopped to watch them.

Brom leaned on the rail with his forearms crossed and said nothing for a long time.

"They're not training," he said at last. "They're peer-reviewing."

"Does it change anything for you?"

"No. It tells me I had it right." He watched Marek name another place. "A house like that makes readers. And a reader believes what's on the page in front of him. He can't help it, any more than I can help being heavy. It's what he's built for." He straightened up off the rail. "So I'll spend my bout handing him things to read."

He gave the far end of the floor one more look, the short spare man in black taking a structure apart out loud while three people wrote it down.

As he looked, Marek glanced up. It was not a long glance. It went along the rail, found Brom, rested on him for the space of a breath, and went back to the captain's coverage. Brom lifted his chin a little, which was as near as he came to a nod in public. Neither of them smiled.

Cael stood a moment longer by the rail after Brom had turned away.

The thing he could not stop looking at was not Marek. It was the noise.

Rhagen said everything out loud. It wrote its whole doctrine down and sold the book in every town on the continent, and on an open floor it argued about its own gaps in front of anybody who cared to listen, and it seemed to lose nothing by it.

Cael had spent a year learning to say nothing, to show a floor exactly what the record could hold and not a hand's breadth more, and he had come to think of silence as the only safe way a fighter could live. Here was a whole house that lived the other way and thrived.

He did not think it would ever be his way. But he walked back across the court behind Brom wondering, for the first time, what it would feel like to be read aloud by people who meant no harm by it.

"Come on," he said. "I've seen their library. Time to forge the book."

---

Lira had gone to bed in the afternoon on Rooke's orders and slept through the supper bell. When she came down, the others had kept her place and her plate, and Brom had kept the end of the loaf, which in Brom was close to a declaration.

He passed it to her without a word. Then he filled her cup from the jug, and while he did it he told her a story.

"The first weeks on the oak," he said, "you lost to me every time. Do you remember? Every single time. You'd come at me straight, as hard as you could, and I'd stand there, and you'd bounce."

He set the jug down. "Then one week you stopped coming at me. You went round. You made me chase you all over the oak until I was blowing like a carthorse, and you touched me on the back of the neck, and I sat down on the floor." He went back to his own plate. "You beat me the times you made me play yours. Don't play hers."

Lira looked at him over the bread for a while. Then she nodded once and ate.

Karis gave Lira a page from her notebook.

She tore it out at the table where everybody could see.

Nobody said anything about it, but every one of them noticed, because in two years none of them had ever known Karis give away so much as a corner of a page. She folded it once and passed it down the board.

It was the panel for the semifinal.

The west tower had posted it at noon, five judges and a clerk, and Karis had sat behind every one of the five from the gallery through the first week. Their names ran in a column down the left of the page, and beside each name she had written one line on the way that judge raised a flag.

At the bottom she had written a finding and underlined it twice.

*These five like to see a thing finished before they rule on it. Three of them run a quarter-beat slow on anything still under construction. Anything you build slowly, they'll wait for. So build slowly.*

"Fight your own fight," said Karis. "They're already sitting in the right chairs to see it."

Lira folded the page again and put it inside her shirt, against her skin.

After supper they stayed at the table for Brom. Nobody called it a study. He only took Marek's notations off his knees, where they had been all through the meal, and spread them among the cups and crusts, and the others drew their chairs in round them.

It was Ephram who knew the doctrine.

Every house on the continent kept a copy of Rhagen's book, and Halcenvane's had come north in Rooke's trunk; Ephram had borrowed it at the second waystation and read it through twice on the tailboard. He found the page he wanted without looking for it.

"First line of the Glass chapter," he said. "*Every structure is its gaps.*" He put the book face down. "Not that it has gaps. That it is them. A fence is only a row of holes with wood round the edges, and if you learn the holes you've learned the farmer, because he decided where every one of them went."

He turned his cup in his hands. "They teach it from the first week. And there's something else, about Marek. His rank came late. Rhagen put his name on the register late, on purpose, and they've never once asked for him to be moved up. They like their best work to sit low on the paper. It makes the doctrine look better when it wins."

"Keeping your best people cheap on purpose." Karis wrote it down. "Institutional banking. It's what Brom does on his own, done by a whole house with a press."

Brom had been listening with his hand flat on the notations. Now he looked round the table.

"I've been him," he said. "All year. Cheap on paper, and every round somebody across the floor who'd got ready for the price." He tapped the pages. "Now we meet. Two bargains on one floor, and you can't get a bargain off a bargain. So it's his trade against mine, both at the proper price. He finds doors. I charge for them."

"And what is it you want?" said Cael. "Not the title. Under the title."

Brom took his time, as he always did, and nobody hurried him.

"Since the slate country," he said, "every man who's fought me has had my old page three in his pocket. He won't. He'll have read it, and he'll put it down after one touch, and then he'll go looking at what I am now." He gathered the pages together and squared them, slowly. "Nobody's looked at me properly since the mending. Plenty of people have looked at the dossier. I'd like to hear what he finds."

*Brom has drawn the best reader on the continent,* Cael wrote afterward, *and the thing he's happiest about is that he'll finally get a proper inspection.*

*He wants to be read. He's mended something and he'd like the best eyes alive to check the work. I sat at that table with my whole year in my pocket, and I couldn't remember one time I'd let anybody check anything of mine.*

---

Rooke had given Lira the whole commercial file on Zerin in the morning, eleven pages bound in grey board with the shop's stamp on its cover. She had read the first ten at the rail between sessions. The eleventh she had kept back for the little room, and for him.

It was not a page of times.

It was the page that every file in that shop's style kept for its end, the page about where a fighter came from, and she read it aloud to him in a low voice, with the window open on the dark courtyard.

*Program entrant at twelve. Kindled at fourteen, within the program. Advanced on schedule, every season.* After that the page went on in the shop's flat, careful manner: the cohorts she had been put in, the coaches and the halls, each step up taken in the very year it was due. Iron Rank Nine at nineteen. Silver assessment pending. And at the very foot, a line that was as close as the shop ever came to praise. *No season wasted.*

Lira laid it on the table and put the lamp on it so the draught would not take it.

For a while neither of them spoke.

"I've been holding this upside down," she said. "Since the quay steps. I've been treating it like a list of punishments." She touched the edge of the page. "That's not what it is. It's an *audit* of the other road."

"Which road?"

"Mine. The one I didn't get." She sat back. "Same Path. Same sort of start. Somebody looked at each of us young and thought there was something there. Fenmark put me in a ledger. Auremont put her in a program. Then Fenmark threw me out, and I went and made myself out of whatever was lying about. A ledger-keeper at Ardenmere. A circuit full of fair-day men. You. Brom. A broom in the common room." She almost laughed.

"And Auremont kept her, and built her properly, every door opened in the right year with the right key. The day after tomorrow the two of us find out what each way of making a fighter is worth."

Cael sat with that while the lamp hissed. He found that he was trying, without meaning to, to imagine his own other road, the version of him somebody might have been supposed to build. There was nothing there to imagine. No program on the continent had a page for a boy like him, and no shop sold a file that began where his began.

Lira had a road she had not been allowed to walk, and a woman walking it, and she could stand on a floor and look at it. He had never had one. He had only ever had the road he was on, and he had laid most of it himself, a stone at a time, a little ahead of his own feet.

She stood up and fetched her kit bag from the corner, and sat down on the floor with it between her knees, and began to rewind the strap on its buckle, very carefully, the way she did small mending when she was thinking about something large.

Cael looked for self-pity in her, from old habit, and could not find any. Whatever that account had once held, she had spent it a long time ago, and gone back over the spending since, and shut the book.

"It isn't her," she told the buckle. "I'm fighting the version of me they were supposed to build." She pulled the strap tight and tested it. "All right, then. Let's see what the scrapyard version does."

---

She found him later on the stair, on the landing between their floor and the hill house's, where a narrow window looked out over the roofs toward the Concourse.

He had gone there to think, and she seemed to know it, because she did not speak at once. She sat down two steps above him with her arms round her knees and looked out of the same window.

"Do me a chart," she said at last. "Her against me. Properly. No coaching. Not what you hope for me. What the instrument says."

He had known since the second evening that she would ask, and he had been building the answer ever since, the way he would have built it for a stranger. He gave it to her that way, because that was what she had asked for, and because he had never once lied to her about a floor.

"You'll be a tenth late," he said. "On every window, all night. Every time the two of you race for a place, she gets there first, by a tenth. It's not much. But it's every time, and a tenth every time adds up to a bout."

He kept his eyes on hers.

"Everything else is level. Your corners against her starts is the best pairing in that whole bracket. I'd pay to watch it if I weren't already going to. And she's never been behind, not once, and you've spent your whole life behind." He let the rest of the chart finish in his head. "Fight her the way the numbers expect and she takes it on points, in four exchanges, cleanly. If you can find the one place nobody's ever taken her, and keep her there, then I don't know what happens. Nobody knows. That's the only reason to go."

Lira sat quite still on the step above him.

"Behind," she said.

"Behind."

She looked out of the window for a long time at the dark shape of the Concourse against the sky and the lamps burning on the fourth floor of the west tower, where somebody was still at work.

Then she stood up, and as she passed him on her way up she touched the top of his head, once, lightly, the way she used to on the oak when a session had gone well.

"I'll be at the rail with the notebook," he said to her back.

"I know. So will Auremont." She went on up without turning round. "Give them something long to write."

He stayed on the landing until the lamps in the west tower went out. He had given her the true chart and nothing gentler, because she had asked for it and because between the two of them anything gentler would have been an insult.

Giving it had not been free.

He had not known until tonight that honesty with a friend had a price of its own, quite separate from the price of honesty with anybody else, and that part of the price was that the friend had to sit there and watch you pay it.

# Chapter 35 — Behind

The morning of the semifinal went the way their fight mornings always went, and nobody remarked on any of it, because remarking was not part of the observance.

They ate early, at the end of the long table, before the house's other guests came down. Nobody tasted anything.

Lira ate what she always ate before a bout, bread and an egg and half a cup of milk, and she left the other half of the cup as she always did, and Brom reached over and finished it for her without being asked, as he had done on every fight morning since the slate country.

Then she checked her kit on the bench under the window. Wraps and spare wraps, the tape, the soft shoes and the hard ones, the strap of the bag she had rewound two nights before. She went through it once, in her own order. Then Brom picked up the bag and went through it again in his.

"You've done that," said Lira.

"I know." He put the tape back exactly where it had been. "It isn't for you."

They walked to the Concourse through the delegation quarter in the order the city had learned to know them by: Brom in front, Lira and Cael in the middle, Karis and Ephram behind, and Rooke last with Gault and the coats. People stepped aside for them at corners.

Twice somebody called Lira's name from a doorway. Once a girl of about ten ran alongside them for half a street, shouting *Bluff! Bluff!*, until her mother caught her by the collar and hauled her back.

Lira did not seem to hear any of it. She walked with her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes on the cobbles a few yards ahead. Cael had walked beside her onto more floors than he could count, and he could read nothing on her this morning but a great evenness. She had done her thinking. Nothing was left in her that still wanted deciding.

On the back of her left hand, very faint, was the ghost of one word in ink. She had scrubbed at it and not quite got it out.

The second floor was full before they reached it.

It was full to the rails and past them.

The square hall under the high glass had been built to seat perhaps six hundred on its tiers, and this morning there were more than that on the benches and more again in the passages behind, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the archways.

The broadsides had been pricing the bout for two days, and Cael had seen them pasted on the walls of the delegation quarter all the way in, one on top of another where the bill-stickers had run out of wall. Some were about Auremont's perfect product and some about the girl Fenmark had thrown out. The best of them simply printed the two names side by side in large type and left the reader to do the sum.

The dais stood against the south rail with its five tall chairs and the clerk's desk. Morning light came down through the glass in long pale panes that lay across the western half of the floor and had not yet begun to move.

On the far side the Auremont block sat in its blue, very still. In its front row were the two scouts Cael had charted all year. The older man had his book shut on his knee. The younger woman had hers open.

Halcenvane had the near rail. Rooke stood at the centre of it with his arms folded, and Gault beside him with the coats and the heat-wrap, and the rest of them along it in a row. High up behind them, in the first row of the upper gallery, Cael caught a glimpse of Withrow's dark coat and Bracken's grey head beside it. She had not said she was coming. She had not needed to.

Zerin came out to her chalk on the western mark, with the light behind her.

Cael had seen her fight three times and had never been this close. She was compact and fair, a little shorter than Lira, with her hair tied back hard and a face that seemed to keep its feelings somewhere else for later. She reached her chalk neither early nor late. She had walked through every bout of the tournament like that, like somebody coming into a room to fetch a thing she had left there.

The referee called them to the centre flag for the formalities.

They stood a stride apart. Zerin looked Lira over from her feet to her face with plain professional attention, as a buyer looks at a horse she has already decided is worth the price. Then her eyes went down to Lira's left hand and rested a moment on the grey ghost of a word.

Cael saw her try to read it. He saw her fail.

The flags went up.

---

The tiers saw speed. Cael, at the rail with the full gaze on her, saw something harder to look at.

Zerin opened at her walking pace.

Her walking pace was everybody else's sprint, and she came across the floor at it with no change in her body at all. There was no gather, no breath, no setting of the hips. She simply went from one place to another place very fast.

Lira read her. She read her well, every line of it, as she read everybody.

And every read was a tenth late.

Cael saw it on the first contact and on every contact after.

Lira would read the line, and the answer would form in her, and it would be the right answer, every time, the answer he would have given her himself from the rail; but by the time it had formed, the moment it would have fitted was already gone. It was as though every window in the fight had been cut a tenth narrower than the windows Lira had grown up fighting through, and nobody had told her the new size.

The first touch came on the shoulder. Zerin entered from the left, and Lira read it and turned to meet it, and turned to meet it a tenth after it had arrived.

Lira spent her one survey burst straight after that, as she and Rooke had agreed she would, not to score but to measure. She burst across the line Zerin was running and let Zerin close on her, and Cael watched her body take the measurement as it happened: how far, how fast, and how little warning. It came back the way the extract had said it would, and a little worse, because the extract had been written by a clerk with a clock and Lira was finding it out with her ribs.

The second touch came low on the hip, from behind, while Lira was still landing from the burst.

The third took longer. Lira began to put a corner together against the north rope, a half-step here and a feint there to close the roads. Zerin went through it before it was done. She did not go round it. She went through, like a draught through a door that somebody is still in the act of shutting, and touched Lira on the ribs as she passed.

Three flags. Clean ones, and not one of them close.

On the far side, the younger Auremont scout wrote something short in the gap after each flag, quickly, without looking down at the page. The older man did not write at all. He sat with his book shut on his knee and watched Lira, and Cael understood that he was waiting to see what she did next, because what a fighter did after a clean loss was the only thing about her that could not be bought from a shop.

"Nought to three," called the clerk, and the exchange was closed.

The gallery let out its breath. It was a long, soft, falling sound.

Several hundred people had paid to see a story, and had watched it go wrong in the first two minutes. Behind Cael a man said, not unkindly, "Well. That's that, then."

Lira walked back to her chalk.

Cael watched her shoulders, because her shoulders were where she kept the truth, and they did not come up. She did not tighten. She stood easily on her mark with her hands loose and looked across at Zerin as she had looked at the extract in the back room. She was not looking at the woman. She was looking at the shape of her. Then she took three slow breaths.

"Fees," said Rooke, very low. "She's paid them."

Cael had the whole exchange charted in his head, the windows and the tenth and all three touches. None of it was any use to her now. She had felt every piece of it from the inside, and she knew it better than he did.

But he could see something forming in the set of her weight as she stood there. It was an adjustment. He knew its name before she made it, because it was older than either of them had words for and it belonged to both of them.

*Stop racing her. Sell her the floor.*

---

When the flags went up again, Lira had given up answering.

Zerin came across the floor as she had before, and Lira was not there to be come at.

She had moved before the entry arrived. She had not moved away from it but sideways, a half-step and a turn that cut the east approach in two and left the half by the rope belonging to her. Zerin went through the place where Lira had been and found it empty. She turned in her stream-round-a-stone way and came back, and by then Lira had moved again and closed a diagonal, and Zerin had to go round it.

"North," said Ephram quietly at the rail. He had both hands on it and his eyes on the floor, and he was calling it the way he called the bench floor for the squad. "North's gone. East half."

Lira was buying floor.

She was not buying it from Zerin. She was buying it from the square, a yard at a time. Each yard cost her a step or a feint or a turn, paid at the moment it was cheapest, and every yard she bought she kept.

She did not burst. She had burst once in the first exchange, to survey, and she did not burst again, not when a road opened in front of her that a burst would have taken, not when Zerin came close enough to touch; and Cael, counting at the rail, understood that she was saving it.

The gallery began to mutter. From the tiers it looked like retreat. A fighter who had lost the first exchange three to nothing was refusing every entry she was offered and backing round the floor with her hands low. A few people on the benches began to say so aloud.

"West," said Ephram. "Gone. She's got three sides."

Zerin's floor was shrinking round her. For the first time in the tournament it was beginning to cost her something to move through it.

The first corner cost her nothing. Lira closed it on her against the north rope, all three roads sold at once, and Zerin stepped out of it with a small change of line and a turn, as easily as a woman steps round a puddle.

The second cost her the turn.

Cael saw it. The older Auremont scout saw it too, because his head came up. Zerin's line bent. For the first time since the opening, the fastest fighter in the bracket had to change where she was going because of where somebody else had already been. The bend was small, and sensible, and plain to anybody who knew what to look for.

The third corner came with its bill already inside it.

Lira had banked a burst. She had held it through two openings where she might have spent it, so that Zerin would learn that this girl did not burst out of her corners. She only walked out of them.

Zerin learned it. At the third corner her line bent the same way it had bent at the second, a small, sensible, predictable bend, because the floor had just taught her that bending was enough.

Lira spent the burst. She spent the whole of it, at the one angle the bend had made certain.

For half a beat Zerin was behind.

Cael could never afterward say how long it lasted. He only knew that it was the shortest thing he saw all week and the largest.

Zerin arrived where she had meant to be, a tenth early as always, and Lira was already standing there. Every piece of Zerin had been built to arrive first. For half a beat she had arrived second, and her body did not know what to do about it. Her weight hung.

Her angle, made for a start that was hers, pointed at a start that belonged to somebody else.

Lira touched her on the shoulder.

It was not a quick flag. It was the kind of contact that comes at the end of a long build, and three of the five judges on the dais were, by Karis's page, a quarter-beat slow on anything still being built. For a moment the flags stayed down and the hall held its breath. Then they went up, all on Halcenvane's side.

The second floor came apart.

It was not a cheer.

It was bigger and messier than a cheer.

For two days the broadsides had told this gallery that nobody in the tournament had taken an exchange off Zerin. Now they had watched somebody take a point off her the hard way, and every one of them understood at the same moment that the exchange was open, and they stood up and shouted at each other about it as people shout at a race when the horse nobody backed puts its head in front at the last turn.

On the far side, the older scout was writing. He was writing in the middle of an exchange. Cael had charted the man at three meets and never once seen him write before the bell.

Zerin took one back at once, without fuss, a clean touch on Lira's hip as she landed from the burst. The noise hardly changed.

She had cut her loss quickly and with no waste, Cael thought, as she must have been taught to, and even the cutting was a kind of tell, because it had been drilled into her.

Then she went back into the floor Lira had sold her, carefully this time, testing each corner before she trusted it.

Lira let her test. By her own count and Rooke's she had one burst left for the bout, and she kept it in her pocket. She closed one more corner against the south rope under the dais, on foot alone. Zerin went out of it the long way, along the rope. There was just room at the end of the long way for Lira to be standing there with a hand out.

Two to one, at the bell. Two to four on the bout.

Brom let out a breath beside Cael. "There it is." He sounded like a man who has watched the first course of a wall go down dead level.

Rooke had his little book out, the one he kept for nobody but himself, and his pencil was moving.

High in the upper gallery, Withrow had not stood up with the rest. Cael looked for her and found her sitting perfectly still with her hands in her lap, looking down at the floor with an expression he could not read at that distance and did not try to.

---

Zerin spent the interval studying.

She stood on her chalk in the light from the glass with her hands at her sides, and her eyes went round the floor, corner to corner, rope to rope. Cael knew exactly what she was doing. She was reading the second exchange back to herself, the way he read the notebook. It was the first time all tournament that he had seen anybody's study run in her direction.

She had come to Norhold with everybody's files in her head and had needed none of them. Now she had found something she had no file for. So she was writing one, standing there, in the space of a single interval.

She came into the third exchange with the corner game half solved.

Lira's corners wanted Zerin to bend late. So Zerin bent early.

She spent speed she had not needed to spend all tournament, taking every corner Lira offered from an angle that got there while the selling was still going on, paying the toll a beat before Lira could set it.

It cost her, and Cael could see exactly what it cost her, a breath that came a little harder at the end of every corner and a line that ran a little wider than she would have liked; but she had more to pay with than Lira had to charge, and she knew it, and she paid.

It was the best fight Cael had ever seen Lira fight. And it was going the wrong way.

For most of that exchange the two of them ground at each other across the pale boards. It was long and close and quiet, and almost nothing in it was easy for the tiers to follow.

Lira sold her yards and Zerin bought them early. Lira closed a corner and Zerin was already through the gap she had meant to shut. So Lira began to sell the gaps as well, and Zerin began to pay for those in advance too.

Cael's chart showed him the thing he had been afraid of since the stair.

Each corner Lira built was still working. But each one was working a little less well than the one before. The yield was falling. Lira's bursts were a store, and the store was going down. Zerin's speed was not a store at all. It was only what she was.

Cael watched one corner go from start to finish and could have drawn it afterward with his eyes shut.

Lira began it against the west rope, in the long light from the glass, with a step to close the north road and a feint to sell the south. The feint was meant to come a beat after the step. Before it came, Zerin was already in the corner and out of it again by the south road, going through the place the feint would have sold, at an angle she had chosen while Lira was still choosing hers. It had cost her a burst of speed she would not have spent an exchange earlier, and Cael saw it cost her, a slight hardening of the breath. Then she was gone along the rope, and the corner was empty, and the feint arrived into nothing.

Lira scored once, from a corner against the east rope that Zerin went into a fraction too soon and had to correct from inside. To close that corner Lira spent the last burst of her rate. Zerin scored twice. Both times it came from an angle that had opened and closed before the corner round it was done.

One to two, at the bell. Three to six on the bout.

"She's paying early," said Karis. "Brom said she would."

"Brom said she'd been taught to."

"For a program that's the same thing. It's what a program is for."

Lira did not come to the rail. She stood on her chalk with her right hand on her left shoulder, as she sometimes did after a long session on the oak, and rolled the left shoulder under it slowly. Once. Twice. It was the shoulder she led her bursts with.

Gault had seen it before Cael had finished seeing it. He already had the heat-wrap in his hands.

At the very end of the interval Lira turned her head and looked across at Rooke. It was not a long look, and nobody else in the hall would have known it for a question. Rooke held up three fingers against the rail, low, where only she could see them, and folded them down again one at a time. That was her rate for a bout. She had spent it.

Lira nodded to him. Then she turned back to her chalk.

---

In the fourth exchange Zerin went to her full speed.

Nothing in the three bouts Cael had charted had asked it of her, and so he had never seen it.

The extract had promised it. The examiner's mark beside the closing figure had promised it. Now she gave it, and the floor changed state round her the way water does when it freezes. It was the same forty feet of pale board under the same glass. It was not the same stuff at all.

Cael felt it go wrong in his own instrument.

For three bouts and three exchanges he had kept her in the full gaze by setting his eyes a tenth ahead of her and letting her walk into them. Now she arrived before the tell.

Entries he still had forming in his head were already finished on the floor. Turns he had placed a beat ahead were complete, and she was coming out of them, while he still watched the spot where they ought to have begun. It was like looking at Hesk's clock while somebody spun the long hand round with a finger.

All year he had trusted his eyes to see a fight a fraction early. For one exchange they could only follow this one from behind. He stood at the rail and watched a friend be outrun by something he could not keep up with either.

Lira knew it before he did.

She had built her corners all through the second exchange and the third, and in the first minute of the fourth Zerin went through every one of them. She did not solve them. She went through faster than they could close, as if they had been painted on paper. Every yard Lira had bought, she lost again in a breath.

Lira cut one diagonal.

Of everything she did that morning, it was the finest.

She cut it fresh, with the last of her floor-sense and a half-burst she could not afford, across the middle of the square from the north-west corner to the south-east. Then she held it for half a beat while Zerin came at it. For half a beat it stood. Zerin had to go round, and Lira was waiting at the far end of it, and touched her on the forearm.

One to nothing. The gallery came to its feet again.

After that it was arithmetic, done at a speed only one of them could vote on.

Lira spent everything. Cael watched her do it and counted every coin.

She spent her last burst, and then she spent the burst after the last, the one she did not have, which Rooke's rule did not allow and which her own body had never once let her spend on any floor before, and Cael felt the cost of it go into her left shoulder from across the hall. She spent her reads to buy position, and her position to buy time. Nothing she gave up was given. All of it had to be taken.

Zerin took it anyway.

She took the first touch off an entry from the left. She took the second off the turn after it.

The third came at the end of a long, close, fierce run along the north rope, with Lira a single stride behind her all the way and gaining, a hand's breadth and then another, so that for a moment the whole hall thought she would get there. She was almost there. The flag went up as her hand closed on the air where Zerin's shoulder had just been.

"One to three," called the clerk. Then, after a moment: "Four to nine on the bout."

Nobody needed the sum explained. Lira could not have drawn level with every point of a fifth exchange, so there would be no fifth exchange. It was over in four.

Zerin did not press.

She had not pressed past what the bout required all morning, and she did not do it now. She stopped where she was when the flag went up, a stride from the rope, and let her hands drop, and turned to face the girl who had just spent everything she owned trying to catch her.

There was no malice in her anywhere. Cael had looked for it all morning and found none. There was nothing in her at all, he thought, except the work. And the work was done.

Lira stood bent over on the floor with her hands on her knees, breathing hard. The second floor got to its feet.

It stood for both of them.

It had come for a story, the girl who was built against the girl who had rebuilt herself, and it had been given a measurement instead. It stood up anyway, every bench of it and the archways too, and it went on standing for a long time.

Cael had seen galleries stand for winners all year. Once or twice he had seen one stand for somebody brave who lost. He had never seen a gallery stand like this, for two people at once, because it had just watched somebody's ceiling and somebody else's floor meet in the same square, and both of them hold.

Lira straightened up slowly. She held her left arm a little stiff at the shoulder. Cael could see the shoulder setting already, tightening round the burst she had not had. She did not look at it.

He watched her face, because he was afraid of what he would find there. What he found was not what he had expected.

There was no performance in it.

She was not being brave for the gallery. She was working. Behind her eyes the bout was already turning into something else, into numbers and corners and the tenth and the half-beat, and he could almost watch her filing it, one exchange after another, in the order she would want it later.

She walked across the floor to Zerin and held out her hand, and Zerin took it.

Whatever the tiers had hoped for, tears or a fist thrown in the air, they did not get it. Two working fighters shook hands on a piece of business. Their grips were firm and their faces were level, and both of them would be going over the figures for weeks.

They said something to each other, too quiet for any rail to hear. Then they let go and walked off in opposite directions, and the gallery went on standing until both of them had gone.

The figures went up on the board at the end of the hall eleven minutes later, at the proper interval. Zerin's was twenty-nine. Lira's was twenty-seven.

Cael stayed at the rail looking at the two numbers after the others had turned away.

Twenty-nine was a Silver figure.

Every person in that hall who could read the board knew it. Zerin had posted a Silver figure in an Iron semifinal, as plainly as the Standard could say anything. And two places below it, two points short, stood a girl whom Fenmark's ledger had once written down as Copper.

He thought about the half-beat in the second exchange, when the fastest fighter in the bracket had been behind and had not known what to do there. Nobody had ever studied Zerin from behind, because nobody had ever got her there. So when it came, there had been nothing in her to stand on.

He thought about his own file, the one nobody had the whole of. Nobody had ever got him there either. He was not sure, standing at the rail, whether that was a strength or only a place nobody had been.

Gault was at the top of the steps with the heat-wrap. Lira came up them with her left arm held against her side, and let him put it on her without a word, and only then, when it was on and tied, did she look round for Cael.

"Long enough?" she said.

He knew what she meant. He looked across the floor at the Auremont block, where the older scout was still writing, steadily, page after page.

"They're still going," he said.

# Chapter 36 — Second Time

Brom's quarterfinal took nine minutes in the hour after noon, against a valley house's Stone who had read both editions of his file and had sensibly decided to trust neither. It did the Stone no good at all. Brom won in three exchanges without once doing anything that anybody in the gallery would still remember by supper, and walked off the floor looking pleased about that too.

His semifinal was at the fourth bell on the third floor, against a woman he had fought before.

Cael knew her before she had her coat off. She had been a sheet in Rooke's profiles stack in the first week of the year, marked more heavily than the sheets round it: a fair-day fighter from a market house in the hills, Iron Skin like Brom, Copper Rank Four on her certificate, with four bouts on her record and every one of them won and every one of them short. *Rank Four on paper. Fights like a Seven. Buried.* At the wool town she had walked Brom backward round his own chalk by the arm in front of a muttering gallery, and he had beaten her two touches to one in the fourth glass, and the figures had come up twenty-three to twenty-two.

She was perhaps eighteen, square in the shoulders, with her hair cropped close to her skull, and she still had the fair-day fighter's habit of standing loose and easy until the instant she was not. The lean coach in brown was at her rail with his arms folded, exactly as he had stood in the sheep market. Her market house had come up through its own region on her back, Karis said, and had brought no other entry to any bracket in the city. For a week she had been cutting through her side of the Copper draw, and not one broadside had printed a line about her.

"She's been watching him," said Karis. "Every bout of his she could reach. I've seen her in three galleries."

Lira stood at the rail beside Cael in her coat, with the heat-wrap tied round her left shoulder underneath it and her left arm held close to her side. She had come straight from the second floor and had not sat down since. When Cael had suggested that she might, she had given him a look that closed the subject.

"He'll be careful today."

"Why?"

"Look who's come to watch."

Cael looked. High on the far side, in the third row, sat a short spare man in black and glass-grey, with a coach beside him holding a book open on his knee.

The bell went, and Brom fought the most careful bout of his life.

Cael had never seen him fight like it, and it took most of the first exchange to understand what he was looking at. Brom did nothing new. He did not funnel anybody anywhere. He did not leave a door open on purpose or rent anything out at a discount. He stood in the middle of the floor and fought her with the plain heavy things the whole circuit already knew he had: the drive, the forearm, the left door that changed hands in the turn. Every one of them was on somebody's page already, and every one of them he did exactly as the page said he would, as if he were reading his own file aloud to the man in the third row and adding nothing.

She came at him high and low, as she had in the sheep market, with forearm and knee and the hard edge of her shin. She went for his left side first, because she had bought the old edition too, and the door held. She went round to his right and found nothing there either. She touched him once, low on the hip, a sharp little touch he could not get his arm down in time to stop, and he touched her once on the shoulder with a drive she saw coming the whole way and still could not get out from under.

One to one, at the bell.

Ephram frowned at the floor. "He's not showing anything."

"He's showing everything," said Karis. "Everything Marek's already paid for."

Rooke said nothing. He stood with his forearms along the rail and his chin resting on them and watched Brom walk his slow circle in the interval, round and round his own corner, and nothing in his face moved.

A man two places along the rail from Cael yawned, loudly, and said to his neighbour that he had come to see the Halcenvane wall and had been shown a wall. His neighbour laughed. Cael did not mind them. He was watching the third row, where Rhagen's coach sat with his book open on his knee and his pencil lifted, and in the whole of the first exchange the pencil had not once come down to the page. Brom had spent three minutes on the third floor of the Concourse giving the best reader on the continent nothing at all to read, and the only people in the hall who knew it were the ones who had been told what to look for.

In the second she changed her plan, and it was a good change. She had watched Brom for a week, and she had learned what every fighter learns who watches him long enough, which is that he does not chase. So she stopped coming to him and made him come to her. She stood off at the very edge of his reach and waited, and every time he came she was gone, a step to the side and back again with the shin. It was a fair-day trick, Cael thought, the kind a fighter learns in a ring of hay bales when the purse depends on never being caught. She took a touch with it, low on the knee.

But Brom had fought the hill fighter in the first week, and Rooke had told him to walk.

He walked. He did not chase her and he did not stand still. He went round the floor at his own pace on a circle of his own, and the circle kept bringing him to her whether she liked it or not, so that her waiting turned, slowly, into being found. He touched her twice in the second half of the exchange, both plain drives through the middle when her step to the side came a fraction short.

Two to one. Three to two on the bout.

"He's walking," said Lira, with grim satisfaction. Then she winced and put her good hand on the heat-wrap.

In the interval the lean coach in brown leaned over his rail and spoke to his fighter for a long time, low and steady, with one hand drawing circles in the air. She listened with her eyes on Brom. Cael thought he could guess the advice: *he walks a circle, so make the circle cost him*. It was good advice. It was also the advice that every coach who had seen the hill-fighter bout would give, and Brom, walking his own slow circle on the far side of the floor, had certainly heard it given before.

In the third she went for the left door again, harder, low under the guard with her whole weight behind her shoulder. It was a good attempt, the attempt a fighter makes when she has decided that the thing everybody calls mended must still have one bad day hidden in it somewhere. It had no bad day in it. The guard changed hands inside the turn so smoothly that the door was never open at all, and she drove her shoulder into a forearm that did not move. She got a touch on the turn after, high on the ribs, from an angle Cael would have marked for her at the wool town. Brom got two.

Two to one. Five to three.

In the fourth she was tired and Brom was not.

It had been building all bout, and Cael had watched it build. Every drive she had taken had cost her more than it had cost him to give, and every time she had gone to his left door she had put her whole weight into a wall that did not give, so that by the end of the third exchange she had paid out, a little at a time and without ever noticing the size of the sum, more of herself against that one door than she had spent in the whole of her week in the city. None of it had been dramatic. All of it had added up. By the fourth her sideways step was a beat slow and her shin came up a hand lower than it had in the first. Brom, who was exactly as fresh as he had been at the first bell, simply went on doing the plain things, one after another, until there were three of them.

Three to nothing, closed early. Eight to three. She could not have drawn level in the last exchange, and the clerk called it over.

They stood on the floor a moment, breathing. Then she walked across to him and held out her hand.

"Second time's the real rating," she said, loud enough for the rail. She said it the way a dealer at a horse fair says what an animal is worth after she has ridden it twice. "The first time anybody can be lucky. The second time is what you are."

"Then you're rated," said Brom.

Something went across her face that Cael had no page for. Then she shook his hand once, hard, and walked off toward the lean man in brown without looking back.

The figures went up eleven minutes later. Brom twenty-four. Her twenty-three.

Brom came up the steps unwinding his wraps. He glanced across the hall at the third row, where the short spare man in black was already on his feet and leaving, with his coach behind him shutting the book.

"Nothing." He sounded deeply satisfied. "Not one page he hadn't bought already."

"You gave her a semifinal," said Lira.

Brom thought about that. "She earned the semifinal. I only gave her the rating." He folded the wraps over his arm. "Somebody ought to file for her. Somebody ought to have done it a year ago, at the wool town, and I'd like to know why nobody has."

*Brom fought carefully today,* Cael wrote that night, *and I'd never seen it before. He fought a woman who had watched him for a week and a man who had watched him for a season, both at once, from the same floor, and he gave the first a real bout and the second nothing at all. She said the second time is the real rating. I think she's right. The first look at anybody is a story somebody tells. The second is a measurement.*

*Most people have only ever had one look at me.*

---

Rooke did not wait for the plates to be cleared.

He sat at the head of the long table with the coaching file open beside his cup and his supper going cold, and as soon as Lira had sat down he turned to her and read her the exchanges, her side first, as he read every result to every one of them, flat and complete.

"Naught-three, two-one, one-two, one-three."

Then he closed the file and put his hand on it.

"Four to nine. And a twenty-seven to her twenty-nine. You know what those numbers are? They're a measurement. They're the first honest one anybody's ever given you of how far you are from Silver." He tapped the file. "A tenth on every window. Two points on the figures. That's the distance. That's all it is, and now you know it to the inch. I've had fighters go their whole careers and never once find out how far off they were. They'd lose to somebody better and tell themselves it was luck, or the floor, or the panel, and they'd never know the size of the gap well enough to start closing it." He looked at her. "You know the size of it now. That's what you bought this morning."

Lira was sitting very straight, with the heat-wrap round her shoulder and her left hand in her lap.

"And you spent a burst you hadn't got," said Rooke.

"I know."

"It's in your shoulder. A week of half work, from tomorrow. Gault looks at it every morning, and if I catch you at full on any floor in this city I'll put you on a wagon home." He picked up his cup. "That's the bill. You can afford it."

Then he stood up at last, with the cup in his hand.

"Best loss I've coached in twenty years. Eat something."

Lira looked down at her plate, which was full and which she had not touched. Then, slowly, she picked up her fork and began.

A note came up the stair while she ate. It was on the registry-grey paper Withrow used for everything, folded once, with Lira's name on the outside in Withrow's square hand. Lira opened it at the table and read it, and then turned it round and laid it in the middle of the board, among the cups, for everybody.

*Semifinalist. Continental. Noted twice.*

Nobody said anything for a moment. Cael read it, and then read it again, and understood the last two words. Once in the standings, where it would be printed and posted and filed with everybody else's result. And once by Withrow herself, in whatever ledger she kept that nobody else would ever see. It was perfectly plain which of the two she thought would last longer.

Lira folded the note small and put it inside her shirt, against the page Karis had given her the night before.

Then Rooke turned in his chair to Brom. The pairing for the Copper final had become arithmetic at the fourth bell, and he had plainly been waiting all evening to say what he thought of it.

"The seeding office got one thing wrong this cycle. They put him first, and you wherever the paper happened to put you. What it wanted was no seeds and an apology."

He reached behind him for the Rhagen file. It was thicker now than it had been at the start of the week, with Cael's charts of Marek's bouts folded in among the shop's pages. He laid it in front of Brom and put one finger on the cover.

"He'll find every gap. Plan to have fewer."

That was the whole of the brief. Rooke sat down again and finished his supper, and nobody at the table thought it needed another word.

---

Lira wanted to see the result on the boards.

She did not say why, and nobody asked. Cael went down with her after supper, through the delegation quarter in the cooling dark to the Concourse's outer court, where the day's results went up on the long east wall in the tournament's flat black type an hour after every bout. A few lamps burned along the wall. A sweeper was working his way across the far side of the court, and two old men were arguing in front of the Gold boards about a bout that had not happened yet.

Lira found the Iron semifinals and stood in front of them, reading. She was not lingering over it. She read the board the way she read everything now, carefully, for the record.

*ZERIN — AUREMONT — 9. LIRA — HALCENVANE — 4.* Under the names, in smaller type, the four exchanges and the two figures.

Cael stopped at the edge of the lamplight and let her read it by herself.

He saw Zerin first. She came across the court out of the dark, from the direction of the Auremont quarter, in her house's off-floor blue and with nobody behind her, at the same unhurried pace she had used on every floor in the city. She did not slow as she came near. She simply arrived beside Lira at the board and stood there, and spoke without any greeting at all.

"Fenmark threw you away?"

Lira did not turn her head. She answered the fact, since that was what had been asked. "Their ledger said Copper."

"Their ledger is garbage." There was no more feeling in it than in a miller condemning a wet sack. Zerin lifted her chin at the board. "Nobody has made me arrive second since I was twelve years old. You did it this morning in front of my whole house, and my coaches are still in a room arguing about how." Then she turned from the board and looked straight at Lira for the first time. "Silver bracket, next cycle. Be there."

She walked off across the court the way she had come, past the sweeper and the two old men, and did not look back once.

Lira stood at the board for a long time afterward.

They walked back through the quarter without saying much at first. At the corner by the cookshop, which was shuttered, Lira stopped under a lamp and looked at the back of her left hand, where the ghost of the word was still just visible.

"She looked at it. On the floor. At the flags. Did you see?"

"I saw. She couldn't read it."

"No." Lira rubbed at it with her thumb, gently, like somebody rubbing a coin. "I'm going to leave it, I think. Till it goes on its own."

They walked on. After a while she said, not looking at him, "Twice in one exchange. She said I did it twice." She sounded like somebody checking a sum that another person had added up for her. "I only counted once."

"It was twice," said Cael. "The second time was the corner under the dais. You'd already spent the burst. She went the long way round and you were there anyway."

Lira thought about that for the length of a street. At the guesting-house the common room was still lit, and the others were in it in their usual places: Brom at the table with the Rhagen file, Karis in the corner with her notebook open and nothing written in it, Ephram reading Rhagen's book aloud upside down to make Brom laugh, and Gault asleep in the good chair by the fire. Cael sat down on the bench by the hearth.

Lira did not go to her own place. She crossed the room instead and sat down beside him on the bench, the way Brom had sat down next to him once. She did it on purpose, and everybody in the room saw her do it, and every one of them went on pretending not to have.

She looked at the fire for a while. When she spoke, it was in the low, even voice she kept for reading out the last line of anything.

"I used to want them to admit what I already was." A coal shifted in the grate. "The ledger. Fenmark. The registry. I wanted somebody with a stamp to write down the thing I knew, so that I could stop having to show it to people." She was quiet for a moment. "Now I want to become something they haven't seen yet."

Nobody in the room moved. Ephram had stopped reading.

"It's a better want," said Lira. "It costs more."

Cael said nothing. He did not think there was anything he could have said that would have been half as true, so he only sat there beside her and let it be said. After a while she leaned her good shoulder against his, briefly, and then sat up again and looked at the fire.

*She lost today and came home bigger,* he wrote that night. *For as long as I've known her she's been building something, and I always thought it was the stamp. It was never the stamp. The stamp was the scaffolding, and tonight she took it down and let me see the shape of the house.*

*She's stopped wanting to catch the girl they built. She wants to be the thing nobody's built yet. I'm not sure anyone else at that board understood what Zerin had just handed her. I think Zerin did.*

*Note for the circle, and for me. Lira has set herself a thing that can't be done. The rest of us are expected to be there when she does it.*

She did not stay long by the fire. When she stood up she did not go upstairs. She went across the room to the corner where Karis sat over her empty notebook and stood looking down at her.

"Get your coat."

Karis looked up. "It's late."

"I know it's late. Get your coat."

They were gone two hours. Nobody in the common room remarked on it and nobody went up to bed. When Gault woke in the good chair and asked where they had gone, Brom said "Walking," as though that settled everything, and it did.

Cael understood well enough what had happened, though nobody had arranged it. They had done this before for one another, more than once, and never called it anything. One of them would go quiet. The others would watch the quiet grow, and know without a word which of them she would take the argument from. For this one it could only ever have been Lira. Of all of them, only Lira had walked back into a hall full of the people who had once owned her, and come out again.

They came in a little after the eleventh bell, cold, with Lira's heat-wrap slipping and Karis's coat buttoned one hole wrong all the way down. Karis went straight up the stair without a word to anybody. Lira stopped by the hearth and held her good hand out to what was left of the fire.

"Well?" said Brom.

"I told her about the quarry town. The coat-rack." Lira opened and closed her fingers in the warmth. "She knew the story. Everybody does. So I told her the part I've never told anybody, which is what the next minute felt like." She frowned at the coals. "And she said it isn't the same for her. She said I was going back to a building, and a building can shrink. She's going back to a person."

Brom said nothing, which was what he said to most things that deserved it.

"I didn't have an answer to that. Not a clever one." Lira turned her hand over to warm the back. "So I told her the only thing I'm sure of. The sentence's ending isn't in the hall, and it isn't in the other girl either. It's in her. Wherever she goes, she's carrying the end of it." She was quiet a moment. "And she wrote it on her hand. Under a lamp, in the middle of the street, with my pencil, because she'd left hers on the table. I've never had anything I said written down before, Brom." She glanced at Cael. "Except by him."

---

Karis found Cael on the stair at midnight, on the same landing where Lira had found him the night before. He had half expected her. She sat down on the step above him, as Lira had, and in the light from the window he could see the ink on her palm, smudged now, a line of Lira's words in Karis's own small square hand.

"The instrument's answer. No coaching. The same as you gave Lira."

He had been building it all evening, between the boards and the fire, and he gave it to her straight.

"Studying her won't work. She studied you back, the whole time you were studying her, and she's still got every page. There isn't anything you could put in your columns tonight that she didn't put in hers years ago." He looked up at her. "If you win, it'll be as somebody she hasn't got a page for."

Karis sat very still for a long time.

"The file is accurate."

"The file is *finished*," said Cael. "You're not."

He watched her take the sentence apart, as she took everything apart, turning it over in her hands to find where it joined. He saw her find the join. A file was closed on the day it was written. The person it was written about went on living after the ink was dry.

"Accurate and finished." She spoke slowly. "Two different ways for a document to fail. The first one's wrong by mistake." She looked down at her palm. "The second's only wrong by date."

She took the notebook out of her coat and opened it at the leaf headed *QUARTERFINAL*. Under the four columns and the one word across them were three pages of close writing that Cael had never seen.

"I wrote it this afternoon. While you were all at Brom's bout. A plan. A good one, complete in every part." She smiled a little, without much humour. "She'll have it already. It's precisely the plan she'd expect me to write, because I wrote it."

She stood up and went down the stair past him into the dark common room, and he followed her as far as the door.

The fire had burned to coals. Karis knelt in front of it, tore the three pages out along the binding, carefully, as she did everything, and laid them flat on the embers. They browned and curled at the corners and then caught all at once, and she watched them burn until there was nothing left but grey, and then she knelt there a little longer with the light gone out of her face.

She did not write anything to take their place. Cael watched for it, and she did not. She shut the notebook on the leaf with the four columns and the one word, and got up, and brushed off her knees.

At the foot of the stair she stopped and looked back at him. Her voice had gone completely flat, which in Karis had always meant the opposite of what it meant in other people.

"Ternhall's file will fight the woman who left. Halcenvane sends the one who arrived."

Then she went up to bed, and Cael stood a long time by the dead fire, smelling the paper.

# Chapter 37 — The Book

The broadside came up the stair with the bread in the morning, and Ephram read it aloud to the table, standing, in the voice of a town crier with a cold.

The Iron semifinal had the front column.

Somebody had set the four exchanges across the top in large figures, *0–3 · 2–1 · 1–2 · 1–3*, and drawn a heavy black ring round the second pair, like a feast day in an almanac. Underneath, a writer who signed himself only with a printer's mark had spent most of a column on the second exchange, and a good deal of it was wrong about what had happened, and all of it was right about what it had meant.

Ephram saved the last line for the end, and read it slowly.

"*Auremont went home with the bout. The bluff went home with the first thing anybody has taken off Auremont all week, and there is no column in the standings to put it in.*"

He sat down. "There," he said. "Now you're literature."

"Rooke said," said Karis.

"I know. I've been thinking about the second half." He looked round at them. "Plan to have fewer. That's not plan to have none. You can't have none. Nobody can. Rooke knows that better than anybody." Rooke, at the head of the table, did not look up from his cup. But Cael saw the corner of his mouth move, very slightly, in the way it moved perhaps twice a year.

"Tell me what you know," he said. "My gaps. All of them. The ones you've charted on the oak."

There was the top of the right shoulder, where the coverage went over from arm to back. And there was the way Brom's back heel settled when he loaded a drive, on any floor that gave at all.

"Four," said Brom.

"Four I've seen."

The west tower had given the Copper final the main floor.

There had been talk about it in the court all week, and Seln had read the schedule's note out at supper without any comment at all, which from Seln was comment enough. Copper finals did not get the main floor. They got the third floor or the fourth, and a gallery of coaches and parents and fair-day men with their arms folded. This one got the great grey oval and its four banks of tiers. The tiers were not full; they would not be full until the Gold. But they were fuller than Cael had seen them for anything but the opening, and the people in them had the look of people who had come on purpose.

"Somebody in that tower knows good work when it's coming," said Rooke.

The officials had marked out a square on the grey stone, sixty feet on a side, with white rope on iron stands. At the very centre of it, inside the ring of plugged sockets where the exhibition ring would one day stand, there was a round of old dark oak let into the stone. It was perhaps eight feet across. Karis said it was the oldest piece of floor in the building, older than the dais and older than the tiers, the place where the flags had been planted before anybody had thought to build a hall round them. Every cycle the stone round it was patched, and every cycle the oak was left alone.

So the final had two floors in it.

There was the stone, fast and hard and a little slick under soft shoes, and there was the oak, which gripped and gave. Between them ran a seam in a perfect ring where the one met the other, with a lip on it no higher than a fingernail.

During the formalities, while the clerk read out the names, Marek walked the seam.

He did it slowly and without any show, heel to toe, once round the whole ring, looking down at his feet as a man looks at a page he means to learn. Then he went back to his chalk on the north side.

Brom watched him do it. When the referee called them to their marks, Brom did not go to his chalk. He went to the oak. He set himself just inside it on the south side, with his weight in the middle of the old dark boards and the seam half a stride behind his heels, and stood there.

A low sound went round the craft-readers in the tiers. Cael heard it from three directions at once.

"What's he doing?" said Ephram.

"Making him change floors," said Lira. "Every way in to Brom crosses the seam. Stone to oak. Fast to slow. Every single one." She shifted her bad shoulder under the wrap. "He's charging at the door before the bout's started."

Across the square, Marek looked at where Brom was standing, and then down at the seam in front of him, and then at Brom again. Cael was watching his face. He saw nothing on it at all, except that the man had read something, and filed it, and gone on to the next line.

High on the east side, over the far rail, the Compact's row was full except for one chair. Ilsev sat in the first seat with her lap-desk squared. Havel was in the second. At the end of the row, behind the grey rail-cloth, the twelfth chair stood empty, with its brushed seat and its pale square where a plate should have been.

The flags went up.

Marek went to the old seam first, and he went to it only once.

Cael had expected it.

Every man since the slate country had come at Brom's left side with the dossier in his head, and Marek owned the dossier the same as everybody else. He came across the seam fast, changed floors without a hitch, and went in low on the left, at exactly the angle page three described.

The mend held.

Brom took the touch on his forearm, let it run in and stop, and drove through it with the other hand before the absorption was even finished, as Rooke's nineteen exercises had taught him to do. The flag went up for Halcenvane.

One to nothing.

One contact had told him everything the dossier was worth, and he had closed that page and put it away, and Cael saw the craft-readers in the tiers see it too. In the space of a single touch, the old edition of Brom's file had stopped being worth the paper.

Then Marek began to read.

It was the doctrine at its full depth, and Cael had never seen anything like it from the rail. Marek did not attack. He surveyed. He came in light and quick and often, at a rate that looked busy from the tiers and cost him almost nothing, and every contact went somewhere different, and every contact was a question.

The first went to the top of Brom's right shoulder, where the coverage of an Iron Skin changed over from the arm to the back. It was very light. The flags did not move. Brom's guard came across a fraction late, and Marek was already gone.

The second went in the half-beat after one of Brom's drives, when the weight he had thrown forward had to come back before he could throw it again. Cael had charted that gap on the oak at home through a whole year of sessions, and he had never once seen an opponent find it live. Marek found it on the second time of asking. The flag went up for Rhagen.

One to one.

The third went to Brom's pivot.

When Brom turned, there was a quarter-beat when his weight was committed to the turn and could not be taken back. Cael had not known it was there. He saw Marek find it, and touch it, and saw the flag go up, and wrote it down with a feeling like missing a step on a stair he had climbed a thousand times.

Brom had chosen the oak.

He found the gap in the ground Brom had chosen himself, the gap in the very position that was meant to cost him. He went in on the stone side of the seam, low and fast, at the instant the board gave under Brom's heel, and touched him on the ribs.

One to three, closed early.

Five stakes in the ground, and he knows where every one of them is now, because he watched every one go in. So now he knows what Marek's going to build on. He had confirmed them. The book told him to take them, and he was the best reader his house had produced in a generation, and the best reader believes the book.

He had taken six contacts on them in the second exchange as well, and every one had been a strike he had chosen to take so that he could charge for it, and the bruising that redirect work always left behind would be going deep now, under the skin, into the muscle.

He did not look at the rail. He looked at Marek.

Marek changed his approach twice in the third exchange. Either change would have won him most bouts at any tier, and Cael thought both were the best work he had seen anybody do at Copper all year.

First he changed his rhythm.

He stopped arriving on the beat Brom had priced and began arriving round it, early and then late, so that the tolls Brom had set were set for a man who was no longer coming at that moment. It was beautiful. It worked once. Marek slipped a touch in between two of Brom's prices, off the beat, and the flag went up for Rhagen.

Nought to one.

Then Brom re-priced.

It took him two contacts. He did not try to follow Marek's new rhythm. He stopped pricing the moments and priced the angles instead, so that it did not matter when Marek came, only where, and on the second contact Marek came out of his touch into a forearm that had been waiting for him at the only exit the angle allowed.

One to one.

So Marek did the bravest thing Cael saw him do all morning. He put the five stakes aside, every one of them, and went surveying again.

It was the right decision and it was a terrible one.

He went looking for new structure, fresh gaps nobody had priced, in light, quick, unflagged contacts all over Brom's body. But every question asked of a wall that hits back has to be paid for, and Brom was hitting back. Every light contact Marek made bought a heavy one in return. And the heavy ones had been adding up since the first exchange. Cael could see it in him now. He was slower by a hair. His reset came a shade late. His step away was half a step short. All the precision of him, the whole fine instrument, was running now against a cost that rose with every contact, and the cost was beginning to show.

Brom's return caught him on the shoulder as he came out of a survey. Two to one.

And then Marek found something.

Cael saw it happen and did not understand it until afterward. It was a sixth gap, somewhere in the way Brom breathed, a moment at the end of an exhale when his whole frame softened for a breath before it set again. Marek touched it, lightly. It was real. Cael could tell from the way Brom's eyes changed that it was real and that nobody had ever found it before.

Brom's answer to that touch was the hardest thing he threw all day. Brom's forearm came across and took him full on the chest as he came out of the touch, and he went back three strides across the seam onto the stone and very nearly sat down. The flags did not go up for that one; it had landed a hair after Marek's own touch and the panel let both lie. But Marek stood on the stone for a moment with his hand on his chest, breathing, and looked at Brom.

And he did not go back to the sixth gap.

He had found it.

It was real. His whole doctrine said to take it. And he stood there and looked at what it had cost, and added that to everything else the morning had cost, and Cael saw him decide that it was not worth the price. It was the first time all tournament Cael had seen Marek decline anything he had found.

He went instead to the oldest gate he had, the half-beat after Brom's drive, the first one he had ever staked, and paid for it as he had paid for it every time before. Brom's return took the third point.

Three to one, closed early. Seven to six on the bout.

Before the fourth there was a short interval, and the main floor hummed through every breath of it.

Cael spent them watching two men stand still.

Marek stood very still on his chalk. Cael knew that kind of stillness; it was the stillness of somebody adding up a column and not liking the total. The survey was spent. The gaps were priced. The cost of the whole morning was in his body now where anybody who knew how to look could see it: a slight roll in the shoulder Brom had hit, a breath that ran half a count too long. And Marek did what Cael had seen him do between every exchange of every bout he had charted. He walked his margins. His eyes went to each of Brom's gaps in turn, one after another, checking each was still where he had left it. The half-beat after the drive. The pivot. The board under the heel. The start of the left turn. The breath.

All of them except one.

Through the whole bout it had been the cheapest gap on the floor. Cael, going back through his charts that night, would find that Brom had priced it a fraction lighter than every other, all morning, from the first exchange to the third, so that it read not as a price at all but as a mistake in Brom's pricing, the one door he had forgotten to charge for.

Marek did not check it. It was the one he was sure of.

High in the west tower, behind the carved stone screen of the fourth floor's long room, Chief Adjudicator Umber had left his desk and come to the window for the fourth exchange.

He did not often come.

He had a great deal of paper on his desk, as he had every morning of a cycle, and most of the bouts on the floors below would be rated by his panels and posted on his boards without any need for him to see them. He had asked to be told when the Copper final reached its last exchange. The senior clerk had told him. He had put down his pen and come.

He stood at the screen with his hands behind his back and looked down through the carved stone at the square on the grey floor and the two men in it.

He knew the bout.

He had read the panel's slates for the first three exchanges as they came up, as he read every slate of every final, and he knew what the five had marked. He did not need the slates to know what had happened. He had sat on panels for longer than either of those two young men had been alive, and before that he had fought, a long time ago and not well, and he could read a floor.

What he loved about the floor below him this morning was not the touches. It was the honesty of the work.

The big one was building something.

Umber had seen it in the second exchange, and been sure of it in the third. Every gap the small one found, the big one had left open and set a price on, and the prices were exact, a fair charge for every one. Except one. One price on the board had been a fraction low all morning, the first gap, high on the right shoulder. A careless eye would read that as the big one's mistake. Umber did not have a careless eye. He had spent forty years training it out of himself.

It was not a mistake.

It was a structure. It had been set in the first exchange and kept true through the second and the third, under pressure, while the big man's forearms were being beaten black, and not once had he let the price on that one gate drift up to match the others.

Umber thought it was very beautiful.

He had come to think, over the years, that the mark and the craft needed each other more than either side liked to admit. A mark with nothing honest in front of it was only a ruler lying on a table. And craft with no true mark to answer to was a man telling himself stories in an empty hall. The tables in the strong room were worth their seals because, cycle after cycle, somebody walked out onto a floor like this one and did a thing well enough to be worth measuring exactly.

He did not think of it as a trick, though he supposed the tiers might. A trick was a thing done once to a man who did not know. This was a thing built in plain sight, for three exchanges, by a man who knew that the best reader on the floor was reading every line of it. The small one could have seen it. Everything he needed to see it had been in front of him all morning. That was what made it honest, and what made it craft. The whole of the big man's answer had been laid out on the floor where anybody could read it, if only they had read the price instead of the gap.

Umber had spent his life in the service of a mark that rated what was demonstrated. Not what was meant, or felt, or hoped for, or claimed. What a body did on a floor in front of five judges. He had heard young officials call that a cold rule. It had never seemed cold to him. It seemed to him the only rule under which a man like the big one down there could be paid in full for a thing like this, built over an hour, in silence, with his own arms.

The five below him would rate it as it was. Umber did not doubt that for a moment. He had struck one of them from the lists a week ago for a quarter of a point of kindness, and the four who were left and the one who had replaced him knew it.

The bell went for the fourth exchange.

He watched the small one test two of the dear gates quickly at the start and pay for both. He watched him stand, a point from the end of everything, and look at the floor, and choose. Umber had seen that look on a great many faces at the end of a great many bouts. It was the look of a fighter who needs one thing to be true and has found the one place on the floor where it still seems to be.

The small one went to the right shoulder, the cheap gate, with everything he had left.

Umber watched it close.

He did not move at the screen. He did not smile. When it was done he stood a moment longer looking down at the oak, and at the two men on it, one standing and one getting up, and then he turned away from the window and went back to his desk and his paper. But the senior clerk, who brought him his tea a little later, noticed that he had not picked up his pen again, and that he was sitting with his hands folded on the blotter, looking at nothing in particular, in the way he did perhaps once a cycle.

From the rail, Cael saw it the way the tiers saw it, and then the way only a handful of people in the building could.

Marek tested the timing gate and paid for it: one to nothing. He tested the pivot and paid again: two to nothing. Nine to six on the bout, and Cael saw Marek understand the arithmetic as plainly as if the clerk had shouted it. One more point to Brom, and the exchange would close at three, and the bout would close with it, because no fighter on earth could draw level from four points down with a single exchange left. Marek had to score, and he had to score now.

Every gate on the floor was dear. The morning had made them dear. Patience was dear too, because every breath he waited the cost in his body rose.

Except one.

He went to the right shoulder.

He went in full, at last, his first full commitment of the whole bout, low across the seam from the stone with everything he had left behind it, at the cheapest gap on the floor, the one he was sure of, the one he had not needed to check.

The gap shut on him like the lid of a strongbox.

For three exchanges Brom had let that shoulder go cheap, so that at the very end somebody would pay full price for it. Now somebody had. Everything the morning had paid into Brom's forearms, every toll and every return and every blow he had chosen to take, came out of them at once, at the one angle he had kept cheap all morning precisely so that it would be taken. The redirect was complete. Marek met it with his whole weight committed and nothing held back, and it lifted him off the stone and laid him down on the oak on his back, flat, with a sound the whole floor heard.

The flag went up. All five of the panel's flags went up, all at once.

"Three to nothing," called the clerk. "Ten to six on the bout."

Brom stood over the oak, breathing.

His forearms hung at his sides, and Cael could see, even from the rail, that they were shaking a little. He had absorbed six clean scores and a great many other contacts that morning in order to sell one number. He rolled his right shoulder once, slowly, the shoulder Marek had touched first and touched last, and stood still.

Marek was on his feet before the flags had come down.

"What did he say?" said Lira.

Brom sat down on the bench behind the rail, heavily, and let Gault take his left arm.

I'd written it to be read that way." He winced as the cold water went on. "And he said he'd never had a page forged on him before. He sounded pleased about it. Then he shook my hand."

He looked down at his forearms, which were already going a deep dull colour from wrist to elbow, both of them, under Gault's careful hands.

"He found six," he said. "I knew about four." He let out a long breath. "He's the best inspection I've ever had."

Nobody at the rail said anything for a moment.

Then Ephram said, "Brom. You're continental champion."

Brom looked up at him as though this had not occurred to him.

"Am I," he said. And then, after a while, slowly: "So I am."

*Brom sold Marek one false line today,* Cael wrote that night, after the ceremony and the letter and everything else. *One gap, priced a fraction light, held true for three exchanges so that the best reader at his tier would read it as a mistake. Everybody who understood it called it the finest piece of craft at Copper this cycle, and I think they were right.*

*I sat at the rail and thought about my own record. Every figure on it is a price I set. Every exhibition I've fought this year, I've fought so that the paper would read a certain way. Brom forged one line, once, in the open, for one bout, and then shook the man's hand and told him exactly what he'd done. Mine is the whole file, every day, and I can't tell anybody.*

*Marek said he'd never had a page forged on him before, and sounded pleased. I don't think anybody who reads my file is ever going to get to say that.*

# Chapter 38 — The One Who Arrived

Ilsev signed the validation for the Copper final in the first seat of the Compact's row, a quarter of an hour after the figures went up, with the main floor emptying beneath her.

It was a plain form. The registry required one for every bracket title at a continental sitting, and the evaluation seat signed it, and it went into the registry's books under the fighter's name and stayed there for good. She had signed perhaps forty of them across her years in the seat. She had never once had to stop and think about one.

*Bracket: Copper. Result: title. Practitioner: Brom, Halcenvane, Iron Skin. Registry tier at entry: Copper, Rank One. Figure: 25.*

She held the pen above the line and did not let it down.

Twenty-five was a strong Iron figure. Everybody on the floor who could read a board knew it, and so did she. It sat in the same band as half the Iron semifinalists of the cycle. And the semifinal before it had been twenty-four, and the bout before that had gone five exchanges against a man the gallery-sheets called buried, and the line on the young man's certificate, the line the registry had written and the registry stood behind, said Copper.

The form had a box for the registry tier, and a box for the bracket in which the title had been won. It had no box for a difference between them.

Ilsev had spent her working life inside forms that said exactly what they were built to say and nothing else, and she had always thought that a virtue in them. A form did not guess. She did not guess either. She looked at the line for a moment longer, and she thought, quite clearly, that the registry had priced this man wrong, and had gone on pricing him wrong through a whole season of evidence, and would go on doing it until somebody with the standing to say so said so in the proper place.

That was a matter for the advancement review. It was not a matter for this form.

She wrote *Copper* in the bracket box, because Copper was where the title had been won, and initialled it, and dated it, and sanded it. The pricing error went into the registry exactly as large as it was, at the tier it had been won at, where anybody who cared to look would one day find it.

She squared the form on her lap-desk and set it with the others.

Along the row to her left, Havel was writing something small in the margin of his attendance sheet. Beyond him the other seats were emptying for the midday interval. At the end of the row the twelfth chair stood empty behind the grey rail-cloth, as it had stood every day of the sitting, with its pale square and its two empty screw holes. Ilsev did not look at it. She had a referral of her own still pending somewhere above her desk, the second she had ever filed on a single matter, and it had taught her a great deal about the difference between what a record said and what a record was for.

She took up the next form.

---

Ternhall came to the second floor in a body.

Cael had never seen a house do it for a quarterfinal. Houses brought their delegations to finals, and to the Gold, and on the last day they all came to everything. For the bracket rounds they sent a coach and whoever was free. But at the third bell on the ninth day, while Karis was still warming up in the hall behind the second floor, Ternhall's whole delegation came up the stair in russet and filed onto the tiers behind the western rail, forty or more of them, and sat down together in a solid block.

At the centre of the block sat a grey-haired man in a russet coat with a chain of office across it.

"Their chancellor," said Karis, when she came out to the rail and saw him. She looked at him for a moment, as she looked at a line of text. "He doesn't come to quarterfinals. Ever. He's not here for her. He's here for the doctrine. It's on that floor this afternoon as well, and he knows it."

She went back to finish warming up.

The second floor was full again, though not as full as it had been for Lira. The broadsides had found the story the day before, *former partners*, and the city liked the shape of it. The light had changed since the morning. The sun had gone round to the west, and it came down through the high glass now in long pale panes that lay across the eastern half of the floor and moved, very slowly, as the afternoon went on, so that a fighter standing still would feel the warmth come onto her shoulder and go off it again.

The dais stood against the south rail, as it always did, with its five chairs.

The Halcenvane rail was crowded. Brom had come straight from the main floor and Gault's bucket, and stood with both his forearms wound in wet linen from wrist to elbow, holding them a little away from his body. Lira stood beside him in her coat with the heat-wrap under it. Ephram had his hands on the rail. Rooke had his arms folded. Higher up, in the first row of the upper gallery, Withrow had taken a seat, and Cael saw that it was four places along from Ternhall's chancellor, and that she had not looked at him once.

Before the bout Cael went back to the warm-up hall behind the second floor, because he had the chart of Ivenne's two bouts in his pocket and thought Karis might want it.

She was sitting on a bench with her eyes shut and her hands loose on her knees. She opened her eyes when he came in, and looked at the notebook in his hand, and shook her head.

"No," she said. "Thank you. But no."

"It's only the two bouts."

"I know what it is. If you give it to me I'll fight it. I'll fight your chart of her, and she'll fight Ternhall's chart of me, and it'll be two pieces of paper on that floor and nobody else." She shut her eyes again. "Keep it. Write in it afterward. I'd like to read what happened."

He put the notebook away. At the door he looked back. She had not moved, and he could not have said, from her face or her hands or anything else about her, what she meant to do when the flags went up. He did not think she could have said either.

Ivenne came out first.

Cael had charted her twice from the gallery and had never been this close to her. She was compact and quick-handed, a little shorter than Karis, with dark hair pinned flat and a face that was very still. She went straight to the western mark, where the floor was in shadow, so that the light from the glass would be behind her when she turned. That was Ternhall doctrine. It had also been Karis's own habit, all the time Cael had known her, on every floor with a window.

Karis came out after her and took the eastern mark, with the light falling across her face.

She was taller than Ivenne and stiller, and she walked out without looking at the russet block at all. Cael had stood at a great many rails beside Karis. He had seen her go into bouts with a plan in every pocket and a page in her head for every exchange. This afternoon, so far as he knew, she had nothing. She had burned it on the common-room coals the night before and written nothing to put in its place.

She stood on her mark and looked across at Ivenne, and Ivenne looked back.

Cael had expected them to say something at the centre flag. They did not. They stood a stride apart while the referee went through the formalities, and neither spoke, and the silence between them had the whole of Ternhall in it.

The flags went up.

---

The first exchange belonged to the file. It is very hard to beat somebody who has read your answers before you have written them.

Karis began by building, because she had no choice about it. Her fire came only at the point of contact, through the hand, and without the frame round it each contact would run its heat back up her own arm instead of down into the floor. So she laid her anchors in the doctrine's own order, as she had laid them since she was a first-year. The first went down at her leading foot. The second went a stride ahead, on the line through the centre of the floor. The third would go to the flank.

The third never went anywhere.

Ivenne was across the centre line and inside the frame before it was finished. She arrived at the second anchor at the exact instant Karis planted it, when Karis's weight had committed to the foot and could not be taken back, and touched her on the hip, and was out again into the shadow on the western side before Karis's hand had come round.

Nought to one.

It went on like that. Twice Karis began an ignition, and twice Ivenne's counter arrived before the contact, not answering it but already there, like somebody keeping an appointment she had made a week before. Every one of Karis's tells paid out exactly as Ivenne had written that it would. When Karis's weight settled onto her back foot before a contact, Ivenne was moving. When Karis's eyes went to the floor where her third anchor would go, Ivenne was already standing on the place. Cael saw it all from the rail and wrote it down, and the writing felt like taking notes at somebody's trial.

Cael tried, from the rail, to see Karis's tells as Ivenne saw them, and found that he could. He had watched Karis for two years and never charted her the way he charted strangers, because there had never been any need. Now he saw what Ternhall had written down. The half-beat settle before every contact. The small glance at the floor. The breath. They had been there all along, plain as a signature, and he had read past them every day as a man reads past his own name.

Nought to two. Nought to three, closed early.

The gallery made a low sound. The craft-readers had come to see a story about two partners, and they had seen instead a fight conducted entirely against a woman's own paperwork, with the woman present only to sign it.

Cael wrote one word in the margin of his chart. *Flawless.* He meant Ivenne, and the file, and the two of them together, and he meant it as the most honest word he had.

Karis walked back to her mark. She did not square her shoulders. She did not reset her stance or run her hands down her wrists or do any of the small things Ternhall's paired students did between exchanges, any of the things that were in the file. She stood on her mark in the moving light, quite still, with her eyes half shut.

Cael knew what she was doing, because he had done it himself a thousand times. She was filing. Behind her eyes, he knew, a notebook lay open, and what had just happened to her was going into it, not as a loss but as a page of measurements of the instrument that had just been used on her. She was finding out exactly how good Ternhall's file was, by having it used on her.

"Three down," said Ephram, very low.

"She knows," said Lira.

When the flags went up for the second exchange, Karis came off her mark without the lattice.

The Halcenvane rail went quiet all along its length, because they knew what they were watching, and nobody else in the hall did. The file had a line in it about this, the cruellest line on the third page, and Ivenne had written it. *Pressed for time, she goes back to shapes she has already written down. She does not invent well, knows it, and declines to try.* Ivenne was standing twenty feet away, waiting on that line. If that sentence was still true, she had already lost.

Every one of them at the rail believed it had once been true. The only question on the floor was whether it was still true now.

At first the gallery did not understand what it was seeing. To the benches it looked as if Karis had simply refused to be an Ember fighter at all. Ivenne could make nothing of it either, and that was the whole of the exchange.

Her counters went where the file sent them, on time, every one, to the places where the file's Karis would be. The Karis on the floor was not in any of them. She had built nothing, so there was nothing to arrive inside before it was finished. She had anchored nothing, so there was no planted foot to catch. She went about the floor plainly on her feet, and when she made a contact she made it in passing, on the move, with no frame round it at all.

Cael saw the first raw ignition go in and saw what it cost.

Karis met Ivenne's counter where the counter was going, not where it had come from, with her palm on Ivenne's forearm at the moment the forearm arrived, and lit it. The flag went up for Halcenvane. And Cael saw Karis's hand jerk back from the contact as it ended, as a hand jerks back from a kettle, because there had been no planted foot to carry the heat down into the boards, and so the heat had gone the only other way it could go, back into the heel of her own palm.

One to nothing.

She did it again, with the other hand, a little later, on the dais side of the floor, where the counter was headed, and the flag went up again. Two to nothing. She did it a third time in the moving light on the eastern side, where anybody with the glass at her back would see no shimmer off a palm until the palm had landed; that one came in the same breath as Ivenne's counter, and the panel gave neither. Every one of them was single, unanchored, unplanned, set down where Ivenne was going to be and not where any doctrine said a contact belonged.

And Cael, at the rail, began to recognise what she was doing.

He knew those pieces. He had watched Karis write every one of them down, for two years, in her small square hand, about other people.

The motion she was using was Lira's: never a step that bought only one thing, every yard of floor paid for twice. Her open gaps were Brom's. She was leaving herself unguarded in three places on purpose, and putting a price on each, so that when Ivenne's file sent a counter to take an opening, the opening cost more than it gave. And underneath it all was the thing she had watched the whole circuit do all year, from a hundred gallery rails, and charted, and never once done herself: holding nothing back. Not a contact. Not a step. Not a plan.

She had never rehearsed any of it. Cael was as sure of that as he was of anything. He had sat across a table from her for two years and seen every page she wrote, and these were notes about other people. They had never been notes about herself. They had never been practised on any floor he knew of. And yet here they were, coming out of her hands in an order nobody had written down, least of all Karis.

Ivenne got one. It was a good one, a clean counter that Karis took on the shoulder because she had chosen to stand in the way of it rather than step round it, so that her palm could find Ivenne's wrist on the way out. The flag went up for Ternhall.

Then Karis took the exchange with a fourth contact, the plainest of them all, a palm on the forearm as Ivenne's counter came through the place she had just left.

Three to one, closed early. Three to four on the bout.

The sound of the craft-readers changed. Cael heard it change, a low murmur becoming a different low murmur, and in the russet block behind the western rail, the grey-haired chancellor had stopped writing in his book.

---

Ivenne adapted, and Cael's chart gave her every bit of her due, because it was brave and it was right.

She put the file down. Cael saw her do it. She came off her mark for the third exchange and her counters stopped going to the places on Ternhall's map. She began instead to read the woman in front of her from nothing, like a stranger met on a floor in a strange town. It was the right fight. It was the only fight left. And Ivenne was good at it, far better than Cael had feared, because Ternhall's paired doctrine was a doctrine of reading, and its best student could read.

She forced two real contests in the first minute. Both of them were honest, and both of them cost Karis. The first was a counter Karis had to take instead of getting ahead of, and Cael saw her left wrist turn under it, badly, and saw her hold the hand close for a breath afterward. The second was a feint that would not have fooled the Karis in the file and nearly fooled this one.

Ivenne took both.

Cael watched how she did it, and admired it even while it frightened him. She had stopped looking at Karis's feet, where Ternhall had taught her to look, and was watching her hands, because the hands were where this new Karis did everything. Twice Karis reached for one of the borrowed pieces, a door left open at a price or a step that bought two things, and found Ivenne already half a beat into reading it. Not all the way. Half. It was enough to make each borrowed piece cost Karis a little more than the time before, and Karis had very little left to spend.

Karis got one back, raw, in passing, off Ivenne's forward knee, and pulled her hand away from it with her teeth set.

Ivenne took the third with a long, patient entry from the west, out of the shadow, that Karis read a fraction too late because her eyes were full of light.

One to three, closed early. Four to seven.

"She's reading her cold now," said Rooke. He had not spoken since the bell.

She was learning a stranger, Cael thought. That was what it came to. And a stranger takes exchanges to learn, and there are only five in a bout.

Ivenne had spent one exchange on the file and one on finding out that the file was wrong. She had three left, at most.

The fourth was the hardest piece of fighting Cael had ever watched Karis do.

She was paying for everything now. Every raw contact went back into the heels of her hands. Her left wrist was weak, and she was keeping it back and leading with the right where she could. And Ivenne was reading her, not well yet, but better every minute, closing the distance between the woman in front of her and the woman she was learning, a line at a time.

So Karis kept moving faster than the reading could follow. She did nothing twice. Every contact she made came from somewhere the last one had not, and every gap she left open she left open somewhere new, so that each time Ivenne learned a thing about her it was already out of date.

The second came from a corner she did not build, only walked past at the right moment, as Lira walked past corners on the oak. The third she took in the moving light on the eastern side, with the sun coming down through the glass behind her and Ivenne squinting into it, and it was nobody's piece at all. It was simply hers, and she had not known she had it until it was done.

She was breathing hard. He had never seen her look so tired, not after a night of reading, not after a week of the charter.

He did the other sum then, the one nobody posts on a board. He knew how much invention a person could do in a day before it ran out, because he had been doing it himself, every day, for as long as he could remember. And he could see that Karis had very little left.

The fifth exchange went quickly, and then all at once it did not.

Karis took the first point raw, off Ivenne's shoulder, and Ivenne took one back. Then Karis took another, and the board stood at nine each, level, with the next touch to decide the bout.

And Cael saw Karis run out.

Up in the russet block, the chancellor was leaning forward with both hands on his knees. At the Halcenvane rail, Brom had taken his forearms out of their linen without noticing and was gripping the rail with his bruised hands. Nobody on the second floor was sitting down.

He saw it in her feet. They slowed. They found the floor the way tired feet find a floor, by habit, by the deepest groove a body has, and Karis's deepest groove had been cut at Ternhall, on the long floor, in her first year, against the girl standing across from her now. Her leading foot went down where a first anchor goes. Her weight followed it, and the second anchor went down a stride ahead, on the line through the centre of the floor.

She was building the lattice. She was too tired not to.

Ivenne saw it at once. Every line of the file came back into her body at the sight of it, and Cael saw her counter commit, fast and sure and full, toward the third anchor, toward the flank, toward the place where the file promised the frame would close.

Karis's eyes went to the floor where her third anchor belonged.

Cael saw it happen and could never afterward have said how long it took. It was less than a beat. In the middle of her own doctrine, in the middle of a stroke she had made a thousand times, Karis felt the file fighting with her, felt Ivenne's counter arriving at the place her own body was about to take her, and broke the stroke off. The third anchor never went down. The frame was left standing open with two sides of it built, like a sentence broken off before its last word.

Ivenne's counter went into the flank, to where the end of the lattice should have been, and found nothing there.

Karis's right palm came from inside the open frame, from the middle of the unfinished thing, at an angle no page on earth had ever recorded, and met Ivenne's ribs.

The ignition went in. The flag went up.

Ten to nine.

Ivenne crossed the floor to her.

She held out her hand, and Karis looked at it, and then at her own, and gave her the back of her wrist instead, because she could not have closed either palm round anything. Ivenne took the wrist without a word. Her grip was entirely correct. Her face was working through some calculation of its own that had nothing to do with the flags.

They let go. Ivenne walked back toward the russet block, where the chancellor sat with his book shut on his knee. Her own coach met her at the rail and began talking at once, quickly and low, with his hands; Ivenne listened to none of it. She stood with her back to him, looking across the floor at the place where the unfinished lattice had been, as though it might still be standing there and she might still learn something from it.

The figures went up eleven minutes later. Ivenne twenty-five. Karis twenty-four.

Karis came up the steps to the rail with her hands held out in front of her, palms up, and Gault was there with cold water before she reached the top. She sat down on the bench beside Brom, and the two of them sat there side by side with their hands and arms in wet linen, looking at each other.

"Well," said Brom.

"Well," said Karis.

The verdicts came in along the rail, one after another, as they always did after a bout, each in the voice of the person who gave it.

Rooke's came first, and it was short. "Nobody taught her that," he said. "Nobody could have. So nobody's going to pay for it but her." He looked at her hands. "She'll pay for it tonight, and she'll pay for it at the next one. It's the first bill of hers I've been glad to see."

Brom's came next, slowly, while Gault wound his forearm. "She left three doors open in the second exchange and charged every man who walked through one," he said. "That's my trade. I want it noted." He considered his own wrapped arm. "I'm invoicing Ternhall."

Lira laughed, and then winced and held her shoulder, and laughed again.

Ephram had been quiet. He leaned in over the rail now with his eyes on Karis.

"The last one," he said. "The lattice with the end left off. Tell me she planned it. Please. Tell me there's a page somewhere with that on it, so I can buy it."

They all looked at Cael. He was the one with the chart.

"No," he said.

He had charted it as it happened, and he knew it the way he knew his own hand.

"She didn't plan it. I watched it come. She started the lattice because she was tired, and it's the deepest thing she's got, older than any of us. Halfway through, she felt Ivenne's counter coming for the end of it, and she stopped. She broke her own stroke off in the middle." He looked at Karis, sitting on the bench with her hands in the bucket. "It wasn't a trick, like Brom's this morning. Brom built his for three exchanges. Hers didn't exist until she did it. It can't be scouted because it wasn't decided."

Karis did not say anything. She sat with her hands in the cold water and looked at the floor where she had stood, and after a while she nodded, once, as she nodded at a finding she meant to keep.

Lira had come along the rail to sit on her other side. She reached down without asking, lifted Karis's right hand out of the bucket by the wrist, and turned it over to look at the palm. The heel was red and blistering. Above it, where the fingers met the palm, the ink of last night's line was still just there, half of it cooked away by Karis's own fire, so that only the last words were left: *in her.*

Lira looked at it for a while. Then she put the hand gently back in the water.

"Found the ending, then," she said.

"Some of it," said Karis. "I'll find the rest at the next cycle."

*She did in one exchange this afternoon what I've done every day since I can remember,* Cael wrote that night. *She went out on a floor as somebody no file had a page for. It took everything she had. Both palms and her left wrist and the last of whatever it is that lets a person make things up as they go.*

*I've been doing it so long I'd stopped noticing that it costs anything. I watched it cost her, and I noticed.*

# Chapter 39 — Three Floors

The standings were read on the main floor at dusk, as they were read at the end of every day of the sitting, and on the ninth day there was a plaque to give.

The tiers were half empty. Most of the city had gone home to its supper, and the people who stayed were the ones who always stayed: coaches, clerks, the delegations with something on the board, and a few hundred of the faithful in the lower rows with bread and cheese in their laps. Lamps had been lit along the lowest tier. A clerk from the west tower stood at the centre of the floor beside a small table covered in grey cloth and read the day's results in a dry voice that reached the top of the bowl without any effort at all.

When he came to the Copper bracket he stopped reading and looked up.

"Continental Copper. Brom. Halcenvane."

Brom walked out across the grey stone to the table. His forearms were still wound in linen from wrist to elbow, and he carried them a little out from his sides. The clerk lifted the plaque off the grey cloth and gave it to him. It was a plain square of dark wood with a brass plate let into it, the tournament's bar-and-ring cut at the top and a line of small type underneath.

Brom took it in both hands.

He did not lift it over his head, or turn it to the lamps, or hold it up to the tiers. He held it flat in front of him at the height of his chest and looked down at it for the length of one long breath, with the whole floor watching him, the way a clerk looks at a document before he signs to say it has arrived.

Cael had seen Lira hold the Copper Crown like that, once, on the bluff. He had not expected ever to see it again.

Then Brom looked up at the tiers. He found the five of them in the third row, where they had come down to sit together, and nodded once. That was the whole of the performance. He walked back across the floor to the tunnel with the plaque held in front of him in his bandaged hands, and the faithful in the lower rows clapped him all the way, and he did not seem to hear them.

He carried it up the guesting-house stair the same way and set it on the long table beside his plate, upright, against the water jug, where it stayed all through supper.

"Does it eat?" said Ephram.

"Not yet," said Brom. "It's new."

The machinery came first, as it always did, and the meaning afterward.

The machinery came up the stair within two hours of the ceremony, in the hand of a Compact clerk in grey who would not sit down and would not take a cup of anything and would not leave until Seln had signed a slip to say the paper had been received. Seln signed it at the end of the table without looking at what he was signing, and the clerk went away down the stair at a pace just short of a run.

Then Seln read it, once, and laid it in front of Karis. "The office notes," he said, to nobody in particular, "that the review is mandatory. It does not note that anyone at the registry has yet read the bout." He went back to his file.

It was a registry notice. It said, in the registry's careful language, that a continental bracket title at any tier required a mandatory review of the titled practitioner's classification, and that the review was hereby noticed. The summons proper would issue from the registry's district seat, to be collected there by the practitioner or his house upon the delegation's return. Until the review was held, the practitioner's classification stood as entered.

Karis read it twice with her salved hands flat on the table on either side of it. Then she opened her notebook to a fresh leaf, wrote a heading, and turned the book round to show Brom before she closed it.

*Pending. Not a gift. A summons.*

"The registry's had you wrong for as long as you've been in its books. Now it would like you to come in and explain how."

Brom read the notice. Then he read her heading. He sat back with his wrapped arms on the table, and out of whatever place other men keep their grievances in, he brought only one sentence.

"Good. I'll bring the plaque."

The meaning came with the evening post.

It came in a plain envelope sealed with dark wax that Cael had seen only a handful of times, always on letters Brom took away and read by himself. Brom took this one away too. He went down to the courtyard with it, to the bench by the pump under the lantern, and was gone the usual length of time, and the table went on with its supper and did not look at the stair.

When he came back he had the envelope folded small in his fist. He went to his kit against the wall, opened it, and put the envelope in at the very bottom, beside nothing else Cael had ever seen him keep there. Then he came back and sat down in front of his plate and the plaque.

"Anything?" said Lira.

"Noted," said Brom.

He said it quite calmly, and went on eating.

The table had learned Brom's grammar over the years, and so it understood without being told roughly what a word like that was carrying. Nobody asked to see the letter. Nobody asked who it was from. That was the courtesy of the house, and the house kept it.

But late that night, as Cael was going up to bed, Brom stopped him on the landing. He stood there in the dark with his bandaged arms folded, looking out of the narrow window at the Concourse.

"You'll want it for your book. You always do. So you'd better have it right." He spoke the line once, flatly, in the voice he used for reading figures off a board. "*Grandmother watched the lists for your name. She found it.*"

Then he went on up the stair without another word.

*One line,* Cael wrote. *He gave it to me himself, on the landing, so I'd have it right. It doesn't say proud. It doesn't say come home. It says she looked, and it says she found him. Which means she was looking. Which means she was looking for a long time before there was anything to find.*

*The registry wants to sit him down and ask how it priced him wrong. A house in Velmere has already worked out the answer in ten words. Two notices in one evening, one from each instrument, and he didn't have to think for a moment about which one to trust.*

---

Withrow came up the stair late that same evening, when the plates had been cleared, which she seldom did. She sat down at the end of the long table with a cup of the guesting-house's poor wine and listened to Brom's plaque being discussed for a while, and said nothing until it had been discussed enough.

Then she turned to Karis.

"The seating office put me four places along from Ternhall's chancellor this afternoon. Nobody arranged it, so far as I know. The seating office has a sense of humour." She turned the cup in her fingers. "He had a book on his knee. He wrote in it all through your first exchange. He never opened it again after that. In the fourth exchange he turned to his coach. He didn't trouble to lower his voice, and half the row heard him say it: *we taught her none of that.*"

Nobody spoke. Karis had gone very still, with her hands in their linen on the board in front of her.

Withrow went on. "I have sat in a great many rows with a great many chancellors. When a student leaves a house like that one, they say she was difficult. They say she was never really theirs. I have never once heard one of them say what he said this afternoon, in that tone, about a student who walked out of his door." She drank a little of the wine and made a face at it. "He's wrong, of course. They taught her a great deal." She set the cup down and looked along the table at Karis. "They taught her all the parts. We taught her they were parts."

Karis looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked down at her hands. Cael thought, though he could not have proved it, that she was trying not to smile, and failing, and did not much mind failing.

After supper she went down to read the result on the boards, as Lira had the night before. Nobody asked her why. When she had been gone a quarter of an hour Cael went after her, to walk her home.

He found her alone under the lamps at the long east wall, in front of the Iron quarterfinals. Her hands hung at her sides. He stopped at the edge of the court, because somebody else was already crossing the stone toward her.

It was Ivenne. She came out of the dark from the russet end of the quarter, alone, in her house's off-floor brown, and walked straight to the board and stopped beside Karis. For a while neither of them said anything. They stood side by side and looked at the printed record rather than at each other, like two people reading the last proof of something they had written together.

Ivenne spoke first, without turning her head. "Ternhall's file on you is the best document I've ever worked from." She paused. "It was perfect through the first exchange."

"I know," said Karis. "I wrote most of it."

The silence went on for a long time after that. Cael stood at the edge of the lamplight and did not move. He thought the two of them were having a whole conversation inside it, about a long floor at Ternhall and a pile of notebooks traded every week and a door one of them had gone out through between one term and the next. None of it was said aloud. None of it needed to be.

"Whatever you transferred for," said Ivenne. She still did not look round. "Did you find it?"

"Yes."

There was no gap before it at all. Everything Karis had gone looking for was in that one word, and neither of them tried to add anything to it.

Ivenne nodded once, a short nod, like somebody closing a ledger she would be going back to for a long while. Then she turned and walked away into the dark the way she had come.

Karis stayed at the board a little longer. When she turned round and saw Cael she did not seem surprised. She came across the court to him, and they walked back through the quarter without speaking, and he kept to her left so that nobody passing in the narrow streets would brush against the bad wrist.

---

Her notebook got two entries that night. Cael saw both, because she left the second lying open on the long table when she went up, face up, beside his own book, where she knew he would be the first one down in the morning.

The first was the bill. She had told the rail she would pay in full and itemise it, and Karis always kept her word to an instrument.

*Quarterfinal. What it cost to fight without the frame. Both palms scorched at the heel, the right worse. A raw contact sends its heat back up the arm when there's no planted foot to take it down, which is exactly what the lattice is for; I knew it and did it anyway. Left wrist strained, from a counter I took instead of getting ahead of. And a tiredness unlike any bracket tiredness I have ever had, the tiredness of making things up while someone is hitting you. Ivenne's paragraph said I invent badly under pressure. Ivenne's paragraph was right about the pressure. By the end I had half an exchange of invention left. Half an exchange is exactly what I won by.*

The second was headed *Finding*, and the heading was underlined twice.

*A file is a likeness of a person on the day the file is finished. Ternhall's was a good likeness, and I beat it because I have gone on living since it was drawn. That is not a method. I can't teach it to myself, and I can't do it twice on purpose.*

*The second finding is the one that matters. I have charted Cael at every rail since Greyvane. Today was the first day I understood what it costs him that nobody can study him back. I left my own file behind for an exchange and a half, and it emptied me to the bottom. He has no file to leave. Every time he walks onto a floor he walks out as somebody nobody has ever written down, and he does it alone, and in all this time he has not once said in my hearing that it was hard. I have written "unreadable" in my notes about him as though it were luck. It is labour. Every day, unpaid, and with nobody to check the work. I ought to have seen it sooner.*

Cael read it at the table in the grey light before anybody else was down, and then read it again.

He had been studied all year. He had been charted and priced and protested and filed, compiled in shops and bought and sold in every meet town on the circuit, and none of it had ever touched him, because none of it had ever been right. He had come to think of that as the whole of his safety.

It had never occurred to him that it might also be the whole of his loneliness, until somebody who loved him had worked it out on paper and left it on the table for him to find.

He did not write anything under it. He closed her notebook very carefully and set it square on top of his own, and he sat there with his hand on both of them until he heard the first feet on the stair.

---

Karis's semifinal was on the afternoon of the tenth day, on the second floor, and she lost it.

Rooke offered to scratch her that morning. He did it quietly, at the end of the long table, with nobody else listening: her hands, he said, were worth more than a semifinal, and no one in the city would think a hair less of her. Karis heard him out. Then she said no, quite simply, and asked Gault for the salve.

Her opponent was Auremont's third Iron entry, a heavy, patient Force fighter whom the circuit called the lattice-breaker, because taking apart fighters who built things was what Auremont had made him for. He was very good at it. Auremont's own file on him said so, and Karis's map said so, and so did the record of every caster he had met that cycle. Each of them had built something in front of him and stood and watched him take it down.

Karis did not build anything.

Across the floor, in the front row of Auremont's block, Zerin sat among her housemates in her blue, watching the lattice-breaker warm up with the same flat attention she gave everything. Once, between his drills, her eyes went along the Halcenvane rail and stopped on Lira, and on the heat-wrap under Lira's coat, and stayed there for a breath. Then they went back to the floor. Lira, beside Cael, did not appear to notice. But she stood a little straighter for the rest of the afternoon, and held her left arm a little less carefully, and Cael thought that she had noticed perfectly well.

Karis came out with salve on both palms and the left wrist strapped, and she fought him as she had fought Ivenne in the second exchange, plainly, on her feet, with no frame and no plan, making her contacts in passing where his pushes were going to be. For an exchange it worked very well. He had never met a caster who would not cast, and he spent the first exchange pushing at structures that were not there, like a man shouldering a door that has already been taken off its hinges. Karis took two points off him in passing. He took one.

Two to one.

In the second he stopped pushing at nothing and began to test her, and he was methodical about it. He pushed at the places she walked past. He pushed at the gaps she left open at a price, and paid the price twice to see whether it changed. He pushed at the moment after each raw contact when her hand came back hot and she could not use it for a breath. He took three points doing it. She took one.

One to three. Three to four.

In the third she changed everything again, and it held for an exchange. She stopped leaving doors open at all and went back to Lira's economy, every step buying two things, and he could not get a third point off her before the bell. Neither could she. Two each.

Five to six.

Cael charted the interval before the fourth with more care than he had charted anything all week, because he could see what was coming and could do nothing about it. The lattice-breaker stood on his chalk with his head down, not resting, working. His coach did not speak to him. He did not need anybody to. He had spent three exchanges paying to learn a thing, and now he was adding it up.

Karis stood on her mark with her palms turned up and open, letting the air at them. Cael watched her look across at the big man and saw that she knew exactly what he was doing, and that knowing did not help. She had nothing left to change into. She had changed three times already that afternoon, and the day before, and the store she drew on was empty.

In the fourth he solved her, and Cael watched him do it and could name every step, which was very little comfort.

The lattice-breaker had spent three exchanges learning the economics of a fighter with no frame, and in the fourth he stopped paying any of her prices. He did not take her open doors. He did not chase the places she walked past. He simply set his pushes so that every contact she made had to come through one of them first. Each raw ignition reached him thinned by the push it had passed through, and cost her palm every bit as much as before. She was paying the whole price for half the heat. Her invention was spent, and she had no frame left to fall back into. He took three points in a row, steadily, without any hurry at all.

Nought to three, closed early. Five to nine. She could not have drawn level with the fifth, and it was over in four.

She gave him her wrist to shake, as she had given it to Ivenne. He bowed to her a little, which nobody from Auremont did, and said something that made her nod.

She came up the steps to the rail with her hands held out in front of her, and Gault had the water ready. The figures came up later: the lattice-breaker twenty-seven, Karis twenty-three.

"Well?" said Rooke.

"He was better," said Karis.

She said it at once, plainly, with nothing behind it. "Not because of my hands. He'd have beaten me on fresh hands too. One exchange later, maybe two, but he'd have done it." She looked down at the linen. "He beat the stranger. The stranger's a day old and doesn't know anything yet. He's had years."

She sat down on the bench, and Gault began to unwind the strapping on her wrist.

"Wait for her at the next cycle."

Cael wrote it at the rail exactly as she said it, and did not write anything after it. He did not think it needed anything.

---

Rooke wrote the standings onto the coaching sheet that evening at the long table, with all of them watching, as he had at the end of every meet of the year.

It took him some time, because there was a good deal to write. Ephram in the round of sixteen, to the lake house's Shield. Lira a semifinalist, to Zerin, four to nine. Karis the winner of her quarterfinal over Ternhall's best, and then a semifinalist, to the lattice-breaker, five to nine. And at the bottom, in Rooke's square capitals, Brom: *Continental Copper.*

Under all of it he wrote one more line, and then turned the sheet round so that they could read it.

*We've now lost to their third-best and their fourth-best, honorably, by margins. The team trial seeds us against the whole set at once.*

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Their whole set," said Lira.

"All of it," said Rooke. "On one floor. On the same day." He folded the sheet into the coaching file. "So eat your supper. Rest those hands, and that shoulder, and those arms." He looked down the table at what was left of his squad: one shoulder in a heat-wrap, two forearms in linen, two palms in salve, and one Blade with nothing whatever wrong with him. "I sent four of you onto three floors this week and I've got four of you back. That's all I ever ask of a week."

"I'm perfectly well," said Ephram.

"I know you are. You're the trial caller. I need one of you in one piece."

That night the crews began building the ring.

Cael heard it from his bed through the open window: hammering, faint, from the direction of the Concourse, and men's voices, and once the long scraping groan of something very heavy being dragged across stone. He lay listening for a while. Then he got up and dressed and went down the stair past the dark common room and out into the street.

The main floor's great doors stood open on the side away from the city. He walked in and stood at the end of the lowest tier.

The grey oval was full of lamps and men. The plugs had been drawn out of the ring of sockets in the middle of the floor and stacked by the dais, and in their places a crew in shirtsleeves was setting the first of the posts. They squared each one with a long level and drove its wedges home with a mallet while another man held the lamp. Barrows of timber stood about the stone. A foreman walked the circle with a measuring rod, stopping at every socket to look and nod.

In the middle of it all, untouched, lay the round of old dark oak where Brom had stood the day before.

A woman sat on an upturned barrow near the tunnel with a can of oil and a rag, wiping each plug as it came out of the floor before it went onto the stack. She looked up and nodded to Cael and went back to her work, and he thought of the steward on orientation day and the grandmother who had oiled these same plugs for forty years.

He stood and watched the ring go up for a long time.

He had been at the rail all week. He had watched three people he loved walk onto three floors and be read by the best files on the continent. Lira had met the fighter the program built and learned, to a tenth, exactly how far she was from Silver. Brom had let the finest reader at his tier take him apart line by line, on purpose, so that he could sell the one false line that mattered. Karis had met the woman who wrote her down and walked straight out of the writing. All three had been studied as hard as anybody could be studied. All three had come off the floor larger than they went on.

He had been the instrument for all of it. For a week nobody had looked at him at all, and he had liked it, and he had learned more about being studied in that week than in the whole year of being studied before it.

Now the ring was going up. In a day or two the galleries would come down to this oval and these posts, and Rhagen's captain would walk out through a gap in the barrier with a doctrine behind him that had been written down and sold and argued over for longer than anybody living could remember. He would set about finding the edges of Cael.

Cael did not know what the man would find. Nobody did. Nobody had ever been able to find out, and as of last night he knew what that had always cost.

The foreman finished his round and called for the next post. A man came past with a barrow of timber and asked Cael, quite politely, to mind his feet.

He stepped back, and found Lira standing behind him.

She had her coat on over her nightshirt and the heat-wrap tied on over the coat, which looked ridiculous, and she was holding her left elbow in her right hand. "I heard it too," she said. "Couldn't sleep. The shoulder talks."

They stood side by side and watched the crew set the next post. The foreman's rod went down into the socket, and came up, and he nodded, and the mallet began.

Lira tipped her head at the posts. "Fifty-odd feet. A bit longer one way than the other. You paced it."

"At the orientation."

"Of course you did." She watched the wedges go in. "You've had a week of watching us be read. Now it's you." She did not look at him. "I'll be at the rail. Charting. With one arm."

"I know."

"Make them write a long one," said Lira, and he heard her smiling in the dark, and they walked home together through the empty streets with the hammering going on behind them.

*Three floors this week,* he wrote, before he slept. *Three of us read by the best files on the continent. Lira lost to the program by a tenth and came home wanting more than she has ever wanted anything. Brom let the best reader alive read every line of him and won on the one line he'd forged. Karis walked out of her own file in front of the people who wrote it, and it cost her both hands.*

*I watched all three. Rooke says I was the best scouting the house had, and I believe him. But I learned more about myself at that rail than about any of them.*

*They're putting the ring up tonight, out of posts and rope and old wood, and it will be done by morning. The thing they're building it for is me.*

*Nobody can study me back. Karis wrote that down last night and left it on the table, and I've carried it round all day. I always thought it was the safest thing about me. Standing in the Concourse tonight watching them drive the wedges home, I think it might also be the hardest.*

*The ring will be ready when it's ready. So, I suppose, will I.*

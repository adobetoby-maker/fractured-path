# Chapter 40 — Property Four

The ring was finished by first light, and Cael went back to see it before anybody could stop him.

He had slept perhaps four hours. Lira had walked him home through the quarter at midnight and gone up to her own room with the heat-wrap still tied on over her coat, and he had lain in the dark listening to the mallets until they stopped, and slept when they stopped, and woken all at once when the window turned grey, as if somebody in the room had said his name.

The main floor's great doors still stood open on the side away from the city, and the crews were going home through them. They came out past him in twos and threes with their tools over their shoulders and their shirts dark down the back, talking in the flat, pleased voices of men who have done a night's work and done it right, and not one of them looked twice at him.

Inside, the bowl was empty except for the foreman, who stood in the middle of the floor with his measuring rod upright beside him like a shepherd's crook, looking at what he had built. Cael stopped at the end of the lowest tier and looked at it too.

It was a ring, but not in the way the bracket squares were rings, which were rope on iron stands, put up in an hour and taken down in less. This one had been built to stay for as long as anybody wanted it. The posts stood in the old sockets, one to a socket, squared and wedged. Between them ran a barrier of heavy planking, chest-high on a man, with a thick rope laid along its top for a coping so that a fighter thrown against it would meet rope before wood. At the north end and the south the barrier did not run straight. It stepped back from the line for a stride and then came on again, so that each gate was a shallow bay in the wall, a little pocket of floor in its own shadow.

In the middle of it all, untouched, lay the round of old dark oak.

Cael walked out to it, and nobody told him not to. The foreman watched him come and said nothing, and when Cael began to pace the ring he only shifted his rod to the other hand, as if he had expected it.

North to south it was fifty-two feet from the barrier to the barrier. East to west it was a few feet less. He walked both lines twice and wrote both figures in the notebook, under the figures he had written on orientation day from socket to socket, and they agreed, near enough, with what he had guessed.

Then he knelt by the oak and put his cheek nearly to the stone and looked along the floor toward the west barrier.

The floor fell away from him. It was not much, and if he had not been looking for it he would never have seen it, but the old stone ran down from the lip of the oak round to the foot of the planking in a long, smooth, even slope, and the planking stood on ground a little lower than the ground he knelt on, by about the thickness of his thumb.

He got up and tried the east side, and the north, and the south, and it was the same everywhere.

"It's crowned," he said.

"It is." The foreman had come up behind him. He was a short man with forearms like hawsers and a face that had been out in a great deal of weather. "Always has been. The first ring stood out in the court, before anybody thought to put a roof on this place. They laid the floor to throw the rain off to the edges." He tapped the stone with the foot of his rod. "Roof's been on two hundred years and more. Nobody's ever voted to change the shape. We patch to the old line. Easier than deciding."

"Does it matter?"

The foreman thought about it. "Not to me," he said. "Might to you. Anybody that's fought in it says it does." He looked at Cael properly then, for the first time, and Cael watched him put the face together with the broadsides. "You'll be the one, then."

"I'll be the one."

"Hm." The foreman shouldered his rod. "Well. It's true. I put the level on every post myself." He went off toward the doors and stopped halfway and called back without turning round. "Mind the gates. The stone's newer in the bays. It's quicker underfoot."

Cael went and stood in the north bay, and then in the south, and found that the man was right. The stone in each pocket had been laid in the last cycle or the one before. It was smoother than the old floor and a shade paler, and it gave his heel back a hair faster than the rest of the ring did.

He wrote that down too, with the rest of it.

*Fifty-two north to south, a few feet less across. Crowned, the oak at the top. Bays at both gates, a stride deep, the stone quick. Whoever stands on the oak stands highest. Everything runs downhill from there.*

He read it over, and then, because the ledger kept everything, he added one more line.

*Four bursts, probably. Stone.*

---

Rooke held the session at the second bell in the hired hall behind the east tower, with the door shut.

It was a bare room with a board floor that had been a wool-merchant's counting floor once and a long window over a yard full of barrels. Rooke had pinned two sheets of brown paper to the wall and written on both in his square capitals, and the book lay open on the trestle under them.

Every house on the continent owned a copy of Rhagen's book. Halcenvane's had come north in Rooke's trunk, and Ephram had read it twice on the road, and Rooke had read it, Cael suspected, a good many more times than twice over the last eight days. The spine was cracked now at a chapter near the middle, under the one word *Coverage*.

They were all there. Lira sat on an upturned crate by the window with her left arm in its wrap across her lap. Brom stood at the back with his forearms folded carefully, the linen yellowed at the edges. Karis had her notebook shut in her salved hands. Ephram sat on the trestle's end beside the book, as if he were guarding it. Gault had stayed outside the door, by agreement, with the travel case at his feet.

"Rhagen's book has a chapter on surveying," said Rooke. "I'll spare you the whole chapter. It says this." He put his finger on the left-hand sheet. "When a Rhagen fighter surveys you, he takes a reading every time you answer him. When he has ten readings of the same answer, he stops asking. He calls it a pattern, and he writes it down, and from then on he fights the pattern."

"Ten," said Lira.

"Ten. They are very exact people." Rooke looked at Cael. "So you'll give him eleven, one for good measure. Eleven times you answer his coverage the same way, out loud, where his whole delegation can see it: you move, you make him reach, and you burn it where it can be burned." He tapped the sheet. "Let him write down that your answer to structure is your feet and your fire. Your feet and your fire are on your sheet. Any panel on this continent can read them."

He turned to the right-hand sheet.

Four cards were pinned to it in a column, each in his capitals.

*ONE. IT COVERS GROUND. NOT A MAN.*

*TWO. WIDE COSTS HIM. NOT STRENGTH. ATTENTION.*

*THREE. A LAYER CAN BE BURNED AT CONTACT.*

*FOUR. IT RENEWS WITHOUT A RHYTHM.*

Round the fourth card he had drawn a box in red chalk, and under it, inside the box, he had written three more words. *No answer recorded.*

"That's the captain," said Rooke. "That's every Shield Rhagen has turned out in the last forty years, and he's the best of them in this city. One, two and three you can answer from your sheet. One, you move, because his guard is laid on the floor and not on you, and the floor doesn't follow you about. Two, you drag it wide, because a wide guard costs him something he can't see himself spending. Three, you put Karis's fire into it at contact, at your two a bout, at the rate the clerks already have on paper." He looked round the room. "And then there's four."

Nobody said anything.

"I've read four nine times," said Rooke. "Ephram's read it twice. Karis read it last night when she thought I was asleep."

"I read it once," said Karis. "It doesn't get longer."

"It does not." Rooke looked at the boxed card for some time. "The salt road had a clock. You found it in two exchanges and you broke it in two more. The Shield reserve had a clock, and when I shuffled it you found the shuffle. Every Shield you've ever met renewed his guard to a count, because that's what Shield is, at Iron, in the regions." He put his finger on the red box. "This one doesn't. That's what the book says. That's what Rhagen has spent forty years making. A guard that's always being renewed, all of it, a little at a time, everywhere, and never to a beat. There's no breath to read. There's no plant. There's no oldest pane. There's nothing to time."

"Then what do you do?" said Ephram.

"Against four?" Rooke said. "From his sheet, nothing. There isn't anything on his sheet that answers four."

He reached up and unpinned the fourth card, folded it in half and in half again, opened his coat, and put it into the inside pocket over his heart. Then he buttoned the coat. He did it slowly and without any show, the way he did everything, and when he had done it he laid two fingers flat on the outside of the pocket and tapped it, once, and looked at Cael.

Cael looked back at him.

He understood the tap perfectly. It was the nearest Rooke had ever come to saying it, which was not very near at all, and it said: *I know there is an answer to four somewhere. I know it isn't on your sheet. I have decided, for good, that I will never ask you where it is.* It said all of that from inside a buttoned pocket, where nobody in the room had to hear it.

"So here's the whole of my instruction," said Rooke. "Nothing goes on that floor that isn't on your sheet. Not one step. If the bout comes down to four — you lose the bout. In-band. On flags. And we go to dinner."

"Understood."

"Say it so I believe it."

"I lose the bout," said Cael. "And we go to dinner."

Rooke nodded. He looked at the three cards left on the sheet and the empty pins where the fourth had been, and something went across his face that Cael could not have named.

"I've drawn a lot of plans on a lot of walls," he said. "Never once drawn a loss on one and called it a good plan." He began to unpin the cards. "Season of firsts."

Lira caught Cael afterward on the stair down into the yard.

"He gave you a way out," she said.

"He gave me a dinner."

"Same thing. Better food." She had stopped two steps above him, so that for once she could look down at him without standing on anything. "Are you going to take it?"

"If it comes to four. Yes."

She studied him. She had been studying him since they were both children, and she still did it as if she expected to find something new.

"You're not afraid of losing," she said. "That's not it."

"No."

"You're afraid of what he'll see if you don't."

He thought about it, because it was Lira and she had asked properly.

"I'm afraid of what anybody sees," he said. "Rhagen charts every exchange and writes it all down and sells what it writes. Whatever I do on that floor this afternoon, every coaching room on the continent will own a copy by the next cycle." He looked up at her. "A year ago I'd have sat up all night working that out on my own, and burned the paper. This morning a coach worked it out in chalk in front of all of us, and put the answer in his pocket."

"And?"

"And I don't know what that is," Cael said. "I don't think I've got a word for it yet."

Lira came down the two steps and stood beside him.

"I have," she said. "It's called having a house." She put her good hand on his arm for a moment. "Go and eat something. Brom's ordered for you. He hasn't told anybody, but there's a plate in the common room with your name on it in gravy."

---

The main floor sold out by noon and sold out again by one, which ought not to have been possible, and by the time Cael came up the tunnel at the second hour after midday there were people standing on the stairs between the tiers and people standing along the top of the bowl under the high windows, three deep, and the stewards had given up trying to count them.

He had heard the noise from the tunnel, and it was not the noise of the brackets. A bracket crowd knew what it had come to see; it talked about seeds and draws and money, and roared when the flags went up for its own house. This was a different sound, lower and wider, like a harbour on a still night. Eight thousand people who had paid to see something they could not describe were talking about it to one another in the only voice they had for that, which was a voice for weather.

The Halcenvane rail was at the north bay, where Rooke stood at the gate with his arms folded and Gault had the bucket and the towels. The others had come down from the tiers to stand along the planking, and Cael saw as he passed that Lira had her chalk-case open on the rope and the charting sheet clipped into it, and her left arm still in its wrap.

"With one arm," he said.

"Told you," said Lira, without looking up. "Go away. I'm working."

Across the ring the Rhagen block sat together in the east tiers in their black and cold glass-grey, the whole delegation, every fighter and coach, with their books open on their knees. Cael had seen them do it at the open warm-up, all of them talking aloud at once as they worked, and he saw them doing it now, the soft busy murmur of a body of people who had come to take notes. Their head of delegation sat at the end of the row with his own book closed. The man who had signed under his captain's filing did not need to write anything down; he had people for that.

High above the east rail the Compact's row was full except for one chair. Ilsev sat in the first seat with her lap-desk squared, and Havel beside her. At the far end, behind the grey rail-cloth, seat twelve stood empty as it had stood every day of the sitting, with its pale square and its two empty screw holes, and a steward at the head of its stair.

Cael looked at it once and looked away.

On the dais at the south end the senior exhibition panel sat in its five tall chairs, the oldest judges in the city, three men and two women, and none of them looked as if anything that happened on a floor could surprise them. The referee stood at the oak in grey with a silver bar at his collar.

The captain came out through the south gate.

He was a man of thirty or a little more, of middle height and broad through the back, with close-cut hair going early to grey at the temples. He walked to his mark as if he had been walking to marks his whole life, which he had, and he did not look at the tiers, or at the Rhagen block, or at Cael. He bowed to the panel, a short bow from the waist, and then to the referee, and then he turned and bowed to Cael a little lower, as one craftsman bows to another before a piece of work.

Cael bowed back.

"Five exchanges at most," said the referee, in the dry voice of a man who had said it a thousand times. "Two touches ends the bout. A clean throw is a touch. If the touches are level at the close, the figures decide." He looked from one of them to the other. "Gentlemen."

The flags went up.

---

The captain did not move. He stood on his mark, a stride south of the oak, and his guard came up around him the way mist comes up off a river at evening: from no one place, from everywhere at once, without any sign that anyone had called it.

Cael had expected panes.

He had spent a cold term learning panes, and a morning in a salt store, and he knew exactly what a pane looked like to the read: a plane in the air where the floor's weight simply stopped, flat and sudden, like the edge of a table. He knew the breath that came before it and the plant of the back foot, and he had been watching for the breath since the flags went up.

There was no breath, no plant, no plane. There was depth.

The read showed it to him as something he had no picture for. The weight of the floor did not stop at the captain's guard. It thickened. It went on into the guard and grew slower and heavier and harder to see, layer under layer under layer, as the light grows heavier going down into deep water, until somewhere a long way in it was too heavy to read at all. It lay in front of the captain in a broad arc, chest-high, from his left hand round to his right and a little way behind him on both sides, and it did not stand still. All of it moved, all the time, very slightly, the way water moves when nothing is moving it.

Then the captain began to walk, and all of it came with him.

Cael gave him the first answer from the sheet. He moved.

He went right, along the arc, not fast, to see how the guard would follow. It followed, but it did not swing round as the panes had swung; it simply was, a moment later, where he was going, as though it had been there already. He went left, faster, and found the same.

He spent his first burst on the third probe, because the probes on foot told him nothing and he needed to know what the guard did to something fast. He burst from the stone at the captain's right side, twenty feet, low, and landed with the landing beat locking his hip, and put out his hand into the edge of the arc as he came down.

His palm went into it, and that was the strange part. It did not meet a wall. It went in as a hand goes into cold water, and slowed, and slowed again, and somewhere a few inches in it stopped going in at all, with no edge to strike and no surface to push against, only more of the same all the way down.

He took his hand out. Nothing had happened to it, and nothing had happened to the guard either; the place where his palm had gone in was already the same as the rest.

*Like throwing stones into a lake,* he thought. *The lake doesn't notice.*

The captain had watched it all without moving to meet the burst. He had simply let Cael come in and let him learn, and then he had taken one step forward, which brought the whole of the guard a step forward with him.

Now he began to survey back.

It was done like a clerk's audit. Every few seconds the captain did something to Cael: pressed the guard forward a foot on one side and watched what Cael did, let it fall back on the other and watched, stepped and waited and stepped. The intervals were even. They were so even that Cael, who counted everything, began to count them before he knew he was doing it, and found the count was steady, and understood after the fourth that the steadiness was not a weakness at all. It was a method. The captain was not trying to hurt him yet. He was asking him the same question, again and again, in the same voice, to see whether the answer changed.

The answer did not change, because it could not. Every answer Cael had was on his sheet, and his sheet was short. He moved, and the guard was there; he moved again, and the guard was there.

The ring was getting smaller.

He felt it the way a man feels a room grow warmer: not all at once, but by the time he noticed, it had been happening for a while. Every step the captain took toward him came with that great slow weight of guard, and every step he took away was a step toward a barrier. The ring had been fifty-two feet at dawn, and now, wherever Cael stood, the captain stood a little closer and the planking a little closer behind.

He tried the read on the captain himself, behind his guard.

That, at least, still worked. The man had weight like any man, and stood on his feet like a fighter who had stood on them every day since he could walk, and the read gave Cael his weight and his direction a fraction early, as it always did, as it had for every opponent of the year.

It was no use at all. The captain's weight told Cael where the captain was going, and nothing about where the guard was going to be, because the guard was not going anywhere. It was already everywhere it needed to be.

He was on the west side of the ring now, a stride from the planking, and the guard was in front of him in its broad arc, and on his left, and creeping round on his right. He had one way out, south along the barrier toward the south bay. He took it, on foot, quick, and the read showed him the captain's weight beginning to come round to follow, and he thought he had judged it well.

The guard had already come round. It was not the captain's hand that met him first but the guard, slowing him the way water slows a runner who has gone in to the knees, so that his next step came half a beat late. Then, inside that half-beat, the captain's right hand came out of the guard as a hand comes out of a sleeve, short and plain, and touched him on the left shoulder, and went back in.

The flag went up on the south side of the dais.

"Touch," said the referee. "Nought to one."

Cael walked back to the north bay, and the sound of the Rhagen block writing came across the ring like rain on a roof.

---

Rooke met him at the gate with water. He did not offer advice, because he did not have any.

"Well?" he said.

"Ten readings," said Cael. "He took ten readings in that one exchange. I counted. He asked the same question ten times and I gave him the same answer ten times." He drank. "I wasn't feeding his chart. I didn't have anything else to give it."

"I saw." Rooke took the cup back. "The clock?"

Cael looked across the ring. The captain stood on his mark with his hands at his sides, breathing easily, and his guard lay round him in its arc. Even at rest it was moving, renewing itself all over, a little at a time, everywhere, the way a river is never quite the same river. He watched it for the whole of the interval with the full gaze, harder than he had ever watched anything, and there was not one single moment in all of it when any part of it was older than the rest.

"There isn't one," he said.

He heard what he had said. All cold term he had worked against the Shield reserve on the sprung oak at home, every second day, a five-beat clock and then a shuffled one, until he could find the oldest pane in two exchanges with his eyes shut. He had broken the salt road's clock in a stone shed at the confluence with two points of heat. He had come home and written in his own hand *I think Shield is solved*, and he had believed it as late as this morning, walking the ring.

He had not solved Shield. He had solved a clock.

Nobody had told him there were Shields without one. Or rather, they had told him, Rooke and the book and a card in red chalk, and he had heard it as a harder clock. He had thought Silver would be Iron with more of everything in it, faster and thicker and better kept, a bigger version of the thing he already knew how to beat. It was not. It was a different thing.

"Silver is not a bigger Iron," he said, half aloud.

Rooke heard him. He did not say anything. He only nodded, once, slowly, the way he nodded when a fighter had come out of a bad exchange with a true sentence.

Behind Cael, at the rope, Lira had stopped writing.

"So the clock's gone," she said. "What's left?"

Cael looked at the ring: at the crown of it, where the oak lay dark and old at the top of everything, and at the long slope of stone running down to the planking on every side, and at the bay in the north barrier, a stride deep, where the stone was quick and new.

"One, two and three," he said.

"Will they be enough?"

"I don't know," said Cael, and the referee called them to their marks.

# Chapter 41 — A Handspan

He gave up the middle of the ring before the captain could take it from him.

It was the first thing he did when the flags went up for the second exchange, and the gallery did not understand it. He did not come forward to his mark. He went backward, off it, and then sideways, across the slope of the crown and down it, until he stood a stride from the west planking with the rope coping at the height of his ribs. A long murmur went round the tiers, the sound of eight thousand people deciding at once that the boy had been frightened by the first exchange.

The Rhagen block did not murmur. The Rhagen block wrote.

Cael let them. He had one, two and three, and the first of them said the guard was laid on the floor and not on him, and he meant to find out how far the floor went.

The captain came after him at his walking pace with the great slow arc of the guard in front of him. Cael waited until it was nearly on him, until the read could feel the floor's weight thickening a few feet from his chest, and then he went north along the planking, on foot, at a run.

That was the first relocation.

He did not run far. Fifteen feet, no more, to the place where the barrier began to bend in toward the north bay, and there he stopped and turned and waited again with his back to the wood. The captain turned to follow. His guard was there, as it had been there all through the first exchange, where Cael was going, a moment before he arrived.

But it was there downhill.

Cael saw it with the read because he had spent the morning looking for it. The captain walked on the crown, a little above him, and the guard he laid out in front of himself ran down the slope of the stone toward the barrier, and as it ran downhill it spread. It did not thin, exactly. It got wider, the way water poured on a tilted board runs wide, and the wider it got the more of it there was to keep, all of it renewing everywhere at once.

*Two,* he thought. *Wide costs him.*

He went south along the planking. That was the second.

He went north again, past the place where he had started, almost to the bay. That was the third. He used his feet for all three and kept his bursts, because the stone sold him four a day and he had already spent one of them learning to throw stones into a lake.

The captain followed every time, correctly. He did not chase. He did not try to cut Cael off against the barrier, which he could have done, at a price, and which Rhagen's book would have told him was a waste. He simply laid his guard along the planking wherever Cael was going, from the high ground above, and kept it fresh, and let Cael run himself out.

Cael ran the western arc of the ring as if it were a corridor, barrier to barrier, bay to bay. Four. Five. Six. On the seventh he burst, from the quick new stone at the mouth of the south bay, twenty feet north along the planking, and landed with the beat locking him against the rope, and on the eighth he burst again, back the way he had come.

The bursts did not beat the guard. Nothing beat the guard. But every time he went fast the captain had to lay the guard a long way downhill in a short time, and every time the guard ran downhill it spread, and the read showed it to Cael spreading, wide and patient and very slightly thinner at its far edge than it was at the captain's chest.

The captain was paying for every foot of it.

He could not see himself paying it. That was what the card had meant. The man was as strong at the end of the eighth relocation as at the start of the first, and his breathing had not changed, and he had not given an inch of the crown. But Cael, watching him from below, could see where the man's attention went. Every time the guard had to cover ground it had not covered before, a little more of the captain went into covering it. It showed in his eyes, which had begun to follow Cael's feet instead of Cael's chest. It showed in the way he had stopped auditing. There were no more even intervals now, no more questions asked in the same voice. The captain was not surveying any longer. He was keeping up.

The ninth relocation took Cael into the north bay itself.

He had not gone in before. The bay was a stride deep, a pocket of quick pale stone in the shadow of the barrier's step, and it was dead ground. Nothing could be laid into it from the crown without being laid round a corner. When he stepped into it, the guard came after him as it came after him everywhere, and he felt with the read how much of it there had to be, poured round the step in the planking and into the pocket to fill it, a great deal of guard for a very small place.

The tenth took him out again and south.

The eleventh took him back into the bay.

He went in hard, on foot, and stopped dead in the pocket's back corner, and the guard came pouring round the step after him as it had the time before. This time Cael did not wait for it to fill the pocket. He went out of the bay's mouth the moment the guard committed to the corner, straight up the slope toward the crown, toward the captain, into the place the guard had just left to come and find him.

For one moment the far edge of the guard was a long way downhill, round a corner, filling a hole Cael was no longer in.

He went through the place where it had been and touched the captain on the right hip with his open hand, and was out again down the slope before the guard came back.

The flag went up on the north side of the dais. Then a second flag, and a third.

"Touch," said the referee. "One to one."

The bowl made a noise Cael had not heard it make before. It was not a roar. It was lower than that, and it came mostly from the lower tiers where the coaches sat, the sound of a great many professionals letting out a breath they had not known they were holding.

The captain stood on the crown and looked down the slope at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, as a man nods when a sum comes out the way somebody else said it would, and walked back to his mark.

At the north gate Lira had her charting sheet held flat against the planking with her good hand.

"Eleven," she said. "I counted. Rooke counted. Ephram counted twice to be sure." She did not look up from the sheet. "Every one of them on the west side. Every one of them on his downhill. He's written down that you run the planking and burn his edges."

"Let him."

"He's going to fight that now," said Rooke. "Ten readings and one for good measure, and he has the pattern. You know what that means."

"It means he'll fight the pattern."

"It means he'll fight it better than you ran it." Rooke handed him the cup. "He's the best Shield in this city. He's just watched a boy show him the weak place in his own book. Rhagen men don't get angry about that. They get interested." He took the cup back. "Watch the crown."

---

The captain did not come down off the crown in the third exchange.

He walked to the oak round in the middle of the ring and stood on it. He set his feet on the oldest piece of floor in the building, at the highest point of the slope, and let his guard go out from him in every direction at once.

Cael understood it at once, and wished that he had not.

From the top of the crown there was no downhill that was worse than any other. The guard ran down the slope on all sides alike, evenly, to every part of the planking, and it spread on all sides alike, and none of its edges was any farther from its centre than another. The captain had not answered the eleven relocations. He had stepped up onto the one place in the ring where they did not cost him anything.

And then he began to arrive where Cael was going to be.

It was the pattern being fought, exactly as Rooke had said. Cael went north along the planking, and the guard was already thick there before he reached it. He turned south, and the captain had taken one step on the oak, toward the south, a moment before he turned. Ten readings and one for good measure had told Rhagen what Cael would do on the western arc, and now Rhagen was standing in the middle of the ring and pricing the twelfth.

The ring did not get smaller this time. It got shorter. The western arc, which had been his corridor, became a corridor with a guard at both ends, and the guard at each end came a little closer every time he ran toward it.

The first time the captain reached contact range, Cael was near the south bay.

He had run south one stride too many. He knew it as he landed. The guard was ahead of him and above him and coming round behind, and the planking was at his left shoulder, and every way out that his sheet held had a piece of the captain's guard sitting in it a moment early. The read gave him the captain's weight coming down the slope behind the guard, the hand coming, a fraction early. A fraction was not enough.

He did something then that was not on any sheet anywhere.

He did not go away from the guard. He turned on his left foot, hard, back the way he had come and in toward the crown, into the thickest part of the guard, where every sheet he had ever been given said not to go. The turn was wrong and he felt it go wrong in his hip, a long hot pull from the front of the left hip down into the top of the thigh. But the guard at its thickest was also the part the captain was not watching, because nobody went there, and for a step Cael was inside the weight of it, slow as a man wading, and then out the other side with the captain's hand closing on the place he had been.

He did not know where it had come from. He had not practised it. Nobody had ever taught it to him. It had come out of his feet because there was nothing else left in them.

The captain stood very still on the oak for half a breath. Then he came on again.

The second time was in the north bay.

Cael did not choose it. The captain chose it for him, step by step down the western arc, with the guard always a moment early, until the only ground left was the pocket of quick stone in the barrier's shadow. He went into it because there was nowhere else, and his back met the planking, and the rope coping pressed across his shoulder blades.

The guard folded round the mouth of the bay. It came in at both corners and lay across the pocket's opening, deep and slow and everywhere. It was closing his exit. It was a little thinner than it had been on the open floor, because the bay was dead ground and the captain was laying it round a corner, but it was there, and the captain was coming behind it with his right hand already moving.

Cael knew exactly what would hold it.

He knew it the way a man knows where the stair is in his own house in the dark. Reydan's give was in him, the thing that took a blow into the body and kept it from going anywhere, and if he let the captain's hand land and simply took it, kept it, held it, then the touch would land on nothing that moved, and he could stay where he was, inside the bay, and let the captain's own reach carry the man in past his guard.

It would have worked. He could feel how well it would have worked.

It was not on his sheet. Nobody official had seen it. It had not been on a scored floor since Ardenmere, and if it went onto this one, Rhagen would have it in their book by morning and every coaching room on the continent would have it by the next cycle.

He kept it where it was.

He kept it in the way a man keeps his hand off a hot pan, by feeling it all the way and not moving. And in the room that left him, which was not much room, he put his right hand out into the guard's edge where it was dragged widest across the bay's mouth, and lit Karis's fire at the contact.

It was one point of heat at the heel of his palm. It went into the guard like a coal dropped into water and did nothing that he could see.

The captain's hand came on.

Cael went down. He did not go away. He dropped straight down the planking until he was sitting on his heels on the quick stone, and the captain's hand went over his head and touched rope, and Cael came up under the guard's edge with his hand out again and lit the second contact.

And this time the guard felt it.

He could not have said, afterward, how he knew. The read did not see it the way it saw a man's weight. It was more like a sound in a room that a moment before had been perfectly quiet. One layer, somewhere in the guard's depth near the place where his palm had touched it, was renewing as the heat went in, and the heat caught it in the middle of renewing, and it came up a half-beat late.

Just one. Out of all of it. And just half a beat.

It was the first time in two and a half exchanges that any part of that guard had been older than the rest.

Cael came out of the bay along the planking on the captain's left side, low, with the hip burning, and the captain turned to follow, and the guard came with him, and the bell went for the end of the third exchange with neither of them touched.

"One to one," said the referee.

Cael walked back to his gate. He walked carefully. He was aware that every coach in the lower tiers was watching how he walked.

---

"What was that?" said Lira, very low. "On the turn."

"I don't know."

"It wasn't anything we've drilled."

"I know."

Gault had his hands on the hip already, through the cloth, pressing with his thumbs. "Front of the hip," he said. "The flexor. Not torn. Pulled." He pressed again and Cael drew breath through his teeth. "It'll take you to the bell. It'll want four days after, and it'll have them."

Rooke stood at the gate and looked at the captain on the crown, and did not look at Cael at all.

"One more," Rooke said. "Or none. Your choice. Nothing off the sheet."

"Nothing off the sheet."

"Then go and see what your fire did."

Cael had his right hand open at his side. It had begun to sting along the channel the two ignitions had run down, from the heel of the palm into the wrist, a dry tight burn like a hand held too long near a stove. He closed it.

He watched the captain's guard through the whole interval with the full gaze.

It was renewing everywhere, as it had all afternoon. But on the captain's left side, low, where the second ignition had gone in, there was one place in the depth of it that was not quite in step with the rest. It was renewing. It was simply renewing late, every time, by the half-beat it had lost. It had become, for the first time, the oldest thing in the guard.

The captain could feel it. Cael saw him feel it, saw the faint turn of his attention toward his own left side, the way a man's tongue goes to a broken tooth. He would mend it. Give him an exchange and he would mend it.

He was not going to get an exchange.

The fourth exchange began with the captain on the oak and Cael at the north bay, where it had all ended, and for the first ten seconds Cael did exactly what the pattern said he would do.

He went into the bay. He came out of it and ran south along the western planking. The guard was there, a moment early, from the crown, as it had been through the whole of the third exchange. He turned in the middle of the arc and ran back north. The guard was there. Rhagen had priced the twelfth relocation, and the twelfth relocation was this, the western planking, run fast from the bay, toward the place where the guard would be waiting.

He went into the bay again.

The captain laid the guard round the corner after him, and into the pocket, and across its mouth, as he had twice already. He laid it into the ground where every reading he had taken said Cael would come out, which was along the planking, low, fast, to the south.

Cael came out uphill.

He spent his last burst of the day on it, from the quick pale stone at the back of the bay, straight up the slope of the crown toward the oak. It was the one way out of that pocket that nobody had ever taken, because it led toward the guard and toward the high ground and toward the man, and the crown had taught the guard all afternoon that relocations ran downhill.

The landing beat locked him four feet from the oak, on the captain's left side, low.

The guard there was renewing late.

It was not much. A half-beat's worth of age in one layer of all those layers. But he had watched it through a whole interval, and he knew exactly where it was, and he went into it with his open hand at the precise moment it was oldest, and his hand went through the slowness as a hand goes through the last of a wave as it falls back, and touched the captain on the ribs under his left arm.

He felt the guard close behind his wrist as he drew it out. A quarter-beat later, and it would have closed round it.

The flags went up.

"Touch," said the referee, "and the bout. Two to one."

The bowl came to its feet.

Cael did not hear it at first. He was standing on the slope of the crown with his right hand open and stinging and his left hip on fire, looking at the captain, who stood on the oak with his guard falling away from him like mist off a field when the sun comes up. Then he heard it. It was the roar of a crowd that has seen a story come out the way stories are meant to: the young unknown against the Silver doctrine, the long struggle, the last brave rush up the hill. It was very loud, and it went on for a long time, and it was about a bout Cael had not fought.

He looked at the north gate. Lira was not cheering. She was standing with her good hand flat on the charting sheet and her eyes on him, and she was not smiling at all.

He looked at the east tiers. The Rhagen block was not cheering either. Every one of them was writing.

---

The captain found him at the planking by the north bay a little later, while the panel conferred and the stewards cleared the ring.

He had his breath back, but only just, and there was a long dark line of sweat down the front of his black shirt. He stood beside Cael at the barrier and looked out at the oak in the middle of the empty ring for some time before he said anything.

"Your coach read our book," he said at last.

"Twice. A good deal more than twice, I think."

"It shows. Eleven." The captain almost smiled. "We say ten. Nobody ever gives us eleven. They're too frightened of the tenth to go past it." He turned his head and looked at Cael properly, with the long, careful, measuring attention Cael had felt all afternoon from behind the guard. "We came to survey you. That was our filing and it was honest. Rhagen will write it all down tonight, every exchange, and sell it long before the next cycle, like everything else we write."

Cael said nothing.

"You'll want to tell your coach what the survey found," said the captain. "He'll want it in a sentence. So here it is." He spoke slowly, as a man reads out a finding he has checked. "We found the edges of what you showed us. We could not find the edges of you. Rhagen distinguishes between those findings."

He put out his hand. Cael shook it with his left, because his right was still burning, and the captain noticed that, and nodded, and went away across the empty ring to the south gate without looking back.

Cael stood at the barrier. Up on the dais the panel's clerk was still at his slates. In a few minutes the figure would go up on the frame by the north tunnel, and the gallery would read it and argue about it, and all of that was ordinary.

What the captain had said was not ordinary at all.

He had said it politely, as a courtesy, one craftsman to another. It was the most dangerous sentence anybody had spoken to Cael since he came to Norhold, and the man who had spoken it had meant him nothing but well.

The figure went up eleven minutes after the flags.

The captain was rated thirty. Cael was rated twenty-seven.

The gallery argued about it all the way out of the bowl, as Cael had known it would. The boy had won. How could the boy have won and been rated three below the man he beat? The coaches in the lower tiers did not argue. They knew the Standard rated the performance and not the result, and they had watched a Silver of the sixth rank stand on the crown and do the thing he had done all his life exactly as well as he always did it, which was par. They had watched a boy with no tier do a great deal of running and win by a hand.

Cael read the frame and felt nothing much. It was the highest figure of his year. It was also, he thought, the first one that was simply true. He had not set it. He had not managed it. He had not had anything left over to manage it with.

Rooke was waiting for him in the tunnel. He had his coat buttoned.

"You stayed on the sheet," he said.

"I stayed on the sheet."

"That turn in the third."

"I don't know where it came from."

"No," said Rooke. "I didn't think you did. Nobody does, the first time." He looked at Cael for a moment in the tunnel's grey light, and his hand went, perhaps without his meaning it to, to the outside of his coat over the pocket, and rested there. "Well. We're going to dinner anyway."

---

The circle met in the back room after supper, with the door shut.

It was a narrow room, a former linen store with one window over the courtyard pump and a table that rocked, and Lira had claimed it on the first night in the city by a custom she had invented on the stair. Gault had been and gone. He had strapped the hip with a wide bandage round the thigh and the waist and left a pot of salve for the hand, and told Cael to sit and not to argue, and Cael was sitting, with his left leg out straight along the bench.

Lira did not wait for anybody to begin.

"The third exchange," she said. "Twice."

She had the charting sheet on the table under her good hand, and she put her finger on it, on two marks she had made in the third exchange's column.

"Here. At the south bay. And here, in the north one." She looked up at him. "I've watched your feet for years. I know what they look like when we've drilled it. I know what they look like when Rooke's drilled it. I know your landing and your heel and the way you favour the right side when you're tired. That turn at the south bay wasn't any of those, and nor was the drop in the north one. Neither of them was anything. You made them up, at full speed, with his hand coming." She did not say it as an accusation. She said it the way she read off a sheet, item by item, because that was what the four of them did for one another. "How close was it?"

"A handspan," said Cael. "Both times."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Brom had been sitting at the end of the table with his wrapped forearms laid on it, watching Cael's face. He had watched the third exchange from the rail with a very particular attention, and Cael had felt it there, at the north bay, the whole time the captain's hand was coming.

"The absorption stayed banked," said Brom.

It was not a question. Cael shook his head anyway.

"It's not on the record," he said. "It hasn't been on a scored floor since Ardenmere. I wasn't putting it on one in front of Rhagen."

Brom nodded slowly. It was the long nod he gave a figure he had checked and found right and did not like. He did not say what he thought it had cost to hold a thing like that still with a Silver's hand coming down on him in a pocket of stone. He did not need to say it.

Karis had her notebook open on the table in front of her. She had not written in it since they came into the room. Cael noticed that, and saw Lira notice it.

"Then I'd like to put something on the record," said Karis. "Ours. Not theirs."

She turned the notebook round so that they could see the page. It was blank.

"I've kept two columns on you all year," she said to Cael. "What you showed, and what you had. I never wrote the second one down, but I kept it. Every meet, the gap between them was the biggest number on my page. It's what we spent the year living on." She touched the empty leaf with the tips of two salved fingers. "Today I'd have had to write the same thing in both columns. Everything you're allowed, and then a handspan, and then nothing."

"The captain's done," said Lira. "He's out of it."

"The captain's a chapter," said Karis. "Rhagen sells its book. Whoever files next will have read today before he steps through the gate, and whoever files after him will have read two days like it." She looked round the table. "I don't think the doctrine's failing. It held. It held perfectly. But every time it holds, it costs a little more to hold it, and there's a little less room." She took her hand off the page. "The doctrine isn't failing. The doctrine is *filling*."

Nobody spoke.

"I'd like that minuted," said Karis. "That it's filling. And that we don't know how deep it is."

"Minuted," said Cael.

They sat with it. Outside in the courtyard somebody was working the pump, the long creak and the gush of water and the creak again, and the table rocked a little when Brom shifted his arms on it.

None of them said the next thing. All four of them had worked it out, and none of them needed to hear it. The room would run out, one day, against somebody better than a Silver of the sixth rank. They did not know who it would be. They did not know when. They only knew it was somewhere in this city, and that the ring by the north tunnel would still be standing when it came.

---

He wrote the entry late, at the window of his room, with the lamps of the ring still burning across the roofs and the hip throbbing in its strapping. He wrote it slowly. He had known for an hour that it would have to be written slowly, and he had put off beginning it until he was sure he could.

*Rated tonight against the fixed mark. Twenty-seven, three under par. The highest figure of the year, and the first one I didn't set. Rooke says the coaches understood it. I think I understand it too.*

*Now the other entry. The one I owe.*

*I said I can beat anyone at my tier. I just beat Silver. So which tier is mine?*

*Iron couldn't seed me. That was the first answer, on a roster at the start of the year, and I took it as a technicality. Silver couldn't find my edges this afternoon, and I took that, at the barrier, as a compliment. Neither of them was a technicality and neither was a compliment. They were the same answer given twice, the second time more slowly.*

*All year I've stood on one sentence. I've tested it on every floor in the region, and it held every time, and because it held I never asked what it was standing on. It was standing on the idea that there was a rung on the ladder with my name on it, somewhere, and that I only had to find out which one. Tonight's honest entry: I have never once stood on the ladder. I've been climbing alongside it, and calling the nearest rung mine.*

*There is no band below me to hide in anymore. All year the panels have been marking a show of hard work with a rest under it. This afternoon there was nothing under it, and the twenty-seven is simply what happened.*

*Where that leaves me, against the ladder, I can't say, and nobody holding a ladder can say either. I wrote "not this season's problem" once, in this same book, and meant it. It was the last comfortable thing I got to say about it.*

He read it over twice, and did not cross anything out.

Then he sat for a while longer at the window with his right hand open on the sill, letting the night air at the burn, and watched the ring's lamps go out one at a time across the city until the Concourse was dark.

# Chapter 42 — The Back Row

The city changed its story about him overnight, and he could hear it doing so before he was out of bed.

The guesting-house keeper was counting the guests on the stair as usual, aloud, and between the numbers he was telling the southern house's coach on the floor above about the bout. He had not seen it. Nobody in the city who had seen it was saying what he said, which was that the boy had gone up the hill at the end like a goat up a cliff. But he had heard it from a man who had been there, and he told it with his hands, and the southern coach laughed, and then the keeper said something Cael did not catch, and the laughing stopped.

"Limping," said the keeper, quite clearly. "Coming off. I had it from a steward."

Cael lay and looked at the ceiling. The hip had stiffened in the night under its strapping into a dull, deep ache from the front of the joint into the top of the thigh, and when he turned over to get up it told him about it at length.

The broadside on the breakfast table had it too. It had the bout in two columns and a woodcut of the captain on the oak with his guard drawn round him as a great bubble of fine lines, and Cael, very small, running up the hill at him. That was the story everybody had paid for, and the paper gave them their money's worth. But down at the foot of the second column, after the figure and the cheering, the writer had put one more paragraph, and it was the one the whole table read twice.

*It has been said all season that the unclassified boy wins without extending himself. Whatever else may be said of yesterday's bout, that cannot. He came off the floor favouring his left side and holding his right hand open. He was paying for every foot of that ring, and we watched him pay it.*

"They've found your bill," said Karis.

"They've found the receipt," said Lira. "Nobody's seen the bill."

Brom read the paragraph again, slowly, with his bandaged forearm laid along the table beside the sheet. "It's better," he said at last.

"What is?"

"This." He tapped the paragraph. "All year they wanted to know what you were hiding. Now they've seen you hurt." He folded the sheet over, carefully, so the woodcut was on the outside. "People forgive a man for a lot of things if they've watched it cost him."

Gault came in while they were eating and made Cael stand up and walk the length of the common room and back. He watched with his arms folded and his mouth turned down at the corners.

"Four days," he said. "As I told you. You may walk. You may not run, and you may not burst, and you may not argue." He looked at the right hand, which Cael had held out to him without being asked. The channel the two ignitions had run down was a dry pink line from the heel of the palm into the wrist, and it had begun to peel at one edge. "That's healing. It'll be pink for a week and sound in four days. You'll not put fire through it before then."

"The lake man's in three days," said Cael.

"I'm aware of when the lake man is." Gault began to unwind the strapping on the hip to look at it. "That's your coach's problem. Mine is that you've four days of careful and the lake man's come along on the third of them. I'll tell Rooke what I've told you, and he'll do what he does, which is find a way round it." He pressed a thumb into the front of the joint. "Does that hurt?"

"Yes."

"Good. It's meant to."

The frame by the north tunnel still had the figure painted on it when Cael limped down to the Concourse in the middle of the morning, and somebody had chalked a line under it in the night.

It was the exhibition frame, the old dark board with its old type and its five short rules and its ruled lines underneath. The first ruled line had Rhagen's captain on it, and the two figures, and the result. The second had the lake house's duelist and the date three days off, and nothing else.

The third line was empty. So were all the lines below it.

He stood and looked at them for a while. All season long, at every meet in the region, the men who filed against him had been men with nothing to lose by it: quarrymen and bargemen and curious Bronzes who wanted to see. At Norhold two Silvers had filed in one evening, and the whole city had boiled over. He had supposed, if he had thought about it at all, that more would come. Here, in the biggest city on the continent, with the best fighters of fourteen houses walking its streets with nothing to do between their brackets, somebody else would want to try.

Nobody had.

Karis found him there. She had come down to read the brackets, and she stood beside him and read the empty lines with him.

"Rhagen will sell its chapter in a week," she said. "Any Silver who files now files against a boy who beat Rhagen's captain on his own crown. If he wins, he's beaten a boy with no tier. If he loses, he's the second Silver to do it, in front of eight thousand people, with Rhagen's book in his hand." She put her salved hands into her coat pockets. "That was a hard sum before yesterday. Yesterday made it a worse one."

"So nobody files."

"Nobody below Gold," said Karis. "I'd put a mark on that, if anyone would take it."

He looked at the empty lines again, all the way down the frame.

---

Rooke took the problem in hand on the second afternoon, in the hired hall behind the east tower, with the door shut and the barrels outside the window going gold in the late sun.

He had Gault's verdict written on the top of his sheet in Gault's own hand. *Walk. No run. No burst. No fire until the fourth day.* Under it, in his own capitals, Rooke had written the lake man's filing, *THE CROWD*, and drawn a line under that too.

"The lake man fights on the third day," he said, "and the third day's inside Gault's four. I could ask the west tower to put him back. They'd do it. They've put this category back once already." He looked at Cael. "I'm not going to ask."

"No."

"I'll tell you why. If we put him back, the city says the boy's hurt. If the city says the boy's hurt, every house in this town that wants to know how hurt will find out by the evening." He tapped the sheet. "So you fight him on the day. Walking. No fire. Bursts..." He looked at Gault's line. "One, if you have to, and you'll not have to. You'll fight him with your eyes and your patience, and nothing else."

"Against a Silver of the fourth rank," said Lira, from her stool by the window.

"Against a showman," said Rooke. "Which he is, and very good, and very dangerous with it. I said at the long table that a showman's timing is built for the back row." He held up one finger. "That's the whole of him. Every passage he fights, he fights so that it ends at a moment the whole bowl can see. He builds his bout like a man builds a speech. He starts quiet. He gets louder. He comes to the end with a flourish, and the flourish lands at the exact moment it'll look best from the top tier." He lowered the finger. "And inside every flourish there's a real blade. That's what makes him dangerous and not just pretty. He's a Silver. The edge-work's true. It just comes wrapped in a show."

"Then what do I do?"

"You stop being his audience," said Rooke.

He looked round the room for Ephram, who was sitting on the trestle with his long legs crossed.

"Get up. You're the lake man."

Ephram had clearly been hoping for this since breakfast. He got up and took a practice lath from the rack by the door, and swept it once through the air, and bowed to all four walls of the hired hall in turn with enormous ceremony, and Lira laughed out loud on her stool.

"He bows to the panel," said Ephram, "and to the referee, and to each side of the bowl. I've read about him. He bows to the stewards." He bowed to Gault, who did not look up from his salve pot. "He'd bow to the pump if it was in the ring."

"Fight like him," said Rooke. "Not as well. Like him."

Ephram did.

He was not the lake man. He was a Blade of Iron's sixth rank who had spent the morning reading about the lake man in a broadside, and he knew it, and he made it funny on purpose. But under the bowing he was a good fighter, and he understood exactly what Rooke wanted. He fought in long passages with a shape to them. He began each one small and quiet and built it, step by step, cut by cut, toward a grand turning finish, and he timed the finish to land on a count that Cael could hear him keeping under his breath.

The first time, Cael watched the passage build and got ready for the end of it, as anyone would, and the lath came round on the count and stopped an inch from his ear.

"That's what he wants," said Rooke. "He wants you waiting for the end of his sentence. Everybody in the bowl is waiting for it, and you're the only one who'll get hit when it comes. Again. This time don't wait."

"What do I do instead?"

"Anything else."

The second time, when the passage began to build, Cael stood still.

He did not step to meet it or step away from it. He simply let it go on, standing on his good leg with his weight easy and the read on Ephram's feet, and when the count came round to the place where the flourish ought to land, he was not where the flourish was going. He had not moved far. He had moved early, half a beat before the end of the sentence, at a moment when nothing about the sentence said that anybody ought to move.

The lath came round and finished its turn in the empty air.

Ephram stood with it held out at the end of its flourish, looking at the place where Cael had been, and his face did something Cael had not seen it do before. It was not annoyance. It was the look of a man who has told a good story and reached the last line, and found that nobody was listening for it.

"Oh," said Ephram.

"That," said Rooke. "Again."

They went on until the light was gone off the barrels. Cael never burst. He never once put fire into his hand. He stood, and walked, and refused to be where the story wanted him, and Ephram built passage after passage at him, louder and grander and sillier, until by the end he was declaiming them aloud in a terrible stage voice, *and now! the blade descends!*, and Lira was crying with laughter on her stool and holding her wrapped arm still with the other hand so that it would not shake.

But by the end Ephram was also doing something he had not done at the start. He had stopped building the passages to the end. He was building them halfway and then breaking off, sharp and plain, in the middle of a phrase, with a cut that had no show in it at all. Twice it nearly caught Cael. Once it did.

"Where did that come from?" said Rooke.

"If nobody's going to wait for the end," said Ephram, breathing hard, "then why have one?" He lowered the lath and looked at it. "He'll work that out. Won't he? Halfway through."

"He's a Silver of the fourth rank," said Rooke. "He'll work it out before you did."

Karis had sat through the whole session at the end of the trestle with a pile of the season's broadsides in front of her, turning them over with the tips of her fingers because her palms were still too tender to grip. She looked up when they had finished.

"He likes the windows," she said.

They looked at her.

"The high windows on the west side of the bowl. In the afternoon they throw bars of light across the floor." She held up one of the broadsides, which had an account of the lake man's bracket bout from the cycle before. "Every account I can find, his flourishes finish in the light. Every one. He fights his passages so they end in a bright bar, where the top tier can see the blade catch." She laid the sheet down. "He'll do it in the ring. The bars cross the ring's west side in the middle of the afternoon."

Rooke looked at the broadside for a long moment. Then he looked at Cael.

"Your sheet," he said quietly, "has an item on it for that."

Cael knew which one. It had been on the sheet since the mill town. Item two of four: a point conceded when the light was against him, or the floor soft, or the crowd loud, so that the record breathed as an honest fighter's record breathed, and nobody looking at it would find a line too straight.

"If the light's in my eyes," he said, "and he comes out of it. Yes."

"Only if," said Rooke. "Don't go and stand in it."

"I won't need to," said Cael. "He'll put me there."

---

On the morning of the fourteenth day, at the mouth of the north tunnel where the Halcenvane fighters did their warming, Cael looked up at the east gallery while Gault was taping his hand, and saw that the chair at the end of the Compact's row was no longer empty.

There had been no announcement. Nobody on the stair had mentioned it. The row's posted roster on the board at the tunnel mouth had the same eleven names it had carried for two weeks. He checked it, and then checked it again, and it had not changed.

But at the end of the row, behind the grey rail-cloth, in the twelfth chair, a man was sitting.

He was alone. He wore a plain grey coat of the cut the Compact's inspectors wore when they travelled, with nothing on it to say his grade. He sat very upright with his hands resting on a closed book on his knee, and he was not looking at the floor yet, or at the tiers. He was looking at nothing in particular, with the patient stillness of a man who has sat in a great many rooms and does not need to fill them.

Cael would have known the stillness anywhere.

He had spent one whole day inside that stillness, in a room twelve feet by fourteen, answering eleven questions. He had watched it look once at a failing frame and never at him again that whole morning. He had never seen the man since. He had imagined him, once or twice, at a table in some high room, writing a note Cael believed said something he would never be allowed to read.

The Archmarshal Vastin had come to Norhold, and he was sitting in the chair that had been held for two weeks for somebody nobody would name.

Along the row the other officers had shifted. It was very slight. Nobody had moved their seats. But Ilsev's lap-desk was at a new angle, a little toward the end of the row, and Havel was sitting straighter than he had ever sat, and the liaison man had stopped talking to the host city's officials. Every one of them had arranged themselves round the man in the twelfth chair, as a field of iron filings arranges itself round a magnet, and the man was paying no attention to any of them at all.

"Hand," said Gault.

Cael gave it back to him. Gault went on taping.

He did what the year had taught him to do. He noted it. He put it where he put everything that he could not use yet, which was in a part of his head that stayed shut until the bout was over, and he finished his warm-up on the good leg and the walking leg, and he did not look at the gallery again.

---

The lake man came out through the south gate bowing.

He bowed at the gate, to the south tiers. He bowed to the panel on the dais. He bowed to the referee, and turned, and bowed to the east tiers and the west, and finally, with a flourish of his sash, to the north end, where the Halcenvane rail stood along the planking. Ephram, at the rail, bowed back before he could stop himself, and Lira kicked his ankle.

He was tall and lean, perhaps twenty-five, with a long, mobile, handsome face that knew exactly where the light was. He carried a Blade's practice sword as if it were an extension of his hand, and he moved across the stone in a way that was very pleasant to watch and, under the pleasantness, perfectly economical. The bowl loved him. It had loved him before he came out, and it loved him more now.

"Five exchanges at most," said the referee. "Two touches ends it. A clean throw is a touch."

The flags went up.

The lake man opened as Rooke had said he would, quietly. He circled, low, testing, the sword point making small easy figures in the air, and the bowl went quiet with him, as an audience goes quiet at the beginning of a song. Then he began to build.

It was beautiful. Cael could see that even while he watched it for the knife. A step in and a cut, small. A step back. Two steps in and two cuts, a little faster, a little wider, and the lake man's whole body beginning to turn into the rhythm of it. Three steps and a spin, the sword going round in a long bright arc toward the east rail, where the light from the high west windows lay across the planking in a long gold bar, and the whole east side of the bowl drew breath to see the blade catch it.

The spin was a show. It was meant for the east rail and every face along it.

Except for its last quarter-beat.

The read saw it a fraction before the sword did. The lake man's weight, which had been going round with the spin, all of it, toward the rail, stopped going round. It came off his back foot and onto his front in the space of a quarter-beat, and his shoulder dropped, and the sword came out of the spin not toward the rail at all but straight in, short and flat, at Cael's ribs, from an angle the whole long beautiful turn had spent two beats hiding.

Cael was not there. He was a foot to the left, on his good leg, because the read had told him where the weight was going and the read did not care in the least what the sword was doing.

The bowl gasped at the spin. It had not seen the cut at all.

At the north rail Rooke had not moved, and neither had Lira, and neither had Karis. They had seen the cut. They had come to see it.

There was no touch in the first exchange. The lake man built two more passages and Cael was not where either of them ended, and at the bell the lake man walked back to his mark looking thoughtful, and bowed to the panel again, a little less deeply.

In the second exchange Cael stopped being the audience.

He did what he had done in the hired hall with Ephram, and he did it better, because the lake man was a better story-teller and his stories were easier to hear. When a passage began, Cael stood. He did not step to meet it. He let it build, step and cut and step, and kept the read on the lake man's feet and his own weight on his good leg, and he waited, not for the end, but for the moment before the end, when the sentence was nearly finished and everything in the bowl was leaning toward the last word.

Then he moved, early, quietly, a foot or two, to a place the sentence had not been written for.

The flourish arrived. It arrived beautifully, in the light, with the sword turning gold, and it arrived at nothing.

The lake man was too good to be caught by it twice in the same way. He began to cut his passages shorter, to bring the end nearer, to finish before Cael could leave. But a shorter sentence still had an end, and the end was still where he wanted the bowl to look, and Cael was still not there when it came.

By the middle of the exchange the lake man's passages had begun to come apart.

It was not dramatic. It was like listening to a man tell a joke to a friend who does not laugh, and watching him tell it again, a little faster, and then a third time, faster still, until the joke is all words and no shape. His flourishes kept arriving at their ends, beautifully, on time, and finding nothing under them. Cael kept refusing them. He refused them flatly and without any show of his own, walking, at the pace of a man reading a long letter he means to answer later.

Late in the exchange, a passage broke in the middle. The lake man had built it toward a great backhand turn, and Cael had moved before the turn, and the lake man had seen him move and tried to change the end of the sentence while he was still saying it. For a half-beat his weight was nowhere. It was not on the turn and it was not on the change. It was between them.

Cael stepped in on the walking leg and touched him on the sword arm above the elbow with the flat of his left hand.

"Touch," said the referee. "One to nought."

Cael walked back to his mark, and as he turned at it he looked up at the east gallery without meaning to.

The man in the twelfth chair had opened the book on his knee. As Cael watched, he wrote something in it. It was short, a line or two. He wrote it without hurry, and closed the book again, and folded his hands on it, and looked back down at the ring.

---

The lake man came out for the third exchange without any sentences at all.

Cael saw it in the first two steps, and felt something cold go down his back. The man had stopped building. He had stopped bowing. He had stopped fighting for the top tier or the east rail or the light, and he had simply come out to fight, plainly, a Silver of the fourth rank with a true blade and nobody left to perform for.

And plain, he was better.

He was faster to the line without the show to carry. His entries came straight and short and early, from nowhere, with no sentence in front of them to warn of them, and the read still gave Cael his weight a fraction early, but now a fraction was all there was. Twice in the first half of the exchange the plain cut came close enough that Cael felt the air of it on his cheek. The second time, the hip told him what it thought of the step he had used to get away, and he lost a beat recovering.

The bowl, which had been a little restless through the second exchange, went quiet. The craft-readers in the lower tiers leaned forward. This was a bout now, and they knew it.

The light had moved.

It had been crossing the ring's east side at the start. Now the bars from the high windows had slid west with the afternoon and lay across the middle of the ring and the western slope of the crown, broad and gold and blinding. Cael had been watching them move all exchange, and he had been keeping out of them, on the walking leg, at a cost.

The lake man saw him keeping out of them. He was a showman. He knew where the light was better than anybody in the building.

He herded Cael into it.

He did it plainly, without a single flourish, three short cuts that left one way out, and the one way out was west and up the crown, into the bright bar. Cael took it. He stood in the light with it full in his eyes, and the lake man came out of the glare at him, straight, low, a plain thrust at the body with nothing round it at all.

Cael could have answered it. He was almost sure of that. A burst, low and to the right, off the bad hip, and he would have been out of the line; or the read and a turn on the left foot, and a step that would have cost the hip another day. He had answers. They were expensive, and he was almost sure.

Item two said a point conceded when the light was against him.

He let it come.

The sword's flat touched him low on the ribs on the left side, and the lake man was past and out of the light before the flag went up.

"Touch," said the referee. "One to one."

Cael walked back to his mark. The bar of light lay across the crown behind him, and he did not look at it.

He had paid a point on schedule, as the program said, and the record would breathe. That was the arithmetic. But walking back to his mark with his ribs stinging he found that he did not know, quite, whether he had conceded that point or simply lost it. The lake man plain had been very good. He had come out of the light faster than Cael had expected anybody to come out of anything. Cael would have answered it, very likely. Not certainly. Very likely.

He put that in the shut part of his head with the twelfth chair, to look at later, and turned and faced the lake man for the fourth.

The lake man had won the third, and he knew how he had won it, and Cael watched him decide what to do with it.

For the first half of the exchange he fought as he had fought the third, plainly and very well, and Cael did not let him herd him anywhere. Cael stayed on the shadowed side of the crown and walked and refused. There were no sentences to refuse now, only cuts, and he refused the cuts as he had refused the sentences, by being early and quiet and a foot away, and by never once waiting for anything.

He watched the lake man's face while he did it.

Plain fighting was costing him. Not his strength; he had plenty of that. It was costing him something he had fought for all his life without ever having to name it, which was the bowl. Eight thousand people were watching a Silver of the fourth rank fight in a way they could not see, quick and flat and unlovely, and they had gone quiet, and the lake man could hear them being quiet. Cael saw him hear it.

In the middle of the fourth exchange the lake man built one last passage.

It was not a good one. He knew that too, Cael thought. It was a passage thrown out of pure frustration as much as craft, a long turning flourish toward the north rail with the sword going round in the last of the light, the old beautiful thing, the thing the bowl loved, because he could not bear to fight one more minute without giving it to them.

The read gave Cael his weight before he had finished the first step of it. It was going round with the turn. All of it. There was nothing hidden in this one, no quarter-beat, no blade waiting at the end. There was only a man who wanted the bowl back.

Cael stepped in under the turn and touched him on the chest with his open hand, and stepped out again.

"Touch," said the referee, "and the bout. Two to one."

The bowl was on its feet, but it was a different sound from the captain's day, warmer and more puzzled, the sound of a crowd that has enjoyed itself and is not quite sure what it has seen.

The lake man took it beautifully. He bowed to the panel and the referee and all four sides of the bowl again, every one of them, and to Cael last and lowest, with real grace and something that might have been relief. Then he came across the ring to the north planking where the crowd along the rail was thickest, and leaned on the rope, and grinned at the faces there.

"Well?" somebody shouted. "Well?"

"He's better than the woodcuts," said the lake man.

The rail roared.

Cael looked up at the east gallery.

The twelfth chair was empty. On the dais the panel's clerk was still at his slates, and the rating clerks were doing their arithmetic, and it would be eleven minutes yet before the figure went up on the frame by the north tunnel. The man in the grey coat had not waited for it. He had written once more, Lira told him afterward, a line or two, after the last touch, while the flags were still coming down. Then he had closed his book and stood, without any hurry, and gone along the back of the row and down the east stair, and nobody in the row had turned to watch him go except Havel.

The figure went up at the eleventh minute, exactly. Twenty-eight for the lake man. Twenty-five for Cael.

By evening somebody had chalked the lake man's sentence across the top of the biggest odds board in the outer court, above all the prices, in letters a foot high. *HE'S BETTER THAN THE WOODCUTS.* The criers worked under it all night and nobody wiped it off.

# Chapter 43 — Twice

The Archmarshal Vastin had reached the city the evening before, by the post road, with one bag.

He had not told anybody he was coming, because nobody had asked, and because he had not been sure himself until the morning he left. The west tower's notice about the exhibition ring had come to his desk among the liaison papers, copied by fast post as everything about Norhold was copied to him now, since he had ordered the files. *The ring to be raised when the brackets clear the main floor. Bouts to be heard in order of filing.* He had read it at his table with the pigeons outside, and turned it face down, and turned it up again. Then he had written in his day book *Norhold, on observation. Not requested by any office,* and told his clerk he would be away for a week, and packed.

He had missed the first bout by two days. He had known he would. The roads did not care what a man had decided at his table.

At the inn under the outer wall he had read the row's report of it by a poor lamp, while the post-horses were led round to the yard below. It was a competent report, in Ilsev's office's careful even hand, and it gave him the exchanges in order and the figures, thirty and twenty-seven, and the duration, and a note on the crowd. Near the end there was one line that was not like the others. *Subject's footwork in the third exchange not previously recorded.*

He had read that line twice. Then he had put the report away and gone to bed, and lain awake for some time listening to the yard.

In the morning he went to the Concourse by the east stair, at the hour the row's officers went up, in the grey travelling coat of an inspector, with nothing on it to say what he was.

A steward stood at the head of the stair. She was a young woman with a list on a board, and she looked at him once as he came up and did not look at her list at all.

"This way, Archmarshal," she said.

He had not given his name. He had not given his grade, and his coat did not give it either.

He followed her along the back of the gallery, past the public benches and behind the row's plated chairs, to the end, where the grey rail-cloth ran on in front of a chair with a pale square on its back and two empty screw holes. She lifted the cloth for him and let it fall again when he had sat down, and went back to her stair.

He sat and folded his hands on his book.

He had read the seating plan in his room, a week before the opening. He had written one line in his day book about it and declined to think the long thought that came after the line. He declined it again now. A held chair was a courtesy. A steward who knew a man's grade by his face had been told it by somebody, and that was all that could be said about it with the evidence in hand, and he had spent forty years refusing to say more than the evidence would hold.

He noticed, though, that he had chosen to sit in it. He could have stood at the back of the public gallery. He could have taken a bench among the city's people and nobody would have known him.

He had come up the east stair instead and let the steward lead him to the chair somebody else had held for him, and he could not have said, sitting in it, whether he had done that to see who would notice, or simply because it was the best seat from which to watch a floor.

Along the row, the officers had noticed. He did not look at them. He knew Ilsev's back as he knew his own handwriting, and he could feel the angle of her lap-desk change without turning his head.

He watched the ring.

---

He watched the boy come out of the north tunnel carefully.

There was no limp, quite. There was a boy walking as a man walks across a room he knows has a loose board in it, keeping his weight on one side and not letting anybody see him keep it. Vastin had seen a great many people walk like that into a great many rooms, and very few of them had been seventeen.

He watched the showman from the lake house bow to everybody in the building, and he watched the bout begin.

He did not watch the showman. The whole bowl was watching the showman, and the showman was very good, and there was nothing to learn from looking where eight thousand other people were already looking.

Vastin watched the boy, as he had sat four feet from him in a room twelve feet by fourteen and watched him while he answered.

In the first exchange the boy was not where the blows landed, and Vastin saw how. He was not quick. Not that afternoon. He was early. He went to the right place a moment before there was any reason in the world to go there.

In the second exchange the boy did something Vastin had never seen done on a floor.

He declined to be told a story.

The showman built his passages as such men did, toward an ending, and the whole bowl leaned toward each ending as it came.

The boy did not lean. He stood on his good leg and let each passage come, and at the moment before its end, when even Vastin felt himself waiting for the last word, the boy was quietly somewhere else. He did it again and again. He did it at no speed at all, at the pace of a clerk turning pages. And the showman, who was a Silver and an artist, came slowly apart in front of him, sentence by sentence, until the boy stepped in through a half-beat in which the man's weight had nowhere to go and touched him on the arm.

Nothing in it was hidden. That was what held Vastin's eye. Every piece of it was done in the open, on the floor, in front of everyone, inside the rule. And not one person in eight thousand had been looking at it.

He opened his book and wrote.

*Second exchange. He refuses the other man's time. Openly, within the rule, and at some cost to himself. Nobody in the bowl is watching that part.*

He read it back, as he read every line, and it was true. He closed the book.

He watched the third exchange with his hands folded on the closed book.

He saw the showman put the show down and fight, and he saw that the man was better without it, a good deal better, and that the boy, on one leg, had to work very hard to stay out of his way. He saw the light from the high windows move across the floor. He saw the boy keep out of it for most of an exchange, at a price, and then be driven into it by three plain cuts, and stand there with the glare full in his face.

He saw the touch land.

He had been watching the boy's face, not the showman's sword, and he saw something on it in the instant before the touch that he could not read. It was not surprise. It was not fear. It was very quick, and it was gone, and he did not write it down, because he did not know what it was, and a thing he did not know could not go into a book as though he did.

In the fourth exchange he watched the showman give up the plain fight and throw one last long beautiful passage at the north rail, for no reason that had anything to do with winning, and he watched the boy step in under it and end the bout.

He opened the book again before the flags had come down.

*Close. Everything I saw today, I already had a word for.*

He read that back too. It was true. It was true in a way that he found, sitting in the held chair with the bowl on its feet around him, he would have to think about for some time.

He thought about three words on a page that had come down to him under a seal from an office above his own, with no routing slip and no initials. *Mitigation. Containment. Trajectory.*

He had answered them at length, in his own language, and been told his answers had been entered. He thought about the digest, and the row's report, and the files in their two boxes, and the seating plan with his grade on it in a stranger's hand. Paper about paper. Some of it competent and some of it careful and all of it written for somebody to read somewhere else, by people who had been told what it was for.

What he had written in his book that afternoon had been written for nobody. He had seen the thing himself, and written down what he saw, in the words he had for it, and the words had been enough.

Below him the panel was beginning its arithmetic. In eleven minutes or so a figure would go up on the frame by the north tunnel, and it would be a good figure, honestly reached by five honest judges, and it would tell anyone who read it exactly what the Standard thought of the afternoon.

He did not want it.

He knew himself well enough to know what he would do if he stayed.

He would read the figure. He would set his own two lines beside it, and he would find, as one always found, that the figure made the lines look a little different. A little smaller, or a little larger, or a little less certain. He had read figures for forty years. He had never once read one without letting it lean on whatever he had seen before it.

So he stood up while the clerks were still at their slates.

He went along the back of the row without hurrying, behind the plated chairs, past Ilsev, who did not turn, and past Havel, who did. He nodded to Havel. He went down the east stair, where the young steward stood aside for him with her list against her chest, and out by the side door into the street, where the afternoon was going gold over the roofs.

He did not know what the boy was. He noticed, walking back toward the outer wall through the crowd, that he had not written that down either. It was not a finding. It was only the place where the findings stopped.

He would go home in the morning, by the post road. There was a calendar on his desk, and a great deal of it would want moving.

---

Cael gave the table the fact over supper, and one sentence with it.

"The Archmarshal came himself," he said. "He reads every piece of paper anybody sends him, and he left it all on his desk and came to look."

The table took that in. Nobody asked how he knew it was Vastin; he had described that stillness to them often enough over the year, and Karis had already had the rest of it from Lira, who had watched the twelfth chair through the whole bout from the north rail instead of watching the floor, and had told everybody afterward, with some indignation, that she had missed the best touch of the afternoon on Cael's account.

"He wrote twice," said Lira. "Once after the touch in the second. And once more after the last touch, while the flags were still going up. Then he got up and went, before the figure. He didn't even look at the board."

"That's not like a Compact man," said Ephram.

"No," said Karis. "It's not."

At the far end of the table Seln had been squaring the evening's papers into the travel file, as he did every night. He finished. He fitted the last sheet under its clip. Then, instead of closing the file, he laid one hand flat on top of it.

The table knew that gesture by now. It meant that the next sentence would be short, and in the office's voice, and that it would be the only one.

"The office notes that the chair was held for him for thirteen days before he sat in it," said Seln. "The stewards' book at the east stair carried his grade against that seat on the first morning of the sitting. His own office's notice of travel reached the west tower yesterday at noon." He did not raise his voice, and he did not lower it. "The Archmarshal did not announce himself. Somebody announced it for him."

Nobody said anything.

Seln closed the file and took his hand off it, and went back to being a man at the end of a table who kept the papers. The table let him. It sat for a while with what he had said, and then, because it was supper and there was bread to pass, it began to talk again about other things, a little more quietly than before.

*He evaluates,* Cael wrote that night. *That's all he has ever done, as far as I know him. He sat four feet from me for a day and asked me eleven questions and wrote down what I said. He wrote a note about me that I believe says I'm not a mistake, and he signed it with his whole name.*

*Today he crossed the country to sit in a chair somebody else saved for him, and watched one bout, and wrote twice, and left before the number.*

*A man who trusted the papers that reach his desk would have stayed at his desk. A man who trusted the panel would have waited for its figure. He did neither. Whatever reaches him about me now, he doesn't think it's enough. He's started checking it with his own eyes.*

*Seln says the stewards had his grade against that chair two weeks before his own clerk told anybody he was travelling. Somebody kept him a chair for thirteen days and told a steward his grade. I can't do anything with that. Nobody at this table can. But I'd like it written down that the chair was full today, and that the man in it went home without our figure, and that I'm seventeen in about two hours.*

He sat and looked at the last line for a while. Then he closed the book and went to bed.

---

He woke late.

It was the first time in the whole sitting.

The guesting-house was quiet in a way he had not heard it, with no feet on the stair and no keeper counting, and the light on the ceiling was the yellow light of the middle of the morning. For a moment he did not know what was wrong. Then he remembered that nothing was wrong at all. It was the rest day. The Concourse was shut. Nobody in the whole city was going to measure anybody until tomorrow.

And he was seventeen.

He had turned it some time in the night, between one day and the next, in a borrowed bed in a city of strangers. He had known it was coming. Every night for a week the date at the head of the Log's page had been one nearer, and he had watched it come without saying so. But knowing a thing was coming and lying in a bed on the morning of it were not the same, and he lay for a while and let the second one be what it was.

The hip had stiffened again in the night, but less. It was the fourth of Gault's four days. He swung his legs out of bed carefully, and the joint complained, and then, when he stood, thought better of it.

There was a note on the common-room table, propped against the water jug, in Lira's quick, tilted hand.

*Blue door. Past the third wharf, on the harbour side; you can smell it from the bridge. Midday. Everybody's coming, so you are too. Many happy returns, in case nobody else has thought to say it. — L.*

He read it twice and put it inside his coat, next to the notebook.

He gave himself the walk.

It was the only present he had thought of for himself, and it was a good one. He went down through the quarter and out by the river gate and along the quays toward the harbour, slowly, because of the hip, with his coat open, and for once he did not walk anywhere for any reason. He looked at things because they were there.

Norhold on its day off was another place altogether. He had known the tournament's Norhold for two weeks, the Norhold of boards and criers and broadsides and the long roar out of the Concourse, and he had thought that was Norhold. It was not. It was a costume the city put on every three years. Underneath it, on the rest day, the real place went on with its own business at its own pace.

Barges were being warped along the quays by men who had nowhere to be until evening.

The lock gates at the harbour's mouth were working, slow and old, for nobody but the gulls. A wedding party was crossing the fish market in its best clothes, with the bride's brothers carrying the chairs. On the steps of the cloth-hall two girls were teaching a smaller girl to whistle through her fingers, and failing, and laughing so hard at their own failure that the smaller one had sat down on the step to wait for them.

But the city's arithmetic did not stop for the rest day. He saw it everywhere, once he began to look.

At the second wharf there was a hiring board, a plain black board with chalk on it, and a dozen men stood in front of it reading. *Grain lighter. Six hands. Copper will do. Iron preferred. Dinner found.* Under that, *Timber, the long dock, two days. Iron or better.* None of the men reading it had ever stood on a tournament floor, Cael thought, or wanted to. They knew exactly what a rank was. It was what a man could lift, and for how long, and what he could be paid for it.

On the corner of the fish market there was a public house with two doors. The front one was propped open on a room full of wharfmen and smoke. The other, round the side, was shut, with a small brass plate screwed to it at the height of a man's eyes. Cael stopped and read the plate. It said that the back room was kept for ranked custom, and it gave the rank in a form of words that the registry had stopped using before Cael was born. Nobody had changed the plate. Nobody had needed to. Everybody who used that door knew what it meant.

On the coopers' row the registry's annex had its shutters down for the rest day, and there was a queue outside it anyway.

He stopped at the end of the row and counted it. Thirty-one people, standing in a line along the wall of a shut office, most of them with a folded paper in their hands. He knew what the papers were. They were result slips from the Concourse. The tournament made ranks, and the registry wrote them down, and until the registry had written them down they were only a story somebody had told about you in a crowded room. These people had come a day early to stand at a shut door so that they would be first when it opened.

He thought about Brom's plaque on the long table, and Brom's summons, and walked on without saying anything to anybody.

At the basin steps there was a likeness-seller with his sheets pegged along a cord between two poles, two for a copper, flapping a little in the wind off the water.

The Auremont Gold had the middle of the cord, and the stack of her sheets on the stall's board was nearly gone. Zerin was beside her, and then the Rhagen captain on his oak with the guard drawn round him like a great soap bubble, and then the Iron bracket's last four with Lira among them, fists up, angrier than life again.

At the very end of the cord, a little apart from the rest, hung a sheet of him.

It was not the sunbeams sheet or the black one. It was new since the captain's bout, and it showed him running up a hill. The cutter had worked hard at it. He had got the hill and the rope and the stride. But he had cut the eyes too old, so that a boy his own age was looking out of the sheet with the eyes of a tired man of forty who had seen a great deal and liked very little of it.

Cael bought it. Hesk would want it. Hesk would say something withering about the eyes and keep it in the workshop drawer with the good files for the rest of his life.

The likeness-seller took the copper and made change and handed him the sheet rolled up, and said "Good luck to the bluff," because the sheet had the bluff's name on it, and did not look at his face at all.

Cael put the sheet in his coat and walked on along the basin, smiling, and could not have said why that pleased him as much as it did.

At the third wharf a woman was playing a fiddle for the holiday crowd with her case open at her feet. She was good. She played as people play who have done it on cold wharves for twenty years and are paid in coppers and do it well anyway. Cael stopped, as Hesk had taught him to stop for anybody who knew an instrument, and listened through to the end of the tune, and put a coin in the case.

She looked up at him as he straightened. Her eyes went to his face, and to the coat, and back to the face, and something in them changed.

Then she grinned at him, and put the fiddle back up under her chin, and played four bars of a quick-march straight at him, sharp and bright and very fast, as he walked away along the wharf. It was the march they played for the colour-guard at the procession. She stopped at the end of the fourth bar, exactly, and went back to her tune.

That was what he was worth, then, to a fiddler on the third wharf. Four bars and a grin. He found it the most restful price anybody had put on him in a year.

The smell of frying fish came along the wharf from somewhere ahead, and under it the smell of tar, and under that, very faintly, something baking. Past the third wharf, on the harbour side, there was a low cookshop with its windows open on the water, and a door painted a deep, chipped, cheerful blue.

He looked at the sun. It was midday, near enough.

He went in.

# Chapter 44 — The Blue Door

Seln had had the parcel since the early post.

The pouch came up from the river gate at the first bell on the rest day as on every other day, because the post kept no holidays and nor did the office that answered it. He unlocked it at the end of the long table in the common room, with the house still asleep above him, and sorted it as he sorted everything, into the order in which it would be wanted.

There was not much. A bundle for Rooke from a coaching house in the south. A thin packet for Withrow, sealed, which went into the inner pocket of the travel file unopened. A card for the boy in a plain square hand he knew from a Greyvane file. An envelope for the boy with the Ardenmere circuit ledger's stamp on the flap, which could only be one person.

And the parcel.

It was the size of a large book and the weight of a small anvil, done up in brown paper and good twine with the knots sealed in wax. The coast clerk's slip was tied to the twine. *Stationery, bound, one.* Under that, in the column for the insured value, the clerk had written a figure, and then, as if he had not quite believed it himself, written it out again in words.

Seln read the figure in both forms. He set the parcel on the table and looked at it for a moment.

There was a note propped against the water jug a little way along the table. It was not addressed to him, and he did not read it. He did not need to. He had heard Lira leave the house an hour before with Brom, talking about a cookshop with a blue door past the third wharf, and he knew the hour she had chosen, because she had chosen it at the top of her voice on the stair.

He could leave the post on the table. The boy would find it when he came down. He could give it to him at supper.

He did neither.

He put Rooke's bundle and Withrow's packet into the travel file and locked the file in his room. He took off the coat he wore for the office, the dark one with the deep pockets, and hung it on its peg, and put on the plain brown coat that he wore for nothing in particular. He put the card and the envelope in its pocket. He put the parcel under his arm.

He walked down through the quarter and out by the river gate and along the quays, at the pace of a man who has a parcel to deliver and no other business that day. He did not hurry. He looked at the barges and the lock gates and the wedding party in the fish market, as anybody might. Once he stopped on the bridge over the inner basin and stood for a while with the parcel against the rail, watching a woman teach a crowd of children to scull, and then he went on.

Past the third wharf the cookshop had its windows open on the water, and the smell of frying fish came out of them, and voices he knew.

He stopped across the lane from it.

Through the nearest window he could see the long table at the back, and the five of them round it, and the plates. He could also see, along the side of the room, the counter, where a man could sit on a stool with a cup in front of him and his back to the wall and see the whole room and everybody in it, and not be at anybody's table.

He looked at the counter for some time.

Then he crossed the lane and went in through the blue door.

---

The proprietor met Cael just inside the door, an hour earlier, with a dish of fried fish in each hand and flour to both elbows.

"You'll be the birthday," she said.

He had not said a word yet. He looked at her.

"Your face is on a cord at the basin steps," she said, "and your friend described you. Tall as that, she said, and thin as that, and you'll look at the room before you look at me." She jerked her chin toward the back of the shop. "You did. They're at the long table. All of it. Don't ask me how she got it. I opened the door at eleven and she was standing on the step, and by a quarter past she'd talked the wharfmen off my best table and borrowed cushions from the chandler next door, and I still don't know what she said to any of them."

"That's Lira," said Cael.

"Is it. Well." The proprietor shifted the dishes. "And the big one. The quiet one with his arms tied up. He was in here three days ago on his own, the morning after your first bout, and he ordered a honey-cake for this table for today, and paid for it on the spot, and told me it was a birthday and I wasn't to make a fuss about it." She considered Cael with her head on one side. "So I'm not making one. But I'll tell you, because nobody else will. I've kept this shop twenty-two years and I've never once heard that man order anything, and there's been food in front of him since he sat down."

She went off toward the back with the fish.

Cael followed her and found the long table.

It stood under the windows at the back of the shop, a single scarred plank of a table that had been pushed against the wall by the harbour for so long that the wall had a dark line along it where the edge rubbed. Somebody had pinned a sheet of paper to the end of it with a fork. It said, in Lira's quick tilted capitals, *TAKEN. HALCENVANE. ASK THE GIRL WITH ONE ARM.*

The girl with one arm sat at the head of the table with her wrap across her lap, and pointed at the bench under the window.

"You're there," said Lira. "Facing the water. It's your day, so you get the view, and you don't get to argue about anything."

He sat where he was told.

Brom was opposite him, with his wrapped forearms resting on the table and a plate of something in front of him that had certainly not been there when the proprietor opened her door. As Cael sat, a girl from the kitchen came and put down a basket of bread between them. Then she brought a pot of mussels in a dark, buttery broth, and a dish of potatoes cut thick and salted with grey salt, and went away again. Nobody had asked her for any of it. Brom looked at the mussels with mild interest, as a man looks at weather he had expected.

"How?" said Cael.

"How what?"

"How do you get food without asking for it?"

Brom took a piece of bread and thought about the question honestly, as he thought about everything.

"I sit down," he said at last, "and I look like I'm going to be here a while."

Karis was beside Lira. Cael looked at her, and then looked again.

She was sitting on her hands.

She had them flat under her on the bench, palms down, both of them, and when she saw him see it she went faintly pink and took them out and put them on the table, and they lay there, empty. There was no notebook beside her plate. There was no document wallet at her feet or pencil behind her ear. She had come to the harbour with nothing whatever to write on, and she had found, by the look of her, that her hands did not know what to do about it.

"I've taken the day off," she said. "From all of it. Lira says I'm allowed."

"I said she had to," said Lira.

"It's harder than it looks," said Karis. She looked at her empty hands with something between amusement and alarm. "I keep reaching for things."

Cael knew what it cost her. Everybody at the table knew. A whole afternoon of things happening and nothing written down was not a rest to Karis. It was a day of her own life left unwritten, on purpose, and she was spending it on them.

---

Ephram came in a quarter of an hour later, out of breath, with grease on one cuff and a folded paper in his hand.

He had gone down to the harbour at first light to look at the lock gates, he said, because somebody at the guesting-house had told him they were two hundred years old and worked by hand, and he had wanted to see that for himself. The lock-keeper had caught him looking. By the time the morning was half gone he had been taken into the lock-house and shown the balance beams and the sluices and the great iron winding-gear. He had been told he was wrong about the date by sixty years, and given tea, and made to wind a gate himself under the lock-keeper's eye.

"It's balanced," he said, sitting down, still breathless. "The whole gate. It weighs as much as this building and it's balanced so exactly that one man can move it with one hand, if he moves it at the right time. If he moves it at the wrong time, nothing on earth can shift it." He unfolded the paper and laid it flat among the plates. "He gave me this."

It was a page torn from the lock-house book. On it, in a crabbed brown hand, were the watches for the week: names, hours, and in the margin, against two of the names, the word *late* underlined.

"You've brought home their duty roster," said Karis.

"He gave it to me."

"You asked for it."

"I asked how they kept the watches," said Ephram, with dignity. "He showed me. Then he tore it out." He smoothed it flat with the side of his hand, very carefully. "Two of them are always late. He's going to have words."

Lira laughed until she had to hold her wrapped arm still with her good hand, and Ephram folded his roster and put it inside his coat, next to his heart, and looked pleased with the whole world.

Seln came in through the blue door a little after that, with the parcel under his arm.

He crossed the shop to the long table. He put the parcel down in front of Cael, and laid a card and an envelope on top of it, and said, "The pouch. This morning's," in exactly the voice he used across the counter at home on the bluff.

"Thank you," said Cael.

Then Seln did what he had done at every meal the delegation had eaten in this city, or almost. His eyes went to the counter along the side wall, where there was a stool and a ledge and a view of the whole room. Cael saw them go there.

And Seln pulled out the end of the bench, next to Brom, and sat down at the table, and took a piece of bread.

For half a breath the table was very still.

Then it went on, and the way it went on was the best thing Cael had ever seen five people do without discussing it. Lira, in the middle of telling Ephram that he would not be allowed to wind anything else today, reached down the table without looking and set a clean cup in front of Seln and filled it from the cider jug, and went on talking. Brom moved the pot of mussels six inches toward the end of the bench, to where Seln could reach it, and said nothing. Ephram turned round and asked Seln, quite seriously, whether the office happened to know what the harbour's lock gates were made of, because the lock-keeper had said oak and Ephram thought he had seen iron, and Seln said, "Both," and Ephram said, "I knew it," and that was all.

Karis did not say anything. She looked at Seln once, with her empty hands on the table. Then she looked across the table at Cael, and he looked back, and neither of them looked away for a moment.

There were six of them at the table now.

Four of them knew both his ledgers, the one the panels kept and the one nobody did. Two of them had chosen, each for reasons of his own and never said, not to ask about the second one. And one of those two was sitting on the end of the bench with a cup of cider in front of him, at the table and not beside it, for the first time since Cael had known him.

Cael ate his fish. He kept his face on his plate. He put the moment away very carefully, in the place where he kept the few things he meant to have for the rest of his life.

"One minute each," she said. "The worst thing that happened to you this year. Not the worst bout. The worst *thing*. Cael times it." She pointed at him. "On Hesk's clock."

"Hesk's clock runs an hour fast."

"It's not the hour I'm worried about. It's the minute. Go on. Me first."

She got to the end before she had properly told the beginning, and had to go back for the beginning, and reached the end again just as Cael said "Minute," and sat back looking pleased.

"You told the end twice," said Karis.

"It's a good end."

Brom went next. He began with the room. He had got as far as the door opening when Cael said "Minute."

"I hadn't started," said Brom.

"You had the room," said Lira. "We all liked the room."

"The room's not the story."

"With you," said Karis, "the room is always the story. The story is how long we wait for it."

Ephram told the morning at the quarry town when his rehearsed entry turned out to have been sold by a steward at the yard gate before breakfast. He told it so that he was the fool of it from beginning to end, as he always did with his own stories, and they laughed at him and he laughed with them.

When it came to Karis she shook her head.

"I'm not doing this year," she said. "This year's in the notebooks, and the notebooks are at the guesting-house, and I'm not allowed them today."

"Then do another year," said Lira. "Do your first notebook. The very first one. You've never told us what was in it."

Karis looked out of the window at the harbour for so long that Cael thought she would refuse. Then she turned back.

"A heading," she said. "On the first page. *Things adults say that the paper does not support.*"

"How old?"

"Ten." Karis turned her cup a little on the wood with her fingertips. "I'd been given the book for arithmetic. I wrote my arithmetic in the front and the heading at the back, and worked toward the middle from both ends."

"And the first entry?"

"My mother. At the healer's, the year before." Karis's voice did not change at all. "*This won't hurt.* And underneath, in smaller writing: *It did. The healer said afterward that it would.*"

Nobody laughed. It was not that kind of story, and everybody at the table heard that it was not. Lira put her good hand on the wood near Karis's, not quite touching it, and Karis looked at the hand and left her own where it was.

"I've never once had to change the heading," Karis said.

The envelope was from Vell, and Lira said it would be a receipt before he had broken the seal, and it was.

It was a ledger of the working kind, bound, with nothing written in it. He had seen Hesk make books like it for other people, now and then, for a surveyor or a ship's master who needed a log that would last. He had watched the men come to the workshop to collect them, and turn them over in their hands for a long time before they paid. He had never thought of having one himself.

The covers were a soft grey-brown leather over board, with a single fine line pressed round the edge of each and nothing else at all. The corners were capped in brass, to save them on tables. The paper inside was heavy and creamy and a little rough to the fingertip, and when he opened the book in the middle and took his hands away, it lay flat on the scarred plank by itself and did not stir. He bent and looked along the spine. It had been sewn so that it would do that for as long as the paper lasted.

Tucked inside the front cover was a slip of the workshop's paper, and on it, in Hesk's square upright hand, four short sentences.

*Seventeen. My trade says an instrument is finished when it measures true — not when it looks done. You measure true. Let the rest of them look. — H.*

He read it once. Then he put his hand flat over it on the page, and kept it there, and looked out of the window at the water, because for a little while he could not have said anything to anybody.

After a moment Lira reached across and took the note out gently from under his hand. She read it to herself. Then she read it aloud, quietly, to the table, and nobody said anything after she had finished. Every one of them had been weighed on instruments that looked finished and were not, at one time or another, by people who had been quite certain of the weight.

The book went round.

Brom held it last of the three, turning it in his wrapped hands with great care, so as not to mark the leather. He opened it and shut it and listened to the sound it made shutting, which was a soft heavy sound like a door closing in a good house.

"He made that to be used," said Brom. "Hard. For a long time." He handed it back. "He thinks you've got a long time of writing in you."

Lira held a leaf up to the window so that the harbour light came through it, and showed them the grain. It was better paper, she said, than anything she had ever been allowed to write on at Halcenvane, and she was going to steal some.

Karis took it last and kept it longest.

She turned the leaves one at a time with the tips of her fingers. The ruling was very faint, a pale grey, about two dozen lines to a page. And a finger's width in from the outside of every leaf ran a second line, ruled from top to bottom, so that every page in the book had a narrow column standing apart at its side.

"He's left you a margin," said Karis.

"For notes."

"For arguing with yourself." She ran a fingertip down the narrow column, top to bottom. "He's never read a word you've written and he knows you'll write something down and come back a year later and want to quarrel with it. So he's ruled you somewhere to do the quarrelling, where it won't spoil the page." She shut the book and set it back in front of him, squared to the edge of the table. "It's exactly right. I'd have designed it myself, and written a memorandum to go with it."

Seln had watched the book go round from the end of the bench and had not reached for it. Now he set his cup down.

"The office has signed for a good many parcels since the bluff," he said. "Kit. Floor declarations. Two crates of the chancellor's papers and one of Gault's instruments, which he would not let out of his sight on the coach. The office does not as a rule mind handing parcels over." He looked at the book on the table in front of Cael, and then, for a moment, at Cael. "That is the first one it has been sorry to hand over."

He sat with the book on his knees for the last of the afternoon, while the light went round on the water, and thought about sixteen.

Sixteen had been at Halcenvane, at the end of an ordinary long day, at the house's long table under a good lamp. Three people had decided that the day mattered, and gone to some trouble over it, and he had not known before that night that anybody but Hesk could keep his day for him. That had been the first one, which proved the day could be kept.

Seventeen had not had to prove anything. It had been a blue door and a scarred table and a cake, and six people, and nobody in the whole city taking his measure for one whole afternoon.

He did not count anything. He let the read lie still, the way a man lets his arm lie still after carrying something heavy all day. He sat at the window with the fourth of Gault's four days ending quietly in his hip and the book warm on his knees, and found that he did not want anything at all that he did not already have.

When they left, the six of them walked back along the quays together toward the river gate, in a line across the stones, as people walk who have eaten well and are in no hurry. Lira was in the middle with her arm in its wrap. Ephram was at the water's edge, looking at the lock gates. Seln walked at the end of the line, on the land side, with his hands in the pockets of his plain brown coat.

He was not behind them. He was at the end. Cael noticed the difference, and noticed that nobody else said anything about it, and did not say anything either.

# Chapter 45 — Again

He wrote the day in the old volume first, because the old volume was still the one he lived in.

It was nearly full, and he had known for weeks that it would run out somewhere on the road home, and he had been rationing it, a few lines a day, small. Tonight he gave it four. *Rest day. The blue door, all six. Brom's cake, paid three days early. Seln at the table.* He looked at the last three words for a while, and did not add anything to them, and shut the book.

Then he opened Hesk's.

He had not written in it yet. He had carried it back from the harbour on his knees in a hired cart and up the stair under his arm, and set it on the table under the window, and walked round it twice, and had a wash, and come back, and it was still there. It lay open in the middle by itself, as it had at the cookshop. He turned back to the first leaf. The paper took the lamp's light softly and gave nothing back.

Every new book of accounts begins by saying what is in the till. Hesk had taught him that before he could spell *accounts*.

He wrote slowly, in his best hand, with Hesk's steel pen.

*Seventeen. The first page of Hesk's book, on the rest day in the middle of the sitting. Kept at the blue door by everybody who matters, and written here by me, as I was taught to write down the numbers nobody else was keeping. Somebody kept this one. That's the first thing this book knows.*

*What I carry, at seventeen. Six, and the one I can't account for.*

*Lira's burst. Six free on the sprung oak; four a day on this city's stone. Against Rhagen I ran eleven relocations in one exchange and only two of them were bursts. Feet carried the rest. The landing beat is no shorter than it was at the year's open.*

*The push I took from the Bronze at Ardenmere. Flat on two plates on the bluff and one in a felt bag at the mill town. Four blows a sitting, paid in the right shoulder. Not asked for since the mill town's plate.*

*Brom's read. Weight and direction, early. It paid for every Silver exchange I won this week. It can't see a weave, and it can't see a price. It sees what's heavy.*

*Reydan's give. Not on a scored floor since Ardenmere. Nobody official has seen it. I held it still at a handspan, twice, in a pocket of stone at the north gate with a Silver's hand coming down. It would have worked. It stays where it is.*

*Karis's spark, by her leave, in her hand. Four instants on a copper pot. Two contacts a bout on a public floor, which is our rule, not theirs. Both spent on Rhagen, both on purpose. Four days of a pink hand.*

*The quiet thing from the stair. A year, near enough. Never used, anywhere, by anyone's count. Rent from waking, spent or not. Drifts in the gaps, never in the work, fewer this month than last. Useful where: nowhere yet. Still the second year of that answer.*

*And at the foot of the list, the still place from session nine. I turned it over again tonight, as I do at every opening and every closing, to see whether I can call it back or say why it came. I can't do either.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

*One stamp, renewed. One enrollment, lawful. Half a tournament behind me and half to come. And one question I opened at the rail the night of the captain and haven't shut, about which tier is mine. It goes into this book first, ahead of everything that comes after it.*

He sat back and let the ink dry in the tooth of the paper.

It looked right. It looked like a page that would still be there to argue with in a year, and he found, reading it over, that he had already found one line he wanted to argue with, and that the narrow ruled margin was exactly the right width to do it in. He did not write in the margin, not tonight. He shut the book on its first page and put his hand on the cover for a moment, and then went down, because the draw posted at dusk and nobody read a draw alone.

Lira did not race her.

Lira had lost to her eight days ago on the second floor, four to nine, at a cost she had listed out loud in front of everybody, and she had spent every hour since learning what the cost had bought. She did not go to meet Zerin anywhere. She let Zerin have the flank every time. She gave her the outside of the floor, all of it, and went instead to the ground behind her, the ground Zerin had just come from and would need to come back through. Zerin did a great deal of very fast running in the second exchange, nearly all of it along the rail, and almost none of it near anything that was being decided.

The two Silvers would not go near Brom's gap. It was the correct answer, and they had ten years of trials behind it, and it held. The price of it was distance. To stay out of the gap they had to go round the barrier's ends, and the ends were a long way off, and every time they went round, the northern triangle was a pair short.

Daeva did her part.

That was all she did. She came out of the east gate with the others and went where Auremont's caller sent her, and did what she was sent to do, quickly and well. Once, near the north-east ramp, she touched Ephram as he crossed from one platform to the other, low on the hip, so cleanly that he hardly felt it, and he went back to his gate to touch the post with an expression of honest astonishment. She did nothing else that anybody could see. She played inside the squad's game, by its rules, like a piece on a board.

Cael watched her the whole time, as a sailor watches a cloud on the far edge of the sea.

Late in the exchange Ephram did his one thing.

He did it so quietly that Cael was the only person on the floor who saw it begin. Ephram was back from the gate, on the north-east platform, holding it alone. Both Silvers were coming for it from the far side of the barrier, the long way, round the south-east end. They would arrive together. A platform held by one against two is a platform lost.

Ephram stepped off it.

He stepped down the ramp and walked away from it, toward the west, with nobody near him and nothing making him go, and he called one word over his shoulder to the rest of them. "*South.*"

The Silvers came up the ramp onto an empty platform and took it, as anybody would. An empty platform is to be taken; every book on the continent says so. But the north-east platform sat right beside the second gap in the barrier, and Brom was still standing in the gap, and the two of them could not leave the platform without walking past him. So they stood on it, both of them, two Silvers holding one platform with a very large man six feet away.

Meanwhile Ephram and Lira went down through the first gap into Halcenvane's own country, to the south platform. Auremont's pair had left it long ago, because it cost double what it scored and they had been needed elsewhere, and now the two of them came down its long ramp and stood on it. Karis held the north-west in her frame. Cael ran the middle.

For the last three minutes of the second exchange Auremont held one platform with two fighters, and Halcenvane held two platforms with three.

It came down to the wire. When the bell went and the marshals' boards came up, the second exchange was Halcenvane's on the objective count, by a handful of quarter-minutes.

Two exchanges. On a sanctioned floor, in front of the whole city, with every scout on the continent in the tiers, the five of them had been the best squad on the floor for two exchanges running. It was on the record. It had been counted.

At the rail Rooke had taken off his coat, which nobody had ever seen him do at a rail, and was holding it in both hands as if he had forgotten what it was for.

The galleries had gone very quiet.

---

Ilsev had been writing steadily since the first call.

She sat in the first seat of the Compact's row with the observation form for the team trial squared on her lap-desk, and she wrote as she always wrote, in the even hand her office had trained into her over a great many years, a line for every event the form had a place for. The form had ten columns, one for each fighter on the floor, five a side. At the head of each column the office's clerk had copied the fighter's name and house and the registry's classification, so that an observer could weigh what she saw against what the registry said she ought to be seeing.

Nine of the columns had a tier at the head of them. The tenth had the registry's word for him, in its square brackets, as it was printed on every paper about him in the Compact's files.

She had written the registry's word herself on her own forms more times than she could count. She had written beside it, every time, on every form she had ever filed concerning him, the same short note about the watch level on his sheet. *Authorizing office: not traced.* She had written it on a referral, once, and then on a second referral, and the second was still pending somewhere above her desk.

She had written the first exchange into the ten columns as it happened. Touches, positions, platform holds, the quarter-minutes as the marshals called them. It had been perfectly possible. It was what the form was for.

It was in the second exchange that the form began to go wrong.

Not wrong. Ilsev did not use that word lightly, and the form was not wrong. Everything she wrote in it was true. But she found, partway through the second exchange, that she was writing the same event into four columns and then into a fifth. The big Copper in the gap had not stopped the Silvers. He had cost them a stride, and the Ember girl's frame had cost them a ramp, and the Wind girl had been on the far side of the ramp half a beat early, and the girl had been sent there by a single word from the boy in the middle. The caller had made a trade on the north-east platform that she would have marked, if she had been examining a cadet, as an error.

There was no column on the form for that. The thing that was happening on the floor did not belong to any one of the five fighters in plain colours. It belonged to all of them at once, and her form had no place to put a thing that belonged to five people.

At the bell for the end of the second exchange, her pen stopped.

She looked down at it. It had stopped in the middle of a line, in the fifth column, under the registry's word.

She did not start it again at once. She sat with it lifted a little off the page and looked at the floor, where the five in plain colours were walking back to their gate, and she was aware, very precisely, as she was aware of everything in her own conduct, that she had never in her working life stopped writing at an interval because she did not know what to write.

Along the row, past Havel and the rotating seats, the twelfth chair stood empty again behind its rail-cloth. The Archmarshal had been in it two days before, for one bout. He had written twice and gone. She had not spoken to him. Nobody in the row had. She found herself wondering, for no reason she could have put in a report, whether he would have wanted to see this, and what he would have written in a book with no columns in it at all.

The referee called the squads to their gates.

Ilsev looked at the half-finished line under the bracketed word, and left it as it was, and turned to a fresh line for the third exchange.

---

The third exchange began at the barrier's south-east end, and Cael felt it before anybody on the floor saw anything at all.

Daeva had come out of the east gate with the others, as she had in the first two, and gone round the end of the barrier past the timber stack into the corner. She was standing there when the call came. She was standing very close to the barrier's face, at its foot, at the place where the long straight line of timber began.

The read caught something wrong.

It was not her weight. Her weight was ordinary, set, a fighter waiting at a corner. It was the air along the barrier. For one breath the read, which had only ever read bodies, gave Cael the feeling of weight somewhere there was no body at all: a heaviness laid along the whole face of the timber, from her corner toward the far one, as though the air beside the wall had been pressed flat and was waiting to snap back.

Then she was in it.

She did not run along the barrier. Cael would think about the word for a long time afterward, and *run* was not it. The air along the timber's face folded, all the way down its length, into a long narrow lane, and she went down the lane as a stone goes down a well. There was a crack at its head like a whip, and a smell like a struck flint, and a pale flicker of light that was gone before he could look at it. She covered the barrier's length faster than anything on the tables of any tier he had ever read.

And all at once the whole floor was something else.

It was not that Halcenvane's game had been beaten. It had been stepped over. The gaps Brom had priced, the ramps Karis had made dear, the corners Lira bought early and the calls Ephram counted from behind the stacks: every one of them was a thing that happened on the floor. They were roads and gates and tolls. The lane did not use the floor at all, not the way they did. It went along the diagonal, the one line nobody had treated as anything but a wall, and it went past every road and gate the five of them had built, the way a river in flood goes past a field's hedges.

She came out of the lane at the north-west end and took the north-west platform.

Ephram was on it, calling the next rotation, and he had barely begun when the lane put her at the top of his ramp. Her hand touched his shoulder. The platform was Auremont's, and what he was still saying was about a floor that had gone. He walked to his gate and put his hand on the post and walked back, and called again from behind the stack, at once and correctly, and by the time it reached anybody it was wrong again.

From the middle Cael could see quite clearly that there was nothing wrong with Ephram's calls. The floor they described simply kept ceasing to exist.

She went to Lira next.

Lira was at the north-east ramp. Everything Lira had built in her fighting life was built on one thing, which was getting to the place a half-beat before it mattered, and that needed one other thing, which was that the beat should be knowable. Lira read the lane coming. She went early, to the right place, with her one word in her mouth. And the lane was there before early. Daeva touched her on the back as she arrived, and was gone. Lira went to the gate and touched the post and came back, and did the right thing again, and it was the right thing again, and it was no use again.

Cael, watching from the middle, felt something he could only call pride.

She went past Brom.

That was the strangest of all of it. Brom was in his gap, the second one, where he had stood since the first call, and the lane went along the barrier's face right past him, four feet off, so close that the air of it pulled at his linen. It never came into his gap. It did not need to. It was not going through the barrier. It was going along it. Brom stood in his gate, and the road went by his gate without using it.

In the middle of the exchange Brom stepped out of the gap.

He did it of his own accord. Nobody called it. He looked at the lane going by, and at the gap he had held for two and a half exchanges, and he understood that the gap did not matter any longer, and he left it and went south to the platform in their own country to be of some use there. Cael had known Brom for a long time. He had never once seen him leave a place he had decided to hold. He thought it was the bravest and most expensive thing he had ever watched him do.

She went to Karis.

Karis was in her frame at the north-west ramp, which was Auremont's again now, and had been made dear for nothing. Her frame priced the ways up to the platform, and the lane did not come up the ramp. It came down the length of the floor and off the end of the barrier and round, from nowhere a ramp was. Karis saw it come. She stood in her frame with her scorched hands open, and the touch landed on her arm, and she walked back to the gate.

Cael watched her the whole way. She did not look once at the frame she had planted and paid for twice and lost. She looked at the lane. She looked at all of it, the whole length of the barrier, both ends, as she walked, and her face had the stillness it had when she was reading a clause for the first time. She was taking notes without a notebook.

For a little more than a minute the squad stopped being a squad. It was five people standing in different parts of a floor, and she came to each of them in turn, like a caller working down a street.

*It isn't that she's better than the five of us,* Cael thought, in the one line his head had room for. *She's going in a direction we haven't got.*

And then the lane turned toward him.

Afterward he could never decide if she had kept him till the end on purpose, or if he had simply been the last one she reached. He spent a whole page on it later and wrote at the bottom, honestly, *Don't know.*

He was in the middle, near the oak round, just south of the barrier's centre, where Ephram had sent him at the start of the exchange to watch both gaps. He felt the air begin to fold along the timber toward him, the long flat pressing weight that should not have been there, gathering. The crack of the lane's head. The flint smell.

The read went further than it had ever gone.

He could feel it going. All year it had read bodies, men's weight and direction a fraction early. All afternoon it had been reaching past them, for the heaviness along the wall that was not a body. Now, with the lane coming down the diagonal at him faster than he had ever seen anything move, he pushed it as far as it would go. It went into the air itself, into the place where the weight was pressed flat and about to snap back, and for the first time it read something that was not a person.

It gave him half a second.

Half a second of warning before the lane arrived. Against anybody else on the continent half a second would have bought him the whole exchange. Against her it bought him two steps.

He went sideways off his mark, two steps, not a burst, nothing on any sheet but feet. The lane came down the diagonal and resolved where he had been standing, on the stone beside the oak round, and spent itself there on nothing. The touch that had found three of the best fighters he had ever known, and passed the fourth by, found empty floor.

He was at the lane's edge, upright, inside the wash of it.

The air pushed at him. His ears popped. His hair stood up off his neck and arms, all of it, and the flint smell was so strong he could taste it. He was close enough to feel how much there was in the lane, how much more of it she was keeping on a leash, and he stood at the edge of it with his hands open and did not move.

And Daeva stopped.

Not for a beat. For one whole second, in the middle of the third exchange of a continental semifinal, with eight thousand people on their feet and roaring, she stopped. She stood at the mouth of the lane she had made, a stride from him, perfectly still.

She looked at him.

It was not how anybody had ever looked at him. Wardens had looked at him, and evaluators, and every registry instrument on the continent, and all of them had been looking for something to write down. She was not. She was reading him. She was looking at him the way he looked at things: taking him in, all of him, weight and stance and where his eyes were, carefully and without any hurry, as an instrument looks at something that is not on its tables and decides, in front of everybody, to stop and find out what it is.

Cael looked back. He did not know what she saw.

One second.

Then she was gone, back into the trial as if she had never left it, and the rest of the third exchange was hers by a long way.

---

In the fourth exchange she let the lane go.

She came out of the east gate with the others and went back to being a piece on the board, quick and quiet and doing what she was sent to do. Whatever she had come to the third exchange to find out, she seemed to have found it.

And the five in plain colours did a thing almost nobody in the building understood.

They had been taken apart in front of the whole Concourse, one at a time, by something none of them had any answer for. Any squad in the trial's long history would have had every reason to come out for the fourth exchange broken.

They came out and put themselves back together.

Cael felt the read come back up to its ordinary depth and settle, and he began giving out the words again from the middle, and the others began answering them. Brom went back to the barrier, but not to his gap. He took a place two strides wide of it, where he could cover the gap and the barrier's face both, and priced the Silvers again from there. Karis planted her frame once more, at the north-east ramp, and planted it with a gap in it, a way through that she could close with one step, so that nothing coming from any direction could make the whole frame worthless at once. Lira stopped going early and began going *ready*, a little back, with her weight even, which cost her ground and saved her from being visited. And Ephram called, from the north-west ramp and then from behind the stack, in exactly the voice he had used in the first exchange, level and clear, counting aloud when he could not see.

It was not enough. It was close.

Once, near the end, Zerin came along the north rail at full speed on a flank run, and her line crossed Cael's in the middle. He saw her see him. She read him for two full beats, at a speed that should not have allowed anybody to read anything, and he saw her decide. She did not try him. She went round, wide, and spent her speed on Ephram's platform instead.

*An economist,* Cael thought, *who won't buy what she can't price.* He filed it. It was the first thing Auremont had ever written about him in real time, and it said: *unknown cost, go round.*

Auremont took the fourth exchange on the objective count by a few quarter-minutes.

The marshals' boards came up. The referee read them out.

Two exchanges to Halcenvane. Two to Auremont. And the trial, by the rules Rooke had read out once in the hired hall, went to the whole objective ledger. The third exchange alone had given Auremont more quarter-minutes than the first two had given Halcenvane together.

Auremont advanced to the final. Halcenvane would meet the other beaten semifinalist for third place.

The Concourse stood up.

It was not the cheer for a winner. Cael heard the difference at once. It was a long, deep, steady roar that went on and on, from every tier of the bowl, and every one of the eight thousand was on their feet for it, and it was for the house in plain colours that had lost. Every steward Cael spoke to afterward told him the same thing. The city stood like that for a beaten house perhaps twice in a cycle, and only when it had watched something it meant to remember.

Cael stood at the west gate with the other four and the Shield reserve, in the noise, and looked up at the bowl.

Somewhere along the north-tunnel wall, he knew, the exhibition frame still hung on its pegs, with the captain's line on it and the lake man's, and every line below them empty. After this afternoon, he thought, they would stay empty. There was nobody below Gold left in this city who would stand across a ring from him for the pleasure of finding out.

The category had waited three hundred years for a bout that needed it. It had got two. It was not going to get a third from anybody who still had a tier to lose.

---

Rooke stopped them in the tunnel.

It was half dark under the north tiers, and the roar of the bowl came down through the stone over their heads as a long dull thunder. He stood in their way with his coat over his arm and waited until all six of them, the five and the white-faced reserve, had stopped walking and were looking at him.

"Before anybody says a word to anybody," said Rooke. "The third exchange wasn't a squad problem."

Nobody said anything.

"I want that understood before you get to the stair, because by the stair you'll all have started blaming yourselves, and I won't have it." He looked from one of them to the next. "Nothing a squad does answers the third. Nothing a bracket fighter does, either. I've stood at a great many rails, and I've never once seen one fighter take a whole scenario floor away from nine others, and nobody else in this building has either. Every coach up there is writing that down right now in his own hand." He shifted the coat on his arm. "So nobody reviews it. Not you, and not me. The first two, and the fourth, we'll take apart tonight, and you'll be proud of every piece. The third goes in the file as weather."

He stood aside, and they went past him up the tunnel toward the light.

That night he did not hold a review at all, not in the way he usually held one. He passed his coaching sheet round the long table instead, face up, with a pencil, and told each of them to write one line on it, their own finding, and pass it on.

Lira wrote *Ready. Not early.* and passed it to Brom.

Brom took a long time over his. He wrote *Left the gap. Should have left it sooner.* and looked at it, and pushed the sheet on to Karis without a word.

Karis wrote *A frame with a door in it,* very small, and then, under that, *She came from where no ramp was. Think about that.* She gave it to Ephram.

Ephram wrote *South.* and nothing else, and went red, and passed it to Cael.

Cael held the pencil over the sheet for a while. Then he wrote: *The diagonal. I had it on paper this morning and didn't know what it was.* He gave the sheet back to Rooke.

Rooke read all five lines, slowly, at the head of the table. He did not say anything about any of them. He folded the sheet into the coaching file, and closed the file, and sat with his hand flat on the cover of it for a moment longer than he needed to.

"That'll do," he said. "That'll do very well. Eat something."

---

Ephram found him later in the courtyard, by the pump, where Cael had gone down to put his right hand under the cold water, though it no longer needed it.

Ephram did not say anything at first. He worked the pump handle for him, three long strokes, and stood back, and waited until Cael had dried his hand on his shirt.

"At Halcenvane," he said, "I told myself I was going to be careful how I remembered it. All of it. You, especially." He looked at the water running away across the cobbles. "I have been careful. I've watched you from the rail for a year, and I've waited for a floor where I'd see it for myself, whatever it is, with nobody telling me what I was looking at. Today I was on the floor." He looked up. "And the best fighter in this city stopped. In the middle of a semifinal, winning, with eight thousand people watching, she stopped to look at you. Like we look at you. Like you look at everything."

He put out his hand.

"I'm done waiting. Whatever you are, I'm glad it's on my side of the floor."

Cael took the hand and shook it.

Ephram let go and stood back, looking faintly embarrassed, as he always did when he had said something he meant.

"I wrote most of that on the road to the mill town," he said. "It's been in my file book ever since, waiting for an afternoon to go with it. It had four more sentences and Lira cut them."

"When did Lira see it?"

"At the waystation. I asked her to." Ephram considered. "She said it was a good speech with a sermon stuck on the end."

He wrote it that night in Hesk's book, the third entry, under the inventory and the line about tomorrow.

*Team trial, semifinal. Two exchanges each, and the ledger to Auremont on the third. For two of the four, the five of us were the best squad in the building, and the marshals counted it, and it's in their book now with ours. Last night I wrote down what the tournament was for, and today it turned out to be true.*

*And the third exchange was hers.*

*She stopped mid-trial to read me. Gold Rank 3, nineteen years old, and the first person at this tournament whose attention feels like mine. Filed under: things that are going to happen whether I schedule them or not.*

He sat looking at the page. Then, for the first time, he wrote in the margin Hesk had ruled for him. It was one word, very small, beside the line about the third exchange.

*Half.*

He was still looking at it when somebody knocked on the open door.

It was Lira, in her coat, with the evening's broadside folded small in her good hand and her left arm out of its wrap at last. She did not come in. She leaned in the doorway and held up the broadside.

"They've got the third exchange wrong," she said. "All of it. They think she missed."

"She didn't miss."

"I know she didn't. I was there." She looked at him for a moment, at the book open in front of him and the word in the margin, which she could not have read from the door. Then she grinned, the grin she kept for weather worth going out in.

"Third-place trial first," said Lira.

He waited. She made him.

"Then your storm."

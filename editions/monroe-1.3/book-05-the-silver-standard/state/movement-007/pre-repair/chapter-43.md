# Chapter 43 — Twice

The Archmarshal Vastin had reached the city the evening before, by the post road, with one bag.

He had not told anybody he was coming, because nobody had asked, and because he had not been sure himself until the morning he left. The west tower's notice about the exhibition ring had come to his desk among the liaison papers, copied by fast post as everything about Norhold was copied to him now, since he had ordered the files. *The ring to be raised when the brackets clear the main floor. Bouts to be heard in order of filing.* He had read it at his table with the pigeons outside, and turned it face down, and turned it up again. Then he had written in his day book *Norhold, on observation. Not requested by any office,* and told his clerk he would be away for a week, and packed.

He had missed the first bout by two days. He had known he would. The roads did not care what a man had decided at his table.

At the inn under the outer wall he had read the row's report of it by a poor lamp, while the post-horses were led round to the yard below. It was a competent report, in Ilsev's office's careful even hand, and it gave him the exchanges in order and the figures, thirty and twenty-seven, and the duration, and a note on the crowd. Near the end there was one line that was not like the others. *Subject's footwork in the third exchange not previously recorded.*

He had read that line twice. Then he had put the report away and gone to bed, and lain awake for some time listening to the yard.

In the morning he went to the Concourse by the east stair, at the hour the row's officers went up, in the grey travelling coat of an inspector, with nothing on it to say what he was.

A steward stood at the head of the stair. She was a young woman with a list on a board, and she looked at him once as he came up and did not look at her list at all.

"This way, Archmarshal," she said.

He had not given his name. He had not given his grade, and his coat did not give it either.

He followed her along the back of the gallery, past the public benches and behind the row's plated chairs, to the end, where the grey rail-cloth ran on in front of a chair with a pale square on its back and two empty screw holes. She lifted the cloth for him and let it fall again when he had sat down, and went back to her stair.

He sat and folded his hands on his book.

He had read the seating plan in his room, a week before the opening. He had written one line in his day book about it and declined to think the long thought that came after the line. He declined it again now. A held chair was a courtesy. A steward who knew a man's grade by his face had been told it by somebody, and that was all that could be said about it with the evidence in hand, and he had spent forty years refusing to say more than the evidence would hold.

He noticed, though, that he had chosen to sit in it. He could have stood at the back of the public gallery. He could have taken a bench among the city's people and nobody would have known him.

He had come up the east stair instead and let the steward lead him to the chair somebody else had held for him, and he could not have said, sitting in it, whether he had done that to see who would notice, or simply because it was the best seat from which to watch a floor.

Along the row, the officers had noticed. He did not look at them. He knew Ilsev's back as he knew his own handwriting, and he could feel the angle of her lap-desk change without turning his head.

He watched the ring.

---

He watched the boy come out of the north tunnel carefully.

There was no limp, quite. There was a boy walking as a man walks across a room he knows has a loose board in it, keeping his weight on one side and not letting anybody see him keep it. Vastin had seen a great many people walk like that into a great many rooms, and very few of them had been seventeen.

He watched the showman from the lake house bow to everybody in the building, and he watched the bout begin.

He did not watch the showman. The whole bowl was watching the showman, and the showman was very good, and there was nothing to learn from looking where eight thousand other people were already looking.

Vastin watched the boy, as he had sat four feet from him in a room twelve feet by fourteen and watched him while he answered.

In the first exchange the boy was not where the blows landed, and Vastin saw how. He was not quick. Not that afternoon. He was early. He went to the right place a moment before there was any reason in the world to go there.

In the second exchange the boy did something Vastin had never seen done on a floor.

He declined to be told a story.

The showman built his passages as such men did, toward an ending, and the whole bowl leaned toward each ending as it came.

The boy did not lean. He stood on his good leg and let each passage come, and at the moment before its end, when even Vastin felt himself waiting for the last word, the boy was quietly somewhere else. He did it again and again. He did it at no speed at all, at the pace of a clerk turning pages. And the showman, who was a Silver and an artist, came slowly apart in front of him, sentence by sentence, until the boy stepped in through a half-beat in which the man's weight had nowhere to go and touched him on the arm.

Nothing in it was hidden. That was what held Vastin's eye. Every piece of it was done in the open, on the floor, in front of everyone, inside the rule. And not one person in eight thousand had been looking at it.

He opened his book and wrote.

*Second exchange. He refuses the other man's time. Openly, within the rule, and at some cost to himself. Nobody in the bowl is watching that part.*

He read it back, as he read every line, and it was true. He closed the book.

He watched the third exchange with his hands folded on the closed book.

He saw the showman put the show down and fight, and he saw that the man was better without it, a good deal better, and that the boy, on one leg, had to work very hard to stay out of his way. He saw the light from the high windows move across the floor. He saw the boy keep out of it for most of an exchange, at a price, and then be driven into it by three plain cuts, and stand there with the glare full in his face.

He saw the touch land.

He had been watching the boy's face, not the showman's sword, and he saw something on it in the instant before the touch that he could not read. It was not surprise. It was not fear. It was very quick, and it was gone, and he did not write it down, because he did not know what it was, and a thing he did not know could not go into a book as though he did.

In the fourth exchange he watched the showman give up the plain fight and throw one last long beautiful passage at the north rail, for no reason that had anything to do with winning, and he watched the boy step in under it and end the bout.

He opened the book again before the flags had come down.

*Close. Everything I saw today, I already had a word for.*

He read that back too. It was true. It was true in a way that he found, sitting in the held chair with the bowl on its feet around him, he would have to think about for some time.

He thought about three words on a page that had come down to him under a seal from an office above his own, with no routing slip and no initials. *Mitigation. Containment. Trajectory.*

He had answered them at length, in his own language, and been told his answers had been entered. He thought about the digest, and the row's report, and the files in their two boxes, and the seating plan with his grade on it in a stranger's hand. Paper about paper. Some of it competent and some of it careful and all of it written for somebody to read somewhere else, by people who had been told what it was for.

What he had written in his book that afternoon had been written for nobody. He had seen the thing himself, and written down what he saw, in the words he had for it, and the words had been enough.

Below him the panel was beginning its arithmetic. In eleven minutes or so a figure would go up on the frame by the north tunnel, and it would be a good figure, honestly reached by five honest judges, and it would tell anyone who read it exactly what the Standard thought of the afternoon.

He did not want it.

He knew himself well enough to know what he would do if he stayed.

He would read the figure. He would set his own two lines beside it, and he would find, as one always found, that the figure made the lines look a little different. A little smaller, or a little larger, or a little less certain. He had read figures for forty years. He had never once read one without letting it lean on whatever he had seen before it.

So he stood up while the clerks were still at their slates.

He went along the back of the row without hurrying, behind the plated chairs, past Ilsev, who did not turn, and past Havel, who did. He nodded to Havel. He went down the east stair, where the young steward stood aside for him with her list against her chest, and out by the side door into the street, where the afternoon was going gold over the roofs.

He did not know what the boy was. He noticed, walking back toward the outer wall through the crowd, that he had not written that down either. It was not a finding. It was only the place where the findings stopped.

He would go home in the morning, by the post road. There was a calendar on his desk, and a great deal of it would want moving.

---

Cael gave the table the fact over supper, and one sentence with it.

"The Archmarshal came himself," he said. "He reads every piece of paper anybody sends him, and he left it all on his desk and came to look."

The table took that in. Nobody asked how he knew it was Vastin; he had described that stillness to them often enough over the year, and Karis had already had the rest of it from Lira, who had watched the twelfth chair through the whole bout from the north rail instead of watching the floor, and had told everybody afterward, with some indignation, that she had missed the best touch of the afternoon on Cael's account.

"He wrote twice," said Lira. "Once after the touch in the second. And once more after the last touch, while the flags were still going up. Then he got up and went, before the figure. He didn't even look at the board."

"That's not like a Compact man," said Ephram.

"No," said Karis. "It's not."

At the far end of the table Seln had been squaring the evening's papers into the travel file, as he did every night. He finished. He fitted the last sheet under its clip. Then, instead of closing the file, he laid one hand flat on top of it.

The table knew that gesture by now. It meant that the next sentence would be short, and in the office's voice, and that it would be the only one.

"The office notes that the chair was held for him for thirteen days before he sat in it," said Seln. "The stewards' book at the east stair carried his grade against that seat on the first morning of the sitting. His own office's notice of travel reached the west tower yesterday at noon." He did not raise his voice, and he did not lower it. "The Archmarshal did not announce himself. Somebody announced it for him."

Nobody said anything.

Seln closed the file and took his hand off it, and went back to being a man at the end of a table who kept the papers. The table let him. It sat for a while with what he had said, and then, because it was supper and there was bread to pass, it began to talk again about other things, a little more quietly than before.

*He evaluates,* Cael wrote that night. *That's all he has ever done, as far as I know him. He sat four feet from me for a day and asked me eleven questions and wrote down what I said. He wrote a note about me that I believe says I'm not a mistake, and he signed it with his whole name.*

*Today he crossed the country to sit in a chair somebody else saved for him, and watched one bout, and wrote twice, and left before the number.*

*A man who trusted the papers that reach his desk would have stayed at his desk. A man who trusted the panel would have waited for its figure. He did neither. Whatever reaches him about me now, he doesn't think it's enough. He's started checking it with his own eyes.*

*Seln says the stewards had his grade against that chair two weeks before his own clerk told anybody he was travelling. Somebody kept him a chair for thirteen days and told a steward his grade. I can't do anything with that. Nobody at this table can. But I'd like it written down that the chair was full today, and that the man in it went home without our figure, and that I'm seventeen in about two hours.*

He sat and looked at the last line for a while. Then he closed the book and went to bed.

---

He woke late.

It was the first time in the whole sitting.

The guesting-house was quiet in a way he had not heard it, with no feet on the stair and no keeper counting, and the light on the ceiling was the yellow light of the middle of the morning. For a moment he did not know what was wrong. Then he remembered that nothing was wrong at all. It was the rest day. The Concourse was shut. Nobody in the whole city was going to measure anybody until tomorrow.

And he was seventeen.

He had turned it some time in the night, between one day and the next, in a borrowed bed in a city of strangers. He had known it was coming. Every night for a week the date at the head of the Log's page had been one nearer, and he had watched it come without saying so. But knowing a thing was coming and lying in a bed on the morning of it were not the same, and he lay for a while and let the second one be what it was.

The hip had stiffened again in the night, but less. It was the fourth of Gault's four days. He swung his legs out of bed carefully, and the joint complained, and then, when he stood, thought better of it.

There was a note on the common-room table, propped against the water jug, in Lira's quick, tilted hand.

*Blue door. Past the third wharf, on the harbour side; you can smell it from the bridge. Midday. Everybody's coming, so you are too. Many happy returns, in case nobody else has thought to say it. — L.*

He read it twice and put it inside his coat, next to the notebook.

He gave himself the walk.

It was the only present he had thought of for himself, and it was a good one. He went down through the quarter and out by the river gate and along the quays toward the harbour, slowly, because of the hip, with his coat open, and for once he did not walk anywhere for any reason. He looked at things because they were there.

Norhold on its day off was another place altogether. He had known the tournament's Norhold for two weeks, the Norhold of boards and criers and broadsides and the long roar out of the Concourse, and he had thought that was Norhold. It was not. It was a costume the city put on every three years. Underneath it, on the rest day, the real place went on with its own business at its own pace.

Barges were being warped along the quays by men who had nowhere to be until evening.

The lock gates at the harbour's mouth were working, slow and old, for nobody but the gulls. A wedding party was crossing the fish market in its best clothes, with the bride's brothers carrying the chairs. On the steps of the cloth-hall two girls were teaching a smaller girl to whistle through her fingers, and failing, and laughing so hard at their own failure that the smaller one had sat down on the step to wait for them.

But the city's arithmetic did not stop for the rest day. He saw it everywhere, once he began to look.

At the second wharf there was a hiring board, a plain black board with chalk on it, and a dozen men stood in front of it reading. *Grain lighter. Six hands. Copper will do. Iron preferred. Dinner found.* Under that, *Timber, the long dock, two days. Iron or better.* None of the men reading it had ever stood on a tournament floor, Cael thought, or wanted to. They knew exactly what a rank was. It was what a man could lift, and for how long, and what he could be paid for it.

On the corner of the fish market there was a public house with two doors. The front one was propped open on a room full of wharfmen and smoke. The other, round the side, was shut, with a small brass plate screwed to it at the height of a man's eyes. Cael stopped and read the plate. It said that the back room was kept for ranked custom, and it gave the rank in a form of words that the registry had stopped using before Cael was born. Nobody had changed the plate. Nobody had needed to. Everybody who used that door knew what it meant.

On the coopers' row the registry's annex had its shutters down for the rest day, and there was a queue outside it anyway.

He stopped at the end of the row and counted it. Thirty-one people, standing in a line along the wall of a shut office, most of them with a folded paper in their hands. He knew what the papers were. They were result slips from the Concourse. The tournament made ranks, and the registry wrote them down, and until the registry had written them down they were only a story somebody had told about you in a crowded room. These people had come a day early to stand at a shut door so that they would be first when it opened.

He thought about Brom's plaque on the long table, and Brom's summons, and walked on without saying anything to anybody.

At the basin steps there was a likeness-seller with his sheets pegged along a cord between two poles, two for a copper, flapping a little in the wind off the water.

The Auremont Gold had the middle of the cord, and the stack of her sheets on the stall's board was nearly gone. Zerin was beside her, and then the Rhagen captain on his oak with the guard drawn round him like a great soap bubble, and then the Iron bracket's last four with Lira among them, fists up, angrier than life again.

At the very end of the cord, a little apart from the rest, hung a sheet of him.

It was not the sunbeams sheet or the black one. It was new since the captain's bout, and it showed him running up a hill. The cutter had worked hard at it. He had got the hill and the rope and the stride. But he had cut the eyes too old, so that a boy his own age was looking out of the sheet with the eyes of a tired man of forty who had seen a great deal and liked very little of it.

Cael bought it. Hesk would want it. Hesk would say something withering about the eyes and keep it in the workshop drawer with the good files for the rest of his life.

The likeness-seller took the copper and made change and handed him the sheet rolled up, and said "Good luck to the bluff," because the sheet had the bluff's name on it, and did not look at his face at all.

Cael put the sheet in his coat and walked on along the basin, smiling, and could not have said why that pleased him as much as it did.

At the third wharf a woman was playing a fiddle for the holiday crowd with her case open at her feet. She was good. She played as people play who have done it on cold wharves for twenty years and are paid in coppers and do it well anyway. Cael stopped, as Hesk had taught him to stop for anybody who knew an instrument, and listened through to the end of the tune, and put a coin in the case.

She looked up at him as he straightened. Her eyes went to his face, and to the coat, and back to the face, and something in them changed.

Then she grinned at him, and put the fiddle back up under her chin, and played four bars of a quick-march straight at him, sharp and bright and very fast, as he walked away along the wharf. It was the march they played for the colour-guard at the procession. She stopped at the end of the fourth bar, exactly, and went back to her tune.

That was what he was worth, then, to a fiddler on the third wharf. Four bars and a grin. He found it the most restful price anybody had put on him in a year.

The smell of frying fish came along the wharf from somewhere ahead, and under it the smell of tar, and under that, very faintly, something baking. Past the third wharf, on the harbour side, there was a low cookshop with its windows open on the water, and a door painted a deep, chipped, cheerful blue.

He looked at the sun. It was midday, near enough.

He went in.

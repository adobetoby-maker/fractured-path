# Chapter 47 — The Empty Board

The morning after the trial came in grey, with a wet wind off the harbour, and the first thing on the long table at breakfast was the other semifinal.

Ephram had it. He had been down to the outer court before anybody else was awake, as he went down most mornings now, and he came up the stair with three broadsides under his arm and a loaf under the other and set them all on the table together, the bread on top.

"Rhagen," he said, sitting down. "We're fighting Rhagen."

Nobody had known for certain. The other semifinal had been fought on the far floor the same afternoon as theirs, and the five of them had been in the tunnel with Rooke and then at the long table with his review sheet, and by the time anybody thought to ask, the result had gone the rounds of the city without coming up their stair.

Karis pulled the top sheet out from under the loaf and read the column aloud, in the flat voice she used for things that were going to be argued about.

Rhagen had touched more people than the river academy had. It had touched a great many more; the flags had gone up for black-and-glass all afternoon. And the river academy had stood on two platforms out of three for most of every exchange, and let itself be touched, and gone to its gate and put its hand on the post and come back, and stood on them again.

"More touches, less ground," said Karis, and put the sheet down. "And the ledger counts ground."

"It's a good lesson," said Lira. "I'm glad somebody else had to pay for it."

"Third-place trial on the twenty-first day," said Ephram. "Against the captain and Marek both, I should think, and whoever they've got who can carry a bag."

Brom had not said anything. He had a folded paper of his own beside his plate, and he waited until the others had finished with the broadsides before he unfolded it, because Brom read things at the table or not at all.

It was a single leaf, printed small, in the clean type of the commercial shops, and a printer in the delegation quarter had been selling it since before dawn for a copper to anybody who already owned the rest of the file, which by the length of the queue Ephram described was a good part of the city. It was meant to go in at the back. It had the shop's mark at the top and a date and a title, and under the title one line in italics.

*Survived Daeva's lane at trial speed.*

Under that the shop had added three paragraphs of what it called analysis. Brom did not read those aloud. He read the line twice, to himself, moving his lips a little, and then he passed the leaf to Cael across the bread.

"New last page," he said.

Cael read it. It was accurate, which was the trouble with the shops; they were nearly always accurate. They wrote down what had happened and left out what it meant, and anybody who bought the page could put the meaning back in for himself.

"It shuts the frame," said Karis.

She did not say it as if it were news. She said it as if she were checking a sum she had already done.

"Anybody below Gold who files against you now, files against that line," she went on. "Win, and he's beaten a boy who got out of the way of something. Lose, and he's the first man in the city to do worse against you than she did. There's no price on that trade anybody sensible will pay." She tapped the leaf. "They printed the end of the exhibitions and sold it for a copper."

"Then I'll have more time to train for Rhagen," said Cael.

He meant it as a joke, more or less, and Lira laughed, more or less. But Brom took the leaf back and folded it along its crease and put it in his coat with the rest of the file, and nobody else at the table laughed at all.

---

The exhibition frame hung where it had hung all sitting, on its pegs at the mouth of the north tunnel, under an old lamp-bracket that nobody lit any more.

Cael went down to look at it after breakfast. He told himself it was on the way to the hired hall, which it nearly was.

It was dark wood, older than anything around it, with the type of a printer who had been dead for a very long time. Five short lines of rule ran across the top of it. Below them the ring's size and the rating provision, and then the ruled lines, a long column of them, waiting. Two were filled. The Rhagen captain's name was on the first, in a clerk's careful white, and the lake man's on the second. Below the lake man the lines went on down the board, empty, to the bottom of the frame.

Beside it, in a glass case on an iron stand, lay the register.

A steward came at the first bell to open it. He was a narrow, tidy man with a bunch of keys on a cord round his wrist, one of the west tower's outer staff. Cael had seen him before, on the mornings of the two Silver filings, and he had looked then like a man who had expected the easiest posting in the building and had been mildly put out to find it wasn't. He unlocked the case. He opened the register at its ribbon and read down the page to the last entry, and looked up at the frame, and checked one against the other. Then he shut the book and locked the case and stepped back to the wall and stood there, with his hands behind him, for the hour the rules required.

There were about thirty people watching him do it. Some had come on purpose. Some had stopped on their way through the tunnel and stayed. Nobody said anything. When the hour was up the steward took his keys and went, and the thirty went too, slowly, as people leave a quay when the ship they were waiting for has not come in.

Cael stood a moment longer, looking at the empty lines.

He had told himself, the night before, that they would stay empty, and he found that he did not much like being right. He went on to the hired hall with the frame still in front of his eyes, and was late, and Rooke said nothing about it, which was worse.

Rooke had them there by the second bell, with the door shut and his coaching sheet open on the trestle at the page with the five lines on it.

"Rhagen," he said. "Four mornings from now. They'll bring the captain and Marek both. They'll bring their structure, which is the best structure on this continent. And they'll have spent last night doing to our semifinal what we did to theirs." He put his finger on the sheet. "So we'll take our own medicine first. One line each. You wrote them. Now you do them."

He began with Lira.

*Ready. Not early.* She had written it herself, and she knew exactly what it cost. Early had been her whole trade since she was small: arrive first, take the corner, make the other fighter's next step land in a place she already owned. Ready was a different thing. Ready was a little further back, with the weight even, waiting for the floor to tell her where to go instead of telling the floor.

He set her against the Stone reserve in a chalk corner and made her stand off it. Twice she went early anyway, out of pure habit, and the reserve went round her.

"Again," said Rooke. "Early is a guess you make for the other man. Ready lets him make it."

The third time she stood, and waited, and went when the reserve went, half a beat behind his start and a stride ahead of his arrival, and took the corner off him without a burst.

"That," said Rooke.

Brom was next. *Left the gap. Should have left it sooner.* Rooke did not make him stand in a gap at all. He chalked one on the floor between two benches and put Brom two strides wide of it, off to one side, where he could reach the mouth of it in a single step and still see past it.

"A gap with a man in it is a wall," said Rooke. "Everybody can see a wall. A gap with a man beside it is a gap. It looks like a way through. Let it look like one."

Brom considered the chalk for a while, with his bandaged arms folded.

"So I'm a toll," he said. "Not a door."

"You're the man who sells the ticket," said Rooke. "You're not the turnstile."

Karis had her frame. *A frame with a door in it.* She planted her three anchors on the boards in the order Ternhall had taught her, leading foot, a stride forward on the line, the flank, and then she took the flank anchor out and moved it half a stride, so that the frame stood with one side open. The opening was real. Anybody could have walked through it. But she could close it with one step, and only she knew which step.

"You'll pay for that door in heat," said Rooke.

"Every frame costs heat," said Karis. "This one costs it when I choose."

Ephram's line was one word, and Rooke looked at it longest. *South.*

"That wasn't a plan," he said at last. "It was a trade. You gave them a thing they wanted so that they'd have to stand somewhere they didn't. Do it again on Rhagen and I'll buy you dinner. Do it twice and I'll start to worry."

Then he looked at Cael's line.

*The diagonal. I had it on paper this morning and didn't know what it was.*

"I can't drill that," said Rooke. "It isn't a technique. It's a habit of looking." He shut the sheet. "So look. Every floor they build you between now and the end of this sitting, you walk it twice, and the second time you walk it you find me the line nobody's pricing. Write it down. Then tell somebody. A thing you've only written down is a thing you've found too late."

The story of the Silver reached the long table at supper that night, the way stories reached it, by way of Ephram.

A Silver from one of the eastern houses had come to the register that afternoon. He was a Five, a big quiet man who had gone out of his bracket in the round of sixteen and had nothing left at the sitting but his number. He had walked up to the frame with a filing form in his hand, filled in. The steward had seen it. Half the tunnel had seen it.

He had stood in front of the frame for a long time. He had read the captain's line, and the lake man's, and the two figures posted beside them. Then he had read the empty line underneath, the one his own name would have gone on.

Then he had folded his form in half and put it back inside his coat, and buttoned the coat over it, and gone away down the tunnel.

"The steward didn't enter anything," said Ephram. "A form you don't hand in isn't a form. So officially it never happened. But I had it from three people, and they all said the same thing about him, which was that he didn't look frightened. He looked like a man adding up."

"What did it come to?" asked Lira.

"Same as Karis's this morning, I expect." Ephram tore a piece of bread in two. "He worked his sums out where the whole tunnel could watch. That's the whole category's problem, standing at a board in a wet coat."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Lira said, "Was it a wet coat?"

"It was by the time it got to me," said Ephram, and the table laughed, which was the first time it had laughed properly since the tunnel.

---

On the second morning it rained.

It was not hard rain. It was the soft, steady, salt-smelling kind that Norhold seemed to keep for days when it had nothing else planned, and it came in under the tunnel's arch and lay in a skin on the stone. The steward came at the first bell with his keys. He walked out of the tunnel's dry part into the wet part, where the frame was, and stopped.

There were two hundred people waiting for him.

Cael counted them, because counting was what he did when he did not know what to feel. They stood in the rain in a loose half-ring round the case, in coats and hoods and under borrowed sacking, with the water running off their hat-brims. There were dockhands, and two ladies with an umbrella between them, and a boy on his father's shoulders, and a knot of students in somebody's yellow. None of them was a fighter. None of them had anything to file. They had come to watch a man unlock a glass case and read a book.

The steward did it exactly as he had done it the day before.

He did not hurry, and he did not look up at the crowd, not once. He unlocked the case and opened the book and read the last entry and looked at the frame and checked one against the other, with the rain darkening the shoulders of his coat, and shut the book and locked the case and went and stood against the wall with his hands behind his back. When the hour was over he took his keys and left. Cael thought it was the best piece of ordinary work he had seen anybody do all sitting, and he wrote it down that night in the old volume's last leaves, in four words, because the old volume was running out.

The broadsides had found the frame by then. They ran it on every front sheet in the outer court, in the largest type any of them owned.

WHO FIGHTS THE ENROLLEE?

Under the awnings the betting rows, which had refused all sitting to take money on his bouts, had found a way round their own sign. They would not book a bout that had not been filed. But they would book whether one would be. By the middle of the morning there was a price chalked on four boards on *no further filing at this sitting*, and it was getting shorter every hour.

He bought a twist of hot bread at a stall on the basin steps and stood under the stall's canvas to eat it, because the rain had come on harder.

Two wharfmen and a woman from the fish market were arguing at the counter. They had a broadside spread on the planks between them, held down at the corners with a weight and a knife and two cups. None of them had any money on the exhibitions, because nobody did. None of them had looked twice at the boy in the plain coat eating bread behind them. They were simply doing what the whole city was doing that morning, which was working it out.

"It's his own fault for being good," said one of the wharfmen. "Nobody wants to be the man he beats."

"Nobody wants to be the man he doesn't beat, either," said the other. "Look at the Silvers. They lost and they're heroes. Who files to be the third hero? It's the same bout twice."

The fishwife had been gutting a fish while they talked, and she finished it and laid it down on the paper, which neither wharfman thought much of.

"You're both looking at it from underneath," she said. "Nobody's going up to him any more. Fine. He's too high to climb to." She wiped her knife. "So somebody's got to come *down*."

The wharfmen looked at her.

"That's all," she said, and picked up the next fish.

Cael finished his bread, and thanked the stall-keeper, and went out into the rain. He found he was smiling, and he was not altogether sure why, and by the time he reached the hired hall he had stopped.

---

He showed Lira the margin word that afternoon.

He had not shown it to anybody. It was one word, small, in Hesk's ruled margin beside the trial entry. *Half.* She looked at it for a while and then at him.

"Half a second," she said. "At the end. Off the air."

"Off something. I don't know what."

"Then let's find out what." She was already pulling off her coat. "Shut your eyes."

The hired hall was a long room with boarded walls and a stack of barrels down one side, and Karis had taken the bench by the window with her notebook, and Brom had gone to the door to keep it. Cael stood in the middle of the floor with his eyes shut. Lira went to the far end.

"I'll come past you," she said. "One burst. I won't say when. You tell me when it gets to you."

He stood and listened, and heard her feet settle on the boards forty feet away. He reached for her with the read, the way he reached for everybody, and found her at once: her weight, low and even, the small gathering of it into the leading hip that came before every burst she had ever made.

"Now," he said.

She went. The burst carried her the length of the hall and past him on his left, a stride off, and her wash went over him a moment later, a push of air on his cheek and his ear and his shirt.

"You called me," said Lira from the other end. "Not it."

He knew. He had called her start, which he had been able to call for years. The push had arrived after, a long way after, at a time the read had not told him anything about at all.

They did it again. Twelve times. Every time he caught the gathering in her hip and every time he called it, because he could not help calling it; it was the loudest thing in the room. And every time the wash came over him late, on its own clock, and he had no idea it was coming until it came.

"You're reading her," said Karis, from the window, on the twelfth. "You're very good at reading her. Read what she leaves behind."

"I don't know how."

"I know you don't." Karis wrote something down. "That's why I said it."

He opened his eyes. Lira was leaning on the barrels at the far end with her hands on her knees, grinning, not much out of breath.

"Your storm," she said. "Is going to take longer than an afternoon."

Karis said the rest of it at supper that night, because somebody had to.

"Two days," she said.

She had the frame drawn in the back of her notebook, from memory, with its two filled lines and its column of empty ones. She had been watching it the way she watched any instrument she had predicted, waiting to see whether it would do what she said.

"It's done what I said. It can't fill from below. Everybody underneath him has priced himself out." She closed the notebook. "So it fills from above, or it dies."

Nobody said what was above.

There was exactly one name above, and every one of them had been not saying it since the tunnel. It had become a kind of rule among them without anybody making it, the way the long table's rules got made: the name was not to be spent before its time. Brom ate his supper. Ephram looked at his plate. Lira, who never in her life had left a thing unsaid that she wanted said, put her cup down and looked across at Cael and said nothing at all.

He went up early. He did not sleep early.

He lay in the dark under the window with the rain on the glass, and thought about the frame on its pegs in the tunnel with the water running past it, and the steward's keys, and the man who had put his form back in his coat. He thought about the fishwife. *Somebody's got to come down.*

He thought about one second, at the mouth of a lane, with the air pushing at him and his hair standing up off his neck, and a woman a stride away looking at his feet and his hands and his face.

Then he made himself stop thinking about it, which took some time, and slept.

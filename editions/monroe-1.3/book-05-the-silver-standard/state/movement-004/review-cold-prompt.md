# Chapter 20 — One Ordinary Result

Rooke looked at the shoulder in the stable doorway, because the stable was the only warm place in the yard.

The waystation sat at the foot of the hill country, where the road came down off the last of the slate and ran out level across a country of hedges and frozen ditches. Behind it the hills stood up grey in the first light. They had been two days coming down out of them. Ahead, by Rooke's reckoning, lay another week of flat road and then the bluff.

"Arm up," said Rooke. "Slowly. Stop when it tells you."

Cael lifted the left arm out to the side. It went to the height of his shoulder before the front of the joint said anything, and what it said was small and sulky, not the hot pull of the slate.

"Forward."

He brought the arm forward, and across his body, and back. Rooke stood with two fingers resting on the seam at the front of the shoulder, where the foreman's lever had gone in, and watched Cael's face rather than the arm. Behind them a horse shifted in its stall and blew. The wagoner was harnessing in the yard with his breath going up white.

"Push my hand."

Cael pushed. Rooke's hand did not move, and neither did the seam.

"That's four days," said Rooke, and took his fingers away. "This is the last half of the fourth. You'll carry nothing heavy before noon, and after noon you'll carry your own kit and nobody else's. Tomorrow it's yours again." He wiped his hands on his coat, though there was nothing on them. "Now the floor."

He had said *We'll talk about the floor* in the quarry town, and he had not said it since. Cael had begun to think that he never would. That was Rooke's way with most things: to put a correction on a sheet and leave the cause where he had found it.

But Lira had said *Tell Rooke the truth about the floor. All of it.* And Lira had been right about the bill.

So Cael told him. He stood in the stable door with his coat round his shoulders and told it in order, as he would have put it in the Log. He had planned a slow first exchange against a grappler. He had planned it on the oak at home, where standing still costs nothing and a late step can always be bought back off the spring. Then he had carried it to slate without asking what slate charged. He had stood still in the cold for most of a glass. The cold had gone into his legs and taken a quarter of a beat off his feet, and on slate there was no spring to buy the quarter-beat back.

"So he got a hand on me," Cael said. "And the hand found the arm. That's the floor's share. The rest of it was mine."

Rooke listened to all of it without moving. When it was done he took the folded training sheet out of his coat. It was the one with *Early eyes, late feet* across the top in his square capitals. He unfolded it against the stable door, wrote under the capitals with a stub of pencil, and showed Cael what he had written.

*Slow on a cold floor: walk the slow. Feet moving inside the wait. If the floor won't let you walk, don't run the item.*

"You stood still," said Rooke. "There's nothing wrong with your design. You put it on the wrong surface and then stood on it like a post. Slow is a choice about when you arrive. It isn't a choice to stop." He folded the sheet again. "Next time you give a man the first exchange on stone, you give it to him walking. If you can't walk on that floor, you don't give it to him at all. You take the item off the card and run another."

"Yes."

"And you tell me before. Not after." Rooke put the sheet away. "That's the talk. It was shorter than I meant it to be, because you'd already had most of it with somebody else."

Cael did not ask how he knew. He supposed Rooke had seen Lira's face on the slate, the afternoon of the strapping, and drawn the obvious conclusion.

It came to him, standing in the straw with the arm hanging easy at his side, that he had priced every bout of the season to the touch and had not once priced a floor until a floor sent him its bill. He knew the oak because he lived on it. Every other surface he had walked onto as a guest, trusting it to behave like home until it showed him otherwise. For the first time he understood that the floor had been in every bout he fought this year, an opponent that filed no challenge and shook no hands, and that it had been keeping its own score the whole time.

They stood a moment longer in the doorway. In the yard the wagoner had got the first pair between the shafts and was talking to them, low and steady, as he always did.

"Where are we?" said Cael. He meant the season, and Rooke knew he meant it.

"Second," said Rooke. "Still second, by less. One meet left on the card, and the line close enough to see." He pulled his coat closer. "What this house needs at the confluence is one ordinary result. Not a brilliant one. Nobody has to be brilliant. Five fighters do what they do on a decent floor, and we come home with our points, and the arithmetic does the rest." He looked at Cael sidelong. "You'll find that harder than brilliant. Most good fighters do."

The courier came through at midday, while they were eating.

The soup at the waystation was the worst soup of the season, which was saying a good deal after the slate country. It was grey, and it tasted mostly of the pot, and Brom was eating his second bowl of it with the steady resignation of a man stoking a stove. Ephram had given up and was eating bread. Gault had his case on the bench beside him, one hand resting on its lid. The reserves were arguing quietly about a bout neither of them had been in.

The courier did not stop long. He was a young man on a tired horse, riding south out of the regional seat with a satchel of printed sheets, and he left one at every inn and waystation on the long road south where a house might be lying for the night. He came in stamping, put a sheet on the counter, drank something hot standing up, and was gone again before most of the table had noticed he was there.

The innkeeper's girl brought the sheet across to Gault, because Gault looked the most like someone who received papers.

Gault did not open it. He passed it down the table to Rooke, unread, with a small formal movement of the hand, and went back to his soup.

It was the regional office's circular, and Cael knew the look of it. One came after every meet: the standings of every house in the region, totalled to date, with a ruled line printed across the table some way down. The region sent three houses to Norhold. The line sat under the third.

Rooke read it once, quickly, holding it up in front of him with his elbows on the table.

Then he laid it flat and read it again, slowly, with one finger moving down the margin. He stopped the finger at a place on the page and left it there. He took a long time over the figures under his finger, longer than any sum on that sheet could need.

Nobody at the table said anything. The reserves had stopped arguing, and Brom had stopped eating, which was rarer.

"The coast meet ran the same week as ours," Rooke said at last, to the sheet. "Two of the houses above the line fought each other into the ground there and split the points between them. And the lake house had a bad meet at home." He turned the sheet round and pushed it into the middle of the table, among the bread. "Add it up. Add anything you like to it. There's no way through the confluence that puts three houses past us. Not if every one of us lost in the first round."

Lira reached it first. She read it with her lips moving, which she did only with figures she cared about.

Karis took it from her and went down it with a pencil, touching each figure with the point and not marking any of them. The region would send three houses north at the year's turn, and for Halcenvane to miss, three houses would have to pass it at the confluence, and two of the three that might have done it had spent the same week on the coast taking points off each other, so that whatever sum you tried, there was no longer a way through. Karis tried four sums. Then she laid the pencil down beside the sheet, very straight.

"Does that mean—" said Ephram.

"It means we're going," said Brom.

Then Rooke shut his eyes.

He did it sitting up straight, with both hands flat on the table on either side of his bowl. He kept them shut for one breath, a long one, in and out, while the fire cracked and the innkeeper's girl clattered plates behind the counter and nobody at the table moved at all.

He opened them.

"A meet to spare," he said, in his ordinary voice. "Nobody gets careless. The confluence still sets the seeding for Norhold, and the index the scouts buy, and I won't take a house there with its seeds in a heap." He picked up his spoon and looked at the soup and put the spoon down again. "Eighteen years. Somebody ought to write to the chancellor."

"The chancellor was informed," said Seln, from the end of the bench, "by an arrangement the office keeps at the regional seat." He was pouring tea for the reserves and did not look up from the pot. "Approximately four hours ago."

There was a short silence round the table.

Nobody asked what the arrangement was. By this point in the year every one of them had learned to leave that kind of question on the table and keep their fingers off it.

Then Ephram began to laugh. It started as a breath through his nose and got away from him, and Brom caught it, and then the reserves, who did not know what they were laughing at and did not care. Lira put her face in her hands and laughed into them. Karis wrote something in the grey notebook, and Cael would have bet a week's bursts on what it was: the time, and the waystation, and the soup.

Gault took the sheet from the middle of the table and smoothed it with the side of his hand. He folded it once, carefully, along a line of his own choosing, and put it inside the lid of his case.

"For the archive," he said. "Bracken will want it." Then, as an afterthought, he took it out again and gave it to Rooke. "It's yours, really."

Rooke put it in his coat, over his heart, where he kept the training sheets.

---

They went on at the first bell after noon, and for an hour nobody in the wagons said much.

It was a strange thing to have been given, and he did not yet know where to put it. Cael sat on the tailboard of the second wagon with his left arm resting on his knee, carrying nothing, as instructed, and watched the hedges go by. He tried to find the moment he would remember.

There was not one. There had been no hall, no board and no voice reading names into a record. There had been a tired young man on a tired horse who did not know what he was carrying, and a sheet of paper among the bread, and a coach with his eyes shut for the length of a breath. The season's whole object had come down the road on a sheet you could fold into a coat.

Lira climbed back over the load and sat down beside him with her legs hanging.

"I keep waiting for it to happen," she said.

"It happened."

"I know it happened. I read it. I added it up twice in my head and once on my fingers." She swung her boots. "I keep waiting for it to *feel* like it happened. At Fenmark, when a house qualified, they rang the bell in the yard. Somebody climbed up and rang it until his arms gave out."

"There's no bell on a waystation."

"There's a bell on the stable. I looked." She was quiet for a while. "I didn't ring it. It would have frightened the horses."

Karis was sitting on a sack behind them with the grey notebook on her knee, and from the look of her pencil she was still adding.

Up in the front wagon Ephram was teaching the reserves the words to something. Brom had gone to sleep against the sacks with his arms folded and his chin down, as he did whenever a day had decided to be good to him, as if he did not want to be awake to watch it change its mind.

"Rooke shut his eyes," said Lira.

"I saw."

"I've never seen him do anything for himself. Not once, all year." She looked down the road. "One breath. That's what he spent on it. One breath, and then he told us not to get careless."

"He's saving the rest."

"Rooke doesn't save. He banks." She almost smiled. "It's different. You'd know."

He did know, and he said nothing.

She sat with him until the light began to go, and then went forward to stop Ephram teaching the reserves the second verse.

Brom woke as she climbed past him. He opened one eye and looked at the hedges, and then at Cael.

"Did it change its mind?" he said.

"No."

"Good," said Brom, and shut the eye again.

He wrote it that night at the waystation's window, on the long table under a lamp, with the left arm free on the board for the first time in four days.

*Qualified. Rooke read it at a waystation table with the bread going stale beside it, and shut his eyes for one breath, and then told us not to get careless. Seln had known since breakfast. Gault filed it in his case and then thought better of it and gave it to Rooke.*

*I've been trying all afternoon to find the hall where it happened, and there isn't one. Nobody read our name aloud. Nobody stood up. A tired courier left a sheet on a counter and rode away. I begin to think this is simply how the year's real news travels: not in the halls where the ceremonies are held, but in transit, in margins, at counters, carried by people who don't know what they've got. The hall comes afterward, to make it look as if it happened there.*

*Shoulder: cleared. Rooke's correction is on the sheet, and it is better than mine: walk the slow, or don't run it. Shadow: rent from waking, as every day; one drift this week, in a gap on the road, none in the work; deployment none. The rest untouched.*

*Rooke says the house needs one ordinary result at the confluence. We don't even need that now. Which means, for one meet, nobody on our side of the rope is fighting for anything but the fight. I've never been to a meet like that. I'm curious what it does to people.*

---

They came up the bluff road on the Third-day evening in a thin dry snow that did not settle.

The coats were on the bell. Two stood at the ferry landing when the wagons crossed, stamping on the boards, and two more at the foot of the bluff road in the lee of the wall. Cael counted them out of habit. Karis wrote the time in the marbled log without looking at them, and nobody else looked at them either.

There was no crowd at the gate. Nobody on the hill knew yet, except the chancellor, who had been told four hours before the delegation, and Bracken, who would certainly have been told by the chancellor. The porter let them in and said good evening and asked after the slate country, and they said it had been cold.

Rooke went straight to the records hall with the circular.

He gave it to Bracken across the counter, and Bracken looked at it for a long moment under the lamp. Then he laid it in a clean folder, wrote on the spine in his small upright hand, and shut the folder.

"The archive," he said, "has been waiting for this one."

That was all he said. But Cael, who was standing by the door with his kit, saw him set the folder on the counter's corner, square to the edge, and leave his hand on it a little longer than a folder needs.

The house found out in the morning, as it found out everything, from the porter, who had it from Bracken's clerk. By the first bell the cohort board had a new line along its foot in Rooke's square capitals. It said *Qualified* and nothing else. By the second bell somebody had drawn a small careful star beside it in chalk, and Rooke had left it there.

Withrow made no speech. She stopped Rooke on the covered walk before noon, and the two of them stood talking for perhaps a minute with their breath going up. Cael passed them carrying his kit to the oak and heard only the end of it.

"I'll travel to the confluence," Withrow was saying. "With the registrar, if he'll come. I've been to none of them this year, and I meant to go to none, and I find I'd like to see one."

"It's two days in the cold, Chancellor."

"Then I'll bring a coat," said Withrow, and went on toward the records hall.

The two weeks between were the quietest of the season.

There was nothing to qualify for, and the work did not care. Rooke ran the squad on the sprung oak every morning as he had all year. He sent Cael's left arm through a quarter of its usual load for three days and then through all of it. On the fourth morning he watched Cael give the Stone reserve the first exchange walking: slow, early eyes, the feet moving all the time inside the wait. He said, "Better," and wrote one word on the sheet.

The oak gave its six free bursts a day, as it always had. Cael spent five of them on the second Fourth-day, in a long session with Lira on the speed line, and felt the familiar bill come in at the hip that evening and go out again by morning.

Ephram copied the season's records into his file book until there was nothing left to copy.

On the Fifth-day Cael took his coursework to the wing's counter, as he did every Fifth-day, and stood in the line behind a Mire second-year and two Current boys. Seln took the folder, read the cover sheet from the name to the signature at his even pace, stamped it, and tore off the slip.

"Thank you for the form," said Cael.

"Mm," said Seln, and took the next folder. Nothing in his face said that he had poured tea at a waystation the week before and told a table something none of them could have known. That was the arrangement, too, Cael thought, going back out into the cold. It was the one the office did not keep.

Brom read the circular's figures once, at the common-room table, with Cael at his shoulder. He went down his own house's column with his finger, stopping at each meet, and said "Same line again," with the deep content of a man finding his tools where he had left them.

Karis read it too, and turned to a clean page in the grey notebook, and wrote at the head of it *The confluence*. Under that, for two weeks, she wrote things she would not show anybody.

The two sheets rode in the delegation's file all that time, folded together in Rooke's coat with the training sheets: Zerin's times and Marek's notations. Nobody mentioned them. Nobody had to.

The confluence was two days north of Ostrand, at the place where the river that ran under the bluff met a bigger one coming down out of the east.

The wagons left on the Second-day with a hired carriage behind them for Withrow and Bracken. The carriage was black and narrow and had been, the wagoner said, a magistrate's once. Withrow sat in it with a rug over her knees and a book open and did not read it. Bracken sat opposite with a box of papers on his knees and read every one of them twice.

They came down to the confluence on the Third-day at dusk, in the deep cold of the year's turn.

The town stood on a tongue of land between the two rivers, built up to the water on both sides. Both rivers were iron-grey and running hard, with ice along their edges in plates and shelves, and the place where they met was a long rough seam of broken water that never froze, even in the deep cold, because the two currents never agreed with each other long enough to let it. Barges lay up at the quays three deep, roped together, their masts down. The hall where the meet was held stood at the very point of the tongue, with water on three sides of it.

The inn was across the square from the hall. Its keeper came out into the snow to meet them, a round red man in an apron, rubbing his hands. He took two bags off the first wagon before anybody could stop him.

"Halcenvane," he said. "I know. I knew from the wagons. Come in, come in, there's a fire." He was looking past Rooke at the others, one face after another. When he reached Cael his face lit up like a lamp turned high. "And you're the lad from the provision. Come and look. Come and look at this."

Behind his counter, on the wall between the casks, hung a frame. In the frame was a printed sheet, the kind a meet office puts out, and Cael knew it before he was near enough to read it. It was the ruling from the wool town on the protest of the town Bronze, the one Ephram had said someone would frame.

"Had it off a carter, back along," said the innkeeper. "Paid a whole mark for it. I read it out to every guest who'll stand still." He put a finger on the glass and read it out now, in a ringing voice, while the bags stood dripping on his floor.

"*The Standard rates performance, not outcomes. The panel finds the performance was rated correctly, and observes that the protest's actual complaint — that the practitioner appears to have won without extending himself — is not a scoring error, and the remedy for it is not available from this office.*"

He beamed. "Not available! I've had men laugh at that till they cried."

"So have we," said Ephram.

Lira had turned away to the fire and was shaking with the effort of not laughing. Rooke said the room was very handsome. Withrow, coming in last out of the snow, looked at the frame for some time with no expression at all, and then asked whether there was tea.

The draw went up in the hall at the eighth bell, by lamplight.

There was one Iron draw this time, sixteen strong, with no split at any rank. It was seeded by formal rank, as the circuit seeded everything, with the foot of the list set against its head. Lira was not at the foot. A host-house Rank One, advanced at the last sitting before the year's turn, had been registered after her, and so stood below her. He drew the top seed.

Lira drew the second seed's line, a coastal Mire Path, Rank Eight.

She stood in front of the board with her hands in her pockets and read down her half of it slowly: the Mire in the first round, then whoever came through the second pairing, and in the semifinal, if the seeds held, the third seed.

The third seed was a Force Path of the hill-river house, Iron Rank Seven, and beside his name somebody had chalked a small square.

"What's the square?" said Brom.

"It's what the circuit calls him," said Karis, without looking at her notebook. "He builds."

Lira looked at the square for a while longer, and then she looked across the hall to the main floor, where a broad dark young man in a quilted jacket was warming up alone by the far rope. He was doing nothing anyone would have stopped to watch. He walked three paces, planted, turned, and walked three more, round and round a little shape of his own on the boards, like a man pacing out the footings of a house.

"Huh," said Lira softly.

Ephram and Karis were in the top half, in different quarters. Brom's Copper draw was eight strong and he was its head.

On the board for the second hall, under a heading nobody at the meet had needed for forty years, five challenges had been chalked against one line.

The second hall was not a hall at all. It was the town's old salt store on the north quay, a long stone barn with a vaulted roof and two great doors at the river end, where the salt barges had once unloaded straight in off the water. Somebody had laid a floor of deal planks over the stone, straight down onto it without any joists, and chalked a square on the planks and hung lamps from the beams. Cael walked out onto it after the draw while Gault held his coat, and put his weight down through one foot and then the other.

Nothing came back. The planks were hard and true and dead, with the stone right under them.

"Five," said Gault, who had watched him do it at three meets now and had learned to read the answer in his shoulders. "Like the mill town."

"Five," Cael agreed. Five free bursts between waking and sleeping, on a floor like that, however he cut the day into bouts.

The steward had pinned the five filings to the board beside the chalk, in the challengers' own hands. Cael read them under the lamp. Two were from academy Irons and gave no reason. One was a confluence-house Blade who had written *the town asked me*. The fourth was in a square, careful, unhurried hand that had filled in a great many manifests: *Bronze. Shield. Convoy guard on the salt road. I have never lost a wagon. I'd like to know whether I'd lose one to you.*

The fifth said only *Iron Rank Nine. Force. Barges.* Under it, smaller, as though added after some thought: *I have read this river all my life. Let's see.*

# Chapter 21 — The Change of Guard

The queue began at the salt store's door before it was light.

Cael saw it from the inn's back window while he was strapping his wrists, a dark line of coats and hats along the north quay, stamping in the snow. He took it at first for a barge crew waiting to be paid.

Then the line went on past the barges, and round the corner of the chandler's, and out of sight up the lane, and he understood that the town had come to see the second hall.

Gault came in with the coat over his arm and looked out of the window beside him.

"The steward sent a boy round at the sixth bell," he said. "He'd like it known that he has a hundred and forty places, and that the fire regulations of the river guild are very clear." He handed Cael the coat. "He sounded frightened."

"Of the crowd?"

"Of the regulations," said Gault. "Stewards always are."

By the first bell the line had doubled. When Cael came along the quay with Gault and Rooke, people turned to look at him as he passed, and some of them called out to him cheerfully, by name.

A woman with a basket of hot chestnuts walked beside him for twenty yards asking whether it was true about the wool town. A carter's boy asked whether he could see the burst from the back.

The steward met them at the door, a narrow anxious man with a list, and let them in sideways.

Inside, the salt store was full to its walls.

Rooke stood at the delegation's corner of the rope with his coat buttoned to the chin and looked at the crowd for some time without expression, as he might have looked at a floor that had turned out to be wet. "Walk between exchanges," he said at last. "They'll make noise at you whether you walk or not, and you may as well be warm while they do it."

They had set benches along both sides of the square and put boards across barrels for the people behind. Behind them again, the crowd stood two and three deep against the stone, under the lamps, with its breath going up into the vault.

Somebody had brought a dog.

The great doors at the river end were shut against the cold, and a thin hard draught came in under them from the water and ran across the planks.

The noise was like being inside a drum, and it did not stop between bouts, or while the steward read out the rules, or even while the steward begged the people standing on the barrels at the back to get down off them before somebody went through the lid into the brine.

His first challenger was the confluence-house Blade who had written *the town asked me*. He was a quick, merry young man of the house's second rank, and the town had certainly asked him, because every time he moved the whole barn roared for him as though he were a brother.

Cael fought the first exchange the careful way and took a touch from it late, on the turn, with the read giving him the Blade's weight a fraction before it moved. The noise went down to a groan.

In the second exchange the Blade came off his mark at the call with the whole barn on his back, and the noise came with him.

It was a great flat wall of sound, the loudest room Cael had ever fought in, and it rolled down off the vault and filled the square to the ropes.

This was item two. *A conceded point when the light is behind me, the floor soft, or the crowd loud.* He had written it at a window at the mill town, and he had not yet run it.

He let the noise have him. He let it take a fraction off his read, because a loud room really does take something off a fighter who listens with his whole body. He let the Blade's second feint get a half-step closer than it should have, and he was a quarter-beat slow to turn from it.

The Blade's palm found his shoulder.

"Touch. Confluence."

The barn went up like a bonfire. The Blade raised both fists and turned round to show the gallery, and the gallery stamped on the boards across the barrels until the lamps swung.

Cael walked his circle at the rope, as Rooke had taught them all, and did not look at anybody.

It was a true point. A loud room did cost a fighter something, and he had only let it cost him a little more than it needed to.

The figure tomorrow would say he had had a merry young man in front of him and a hundred and forty people shouting, and that it had made a difference. It would be right.

He took the second touch in the fourth exchange from firm planks, with one burst, along the line he had chosen. *One*, he counted. *Of five.*

"Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

The Blade shook his hand at the centre and grinned at him. He had a story now that would last him a lifetime in a town this size: the provision boy had come to the confluence, and the town had shouted, and the boy had lost a point to it.

"They're always like that," he said, nodding up at the vault. "Wait till you see them for the bargeman."

The main hall was across the square, and when Cael crossed to it at noon to watch the Iron, he found it half empty.

It was a handsome building, the river guild's own, new that year.

It had a sprung floor of pale oak that was the best floor of the whole season, and seating on all four sides for six or seven hundred, and a dais at the east rail where the panel sat behind its little boards.

The gallery had perhaps two hundred people in it. They sat in the warm with plenty of room, eating pies, and looked faintly guilty when the salt store roared again across the square.

Withrow and Bracken sat in the front row of the east side, below the panel's dais, with a rug across both their knees. They had sat there since the first bell, Bracken said afterward, because the chancellor of a house watches her house's brackets, and because somebody had to.

Withrow turned her head as Cael came along the rail and asked him, in a perfectly level voice, whether the salt store was as full as it sounded. He said it was fuller, and she nodded, and turned back to the floor.

He found the others on the bench by the north rope. They had done well, and they had done it in the ordinary way.

Lira had beaten the coastal Mire in the morning, two touches to nothing in three exchanges.

A Mire fighter worked by making the floor feel deeper than it was, and Lira had declined to put her feet where he wanted them. She had spent one burst, Karis said, and never once crossed the place he kept pointing her at.

She had then beaten a valley Blade in the quarterfinal, two to one in four, which she said was one more touch than she had meant to give him.

Brom had taken his Copper quarterfinal and his semifinal in a morning, without anybody in the gallery noticing that either had happened.

Ephram had beaten the eighth seed in the first round.

Then in the quarterfinal he had drawn the top seed, the river academy's first entry, a tall heavy Stone who had headed the confluence's Iron list since the seeding went up.

They had been level after four exchanges, a touch apiece, and the figures had decided it. Ephram had twenty-five and the Stone twenty-four.

"I watched his feet," said Ephram, when Cael asked. He said it lightly, but his ears had gone red. "The whole bout. I didn't look at a single shoulder."

"He beat the top seed," said Lira to Cael, as if Ephram were not there. "Did you hear? He beat the top seed and then came and sat down and wrote it in his book."

"I'll write it properly tonight," said Ephram.

Karis had won twice, and both times on the figures. Both bouts had been level after four exchanges, a touch apiece, and both times the panel had given it to her by a point. She did not seem surprised.

"I've watched this panel since breakfast," she said. "Three of the five mark control above everything. One of them marks it twice." She turned a page. "So I fought for control. It's not very exciting to watch."

"It's very exciting to watch," said Brom. "Just not for them." He nodded at the gallery and its pies.

On the far side of the hall the hill-river house's Force fighter had finished his quarterfinal a few minutes before Cael came in. He had won it two to nothing in four.

Cael had missed it, and he was sorry, because he wanted to see the man build.

"You didn't miss much," said Lira. "Or you missed everything. I can't tell yet." She was looking across the boards at the far rope, where the builder sat on his bench with a towel round his neck and his eyes closed. "He made one shape in each exchange. The same shape every time, three corners and a door. The other boy never even saw the door until it shut."

She described it while the next bout was called, drawing it on her knee with one finger. The builder did not attack. He walked to a place on the floor and stood there, and that was one corner.

He walked to another, so that the first was behind his opponent's shoulder, and that was the second.

Every step he took was modest and every one of them was sound, and by the time the other boy noticed that there were three of them, the only way out of the triangle they made was the one the builder was standing in.

"And you?"

"I saw it shut," said Lira. "That's not the same as seeing it." She looked away from the builder at last. "Ask me tomorrow."

The caravan captain was waiting at the salt store's rope after noon, in a sheepskin coat with the wool turned in, and he took it off without hurry and folded it over the rope.

He was a lean, weathered man of middle age, a head shorter than Cael had expected from his hand, with grey in his short beard and a convoy guard's habit of looking at everything once and not again.

He had spent his working life on the salt road, the steward said, riding guard on the wagon trains that came down out of the eastern hills to the confluence's barges. He was Bronze, and he was a Shield.

Before he went to his mark he walked the square once, slowly, round its whole edge, and pressed the planks here and there with the heel of his boot.

He tested them like a carter trying the bed of a wagon he has been told is sound, without any particular suspicion and without taking anybody's word for it.

At the river end he stopped and held his palm low to the crack under the great doors, feeling the draught, and then walked on.

"He's pricing the floor," said Rooke, at the rope, to nobody. It was the nearest thing to approval he had said all morning.

Cael had fought Shields this season. The valley house's Shield at the mill town had sat behind his coverage for three exchanges and renewed it steadily, as some men whistle. The Shield reserve at home renewed his on a count you could hear him keep.

But Cael had never fought a Shield above Iron, and he had never fought one who did it for his bread.

"Exchange."

The captain did not come off his mark. He set his coverage first.

Cael watched him do it, and it was like watching a man pitch camp.

The captain took a breath, planted his back foot, and the first pane came up in front of him: a flat clear stiffness in the air at arm's length, which the read showed as a plane where the floor's weight-language simply stopped.

Then another breath, another plant, and a second pane came up on his left. A third breath, a third plant, and a third on his right, a little back, covering the corner.

Three panes, like guards round a wagon at a halt, each of them standing at its own post, with the captain at the middle of them, quite still, breathing slowly, and looking at Cael over the front pane as a man looks over a gate at a field he means to cross whatever is in it. Then he began to walk.

He walked straight at Cael, steadily, and the panes walked with him; he did not swing them about or throw them, but brought them across the floor as a convoy brings its guard down a road, all together, at the pace of the slowest, without hurry and without gaps.

Cael tried the left side with a probe. His palm met the pane at arm's length with a dull dead push, like pressing a door that is locked from the inside, and slid off.

He tried the right, on foot, and met the same.

He spent a burst to get behind the man, from firm planks, and came down out of the landing beat at the captain's back.

A fourth pane was there already. The captain had not even turned round. He had simply taken a breath and planted a foot as Cael began to move, and the pane had come up behind him as Cael landed.

*Two*, Cael counted. *Of five, today.*

So he stopped trying to get round, and watched.

That was the thing about panes, he found. They did not last. Each one stood for a while and then began, very slightly, to thin.

It was nothing the eye could see. But the read could feel the edge of the floor's weight-language come back a little at the plane where it had stopped, like warmth coming back into a hand.

And before any pane could thin far, the captain renewed it.

Breath. Plant. Pane. And a fresh one stood where the old one had been.

He did it to one pane at a time, never two. Cael watched four renewals before he saw the order, and four more to be sure.

The captain always renewed the oldest first. Rear, then left, then front, then right, and round again in the same order, each one at the moment it began to thin.

It was the order in which a careful man changes the guard on a wagon train, so that there are always three fresh men out and only ever one coming off.

The glass ran out with nobody touched. "Even."

The captain walked back to his mark without looking at him. He did not need to look. He had spent the exchange learning Cael too, from behind his panes.

---

In the second exchange Cael went for the change of guard.

He had the order and the count. He knew which pane would be next to renew and nearly when, because the captain's breath came before it, and the read could take a breath off a man's ribs a fraction before he drew it.

In the half-beat when the old pane was gone and the new one not yet set, that side of the captain was open.

He went for the left as it came round, on foot, fast.

He was early on the breath and late on the pane. The captain drew, planted, and the new pane came up into the open half-beat a hand's width in front of Cael's palm. It came up faster than any step Cael owned.

And the captain had known he would come. He had learned the boy in the first exchange, as Cael had learned him.

As Cael's palm struck the new pane and stopped dead, the captain turned the pane a little on its hinge, so that Cael's arm went wide with it. His own right hand came round the end of it, short and neat, and touched Cael on the ribs.

"Touch. The convoy."

Somebody in the barn shouted *The salt road!* and the whole store took it up.

Cael walked his circle. The planks were cold and dead under him, and the draught from under the river doors ran over his ankles.

He could not beat the renewal with his feet. That was the fact.

A new pane came up faster than a step, and faster than a burst could set and land, and a man who renewed by the clock always had the next guard ready before you arrived.

You could find the gap and still not get through it, because the gap was only a half-beat wide and the captain owned both ends of it.

But a gap a man had to keep paying to close was a cost, and a cost could be taxed.

He thought of Lira at the mill town, and of the builder's three corners and a door. *Tax it at the edge while it's still being built.*

A pane was being built every time the captain drew a breath, and for that half-beat it was not yet a pane. It was a declaration halfway through being made.

He had two contacts. That was the circle's rule for public floors, written down in Karis's hand and kept ever since: the ignition at a point, on contact, twice in a bout, and never once more.

The record had it already, on a thin exhibit at the back of the Greyvane transcript that nobody ever read, and on the wing's copper vessel, four instants long. It would be no news to any paper. It would only be news to a hundred and forty people in a salt store.

He came back to his mark at the call and stood waiting, walking on the spot so the planks would not take his feet's warmth, and counted the captain's guard round once more in his head.

"Exchange."

He went for the rear as it came up for renewal.

This time he did not try to beat the pane. He tried to meet it.

The captain drew his breath, planted, and the new pane began to come up, and Cael's palm arrived at the place where it was rising at the moment it rose, and he lit the ignition at the contact.

It was one point of heat, the size of a thumb, at the heel of his hand. It went into the half-made pane as it set.

The pane came up wrong. Cael felt it through his palm: a stiffness that did not stiffen evenly, cracked across like ice on a pail.

The captain felt it too. He drew a second breath and planted again and declared the rear pane a second time, cleanly, and it stood.

It cost him a beat.

It was only one beat, but his guard ran on a clock, and the clock had just stopped for a beat and started again, so that the left pane, which should have been renewed next, was renewed a beat late.

For that beat it stood old and thin, past its proper time, and the read felt the floor's weight-language seep back through it like water through a worn sack.

Cael was on the wrong side for it. He had been at the rear. He burst, from firm planks, round and across to the left, and the landing beat locked him there a stride from the thin pane as the captain drew the breath to renew it.

He was a stride too far. The pane came up fresh in front of him. The glass ran out.

"Even."

*Three of five gone*, he counted, walking. *One contact gone. One left.*

It did not matter that he had missed. He had seen it.

The tax on one pane did not stay on that pane. It went down the line, because the captain's whole guard was a single clock, and a clock that runs a beat late runs a beat late everywhere.

---

He did not burst again.

In the fourth exchange the captain changed his clock. He was a good man at his trade, and he had felt the cracked pane and the late renewal and understood them at once.

He shortened his count. Now he renewed each pane sooner, before it had begun to thin at all, so that a beat lost to a tax would come out of slack and never out of the pane's own life.

That was the right answer, and he paid for it. He was drawing breath more often now and planting more often, and his walk across the floor grew shorter in the stride.

He was a convoy changing its guard twice as fast to keep it fresh. That was more men on the road and less ground covered.

Cael gave him two full rounds of the new clock to listen to. He walked the slow inside them, as Rooke had written on the sheet, with his feet moving all the time on the cold planks.

Rear, left, front, right, at the shorter count. Rear, left, front, right.

Then he went to meet the left pane as it came up, and lit the second contact into it.

The left pane came up cracked. The captain drew and planted and declared it again, and it stood, and it had cost him the beat.

And now there was no slack in his clock, because he had spent his slack buying freshness, so the beat came straight out of the next pane's life. It came out of the front pane, which stood one beat past its time, thinning, at the very moment Cael arrived in front of it on foot.

He had walked round the captain's left while the second declaration was being made, and he had not needed a burst to get there.

He put his right palm through the thinning front pane, into the soft dead air where it was failing, and flat on the captain's breastbone.

"Touch. Halcenvane. One to one."

The barn made a sound Cael had not heard a crowd make before, a sort of gasp that turned into a roar halfway through.

There was one exchange left of the five the rules allowed. The captain went back to his old long count, which was honest of him, because the short one was costing him more than it saved.

Cael had no contacts left and did not need them. He had the clock now, both clocks, and it was enough to keep himself where the panes were not.

Late in the exchange the captain renewed his right pane a fraction early, to cover a step Cael had only pretended to take. Cael took the step on the other side instead, and touched him on the hip.

"Touch. Halcenvane. Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

The captain stood still for a moment with his hands at his sides. Then he let his panes go. Cael felt them go, all four at once, the floor's weight-language coming back across the square like a tide.

He came across to the centre and took Cael's hand, and held it, and looked him in the face for the first time in the bout.

"You found the change of guard," he said. "Most never hear it. You heard it and then you charged me toll on it." He turned Cael's right hand over in his own and looked at the heel of it, where the ignition had been. "That's a hot hand for a Bronze to meet on a cold day."

"It's on my record," said Cael.

"I don't doubt it. Everything about you's on a record somewhere, they tell me." The captain let go. "We say on the salt road that a convoy's only as fresh as its oldest guard. I've said it to young guards for most of my life." He reached for his coat on the rope. "Never had it said back to me before. Well. I've lost a wagon after all."

He went off through the crowd with the sheepskin over his shoulders, and people stood back to let him by.

Several of them clapped him on the back as he passed, as though he had won, and Cael thought that in the way the salt road counted such things he very nearly had.

Lira was at the rope when Cael came round. She had come across from the main hall between her bouts, still in her wraps, and had got in at the back by telling the steward she was his sister.

"I saw the last two," she said. "The cracked one, and then the walk." She looked at him with her head on one side. "You charged him at the edge while it was still going up. That's mine. I did that to the pillar at the mill town."

"I know. I thought of you."

"Good," said Lira. "Now give it back. I've a builder tomorrow." She went off to her own floor at a run, with her coat flapping.

Rooke said nothing at all when Cael came off. He held out the coat, and when Cael had his arms in it he took the training sheet out of his own coat and wrote one line under the line about walking the slow, and put it away again without showing it to anybody.

Cael walked his circle at the rope. He found that he was smiling and could not stop.

*Shield runs on a clock*, he thought. *Every Shield I've ever met keeps one. Find the clock. Charge toll on the change of guard. That's the whole of it.*

---

The third bout of the day was an academy Stone from a house in the eastern hills, who fought honestly and quite slowly and seemed glad of it afterward, and Cael beat him two to nothing in three exchanges while the queue outside the doors grew longer in the dark.

He used one burst, so that four of the floor's five were spent, and the hip let him know about it on the walk back along the quay.

When he came out of the salt store into the dusk, the steward was standing on the step with his list held against his chest. He was arguing with forty or fifty people who were not going to be let in.

They had waited in the snow all afternoon. Some had come in from farms up the valley that morning, and one man said he had walked from the next town.

The steward kept saying *a hundred and forty places* and *the regulations of the river guild*, and they kept not going home.

In the end Gault went and stood beside the steward, said nothing whatever, and simply held his case in front of him by its handle. The crowd seemed to find this official enough, and drifted away up the lane in twos and threes, looking back.

Across the square, through the main hall's high windows, Cael could see the Iron quarterfinal still running in the lamplight, to a gallery with whole rows of empty benches in it.

"That," said Karis beside him quietly, "has never happened at a regional meet. Not once. I'd have found it."

"What has?"

"A steward turning people away from an exhibition." She was writing as they walked. "While the seeded brackets play to empty seats next door. It isn't supposed to be possible. An exhibition counts for nothing." She closed the book. "Somebody's going to have to put that in a report. I'd give a great deal to read how they phrase it."

Lira did not come down to supper until it was nearly over. She had spent the end of the afternoon in the main hall's gallery after her quarterfinal, alone, watching the builder fight his last bout of the day from the back row, and she came to the table with her hands cold and her eyes far away and ate what Brom put in front of her without looking at it.

"Well?" said Ephram.

"He's very good," said Lira. "He's the best one this year that isn't on a sheet in Rooke's coat."

She put down her spoon. "Every boy who fought him today fought inside his shapes. They tried to get out of them, and they tried to break them, and they were clever about it, and it didn't matter, because the shapes were his and the boys were only living in them." She looked round the table. "I'm going to find out tomorrow whether I have to."

He wrote the day that night at the inn, with the river loud under the window.

*Three today, on dead planks over stone; five free, four spent. The Blade got his point from the crowd, item two, and a true one. An academy Stone, honest and slow. And a Bronze Shield off the salt road.*

*The Shield is the one to write down. His coverage comes in panes, a few at a time, and every pane is a separate declaration. Breath, plant, pane, each time. They thin, so he renews them, oldest first, on a clock, like a man changing the guard on a wagon train.*

*You can't beat a renewal with your feet; the new pane comes up faster than anything I own. But you can meet it. Ember at the contact, on the pane that's coming up, cracks it as it sets. He has to declare it twice, and the beat that costs him runs down his whole clock and comes out of some other pane's life. Two contacts, the circle's rate. The second one was the bout.*

*It's the first new thing I've learned on a floor since the slate. I think it may be the whole answer to Shield. Every Shield has a clock. Find it, and tax the change of guard.*

*I'm pleased with myself tonight, and I'm writing that down too, so I'll know later what it looked like.*

Across the landing, behind Lira's door, the lamp burned until very late.

# Chapter 22 — Rent

Lira warmed up for the builder in the corridor behind the main hall, alone, with her back to a stack of folded trestles.

She did not do her bursts; she did the speed line on foot, up and down the flagstones, with her hands loose and her eyes on nothing, and then stood still for a long time with her palms flat on the cold wall and her head down between her arms, breathing. Cael watched from the door and did not go in. He had learned a long time ago which of her silences wanted company and which wanted a witness, and this one wanted neither.

When the steward called the first semifinal she straightened up, shook out her hands, and came past him into the hall.

"I'm not going to fight his bout," she said as she went by. She did not stop. "I watched three of them yesterday. Every boy who went in there fought inside it. I'm going to fight mine and let him visit."

The hall was fuller than it had been the day before. The salt store's exhibitions did not start until after noon, and some of the crowd that had been turned away at its door the night before had come across the square instead, to see the provision boy's people. They filled the north side and spilled round into the west. Withrow and Bracken were in the front row of the east side again, under the panel's dais, with the rug across their knees.

Cael stood at the north rope between Rooke and Brom, and Karis sat on the bench behind them with her notebook open on her knee. She had a semifinal of her own to fight in an hour, and she had decided to spend the hour watching this.

"Whoever wins this," she said, "wins the region. Everybody in the hall knows it, and the final will be a formality to everybody but whoever's in it." She looked at the far rope, where the builder was taking off his quilted jacket. "Ephram will mind very much being a formality."

"Exchange."

The builder came off his mark at a walk.

He was broad and dark and not tall, with a heavy, sloping, patient way of carrying his shoulders, like a man who has spent his life carrying beams up ladders and has stopped noticing that they are heavy. He walked four paces toward the north-west corner of the square, stopped, and planted both feet. Cael felt it through the read before he understood what he was feeling. The floor's weight-language did not simply carry the man's weight down into the boards. It carried something forward off him, a long way forward, a held push laid out across the oak in front of him like a plank set across a ditch.

That was the Force: he did not throw it, but set it, and left it there, and walked away from it.

Lira stayed in the middle of the square and watched him do it.

He walked three more paces along the north rope and set another. Then he turned and came back toward the centre on a slant and set a third, and Cael saw the shape close in his head before it closed on the floor. There were three held pushes in a triangle round the north-west of the square, each a stride or two long and running at an angle to the next. Between them lay a pocket of open floor, and Lira was standing in its mouth.

The door was the gap between the last push and the builder himself.

He stepped into it.

Lira went out through the side of the triangle with one burst, low and flat, before the door shut. She came down out of the landing beat on clean floor by the south rope with her hand already up.

The builder had expected exactly that. He had built the triangle to have a side a fighter could burst through, and he had built it so that whoever went through it landed in one particular place. As Lira's landing beat locked her there, he was already walking to it, without hurry, and he put a flat palm on her shoulder before the beat let her go.

"Touch. Hill-river house."

There was a sound from the north side of the gallery, like air going out of a bag.

Lira walked her circle without looking at the builder or at the rope. She looked at the floor where the three pushes had been, and Cael could see her drawing them again behind her eyes, the way she drew a new floor's soft places.

Rooke said nothing at all.

"She let him close it," said Brom, very low. "She stood in the mouth and let him close it, and went out the side he made for going out of."

"She bought a look at it," said Cael.

"It cost her a touch."

"She knows."

The rest of the first exchange was quiet. The builder made a second shape, a long one this time against the east rope, and Lira did not go near it at all, but walked round the outside of it with her hands loose, watching him set each push as a buyer watches a mason lay the courses of a wall she has not yet agreed to pay for. The glass ran out with no second touch, and the gallery on the north side murmured, disappointed.

At the rope Lira took a mouthful of warm water from Gault, spat it into the bucket, and said one thing to Rooke.

"He needs a beat for each corner," she said. "He stops and plants, and then he sets it. It's a beat every time."

"Yes," said Rooke.

"That's all," said Lira, and went back to her mark.

---

In the second exchange she began to collect rent.

The builder started the same shape. He had no reason not to: it had won him every bout of the meet, and it had just won him a touch off the best Wind fighter in the region. He walked to the north-west and stopped, and planted.

Lira was already there.

She did not try to stop him setting the push. She arrived at the place where it would run, a stride in front of him, in the beat when he had planted and not yet set, and she struck at his lead shoulder with an open hand. It was nothing like a touch, only a quick glancing slap at the joint with the backs of her fingers, the kind of blow that does no harm and has to be answered.

He answered it. He had to, because a palm that close to the shoulder might become a touch, and a fighter who is setting a push cannot also cover his shoulder. He gave the beat to covering it, and then set the push a beat late, and set it shorter than he meant, because he had less time to lay it out; by then Lira was gone, three strides away, walking.

He went to the second corner. She was there too.

She did not burst to get there. She had seen where the second corner would be from the angle of the first. It was where it always was, because a builder who has a good design does not vary it, and she walked across the square to it while he was still finishing the first. She was waiting a stride off the place when he arrived, and in the beat after he planted she came in at his ribs. It was another glancing nothing, and he had to cover it, and the second push went down late and thin.

The third corner she left alone.

He set it cleanly and in full, and stepped into the door to close the shape, and closed it, and found nothing in it. Lira was out on the far side of the square, by the south rope, standing on clean floor with her hands loose. She had not been inside his triangle at any time since the first exchange.

He had built a whole structure, and paid for it, and closed it on empty boards.

"Look at his breathing," said Karis behind Cael, very quietly.

Cael looked, and saw the builder's shoulders going up and down. Each push cost him something to lay out and more to hold, and he had held three. Lira had spent one burst in the bout so far and walked for the rest of it.

"She isn't trying to touch him," said Brom.

"She's charging him for every board he builds on," said Karis. "At the corners, while they're going up. Look at his second one. It's half the length of the first. He's paying more for less."

The builder knew it. He was a good fighter, and a good fighter in his first big bout of the year knows when he is losing ground that does not show on a slate. He started the shape a third time, then changed it as he went. Instead of three corners he made two, set side by side along the east rope like the jaws of a vice, and began to walk Lira back toward them.

She let him walk her. She went back step by step, and on the step before the jaws she slapped his lead shoulder at the plant, and he covered it, and the second jaw went down late. She walked out round the end of it.

The glass ran out with no touch on either side. "Even."

There was a buzz along the north side. Some of the gallery did not know what it had just watched, and some of it did.

Lira came to the rope for her water and said nothing to Rooke this time, and Rooke said nothing to her. He was watching the builder, who stood at his mark with both hands on his hips, getting his breath. He was looking at the floor, as Lira had looked at it after the first exchange, and Cael could see him pricing the second exchange and not liking the figure.

"He'll change it again," said Rooke. "He's got another design in him. Maybe two."

"It doesn't matter," said Karis, writing. "She's stopped looking at his designs."

In the third exchange the builder changed it twice, and Karis was right.

He opened with a new shape that Cael had not seen him use in three bouts. It was a long line of short pushes, set one after another along the diagonal like stepping-stones, to cut the square in half and pen Lira in the smaller part. It was clever, and it was sound, and it would have beaten most of the Irons in the region.

Lira did not look at the line. She looked at him.

Every push he laid needed the same thing first, whatever the design: a stop, and a plant, and a small pause while he chose where the next one would run. Cael had seen it with the read since the first exchange: a fraction of a beat in the knees and the hips, a settling, before the Force went out of him onto the floor, and it was there in the triangle and in the vice and in the stepping-stones alike. The shapes were different. The man underneath them was the same.

Lira had found the man.

She came in at the fourth stepping-stone, not to strike at the shoulder this time but to pass him. She arrived on the settle, the instant of choice, when his knees had committed to planting and his eyes had not yet chosen the next angle. She went by him on the inside so close that her shoulder brushed his sleeve, turned behind him, and put her palm flat between his shoulder blades.

"Touch. Halcenvane. One to one."

The north side of the gallery came up off the benches.

The builder turned round slowly. He looked at Lira for a long moment, and Cael saw the moment when he understood that she had not beaten his line. She had walked straight through the pause he took before every line he had ever built.

He tried the second change then, at once, with half the glass still to run. He stopped pausing, and laid his pushes on the move, without planting, each one quicker and shorter and weaker, trying to give her no settle to arrive on.

It was the right idea and it cost him more than everything before it. A push laid on the move is laid without the floor under it. It came out of his own body and nothing else, and Cael could see him spending it, a breath at a time, like a man paying for the same goods twice. The pushes were thin enough now that Lira walked through two of them without slowing. She did not touch him again, because she did not need to. She kept him building, and kept walking through what he built, and the glass ran out on a man who had spent half his strength on structures nobody was standing in.

"Even."

"She could have had the second there," said Brom.

"She could have," said Rooke. "She'd have had to burst for it, from the wrong foot, into a man who still has his hands. That's a coin toss. She doesn't want a coin toss. She wants the panel."

"The panel," said Karis, "is three people who mark control above everything, and one who marks it twice." She closed the notebook. "She's been fighting for them since the first exchange. I don't think she's even thinking about them. It's simply where she lives now."

The fourth exchange was the quietest of the bout, and Cael thought afterward that it had been the best.

The builder came out to it still building, because building was what he was. But he was building now against the Lira of the first two exchanges, the one who had come to his corners and slapped at his shoulders, and that Lira had left the floor some time ago. He set his corners where she had come to tax them, and covered his shoulders against slaps that did not come, and left gaps open on the side she had used to walk round the vice.

Lira was not there. She had moved on.

She ran a new thing, one he had not seen. She stayed far from him, at the length of the square, and made him come to her, so that every push he laid was laid toward her and never round her. Then she simply was not where it pointed, because she was walking, early and easy, a stride to one side or the other, so that each push ran past her into empty floor like a ditch dug beside the road. Twice she came in close on his settle, as she had for the touch, and twice he covered just in time. He had learned that much. Twice she went away again without trying to finish, as though finishing were somebody else's business.

The glass ran out with the builder standing in the middle of the square, breathing hard, surrounded by pushes he had laid on empty floor, and Lira walking her circle by the south rope with her hands in front of her, looking as fresh as she had at the first call.

"Level after four," said the steward. "The figures decide."

The panel took its eleven minutes.

They were the longest eleven minutes Cael had sat through all season, and Lira did not sit through them at all. She walked up and down the corridor behind the hall again, past the stacked trestles, with her coat round her shoulders, and would not let anybody come with her.

The clerk chalked the figures on the board at the east rail.

Lira had twenty-seven, and the builder twenty-five.

The north side of the gallery made more noise than the main hall had made all meet. Somewhere in the front row of the east side, under the panel's dais, Withrow's hands came together twice beneath the rug and then stopped, because she had remembered that she was the chancellor of a house, and the chancellor of a house does not clap her own fighters in somebody else's hall. Bracken looked at the board, and at the chancellor, and then very deliberately at nothing.

The builder crossed the square and shook Lira's hand. He held it a moment longer than was usual.

"I couldn't find you," he said. He sounded more puzzled than anything. "I built all morning and I couldn't find you in any of it."

"You built very well," said Lira. "I'd have lost to the first one, a year ago. I'd have gone in and lived in it." She let go of his hand. "I'm sorry. It was good work."

Cael wrote the bout down that night, and found the sentence harder to write than any he had written about her before.

*Lira, the semifinal, the region's title in all but the paperwork: level after four, twenty-seven to twenty-five on the figures. I started to write that she took away his structures, and it isn't true. She didn't take anything away. She let him keep all of it, and charged him for it, and then walked through the man who built it. The honest version is this: she has stopped fighting inside other people's bouts at all. They come and visit hers now, and she's a good host, and they leave poorer.*

*Rooke said one word to her at the rope afterward. I didn't hear it. She did, and went red to the ears, which I have seen happen to Lira twice in my life.*

Karis fought her semifinal an hour later, against Ephram.

It was not a family argument this time, but an exam between two people who had sat next to each other through the whole course and copied nothing from each other's slates. Karis knew all of Ephram's entries, and Ephram knew Karis read panels, and both of them knew the other knew, so that the whole bout had a faint air of two clerks auditing each other's books in public, politely, with nothing whatever to hide. Ephram took the first touch off the plain entry, the one he had been mixing in since the first squad session. Karis took the second exchange's touch on contact with the ignition, a single point of heat at the heel of her palm on Ephram's forearm as he turned. The steward hesitated, as stewards always did with Ember, and then called it.

In the fourth Ephram took his second off the late half-turn, the move that had won him the mill town, which Karis had charted and could not stop. Two to one. The figures, which nobody needed, were twenty-four to twenty-two.

Karis shook his hand at the centre and said something that made him laugh. Then she came back to the bench and sat down and opened her notebook.

"Third," she said, before anybody could say anything. The confluence fought no bout for third. It stood both beaten semifinalists third together, as it had always done, so Karis and the builder would share the place. "It's a very good third. The pool was worth more than the placing." She turned the pages over one by one, so they could see. "Two panels, from the rope and from the floor. The best Iron in the region, from four feet away, all morning. Two people I'll meet again at Norhold, charted. And Ephram's late half-turn, which I couldn't stop, and now know why." She closed the book. "I'd pay a quarterfinal for any one of those. I paid a semifinal for all of them."

Brom's Copper final came between the semifinals and the Iron final, because the Copper draw was smaller and finished early.

It was a patient bout, and it was nobody's spectacle, and everybody who knew Copper watched it with great respect. Brom's opponent was a hill-country veteran in his middle twenties, a farmer's son with enormous hands who had fought in the Copper since before Brom was on the circuit and had never risen in it and never wanted to, and he fought Brom honestly and well for four exchanges. "Two to nothing. Bout to Halcenvane."

The veteran shook Brom's hand at the rope with both of his.

"You've a very dull way of winning," he said, and shook his head, smiling. "I'd heard the stories. I'd hoped you'd be more fun."

"I'm sorry," said Brom.

"Don't be. Dull wins." The veteran clapped him on the arm and went.

Karis wrote the line in her notebook while the clerk was chalking it.

"Four meets, four titles, no bout lost," she said. "In the Copper." She tapped the line with her pencil. "That's very flat, Brom."

"It's my line," said Brom. "I like it flat."

"He knows her better than she'd like," said Cael.

Lira had fought four exchanges that morning against the best fighter in the region, and spent less than anybody could have believed. But she had spent something, and Cael could see it in the way she stood at her mark. She stood a little heavier on her heels than she had at the first call, and her hands were a fraction slower to settle.

Ephram could see it too. He had been looking for it.

"Exchange."

He went straight in with the late half-turn, and then did not finish it. He turned the front foot half round, which was the move, and when Lira went to where the move would arrive, he was not there. He had gone plain, straight through the middle of her, with the first weight and nothing behind it. It was the plainest entry he owned, and the one least worth watching, and he had kept it back all meet for this.

His palm landed on her collarbone.

"Touch. Halcenvane." The steward looked from one of them to the other. "The—Blade."

The gallery laughed and then cheered.

Lira walked her circle with her eyebrows up. At the rope she drank her warm water and looked across at Ephram for some time.

"He's been saving that," she said.

"Since the first squad session," said Cael.

"He'll have saved something else, then." She handed the cup back to Gault. "He's a saver. He saves pages."

She took the second exchange off him, and it cost her more than anything had cost her all morning.

Ephram did not come at her this time. He waited. He stood off at the length of the square and let her come, and she came, because she was the one who had been touched and a Wind fighter who has been touched wants the floor back. She tried him on the outside, at speed, and he gave her nothing but a turn. She tried him on the inside and he gave her another. He was watching her feet, as he had learned to on the slate, and her feet were a fraction heavier than they had been that morning, and he could see it.

Cael could see him seeing it. The read showed Ephram's weight settling a hair earlier on each of her approaches, a man getting used to a rhythm and starting to lean on it.

Lira saw it too. On her fourth approach she did not approach. She stopped a stride short, on the very place where he had begun to expect her to arrive, and he had leaned for the arrival, and for a beat he was leaning at nothing. Then he threw the late half-turn to cover himself, and this time he finished it, because there was nothing else to throw. She burst once, from firm oak, round the outside of the turn. She was a quarter of a beat ahead of him and no more, and her palm was on his ribs before his front foot had come all the way round.

"Touch. Halcenvane, the Wind. One to one."

"She bought that," said Brom, very low. "She paid him a whole exchange of looking tired to buy it."

"She was tired," said Karis.

"Yes," said Brom. "That's why it was cheap."

The third ran out untouched, and was the best exchange of the meet that hardly anybody in the gallery saw. They went at each other across the whole square, quick and light and close, and each knew the other's next step a fraction early, so that for the whole glass neither of them could turn the fraction into a touch, and the steward's flag hung at his side and twitched twice and never rose. Ephram was watching her feet, as he had learned to on the slate, and she was watching his heel, as Rooke had taught them all to in the first week of the year. Twice Ephram came within a hand of her ribs. Once she came within less of his.

"Even."

"He could win this," said Karis, very quietly.

"Yes," said Rooke.

In the fourth exchange he nearly did.

He made her come to him, standing his ground in the middle of the square on good oak and letting her spend herself on the approach, and she did. She tried the outside, and he turned. She tried the inside, and he turned again. On her third try she committed a little more than she meant, because she was tired and he was not, and he saw it. He came off his back foot into the gap with the plain entry, the one he had saved, and his palm went out for her ribs.

Lira was already moving. She had committed on purpose, a half-step, to give him the plain entry, because she had worked out in the second exchange that it was the only thing he had left that she had not seen twice. As his palm went out she turned inside it and touched his chest.

The two palms landed so close together that the gallery heard one sound.

The steward's flag went up for Halcenvane, the Wind.

"Touch. Halcenvane, the Wind. Two to one. Bout and title to Halcenvane."

There was a long breath in the hall, and then the north side of the gallery stood up.

Ephram stood very still in the middle of the floor, with his arm still half out, and then lowered it. He looked at the steward, and nodded once, and looked at Lira.

"How much?" he said.

"A quarter of a beat," said Lira. She was breathing hard. "Less. Your hand was cold."

"It's a cold hall," said Ephram. He held out his own hand, and she took it. "That was the best I had."

"I know," said Lira. "I'd have been very angry if it wasn't."

The figures went up a few minutes later: Lira twenty-six, Ephram twenty-five. It was her fourth title of the year, and she had not lost a bout. Ephram stood second in the region's Iron.

He did not go to the stove this time. He walked back to the delegation's rope with his chin up and his ears very red, took his coat from Gault, and put it on. Then he took out the file book and wrote in it standing, as he had at the quarry town.

Cael went and stood beside him and did not say anything.

After a while Ephram stopped writing.

"I had her," he said. "In the fourth. I had her for one beat, and I knew I had her, and I went." He looked at the page. "She'd given it to me. She gave me the one thing I'd saved, so she could see it coming." He closed the book. "That's the account. I'll want it at Norhold."

Rooke had come up behind them. He did not say anything about the bout. He only said, "Second in the region, on the year's last floor, to the region's champion, by a quarter of a beat. That's a seeding, at Norhold. Bank it."

"I've banked it," said Ephram.

Rooke looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, and went to find Lira, and Ephram went across the hall to help the reserves carry the trestles back to the corridor, as though it were any afternoon at all.

# Chapter 23 — The Bed of the River

The salt store turned people away again that afternoon, and this time nobody at the meet pretended to be surprised.

Cael fought his fourth challenger there at the third bell, between Brom's final and Lira's. He was a coastal Wind from a house on the estuary, quick and bright and a little in love with his own burst, and Cael liked him at once. He beat him two to nothing in three exchanges and spent one burst doing it. He could not afterward remember much about the bout except the noise, and the coastal Wind's open delight at being beaten by somebody he had read about. At the rope the boy asked whether he might write to him, and then, before Cael could answer, whether he might write to Lira, and then went red to the ears and said never mind.

When he came out of the river doors to cross back for the Iron final, the lane along the north quay was full from the store to the chandler's corner. The steward stood on an upturned crate with his list, telling them that there was no more room and there would be no more room. Some of them had been there since noon.

Across the square, the main hall's Copper final had played to rows of empty benches. A man selling hot pies at its door had picked up his tray at the second bell and carried it round to the salt store, where he sold out in a quarter of an hour.

"I watched him do it," said Brom, who had come out to find Cael. He said it without rancour, in the tone of a man reporting the weather. "The pies went for my final, and then they came for your Wind." He considered. "They were good pies, too."

The last bout of the meet was in the salt store at dusk, by lamplight. The brackets had finished an hour before, so for the first time the whole delegation could come and watch.

Withrow came. She had not set foot in the salt store all meet, on the principle, Bracken said, that the chancellor of a house watches its brackets and lets its exhibitions look after themselves. Now the brackets were over, and she walked across the square from the main hall on Bracken's arm, in the snow, with her collar up. The steward, seeing who she was, found two places on the front bench that had certainly not been empty a moment before. The barn was so full that people were sitting on the beams under the vault with their legs hanging down, and the steward had given up asking them to come down.

The barge-master was waiting at the rope.

He was the oldest man Cael had fought all year, and very likely the biggest, though not in the way the foreman had been big. He was long in the arm and deep in the chest, with a grey beard cut close and a face weathered to the colour of a wet hull. He wore a barge-master's short blue coat with brass buttons, and he took it off and folded it and hung it on the rope post with great care. It was very likely the only good coat he owned. He smelled faintly of tar and river.

He was an Iron Rank Nine of the Force Path, the steward said, and had worked the barges on these two rivers since he was a boy. In the deep cold, when the barges lay up, he fought. He had fought at the confluence every year for longer than the steward had kept the list. The gallery loved him, and it was easy to see why, because he was what the whole town was made of: the rivers, and the barges, and the long hard trade of the deep cold, when the ice came down in plates and a man who misread the water for one moment lost a boat and everything in it. When he raised one hand to the beams, the beams roared back.

"He was second seed in the Iron here twice," said Karis, at Cael's elbow. "Before he stopped entering. He says the brackets are for young men who want to go somewhere." She looked at her notebook and then shut it. "He's never wanted to go anywhere. He's been everywhere these rivers go."

Cael walked out onto the dead planks and put his weight down, one foot and then the other. *One gone today*, he counted. *Four left.*

The barge-master watched him do it from the far mark, with his big hands hanging, and nodded slowly. He approved, it seemed, of a man who tested the deck before he trusted it.

"Exchange."

---

The barge-master came to the middle of the square and stood there, and the floor began to move.

That was how it felt through the read. It was not like the builder's pushes, laid down and left, nor like the captain's panes, set and renewed. It was one push, long and steady, going out from the man's body across the planks in a broad slow sweep. It kept going and did not stop at any edge, but moved across the square like water moving across a flat, and wherever it went the floor's weight-language leaned with it, as reeds lean in a stream, all one way and all at once.

Cael watched it go across the planks for a few heartbeats before he went near it. He had never felt a Force fighter work like this. The builder had made walls and left them standing. This man made weather, and stood in the middle of it, and let it do his work.

Cael stepped into it to probe, at the documented rate, the careful way.

The push took his front foot and moved it.

It was not a blow and it did not hurt. It was as if the boards under his foot had become the deck of a boat in a current and the current had decided, politely and without discussion, that his foot belonged a hand's width to the left. He came down a hand's width to the left, and the next step had to start from there, and the barge-master was already somewhere else.

The gallery shouted. They had seen it before. It was what they had come for.

Cael gave ground and tried again from another angle. This time he set his feet hard as he went in, to stand against it, as he would have stood against Brom.

That was a mistake. He felt it the moment he made it.

The current did not push harder against a man who braced. It went round him, on both sides, and then the barge-master shifted it, the whole broad sweep of it, as river water shifts under ice from one bank to the other, and the floor that had been leaning left was suddenly leaning right. Cael's braced feet were set for left. He went right with the floor whether he liked it or not, two strides, fast, and the second stride came down on nothing he had chosen.

He went down onto one knee and caught himself on his right forearm, flat on the planks.

It was an honest stumble, the first he had made on a public floor all year that he had not planned. The arm took all of it. He felt the dead deal planks come up into the length of the bone from wrist to elbow, with the stone right under them, and knew before he was up again that it would be purple by morning.

No flag rose. He had not been thrown and nobody had touched him. The floor had simply moved, and he had fallen off it.

The barn went mad. Cael heard somebody on the beams above him laughing so hard he nearly fell off.

He got up. The barge-master was standing in the middle of the square where he had been all along, with his hands at his sides, watching him without any particular expression, like a skipper watching a new hand find his feet on a moving deck.

The glass ran out. "Even."

At the rope Gault took one look at the forearm and reached for his case.

"After," said Cael.

"After," Gault agreed, and took his hand off the case, and looked at the arm again as though he were writing its figure down.

In the second exchange Cael stood off and watched, and paid for the watching.

He kept away from the middle and let the current come to him, and every time it reached him he gave way to it, as a boy wades out into a river to learn how it pulls before he ever tries to swim it, letting it take his legs a little at a time and keeping his face toward the bank. He let it carry him a stride, two strides, never more, and walked back out of it. He was looking under the push for whatever it rested on.

The barge-master understood what he was doing almost at once, and did not like it. He began to shift the sweep more often, left and right and back, faster than a river ever shifts, so that Cael was always stepping out of one lean into another. On the third shift he caught Cael between two of them, with one foot leaning left and the other being moved right. He came in behind his own current, three long easy strides, and put a palm the size of a shovel flat on Cael's chest.

"Touch. The confluence."

The beams roared. The front bench, where Withrow sat, did not move.

---

Cael walked his circle with the forearm held against his side and thought about rivers.

He had grown up in Denvash, inland, with no river worth the name. But he had stood on the ferry landing under the bluff for a year now, and watched the water come down past Ostrand, and he had watched the barges here at the quays since he arrived. A river's surface shifted and swirled and went every way at once. Underneath, it went one way. It went the way the bed went, because the bed was stone and the water was only water.

The current had a bed too. He had felt it in the second exchange without quite knowing what he felt.

The barge-master's sweep went left and right across the floor. But it always came out of the same place, and that place was his back foot. The big man planted it at the start of every sweep, and the whole push ran out from it along one line, like water running out of a sluice. The lean that moved Cael's feet was on the surface. The line it ran along was underneath, and it only changed when the back foot moved.

The read could see a back foot. It saw nothing better. It could see where weight went down into the floor and which way it meant to travel, a fraction before it travelled. That was all the read had ever done, and here it was enough.

He thought, walking, of Lira on the builder's floor that morning, and of how she had stopped looking at the shapes and looked at the man. It was the same lesson in different water. The shapes were on top. The man was underneath, and the man did one thing every time, whatever the shapes did.

He had been standing across the grain, trying to hold his ground against water. You did not hold your ground against water. You found which way it was going and went with it, faster.

"Exchange."

He went out into the current and did not brace.

The sweep came, and took his front foot, and moved it a hand to the left. This time he let it. More than that, he went with it on purpose, a half-step further than it meant to carry him, because he had felt the back foot plant and knew which way the bed ran under the lean. The barge-master's push had meant to put him a hand to the left. He arrived a stride to the left instead, inside the line, closer than the current had any business bringing him.

He burst along the grain, from the planks, in the direction the push was already going.

It was the easiest burst of his season, because the floor was already leaning that way and the man's own Force was at his back, carrying him along the line it had chosen as a river carries a log, without effort and without any opinion about where the log would like to go. The burst and the current together took him three strides further than his record said a burst could go on dead planks. But it was the current that paid for the extra strides, not the burst. Any panel that knew Force Path work would see a fighter carried, not a fighter flying.

The landing beat locked him at the barge-master's left side, at an angle the big man's own push had chosen for him, close enough to touch.

He touched him on the ribs.

"Touch. Halcenvane. One to one."

*Two of five*, he counted, standing in the landing beat with the barn coming down round him.

The barge-master stepped back. He looked at the floor between them for a moment like a pilot who has run aground where there was deep water yesterday. Then he planted his back foot again, somewhere new, and started a fresh sweep along a new line, because he was an old hand and an old hand does not wait to be beaten twice the same way.

Cael watched the back foot plant. He saw the new line a fraction before the floor did.

He did not burst at once. He walked into the current along its new grain, let it lift him like a deck on a swell, and went with it. When the barge-master shifted the lean across his path to put him off his feet, Cael was already moving along the line underneath the lean, not across the top of it. He had nothing to be put off.

He burst once more, late in the exchange, along the new grain.

*Three of five.*

The landing beat set him at the big man's back. He put his palm between the shoulder blades, on the rough grey wool of the barge-master's shirt.

"Touch. Halcenvane. Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

There was a moment's silence in the salt store, the first all day. Then the beams came down.

---

The barge-master did not move for some time. He stood with his back to Cael, his hands at his sides, looking at the river doors at the far end of the barn, beyond which the water ran.

Then he began to laugh.

It started somewhere low in his chest and came up slowly, like his current across the floor, and it did not stop. He turned round still laughing and wiped his eyes with the back of one huge hand.

"You found the bed," he said. "You found the bed in two exchanges, lad. I've known bargemen thirty years on these rivers who never found it, and drowned." He came across and took Cael's right hand carefully, by the fingers, because he had seen the forearm. "Rode it too. Rode my own water into my own back. That's a thing I'll be telling at the quays till they bury me."

"I fell off it first," said Cael.

"Everybody falls off it first. That's how you learn there's something to fall off." The barge-master held his hand a moment longer and looked down at him with his head on one side, as he might have looked down into a strange barge that had come alongside his own in the dark. "On the river we hail a craft we don't know. *What are you carrying?* It's only manners. It's how you know whether to give her room." He paused. "So. What are you carrying?"

Cael thought about it honestly. Somewhere behind him in the crowd were the people who knew the answer as well as anybody could, and none of them could have said it in a barn.

"I don't know yet," he said.

The barge-master looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded, slowly, and let go of his hand.

"Then I hope it's cargo," he said, and went to get his coat.

Gault strapped the forearm in the corner by the river doors while the barn emptied round them. There was nothing wrong with it but the bruise. That ran the length of the bone on the outer side, wrist to elbow, already darkening under the skin.

"A week," said Gault. "It'll look worse than it is for most of it." He tied off the strapping and sat back. "The figure will remember the fall."

"It ought to," said Cael. "It was a real fall."

"Yes," said Gault. He looked at Cael over the top of the case for a moment, as he had looked at him across a panel table at the mill town, and something in his face was nearly amused. "It's a pleasant change."

Cael did not ask what he meant. He thought he knew.

Lira had come round from the front bench while Gault worked. She looked at the strapping and then at Cael's face, and seemed satisfied with both.

"You fell off a river," she said.

"I fell off a river."

"Good. It was the best thing in the bout." She sat down on the bench beside him. "The whole barn saw you fall, and get up, and work it out. Nobody who saw that will ever think you're a machine." She bumped his good shoulder with hers. "Not even the ones who write it down for a living."

He wrote the bout that night at the inn with his left hand, slowly, because the right one was strapped.

*Five bouts at the confluence, five won. The barge-master is the one to keep. He fights with a current: one long push across the whole floor, shifting left and right like water. You can't stand against it; it goes round a braced man and then moves the floor under him. But it has a bed. It comes out of his back foot along one line, and the shifting is all on top. Read the foot, find the line, and ride along it instead of across. Then his own water carries you, and the burst only has to steer.*

*Bill: one honest fall in the first exchange, on the right forearm, on dead planks over stone. A week of bruise. It'll cost me something on the figure, and for the first time all year I'm glad to pay a cost I didn't design. Twenty, twenty-six, and now whatever this is. The record can hold a fall. The record now breathes.*

*Bursts: five free on those planks; three spent today, four yesterday. Ember none today; two yesterday, on the captain. Shadow: rent from waking; deployment none.*

*He asked what I'm carrying. I told him I don't know. It's the truest thing I've said to a stranger all year, and he seemed to think it was a good answer.*

The digest came up the steep stair with the morning's papers a few days into the new term, bound in grey board, with the observation office's mark on its cover.

Vastin put it at the bottom of the pile. He had an old rule about the paper he most wanted to read, which was that it went last. A man who starts his day with it reads everything else by its light, and his day comes out crooked.

The pigeons had been fed. The porter had folded his paper bag and gone in. The court below the window was empty and grey, and the snow lay along the tops of its walls in a long clean line.

The summary was what such summaries always were. The observation office kept seats at every regional meet on the circuit and gathered their reports at each year's end into a digest. It was competent, thorough and dull, in a clerk's even hand.

Four meets in the western region. The enrollment of the unclassified practitioner noted, with its basis and its renewal. Thirteen exhibitions under the demonstration provision, thirteen won. The figures appended in a column, from twenty to twenty-six. A note on the house's results, and its qualification, and its finish, first in the region. A note on attendance at the confluence.

The note on attendance quoted the steward's closing report. Vastin read the quoted sentence twice, and found that he had smiled, which he had not expected to do that day.

He went back to the column of figures and read it down slowly. Twenty-three, twenty-two, twenty-four, and on, with a spread in the middle of the year so narrow that a careful clerk had put a small mark beside it.

Then a twenty-six and a twenty on the same morning in the slate country, and after that the confluence's five, wandering about between twenty-one and twenty-five like the figures of any good young fighter on any ordinary set of floors.

He had sat four feet from that young fighter in a room twelve feet by fourteen and asked him eleven questions. He had watched him on a frame that failed.

He knew, as well as anyone in this building knew anything about him, what the boy looked like when he was being careful. The column in front of him looked like a boy who had been careful for half a year and had then, at some point in the slate country, decided to be a little less so.

Or it looked like a fighter who had simply had a bad day on a cold floor and a good one on a warm one. A column of figures could not tell you which, and Vastin had spent his life refusing to make columns say more than they said.

He did not write anything in the margin about it.

He corrected two sums in the margin, because they were wrong, and set his initials in the box at the foot. Then he turned the last page and found that there was another page under it.

It had not been bound in with the digest. It had been pinned to its back, under a seal, and the seal was that of an office above his own.

He laid it on the table and did not read it at once.

He knew the seal. Everyone in the building knew it, as everyone knows the colour of a door they have never been through.

That office did not write to men at his desk. When it wanted something from this floor, the wanting came down through two or three floors between and arrived in the ordinary form, signed by somebody ordinary.

The last time anything had come to him from that height under its own seal was eleven years ago. He remembered it chiefly because it had concerned the drains.

There was no routing slip with the page. There were no initials anywhere on it.

He turned it over, and turned it back, and thought of two letters in an unfamiliar hand on the fourth line of a slip at the beginning of the year, and did not let himself suppose anything about them.

Then he read it.

---

It was a query, and it was short, and it had three numbered items.

Item one wanted the evaluating office's view on *mitigation options*, in respect of the subject's appearance at Norhold.

Item two wanted to know if the watch already in place could bear a *containment framing*, if one became necessary.

Item three wanted the subject's *exposure trajectory* set out through the finals, with the public's interest in mind.

He read it once and turned it face down on the table. Then he turned it up again and read it slowly, a word at a time, with one finger under the line, as he had been taught to read a charter when he was very young and had not read one since.

They were not his words. In forty years he had never once used any of them of a person.

*Mitigation* was a word for floods and fines. *Containment* was a word for fevers and fires. *Trajectory* was what a thing had when it had already been thrown, and you were only working out where it would land.

None of the three was a question. Each was the answer to a question, wearing a question's clothes.

An office that asked how a matter might be mitigated had already decided that the matter was a harm. An office that asked whether it could be contained had already decided that it was spreading. An office that asked for its trajectory had already decided where it was going, and wanted to know only how fast.

Somewhere above him, the grammar had changed.

He had watched grammar change before, in his time. It was never announced. A word that had lived for years in one kind of paper would begin to turn up in another kind, a little out of place, like a farm cart in a city street. Nobody remarked on it the first time, or the second. By the tenth time it had stopped looking out of place, and the people who used it had forgotten that it had ever been anywhere else. He had once seen *resettlement* travel in that way from a report on a flooded valley to a report on a quarrelsome town, in less than a year, and nobody who used it in the second report had ever read the first.

The words always came from above, and they always came quietly, and they always arrived before whatever they were the words for.

He did not let himself go further than that. He did not know what the office above him intended, and it was not his function to guess. His function was to evaluate what was in front of him. What was in front of him was a page with three words on it that did not belong to the thing they were about.

He sat for a long time with his left hand flat on the page. The hand had begun some years ago to dislike evenings as well as mornings, and it disliked this one.

Below the window the court grew dark, and the lamp in the porter's lodge came on, and nobody crossed the snow.

He could refuse to answer. That was not his habit and would not be read as anything but what it was. He could answer in the query's own words, which was what it wanted, and which he would not do.

Or he could answer the questions it ought to have asked, in the words a question ought to be asked in, and let the office above him see the difference for itself.

He made himself price it first, as he priced any advice before he gave it. At the beginning of the year an advisory of three sentences had cost him nothing, because it had only declined to misread a charter.

This would cost something. A man who answers an office above him in his own language, when it has written to him in its language, has told that office that he noticed.

He did not know yet what the noticing would cost. He knew it would not be nothing, and he found, turning the pencil over in his fingers, that he was prepared to pay it without knowing.

He took a sheet of paper and a pencil and began.

He wrote the first answer long, as he wrote everything he meant to make short, and then struck it through corner to corner and wrote it again in ink on a clean sheet. He did the same with the second and the third. It took him most of the evening, because each answer had to be built so that a reader could not lift a sentence out of it and use the sentence to mean the opposite.

When he had finished, the three answers lay one under another on a single page, and each began by turning its noun back into a question that an evaluator could honestly answer.

*Mitigation options. Read as: whether the subject's conduct presents a harm requiring mitigation. Evaluation: the subject's enrollment is lawful, adjudicated on the public record and renewed under the Compact's own observation. His conduct across four meets, as recorded by this office's seats, is lawful at every recorded point. Nothing in any record this office has examined describes a harm. Where there is no harm there is nothing to mitigate, and this office can offer no options for it.*

*Containment framing. Read as: whether the present observation arrangements are adequate to observe. Evaluation: they are. Whether anything observed under them is to be read in some other light is a matter for whoever means to read it so. Arrangements have no opinion. If the querying office has a particular concern, this office will evaluate it on receipt.*

*Exposure trajectory. Read as: how public attention to the subject is likely to develop at the finals. Evaluation: it will follow his results, as attention follows any fighter people pay to see. His results have been good. This office notes that being watched is not an offence.*

He read the page through once. Every sentence on it was true, and every sentence was an evaluation, and not one of them used the query's language except to translate it.

He signed it with his name entire, and took it down the steep stair himself before the evening dispatch closed, and the dispatch clerk logged it and gave him a slip.

He put the slip in his coat. It weighed nothing, and he was aware of it all the way back up the stair.

The reply came inside the week. It was a single line, under the same seal, on a half-sheet:

*Acknowledged. The submission of the evaluating office is entered.*

He read it at the window with the porter feeding his pigeons below. Then he laid it on the table beside his own three answers and looked at the two documents together for some time.

Acknowledged. Entered. It was not a rebuke. A rebuke would have been a kind of engagement, an answer to his answer.

This said only that his page had arrived and had been put somewhere. It did not say where, and it did not say that anybody had read it, or would.

The conversation the query belonged to had not stopped. It had simply been taken into another room, and the door had been closed from the other side, gently and without any sound at all.

He had been in the Compact forty years, and he knew that kind of quiet. He had sent it down the stairs himself, once or twice, to offices whose questions he had not wished to hear a second time.

He filed the half-sheet with the query and his answers, in the drawer where he kept the struck draft of his advisory from the beginning of the year. Then he went home.

He did not think about the query. He thought, for no reason he could have given, about the steward at the confluence, a man he would never meet, sitting up late over his closing report with his pen, and deciding after some struggle to write *it must be admitted*, and being glad of it.

The next morning he came in at the second bell, as always. He read the day's first bundle, as always. And then, before he had opened the second, he wrote out a direction in his own hand and gave it to his clerk.

It ordered the observation files for the continental finals at Norhold to his desk. It wanted all of them: the seating of the Compact's row in the Concourse gallery, the rotation of the observation officers, the liaison papers between the observation office and the tournament's adjudication office, and every instruction that had gone out under any of them.

Nothing in his grade required him to see those files. Nothing in his duties touched them. The finals lay a long road and two offices away from anything he was answerable for.

His clerk read the direction twice, entered it in the day book, and went to fetch the files. If he wondered about it, he did his wondering in the corridor.

Vastin did not explain the order to his clerk. He found, sitting at his table with the pigeons outside, that he had not explained it to himself either.

He looked for the reason, as he would have looked for a missing figure in another man's column, and it was not there. There was only the order, written in his own hand, and the knowledge that he had wanted to write it.

He let that stand. A man who keeps an honest instrument must sometimes write down a reading he cannot yet account for, rather than leave it out until he can. He wrote nothing about it anywhere.

The files came up the stair in the afternoon in two boxes. He had them set on the chair beside the table, where he could see them while he worked.

# Chapter 25 — A Bracket of Ceilings

Then he packed.

He packed as he did everything, from a list, ticking as he went. There were shirts and collars and the good coat, and the recording case with its glass and its rolls and the spare spindle. There were the three manuals a seat officer carried to a continental sitting: the observation manual, the rotation manual, and the thin grey one on courtesy toward foreign officials, which nobody had ever been known to open. He laid the three manuals in the bottom of the kit, flat, one on another.

Then he went to the drawer.

The notebook had a cardboard cover, soft at the corners, and ninety pages, and four of them had been written on. Between postings it lived in the locked drawer of his desk, under the spare blotters. On postings it lived in the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, where his hand could find it without anybody seeing his hand move. Those were the only two places it had lived since a cold morning at Ardenmere, and it had never once lived anywhere else.

He took it out of the drawer, and stood holding it, and looked at the kit.

Then he opened the kit and put the notebook in between the second manual and the third, where it lay flat and grey among the grey manuals and looked like nothing at all. It looked like a man's working notes on the rotation rules, and anybody turning over the kit would have passed it without a second glance.

He closed the kit, and buckled it, and set it by the door.

It was only when he had turned down the lamp and was standing at the window in the dark that he noticed what he had done.

He had not put it in his pocket. He had always put it in his pocket. A notebook in a pocket goes where the man goes, and stays with him, and comes out only when he chooses. A notebook in a kit is luggage, carried to a place and unpacked there and laid out on a table among the papers of the work. He had packed it like part of the work.

He stood at the dark window for some time with that.

He had been a monitoring officer for most of his working life, and the first thing a monitoring officer learns is to watch for the moment when a pattern changes. A man who has crossed a certain bridge every morning for ten years and one morning takes the ferry has not done anything unlawful; he has only done something new, and the new thing is worth writing down in a ruled book. Havel had written down a great many such things about a great many men.

He did not write this one down. There was no fifth entry. The rule of the notebook was that it held what had happened and nothing of what he made of it. He had broken that rule once, on the bluff, and did not mean to make a habit of it.

But he noticed, standing there, that he had changed his pattern. He noticed it as precisely as he would have noticed it in anybody else, and then he went to bed without unpacking the kit to put it right.

Rooke reorganized the house for Norhold on the first morning of the cold term, and he did it on a single sheet.

He pinned the sheet to the board in the long upper room over the Blade hall, where the squad had met at the beginning of the year. It was cold up there now, and the tall windows down one side were white with frost to the height of a man's head, and the lamps had been lit at the seventh bell because the day had not properly come. The five of them stood in front of the board in their coats with the two reserves behind them, and read it.

At the top, in Rooke's square capitals, it said: *A BRACKET OF CEILINGS.*

Under that were five lines, one for each of them.

*LIRA. Zerin, from the times. Build her on the oak until you can fight what you've built.*

*BROM. Marek, from the notations. Find your joins before he does.*

*KARIS. Norhold. All of it. From its provinces.*

*EPHRAM. The round of sixteen. And: trial caller.*

*CAEL. Shield. The Shield reserve, every second day. Learn his clock until you could keep it for him.*

"Questions," said Rooke, from behind them. It was not a question.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Ephram, who had read the fourth line three times, turned round.

"Trial caller," he said.

"Trial caller," said Rooke. "The team trial at Norhold is squads of five, on a scenario floor, against objectives. Somebody has to call it. It's you."

"Why me?"

Rooke looked at him. Behind him the frost on the windows caught the lamplight and glittered.

"Because you write everything down," said Rooke. "Because you know where everybody on this floor is weakest, and you've never once used it against them outside a ring. Because you lost the final at the confluence by a quarter of a beat and came off the floor and carried trestles." He turned back to the board. "And because one day somebody is going to make you captain of something, and I'd like you to have practised first."

Ephram stood very still. His ears went slowly red, all the way round.

"Yes," he said at last. "All right. Yes."

"Good. You'll learn the trial rules when Norhold prints them. They don't send them before the orientation; they never have. Until then you'll learn the floor." Rooke tapped the fifth line. "The squad is five. Four of you are certain. The fifth place is his, until a clerk at Norhold tells me otherwise. The Shield reserve learns the fifth place too, every drill, in case a clerk does." He looked at the Shield reserve, who had gone pale. "You'll travel. You'll want to be ready."

"Yes, sir."

Karis had been reading the third line all this time with her lips pressed together. Now she looked up.

"All of it," she said.

"All of it. Fourteen delegations, near enough. I don't want their fighters. I'll have their fighters from the index, the same as everybody else. I want to know where they come from." Rooke folded his arms. "Every house fights the way its country taught it. Coast houses fight on wet floors. Hill houses fight on stone. Rhagen fights by doctrine and Auremont by program, and the river houses fight as if the floor might move. I want Norhold built from its provinces, so that when a fighter steps out of a gate, we know what ground he grew up on before he's taken his coat off."

"That's a great deal of reading," said Karis.

"Yes," said Rooke. "You'd be insulted by less."

She did not deny it.

Cael read his own line again. *Learn his clock until you could keep it for him.* He thought of the caravan captain's panes, and the clock, and the change of guard, and felt the small warm satisfaction of a man reading the description of a lesson he has already learned.

"Why Shield?" he said.

"Because whoever volunteers for you at Norhold won't be a quarry foreman," said Rooke. "He'll be sent. He'll come from a house that wants to survey you, and survey is what doctrine houses do, and doctrine houses put Shields at the front of everything." He turned to go. "You had a good bout with a Bronze at the confluence. I watched it. Find out if it was the Bronze or you."

He went down the stair, and the squad stood in front of the board for some time afterward in their coats, each reading his own line and then, when he thought nobody was looking, everybody else's.

"A bracket of ceilings," said Brom at last, slowly. "He's been saving that since the slate country."

"He said it at the coaches' supper," said Lira.

"He said it," said Brom. "Now he's built it."

Karis was still looking at the third line. "He's never given me a whole city before," she said, to nobody in particular. She did not sound displeased.

Cael looked at the five lines a while longer. A season ago the board in this room had carried meets and dates and floors, and the season's whole question had been a line on a circular. Now there was no line anywhere on the sheet. There were five people's names, and against each of them the hardest thing that person would meet at Norhold, written down plainly by a man who had thought about each of them separately for a long time. He understood, standing there in his coat, that Rooke had stopped coaching a house toward a threshold. He was coaching five fighters toward five ceilings, one at a time, and he had been planning it since the slate country.

Lira built her Zerin on the sprung oak in the second week, with chalk and a counting-string and the extract from the quarry town flattened under a stone at the edge of the floor.

She did it the only way it could be done, which was from the numbers. Nobody at Halcenvane had ever seen Zerin fight, and there was no likeness of her in Ostrand and no account of her bouts in any meet record the house could buy, because Auremont fought its own region's meets far away to the east and sent nothing west but scouts. So there were only the figures in the extract's block. Burst intervals and closing speed, the length of a long sequenced run, a time for a turn. Beside the closing speed stood the small mark and the examiner's initials that meant somebody had refused to believe it and measured it again.

Lira had had them by heart since the quay steps.

The reserves were pressed into service as Zerin's furniture. The Shield reserve held one end of the counting-string and the Stone reserve the other, and they stood at the chalk marks like a pair of gateposts while Lira ran between them. Brom kept the count on the bench with a spoon against his mug, because he said it was the only instrument in the house he trusted, and Gault, passing through, stopped to watch and went away again looking pained.

"Mark me a burst," she said to Cael, on the first morning. "Her distance. From her closing speed and her interval."

He worked it out with a pencil on the back of the extract and chalked two marks on the oak. They were a good deal further apart than Lira's own.

She looked at them for a while, and then went to the first mark and burst to the second at her full rate, and came down out of the landing beat a stride short.

"Again," she said.

She did it again, and came down a stride short again. The third time she came down half a stride short and stood on the oak breathing hard, looking at the second mark as though it had said something rude to her.

"She does that," said Lira, "and then does it again before I've got my breath. That's the interval." She pointed at the figure on the extract with her toe. "Look at it. Look how short."

They worked at it all morning, and every morning of that week. Lira ran Zerin's distances and could not quite reach them. Then she ran Zerin's intervals at her own distances and could reach them, just, three times running and not a fourth. Cael stood off with the read and watched her weight, and called the moment of each burst before it came. He was looking for the thing every Wind fighter has: the moment before a burst when the body draws itself together, setting the hips and loading the foot, like a cat gathering itself on the top of a wall before it jumps. On the oak, in the old days, he had called Lira's gathers a fraction early every time, and it was how the read had first learned her.

In the second week they tried to build Zerin's gathers.

They could not, though they sat on the edge of the oak with the extract between them and the chalk marks all round and did the arithmetic three times, and it came out the same each time. Between the end of one of Zerin's bursts and the start of the next there was not enough time for a gather, or for any gather either of them had ever seen. Lira's own was the quickest Cael knew, and it would not fit into Zerin's interval with anything to spare.

"So she doesn't gather," said Lira.

"Everybody gathers."

"Then she gathers somewhere we can't see it." Lira pulled her knees up and put her chin on them. "While she's still landing, maybe. In the landing beat. Or before the first one, and then she never has to again." She glared at the figures. "Or the examiner can't count."

"He measured it twice. He signed it."

"I know he did." She was quiet for a while. "I can't build what I can't find. I can build her distances, and I can almost build her intervals. I can't build the bit in the middle, and the bit in the middle is the whole of her."

Cael looked at the chalk marks on the oak, the second always a stride too far from the first.

"Then don't build it," he said. "Build everything round it. When you meet her, you'll find the middle in the first exchange or you won't. Either way you'll know the rest already."

Lira looked at him sideways.

"That's what you do," she said. "With everybody. You build everything you can see, and leave a hole the shape of the thing you can't, and go and find out."

"Yes."

"I hate it." She got up and went back to the first mark. "Again."

It was in that week, too, that she began running her own intervals shorter, without saying she was doing it, and Cael noticed it on the read in the timing of her landings, which came a little sooner after each other every morning, a fraction at a time, like a pound added to the bar each week and never mentioned. By the end of the block she was not much nearer Zerin's figures, but she was nearer them than she had been at the quay steps, and Cael wrote the difference down in the Log without telling her he had.

Brom pinned Marek's notations to the wall above his bed on the first night of the block, where he would see them first thing every morning and last thing every night.

He did it with four tacks and great care, squaring the pages to the joint of the plaster, and then sat on the bed and looked at them. Cael, coming in to borrow a strap, found him there and did not go out again.

"You don't read things alone," said Cael.

"I'm not reading them." Brom did not take his eyes off the pages. "I've read them. I read them at the quarry town with you at my shoulder, and again on the road, and again at the confluence. I know what they say." He nodded at the wall. "I'm letting them look at me."

The notations were as short and dry as they had been in the slate country, and every first exchange in them was a verdict. Every touch was marked against a join, a place where one thing turned into another: the change of a guard from one hand to the other, the turn of a reset, the line of a shoulder as it set. Beside every touch stood the same small mark, *the same join, found and then worked*.

"He'll find every join I've got," said Brom. "That's what he does. So I've been asking myself where my joins are." He pointed, without looking, at his own left side. "That's the one everybody will tell him about. Page three. The old seam. It's mended, and everybody who's bought my file knows it's mended, and they'll tell him anyway, because it's the only thing on page three worth the price."

"It's mended."

"It's mended for a man who comes at it straight. Rooke's nineteen exercises mend it for that." Brom turned his left hand over and looked at the palm. "Marek doesn't come at things straight. He comes at the place where they change. And my left side changes. When I turn to my left the guard goes from the right hand to the left, and there's a moment, a door, where it's in neither." He closed the hand. "Nobody's ever gone through that door. Nobody's ever been quick enough. He will be."

"So close it."

"You can't close a door you have to use. I turn left. Everybody turns left." Brom stood up. "But you can make it the strongest door in the house. So strong that when he finds it, he finds it's the last place he wants to be."

He worked it for the whole of the block.

He worked it in the Iron Skin hall in the mornings with the Stone reserve, turning left a hundred times, two hundred, while the reserve came at the door with an open hand. He worked it on the oak in the afternoons with Rooke, who came at it with a stick. He worked it with Cael in the evenings, because Cael's read could see the door open a fraction before it opened, and could tell him whether it was open a hair less than yesterday. It was. And then a hair less again.

In the fourth week the Stone reserve stopped getting through it at all, and in the sixth Rooke's stick stopped getting through it. In the eighth Cael watched Brom turn left with the read at its sharpest, and the door was hardly there at all: there was only a heavy man changing hands so smoothly that the change happened inside his body, under his skin, where no hand could reach it.

"That's the strongest door in the house," said Rooke, on the evening he saw it. He said it to the board rather than to Brom, and wrote one word on Brom's line, which was *Sound*.

Brom read the word later, when Rooke had gone, and came back to the residence looking as Cael had seen him look perhaps four times in his life. It was the face of a man who has been priced right, by somebody whose pricing he trusts, and finds that he agrees with the figure.

The notations stayed on the wall above his bed until the night before they left. Every night, before he blew out the lamp, he looked at them for a while, and they looked back.

Ephram learned to call the trial on a floor made of benches.

Rooke built it for him in the long upper room at the end of the second week. There were three benches for platforms, one of them with a cushion on it to stand for higher ground, and a line of chairs down the middle to stand for a barrier, with two gaps. A flag on a stick stood at each end for the objectives, and chalk lines marked everything else. It looked like the aftermath of a very poor dinner party. Rooke said that every scenario floor in the world looked like that until it was built, and that most of them looked like it afterward.

The squad was five: Lira, Brom, Karis, Ephram, and Cael in the fifth place, with the Shield reserve standing at the side to step into it when Rooke said. Every third drill Rooke said it, and Cael stood at the rail instead and watched his own place being filled by somebody else. It was a strange thing to watch. The four of them moved round the Shield reserve as they would have moved round him, a little more carefully, and the floor went on working without him in it. He had never once been on the outside of the four before. He found that he did not mind it, and that it taught him more about them in a morning than a month inside. The Stone reserve and two Blade third-years borrowed from the cohort made the other side, with Rooke behind them, calling theirs.

Ephram stood at the back of his own squad with his file book in his hand and nothing in his face.

"Call it," said Rooke.

Ephram opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it again.

"Lira—" he said, and stopped, because Lira had already gone, fast down the side of the chairs, the way she always went. She was through the first gap and onto the cushioned bench before he had got her name out. Brom had gone after her because he always went after her, and Karis had stopped to look at the other side's arrangement, and Cael had gone where nobody else was, because that was what he did on any floor. The squad had become four people doing four sensible things in four directions. Rooke's three Blade and Stone fighters walked through the gap behind them, and took the flag, and stood holding it, looking embarrassed.

"Again," said Rooke.

It went like that for three days.

It was not that Ephram did not know what to call. He knew. He had always known; he had the whole floor in his head the moment he looked at it, every gap and every bench, and where each of them was weakest. What he did not know was how to make four people who had fought alone all their lives hear him. He called too late, and he called too much. He called Lira's name when Lira had already decided, and Brom's when Brom had already moved, and to them it sounded like somebody behind you reading out the instructions after you have built the chair.

On the fourth day he stopped calling names.

He stood at the back with the file book shut and watched the first three breaths of the drill without saying anything at all, and then said one word, quite quietly.

"North."

Lira went north, and not because she had been told: she had been going to go somewhere, and the word arrived just before she chose, and north was a perfectly good place to go. Brom went after her. Karis, hearing *north*, looked south, and saw what Ephram had seen: the other side had left its flag with one man on it. Cael, who had been about to go wherever nobody was, found that where nobody was had just been named.

"Gap," said Ephram. "Second."

They took the flag in eleven breaths. Rooke's side never got to the barrier.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Lira, standing on the cushioned bench with the flag in her hand, turned round and looked at Ephram for a long while.

"One word," she said.

"You don't need more," said Ephram. His ears were red, but his voice was perfectly steady. "You know what to do. You all do. You only need to know which way the rest of us are going before you go." He opened the file book and wrote something in it. "I was telling you how to fight. You don't need that. You need to be told where the floor is."

Rooke, behind the other side, wrote something on his own sheet.

"Again," he said. But he said it differently.

By the end of the block Ephram could call the floor in a dozen words a drill, and the squad moved to them without hearing them. He could tell, too, from the back, a breath before anybody else, when a drill was going wrong. Cael watched him do it on the last morning, standing at the back of the squad with the file book closed under his arm, looking at the floor much as Rooke looked at it, already several minutes ahead of everybody on it and waiting, with some patience, for them to catch up.

He was going to be a captain. Everybody in the room could see it now, except possibly Ephram.

Cael's own line on the board meant the Shield reserve, every second day, on the sprung oak, for the whole of the block.

The Shield reserve was a third-year from a farming family in the river country, broad and fair and slow to speak, with a habit of apologizing to the floor when he slipped on it. He had been told in the first week of the year that he would travel with the squad and fight for nobody, and he had taken it without complaint. Now he had been told that he would spend ten weeks letting the provision boy take his coverage apart. He took that without complaint too, and asked only whether he might keep a note of what the provision boy did, so that he could learn something from it himself.

"Keep it," said Rooke. "Show it to me."

On the first morning Cael found his clock in four renewals.

It was an honest clock and a slow one, the clock of a young man who had been taught to count and had never been asked to stop. Breath, plant, pane; then the same again on the next pane, at a steady five beats, round and round. He renewed the oldest first, as the caravan captain had. Every Shield on the circuit seemed to have been taught that, and Cael supposed it was simply true, as it was true that you led with your strong side.

By the end of the first morning Cael could stand off and say the reserve's next breath aloud, a beat before he drew it.

"Now," he said, on the second morning, and lit the first contact into the pane that was coming up.

The pane came up cracked, as the captain's had. The reserve felt it go wrong under his hands and drew breath again to set it, and lost the beat, and the next pane along stood a beat too long and went thin. Cael walked into the thin place and put his palm on the reserve's shoulder.

The reserve looked at the shoulder, and then at Cael, and then at his own hands.

"That's not fair," he said, after some thought. "I didn't do anything wrong."

"You didn't," said Cael. "That's why it works."

He kept to two contacts a round, every round, though there was no crowd on the oak and no panel and nobody to count them but the Shield reserve. It was the circle's rule for public floors and had nothing to say about private ones. But a habit kept only where people were watching was not a habit. It was a performance. He had spent a year learning the difference, and he did not mean to forget it in a warm room at home.

In the third week Rooke took the reserve aside and spoke to him for some minutes at the far end of the oak. When the reserve came back, his clock had changed.

He no longer renewed on five beats. He renewed on four, then six, then five, then four again, in no order that Cael could find on the first morning. It was a clock with its numbers shuffled. For two sessions the reserve held his coverage against Cael for whole rounds, and went off the floor afterward looking quietly astonished at himself, and wrote a great deal in his note.

On the third session Cael found what had not changed.

The numbers had changed. The plant had not. Whatever the count, the reserve still had to plant his back foot before a pane came up, because a pane was a declaration and a declaration needed the floor under it. The read could see a planting foot through any shuffle of numbers. Cael stopped listening for the beat and started watching the foot. Every time it went down he knew a pane was coming up a breath after, and which pane, because the reserve still renewed the oldest first. Rooke had shuffled his count. He had not shuffled his order, and perhaps no Shield could.

Cael lit the first contact on the next plant, and the cracked pane cost its beat, and the beat came out of the next pane's life, the same as ever.

"He's found it again," said the reserve, to Rooke, from the floor. He did not sound upset. He sounded as if he had been shown a new road home.

"He has," said Rooke. "Write down how."

By the sixth week the reserve could not keep a pane on Cael for a whole round, whatever he did with his numbers. By the eighth he had stopped trying to hide the plant and had started trying to make it smaller. That was a better idea, and it made the work harder, and Cael enjoyed it more than anything he had done on the oak all term.

He spent his bursts on it, too, when the work needed them. The oak gave its six a day, as it always had, and on the Shield reserve's mornings he seldom used more than three. The rest of the day's price he kept for the speed line with Lira in the afternoons.

He wrote it in the Log at the end of the eighth week, and found that he was smiling while he wrote it.

*The Shield reserve, eight weeks. He started with one clock and Rooke gave him a shuffled one, and both of them come down to the same thing: before a pane there's a plant, and the oldest goes first. Shuffle the numbers and the plant still shows; change the numbers and the order still holds. Watch the foot, find the change of guard, tax the pane that's coming up. Two contacts a round, and the beat it costs runs down the whole clock.*

*I think Shield is solved. Not easy, but solved, like a lock once you've found where the pins are. I'd like to meet a Shield at Norhold now. I'd like to see whether the doctrine houses keep their clocks any better than the salt road does.*

*Rooke watched the last session from the rail and said "Good" once, and then "One reserve", and went away. I think he meant it as praise. I've written it down in case he didn't.*

# Chapter 26 — The Melt

Karis built Norhold from its provinces in the top-floor reading room, at the long table under the window, with the door shut.

The card was still in its brass holder on the door, in Bracken's small upright hand: *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* Nobody had ever taken it down.

Inside, the room had changed a good deal since the recess. Karis had pinned a map of the whole continent to the long wall, a cheap printed one from a stationer's in Ostrand, and over the weeks it had disappeared under paper.

There were slips pinned to it in every quarter, each with a house's name and a few lines in her square hand, and threads of coloured wool running from the slips to places on the map.

Cael came up on a Third-day afternoon in the fifth week with two mugs of tea, because she had not been down to the common room since breakfast. He found her standing in front of the map with a pin in her mouth.

"Fourteen," she said round the pin, without turning. "Fourteen delegations, and I have thirteen. The fourteenth is a lake house that qualified at the year's turn on a recount, and nobody's printed anything about it yet. I've written to their registrar. Bracken says he'll answer, because registrars always answer registrars' friends." She took the pin out and stuck it in a slip. "Look."

He looked.

She had not pinned up fighters. She had pinned up places. Every slip was a house, and every house was pinned where it came from, and under each house's name she had written the same few things.

There was the floor it trained on, oak or stone or plank or something stranger. There was the weather of its country, and what its people did for a living, and which way its rivers ran. There was what a fighter from there would think was normal before he had ever been told what normal was.

"The coast houses," she said, and touched three slips along the western edge. "Every one of them trains on wet boards, because everything on that coast is wet. They're all a fraction long in the stride, every fighter in every house, because a long stride on a wet floor is how you don't fall over. They don't know they do it. Nobody's ever told them, because everybody they've ever fought does it too."

She moved her hand inland, to a knot of slips in the hills.

"Stone. All of them. They warm up for an hour because the floors are cold, and they never stand still between exchanges, and they've never in their lives been told to walk a circle. They just do." She looked at him sideways. "You'd have saved a shoulder if you'd been born up there."

"I'd have been a different fighter."

"Everybody is. That's the whole point." She moved east, to a single slip with three threads running out of it, and her voice changed a little. "Auremont. A program, not a place. They take children at twelve and give them a floor that's the same in every room and a schedule that's the same every year. Their fighters don't have a country. They have a curriculum." She touched the slip once and took her hand away. "That's what makes them hard to read. You can't read the ground a fighter grew up on if somebody built the ground."

Beside Auremont there was another slip, with one thread.

"Rhagen," said Karis. "Doctrine. Everything they do is written down first and taught second. Marek's from there. So is whatever they send to look at you." She considered the slip. "Rooke's right about one thing. If a doctrine house files on you, it'll be to find out what you are, not to beat you. They won't care about the touches. They'll care about the survey."

He drank his tea and looked at the map for a long time.

He thought it was the best piece of work he had seen anybody in the house do all year, and he told her so. Karis said, "It's a draft," in the voice she used when she was pleased and did not intend anybody to know.

She needed a host rule about delegation quarters. She went to the shelf for the certified transcription of the tournament charter and took it down and opened it at the back, where the schedules were, and turned pages until she found the rule.

Cael watched her do it.

He watched her hand go past the schedules into the oldest part of the book, the founding articles in their old type. It stopped there for a moment, flat on a page.

A narrow slip of paper stood up out of the binding at that place, one of her own, with a line of her writing on it that he had seen her write by the common-room fire at the beginning of the year and had never read.

She did not open it. She did not take the slip out. Her hand lay on the page beside it for perhaps the space of a breath.

Then she turned the pages back to the schedules, found the rule she wanted, and copied it into her notebook in her square hand.

"After the season," she said, to the book rather than to him.

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't. I said it to me." She shut the transcription and put it back on the shelf with its spine square to the edge. "I keep deciding it, every time I go past. It's very tiring. The season first. Then the other thing." She came back to the map and picked up another pin. "It isn't going anywhere. It's been in that book longer than the house has been on this bluff."

Below the window, at the foot of the bluff road, two coats stood in the snow in the lee of the wall, and two more stood at the ferry landing on the far side of the river, small and dark against the white, where Cael could see them from here as he could see them from every high window on the hill.

It was a little before the sixth bell, and at the sixth bell they would change.

Karis did not look at them. She took the marbled log out of her satchel, opened it at the day's page, and when the bell went she wrote the time, and the number, which was four, and the line, which was the same as the line above it.

She had written that line every day since the recess. She had never once had to change it.

"All this snow," she said, closing the log. "And not one man added. Whoever pays them must pay them by the year."

---

Hesk's letter came up on the evening coach in the sixth week, with snow on the satchel.

It was not a long letter. Hesk's letters in the cold months were never long, because his workshop was cold and his hands were stiff and he said so. This one was written in the same hand it always was, a little larger every year, bearing down hard on the down-strokes.

*Your circular came down the coast road on the same coach as a new belt for my lathe, and I am not sure which of them I was gladder to see. The belt runs true. So, I gather, do you.*

*It is very cold here. I have had three barometers brought in this month by men who swear they are broken, and none of them is. A glass read in a cold room reads short. Warm it, and wait, and it comes right. I tell every man this. Most of them look at me as though I had picked their pockets.*

*You will be going to a big city. In a big city every clock disagrees with every other clock, and every one of them is certain. Do not set yours by the loudest bell. Pick one clock you have reason to trust, and keep to it, and let the rest argue among themselves. The travelling clock I gave you will be no help at all. It never has been. Set it an hour fast as you always do and you will be right by accident, which is how that clock has always been right.*

*Tell the Wind girl the workshop is still open. Tell the big one I have heard about his line in the Copper and that a flat line is the hardest thing in my trade to make. — H.*

He read it twice at the common-room table. Then he took it across to the fire, where Lira was sitting with her boots up and Brom was mending a strap, and read them the last two lines aloud.

Lira said nothing for a moment. Then she said, "Tell him I know," and went back to her book. She did not turn a page for some time.

Brom put down the strap and looked at the fire.

"The hardest thing in his trade," he said slowly. He seemed to turn it over, as he turned over every true thing anybody told him, to look at the underside. "Then I'll keep it flat."

The letter went behind the observation notebook's front board, with the others.

He thought about the clocks for a while afterward, sitting by the fire with the notebook on his knee. Hesk had never once in his life given advice that was only about the thing it seemed to be about. In a big city every clock is certain, and you keep to one you have reason to trust. Cael knew which one he had. It had four hands, Lira's and Brom's and Karis's and the Log's, and between them they had kept better time this year than any bell on the continent.

He wrote the Log that night, the long inventory he wrote once a block, at the window of his room. Below the window the ferry landing lay white and empty except for its two coats.

*Inventory, the sixth week of the cold term.*

*Wind: six free a day on the sprung oak, as ever; the landing beat no shorter. Three spent on most Shield mornings, the rest on the speed line with Lira. The hip after, as ever.*

*Iron read: in use all day, on everything. Zerin's numbers have taught it nothing, because there's nothing in them to read but numbers.*

*Pressure: flat. The right shoulder hasn't been asked for anything since the recess, and says so with some satisfaction.*

*Compression: shown nowhere. Not at the confluence, not on the oak. The barge-master's floor wanted it once, for the fall, and I gave it the forearm instead. The forearm cleared in a week.*

*Ember: two contacts, the circle's rate, on the caravan captain at the confluence, in front of a hundred and forty people. It was on paper already, at the back of the Greyvane transcript, and it's on a meet record now as well. Two a round on the Shield reserve since, on a floor with no crowd, because a habit kept only in public is a costume.*

*Shadow: rent from waking, every day, spent or not. One drift this week, in a gap, on the stair. None in the work. Deployment: none. Still nowhere.*

*Six. The record holds two of them public, and has been shown a third at its documented rate. Nothing on any floor this year has been news to any paper. The doctrine holds.*

---

Seln wrote the block's report at the copying table behind the wing's counter, on the last Fifth-day of the term, after the counter had closed.

The wing was quiet. The day's forms lay squared in their trays, and the term's last coursework folders were stacked for return with their slips torn off.

Outside the high window the light was going blue over the snow, and the ferry landing on the far side of the river showed two dark marks against the white, as it had shown every evening for ten weeks.

He wrote the report in the office's standard form, as he wrote every report, slowly, and read each line back as he went.

*Subject in residence for the term. Training per house schedule, in preparation for the continental finals. Capabilities exercised in training consistent with those on record. No undisclosed capability observed. Travel per the attached roster. Schedule continues. Routine.*

He read the first line again. The subject had been in residence for the term. The wing's own counter sheet said so, and the subject had brought his coursework to the counter every Fifth-day and said *Thank you for the form*, and Seln had said *Mm*, and taken it.

He read the second. The subject had trained per the house's schedule. The schedule was pinned to a board in the long upper room in the coach's square capitals, and Seln had read it on the way to somewhere else, as he read everything on the way to somewhere else.

He read the third. The capabilities exercised were consistent with those on record. The subject had spent two ignitions a round against the Shield reserve on the oak, every second day.

The record held the ignition: a thin exhibit at the back of a public transcript, a reading on the wing's own vessel, and now a meet record from the confluence. Consistent.

He had watched one of those sessions from the rail of the oak, on his way to somewhere else, and counted. Two a round. Every round. With nobody there to count but a third-year reserve and a clerk on his way to somewhere else.

He did not ask himself why the boy kept to two where nobody was looking. He noted that he had noticed it, and set the noticing aside, in the place where he kept things that were not the office's business.

He read the fourth line. *No undisclosed capability observed.* That was true. It was true in the way his reports had been true all year: every word of it would bear any weight anybody cared to put on it, and none of it carried more than its own.

He had observed nothing undisclosed. He had arranged, for most of a year, to be on his way to somewhere else whenever there might have been anything to observe, and that was not a thing a report was required to state.

He read the fifth. *Travel per the attached roster.*

He had written the roster himself that afternoon, in fair copy, for posting on the board in the covered walk and for the regional office and for Norhold. Eleven names. Five competitors, the coach and the evaluator of record, two reserves, a wagoner.

On the last line, in the same hand as the rest, *S. Seln, assessment office: records and floor scheduling.*

He had looked at that line for some time before he blotted it. It was the second roster this year with his name on its last line. Nobody had asked him to put it there either time. He had simply been the person who wrote the roster, and the roster had to be complete.

He signed the report, sealed it with the office's plain seal, and put it in the pouch for the north. The pouch would go down the bluff on the morning coach, past the two coats at the foot of the road. They would not look at it. Nobody ever looked at the pouch; that was what a pouch was for.

Then he took up the fair copy of the roster and went down the wing's stair to the covered walk with it, and pinned it to the board under the crooked frame, square to the edge, with four tacks.

There was nobody in the walk to see him do it. It was nearly dark, and snowing again, a little. He stood looking at the board for a moment with his hands in his coat. Then he went back up to the wing to put out the lamps.

True. Complete in form. And, this once, not quite empty: there was a roster attached.

---

The roster was on the board in the morning, and by the first bell the whole house had read it. By the second bell the provisional seedings had gone up beside it.

They came from the continental index, the commercial one the scouts and the coaches bought, printed in the capital and sent out by coach to every qualified house.

Rooke had paid for an early copy, as he had paid for the extracts in the slate country. He pinned the Iron page next to the roster without a word and went away to the oak.

It was not the draw. The draw would not be made until the delegations were in Norhold, at the orientation, by lamplight, in front of everybody. But it was the seeding the draw would be made from, and every house on the hill knew by now how the circuit made a draw from a seeding.

Lira read it at the board with her hands in her pockets. She read it once quickly, and then took a pencil out of her coat and did the draw herself, on the back of her hand, foot against head, as it would be done. Then she did it again, to be sure.

Cael, standing behind her, did it in his head. He came out where she did.

If the seeds held through two rounds, Lira's semifinal line was Zerin's.

She did not say anything at the board, or at breakfast, or on the speed line in the afternoon, where she ran her intervals a fraction shorter than she had the day before and did not mention it.

At supper she ate what Brom put in front of her. Afterward she went up early, before the fire had properly settled, and said good night to nobody in particular.

The lamp under her door was still burning when Cael went up at the tenth bell. It was still burning when he woke a little after the second hour of the night with the cold on his face and did not know what had woken him.

He got up and put on his coat and went out.

She was on the wall above the river, where she always went, sitting on the coping with her coat round her and her knees up, looking north.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared and was full of stars, and the river below was loud and dark and fast, with ice going down it in plates that knocked together in the current with a sound like somebody far off moving furniture.

He sat down on the coping beside her, not too close. She did not look round.

"I've built her for ten weeks," said Lira, after a long while. "Her distances and her intervals and her turn. Everything on the page. I can do all of it now, nearly. Not as fast. Nearly." She was quiet. "And I still can't find the middle. Ten weeks, and there's a hole in the middle of her the shape of the one thing I need."

"You'll find it in the first exchange."

"Or I won't."

"Or you won't," he agreed. "And then you'll know."

She laughed, a little, under her breath.

"You said that at the beginning of the block," she said. "I hated it then too." She pulled the coat closer. "I'm not frightened of her. I want you to know that. I've been frightened of a great many people in my life, and some of them were behind desks, and none of them is her." She watched the ice go down the river. "I'm frightened of finding the middle and finding it's something I can't do. That I've been right all year that I'm good, and she's simply better, and there was never anything I could have done about it except be born at twelve in a room with the right floor."

He did not say anything. There was nothing true he could have said that was also comforting, and she would have known the difference.

They sat for a long time. The stars went round a little. Somewhere down in Ostrand a dog barked, and stopped.

"Put down that I'm not glad tonight," said Lira at last. "In the Log. I said I was glad, on the quay steps. Put down that tonight I'm not." She stood up and brushed the snow off the back of her coat. "I'll be glad again in the morning. I usually am." She looked down at him. "Go to bed. You've a Shield at the second bell, and he's started making his foot smaller."

She went in. He stayed on the wall a little longer.

He put it down when he went back up, by the light of a candle end, in four words. *Not glad tonight. Morning.*

---

The melt came the next night, at the very end of the tenth week, all at once, in the dark.

Cael woke to the sound of water. It was running everywhere, off the roofs and down the gutters and along the covered walk, and in the morning the snow on the bluff had gone grey and soft and was sliding off the tiles in long wet sheets. The river under the wall had risen a man's height overnight and ran brown and heavy and very fast, carrying whole trees down past Ostrand toward the confluence, and the bluff road was a stream for a day, and then deep mud, and then, on the morning of the Second-day, a road again, or near enough.

They left on the Second-day at first light.

There were two wagons, as there had been all year, loaded the night before under Rooke's eye, with the reserves riding on the second and Gault's case wedged in among the kit where he could reach its handle.

Seln had a corner of the first wagon's tailboard with the travel file on his knees.

Withrow came down to the gate in her dark coat to see them off. She and Bracken would follow in a week with the hired carriage, by the faster road.

She did not make a speech. She shook Rooke's hand, and then, to everybody's surprise, each of theirs. She went down the line of the five of them in order, and then the reserves, and then Gault, and then, after the smallest pause, Seln.

"I'll see you in the city," she said, to all of them.

The wagons went out through the gate and down the bluff road in the thin early light, with the mud sucking at the wheels and the whole valley below them steaming in the first sun.

At the foot of the road the two coats stood in the lee of the wall, as they had stood every day of the year.

Cael watched them as the wagons went past. They did not move or turn their heads, but stood where they had always stood, on the bell, and watched the wagons go by as they would have watched a cart of turnips go by on any market morning of the year.

At the bridge into Ostrand there were people.

Nobody had arranged that either. It was market day, and the town was out, and somebody had seen the wagons coming down the bluff. By the time they reached the bridge there were perhaps two hundred people standing along it in the mud, with more coming down the lanes.

There were the baker's boys and the ferry crew and the women from the fish steps, and the man who sold hot chestnuts at the bottom of the hill. There were children on their fathers' shoulders. A great many of them had never set foot on the bluff in their lives.

They did not cheer, exactly. They stood along the parapet and called out names as the wagons went by, all five, and some of them the reserves' names too, which the reserves had not known anybody in Ostrand knew.

The Shield reserve went pink to the ears and waved with both hands.

Somebody threw a bunch of early snowdrops into the first wagon. Brom caught it, and looked at it in his big hand, and did not know what to do with it, and in the end put it in his buttonhole, where it stayed for three days.

On the far side of the bridge the road ran north between the flooded fields.

Cael sat on the tailboard of the second wagon with Lira beside him and looked back. The bridge was still lined with people, getting smaller.

Above it the bluff stood up dark against the morning, with the house on top of it, and the wall where Lira had sat a few nights before, looking north, in the starlight.

At the ferry landing, the other two coats were still standing on the bell.

"They're not coming," said Karis, behind him. She had the marbled log open on her knee, and she had written the time, and the number, and then, under it, a line she had never written before.

"No," said Cael.

"They don't need to." She closed the log. "Norhold prints a program. We'll be in it every day, with the hour and the hall. Anybody who wants to know where we are can buy it for a copper." She put the log away in her satchel. "We don't need watching any more. We've been published."

The watchers grew smaller behind them, two and two, standing in the melt on the bell, and then the road bent and the bluff went behind a line of willows, and they were gone.

They lay that night at the first waystation on the north road, a long low inn with a yard full of mud and a fire nobody could get near.

The soup was very good, which Brom said was an omen. Ephram said that was not how omens worked, and Brom said he had never seen Ephram eat a bad bowl of soup and call it an omen either, which was fair.

Cael wrote the Log at the end of the long table with a candle, while the others argued.

*Out. Seventeen days to the city, if the roads hold. Two wagons, eleven on the roster, and the chancellor in a week. Ostrand on the bridge. The watchers on the bell, not following, because we're printed now wherever we go and nobody needs to stand in the snow to find us.*

*In the file on Rooke's knee: a city we've never seen, two sheets we haven't solved, and a Shield I think I have.*

*We'll see.*

Chapter 7 — Adjacent

The ferry took the second wagon last, and Cael rode it over sitting on the tailboard with his legs hanging above the brown water, watching the bank they had left.

He had not decided to sit there. He had climbed in at the yard gate in the half-dark with his kit and found that his body had already gone to the back of the wagon and put its shoulders against the last hoop of the frame, facing the road behind, as it had done on every road for longer than he cared to count.

Lira had looked at him from the front bench and then looked away, and said nothing, which meant she had noticed.

On the landing the two grey coats stood where they always stood. One was writing in a small book against his knee. The other watched the ferryman's rope come in hand over hand, and when the wagon's wheels rolled off the far ramp onto the south road, he lifted his chin a little. A parcel, delivered.

Neither of them moved toward the ferry. Neither of them would. Cael watched them shrink to two grey marks on a grey landing, and then the road bent into the willows and they were gone.

That was the first thing the morning taught him, and it took him the length of the willows to understand it.

Nobody was going to follow them. Nobody needed to.

The meet card had been printed on cream stock and sent to every house in the region, with a block of five names under Halcenvane and the hall, the floor and the bell beside every bracket bout. Anyone who wanted to know where Lira would be at the first bell on the First-day could buy the card for a copper and read it. Where Cael would be was harder to print, because nobody had challenged him yet, but the card said plainly enough that he would be there.

A watcher on a road behind a wagon was a man paying good wages for news the printer gave away.

He sat on the tailboard anyway. Behind them the south road filled slowly with the morning's traffic: a carter with fleeces roped high, two women walking with baskets, and after the first hour another academy's wagon in green paint, keeping a polite distance and clearly bound for the same place. All of it was listed somewhere. All of it was on its way to be counted.

Brom came back along the wagon bed at the second hour, stooping under the hoops, and sat down beside him on the tailboard with a loaf. He broke it, gave half to Cael, and ate his own half looking at the road.

"Nobody there," said Brom.

"Nobody."

"You'll sit here anyway."

"I'll sit here anyway."

Brom nodded, as if that were a reasonable answer to a reasonable question, and the two of them sat with their legs over the road and ate bread while the green wagon came no nearer. After a while Brom said, "It's a good seat. You can see where you've been." Then he went forward again to sleep on the kit bags.

Cael stayed, and found by the middle of the morning that he was no longer watching for anyone. He was only watching the road, which was a different thing, and new.

The delegation had sorted itself between the two wagons without anyone giving an order. Rooke rode up beside the wagoner of the first, with the profiles in a satchel on his knees and his hat tipped down over his eyes, and did not appear to sleep. Seln sat behind him on the box of files.

Gault had the second wagon's front bench, with the case on his lap and both hands resting on its lid, and whenever a wheel found a rut he steadied it without looking down. Karis read. The two reserves played a game with pebbles on a board chalked onto the wagon bed, and argued about its rules, which neither of them seemed to know.

They lay that night at a waystation whose yard was already crowded with other houses' wagons, green and blue and one in a red so new it still smelled of paint, and Cael stood out in the yard after supper and counted them, and found that he was not counting watchers any more but competitors, which was a much pleasanter arithmetic. They went on at first light, and came down into the wool town on the Seventh-day afternoon with the sun behind them.

It was a town that smelled of what it sold. Wool lay in the warehouses along the river in pale stacked bales, and the barges at the quay rode low under more of it, and the air in every street had the warm, greasy, sheepish weight of fleece.

The guild hall stood at the top of the main square, broad and old, with its windows set deep in stone. Behind it, where the ground fell away toward the river, lay the meet ground.

Ephram had told the truth about it. The meet ground had been the town's sheep market for longer than anyone had kept its accounts. When the market moved to the far bank, the guild had roofed three of the old pen rows on timber posts and laid pine boards across the cobbles, and called each of them a floor.

The oldest wall of the market still ran along the east side, rough grey stone with iron rings set in it where the drovers had tied their flocks. The floors were numbered on painted boards.

The main floor was the largest, under the highest roof. The third was the smallest, a roped square in the far corner, hard up against that old wall.

There were two boards in the square in front of the hall. One held the meet card, printed large, with the delegations in their order and the bouts in their columns. The other stood a few feet to its left, and somebody had chalked it in a fast round hand with names and short figures.

Rooke stopped in front of the second board. He read it from top to bottom without expression, and then turned his back on it and never looked at it again.

"What does it say?" Lira asked him.

"It says somebody in this town thinks he knows more than the panel," said Rooke. "Credentials. Line up."

They lined up. Rooke's rule was that the delegation queued with everyone else, at every table, whatever was offered, and nobody had ever offered them anything, so the rule had never been tested.

The credentials table stood under the hall's porch. Behind it sat a clerk of perhaps fifty, with ink on two fingers and a stamp in his right hand.

He worked the queue at one steady pace, the same for every house: name, card, stamp, next. He did Halcenvane's first four in under a minute. Lira, Brom, Karis, Ephram. Stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp.

Then he reached the fifth line, and the stamp stopped in the air.

He looked at the card. He looked at Cael. He looked back at the card and read the line again, his lips moving very slightly over demonstration-exhibition provisions.

"Cael," he said. "That's you."

"Yes."

"The Greyvane boy. The one with the assay." The clerk said it with no particular feeling at all, like a man naming a road he had heard of and never walked. "My niece trains at the river academy, two towns upriver from here, and last year she sent off for the transcript. Paid the copying fee out of her own allowance."

The stamp came down on Cael's credential, thump, at the very pace it had come down on the other four. "Read it to her whole year at supper, she says. They argued about the third day for a week."

Cael found he did not know what to say to that. "I hope the copy was a good one."

"Certified," said the clerk. "She checked." He slid the credential across. "Next house."

Cael stepped out of the queue holding a slip of card with his name on it and a stamp still wet. Behind him the clerk was already reading the next delegation's block at his one pace. For the clerk, nothing had happened.

He wrote it that evening at the inn, at the corner of a table that Brom had somehow secured without appearing to ask for it.

A girl I'll never meet paid her own money for a copy of my hearing and read it aloud at supper. Nobody carried that story into this town by talk. It came in on certified paper, through a copying office, at the public rate. People say rumour runs ahead of a man. Mine doesn't. My record does.

A record can't be steered. That's what makes it a record. I've spent a long time being glad of that. I find I'm still glad of it here, two days from the hill, among people who've read the hearing but never seen the face. I just hadn't thought the record would arrive first and leave me to catch it up.

The validation table stood at the far end of the porch, apart from the credentials, and it had two people behind it in the Compact's grey with their badges pinned at the collar.

Gault had told them it would be there, seated and badged, at every meet of the season. Cael had believed him, and had still not been ready for how ordinary it looked.

It was a folding table with a green cloth and an ink stand. A printed list lay on it, thick as a small book, which was the registry's extract for the region. The two officers had the patient look of people who would be sitting at that table until dark.

He looked at their faces. He had a long list in his head of grey coats and Compact faces, from roads and halls and one room twelve feet by fourteen. Neither of these two was on it. He noted that, and found it a relief, and noted the relief as well.

Seln had the delegation's credential file. It was his work on the roster: records and floor scheduling. He laid the file open on the green cloth in front of the senior officer, a woman with grey hair cut short and a pencil she held like a scalpel, and stepped back half a pace.

Cael stood at his shoulder, because the file was partly about him and Rooke had said he should see it handled.

She checked the first four names against her list. She did it fast, with the pencil's tip moving down the extract's columns and back up to the file, a tick in her margin for each. Lira, a tick. Brom, a tick. Karis, a tick. Ephram, a tick.

Then the pencil went down the extract's columns looking for the fifth name, and went down them again, and stopped.

Cael watched her understand that the list in front of her had nothing to say about the person in front of her. He watched her not look up, and turn a page of the extract that she must have known would not help, and turn it back.

Then she did a thing he had seen only good clerks do. She put the pencil down entirely, laid both hands flat on the table, and thought.

"Enrollment basis," she said at last. She said it to Seln, not to Cael. She held out her hand without looking.

Seln already had it. The certified extract of the enrollment record was in the file's front pocket, with a thin paper tab at the provision's page. He drew it out and put it into her hand, and stepped back again, and his face did nothing at all.

She read the whole extract. It took her four or five minutes, during which the line behind them shuffled and coughed and did not once complain, because everyone in it could see that she was reading every word, and that she would read theirs the same way.

She read the clause, and the provision under it, and the renewal stamp, and the Greyvane citation, and she turned back once to check a date against a date.

Then she took up her pencil and initialled the fifth line of Halcenvane's block in her own register, and beside her initials, in a small cramped hand, she wrote two words. Cael read them upside down: provision, extract.

She handed the extract back to Seln and looked up at Cael for the first time.

"You'll carry that to every table of the season," she said. "Every one. Don't let it leave the file. They won't be quicker about it at the next meet than I was at this one."

"I understand."

"I don't mean it as a slight." She tapped the extract's thick list with the end of her pencil.

"This is what I check people against. Every fighter in this region is in here, somewhere, with a Path and a rank and a line. You're not in it. You're not outside it either, or I'd have a form for you." She considered him, and he watched her choose the last words with care. "You're adjacent to it."

She closed the register.

"Good luck to your house," she said. "Next."

Seln put the extract back in its pocket, tab outward, and closed the file. On the way back along the porch he walked a half-pace ahead with the file under his arm, and did not look round. At the porch steps he went one way, toward the stewards' table and the floor schedules, and Cael went the other.

He put the word in the Log that night, under the clerk's niece, on a line by itself: Adjacent to it.

I've been called every word the registry owns: error, anomaly, case, question, unclassified. Each was somebody's best try at a thing they couldn't see.

Tonight a woman at a folding table in a wool town, who'll never think of me again after the season, found the true one in about four minutes. Beside the list. Not in it, not against it, but next to it, at its own height, held on by a paper tab.

I'd have paid her for that word. It cost her two initials and a minute's thought, and she never knew she'd given me anything.

The stewards' table closed at the sixth bell, and by then there were two challenges on it with Cael's name at the head.

The exhibition steward showed him the sheets himself. He was a thin man with a cold who had evidently been given the demonstration provision that morning, along with a copy of the charter and an instruction to make it work.

He read the format to Cael off a slip in his own hand, slowly, the way a man reads a recipe in a language he does not speak, and twice he stopped to blow his nose and lost his place and had to find it again with his finger.

"Demonstration performances," he said. "Scheduled into the gaps in the card. Opponents by challenge, filed here, voluntary on both sides. Rated by the standing panel of the floor against the Standard, figure only, entered in the meet record. Five exchanges at most, which is the old form; two touches ends it; short of two, the one with more; level, the figure. No standing. No points. No weight in the house total." He lowered the slip. "So you fight whoever asks. And nobody wins anything."

"And people asked," said Cael.

"Two." The steward sniffed. "The ganger filed his while your wagoner was still taking the horses out."

The first sheet was from an Iron Rank Six of the quarry guild, a ganger who worked the stone pits upriver and fought on fair days and guild days and anywhere else that would have him. In the space on the form marked Reason, if any, he had written: Want to see it before the story's finished.

The second was from a Bronze Rank Three of the town itself, who was, the steward said with a cold man's lack of interest, his own cousin. That form gave no reason, and did not need to. Cael had seen that kind of signature before, large and certain and filling the line.

"The ganger's on the third floor tomorrow, after the noon bell," said the steward. "My cousin's on the Second-day morning. The third floor's the one by the wall."

When the halls had emptied, Cael went to look at it.

The pine on the third floor had been laid long ago and walked on every week since. Its boards ran east to west, toward the old wall, and the wall stood close behind the rope on that side, four or five feet away at most, grey and rough, with the iron rings at the height of a man's chest.

He walked the square once at a stroll, letting his feet tell him things. In the middle the boards were firm, and toward the west rope they were firm too. Toward the wall they began, about two paces out, to give.

It was very slight. He would not have noticed it in shoes on a street.

He set his left foot on the board nearest the wall and pressed down through the ball of it, and felt the pine flex and come back, a little sullen, a little late.

Underneath, he thought, the old cobbles had sunk where the drovers had stood their sheep against the wall for a hundred years. The pine bridged the dip, and the bridge sagged.

Ephram had said the main hall. He had been wrong about the floor and right about the wall.

Cael crouched and laid his palm on the board, and thought about what a burst asked of the floor. It asked the floor to stay where it was. The leading hip loaded against the board, and the board pushed back, and the shove of air came out of that pushing.

On sprung oak he had six free. On the second floor at home he had five. On a board that gave like this one, every burst would put a little of its push into the pine instead of into him, and the hip would make up the difference.

He could not know the figure without spending one, and he was not going to spend one the night before a bout, so he gave it a cautious guess. A burst launched from the soft strip by the wall might cost him a burst and a half.

So the wall was where the floor was dear. And a fighter who wanted Cael against the wall would be pushing him onto the dearest boards in the town.

He did not know yet how the ganger fought. Rooke's forty-one profiles did not run to the quarry guild's fair-day men, and the form told him only that the man could write and wanted to see.

But a fighter who spent his days moving stone would know what a wall was for. Cael laid the soft strip in his head beside the iron rings, in the place where he kept things that might matter tomorrow.

He stood up and dusted his palm on his trousers. Somebody had chalked a single word on the day board at the corner of the floor, in tall unsteady capitals, by a hand that had never written it before. DEMONSTRATION.

Lira did not eat much at supper, and nobody remarked on it.

She sat at the end of the corner table with her wrists already strapped, a day early, and turned her cup round and round on the board.

Rooke had given her the opening slot's terms at the meal. The draw had done what he had said it would do and put the foot of the Iron seeding against its head: the river academy's first entry, an Iron Rank Seven, a Stone Path, broad and patient, whose margin on the profiles sheet said honest; slow to read; slower to change. She had read it twice and handed it back.

"You're quiet," said Cael, when the others had gone up.

"I'm thinking."

"About him?"

"About the paper." She put the cup down. "Every bout I ever fought, there was a man somewhere afterward explaining what it didn't count for. A Copper beat an Iron, so the Iron was ill. A Copper beat a circuit, so the circuit was soft. Somebody always had a reason ready." She turned her strapped wrist over and looked at the inside of it.

"Tomorrow there isn't a reason. Iron against Iron. If I win, I won. If I lose, I lost, and that's all it means."

"That sounds better."

"It is better." She stood and picked up her kit. "It's also new. I've spent a long time being underestimated. I was good at it." At the stair she said, without turning round, "Come and watch from the rope. Not the gallery."

He came and watched from the rope.

The main floor in the morning was full before the first bell. The wool town had decided, it seemed, that the season's first proper bout was worth its breakfast, and the gallery benches along the hall side were packed shoulder to shoulder with people holding bread and cups.

The judges' table stood on the north side with its five slates laid out. The floor steward stood at the near rope with the glass.

Cael had walked this floor too, the evening before, and knew its shape. The pine here was laid over the oldest rows of the market, where the footings of the pens had never been dug out.

So the floor rose, very gently, from the river side up toward the hall, a long shallow ramp that a man would feel in his calves before he saw it. The high side was the strong side, as anyone who had warmed up on it knew.

The Stone entry knew it. He walked to his chalk on the high side and set himself there, wide and low, with his feet a little more than a shoulder apart and his weight sunk into his heels.

Stone Paths read the ground. They felt a fighter's steps arrive through the boards before the eyes had them, and they rooted, and they countered off the root. He had the hill, and a Stone on a hill is a fortress somebody has built on purpose.

Five judges sat at the table on the north side with their slates face down in front of them and their chalk laid square beside the slates, and none of them looked at the others, and none of them looked at the gallery; they looked at the two chalk marks on the floor and at nothing else, and that one posture, Cael thought, was the whole of their training.

The steward turned the glass. "Exchange."

Lira gave him the first exchange.

She gave it to him so deliberately that Cael, at the rope, nearly laughed. She came off her chalk at circuit pace and went round him on the low side, along the bottom edge of the ramp, never up it. She offered an entry and pulled it back. She offered another from a new angle and pulled that back too.

Each time the Stone turned on his root to meet her, and each time he countered a little after the moment when a counter would have mattered, and she was gone.

Halfway through the glass she let one of his counters land, a forearm across her shoulder that was very nearly a gift.

"Touch," called the steward. "River."

She went back to her chalk without looking at anyone.

Cael had watched her buy things with exchanges before. This time he could see the receipts.

The Stone's ground-sense ran through his front foot only; when she came at him from behind the front foot, he turned to her a shade late, like a man listening with one ear. His counters came a full beat after his read, not half a beat, because he waited to be sure.

And the low edge of his slope, the downhill margin, he guarded more lazily than its crown, a handspan lazier, because in all his life nobody had attacked a Stone uphill.

Three things, for one touch. Lira thought it cheap.

"Exchange."

She came up the slope.

She did not come at him. She burst past him, one burst at full stretch, close along his left side where the low edge was lazy, and the pine boomed under her landing a full stride above him on his own hill.

The Stone had read her coming and turned to meet a fighter in front of him.

There was no fighter in front of him. There was a fighter above and behind him, and everything about his stance was built to face down a slope, not up one.

He had to turn uphill to find her. Turning uphill, rooted, took him the beat that his counters always took. She had already used that beat. Her palm went onto his ribs before his weight had finished moving.

"Touch. Halcenvane."

The gallery made the sound a crowd makes when it has just been told the shape of something.

The Stone stood on his chalk, breathing, looking at the floor in front of him. Then he did what Rooke's profile had said he was slow to do. He changed.

When the glass turned for the third exchange, he did not go back to the high side. He walked down off it into the middle of the floor, where the ramp levelled out, and planted there with both feet wide, his arms out a little from his body.

He was no longer reading through one foot. He was reading through both. He had given up the hill and built a wall.

"Good," said Rooke quietly, behind Cael. He did not sound displeased.

It was a good answer. Cael watched Lira take it apart, and saw that it cost her.

Her first burst of the exchange went into learning the wall. She came across his front and out again, testing how far round his double root could turn him and how fast. Not fast, but far. He turned the whole way, and the turn was solid.

Her second burst did not test anything. Each time the Stone shifted his weight from one planted foot to the other, there was a moment, half a beat long, when neither foot was quite reading. It was the gap a man leaves when he changes his grip on a heavy thing.

Lira had seen it on her first pass.

On her second she went into it with everything she had. She came out of it on the far side of the wall with her hand on the Stone's back.

"Touch. Halcenvane. Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

The gallery let its breath out in a cheer that the wool town did not seem to have planned.

Cael counted. One burst in the second exchange. Two in the third. And he had seen the first one, early in the first exchange, along the bottom of the ramp, which she had made look like footwork. Four bursts. Her working rate for a bout of three exchanges, by Rooke's book, was three. She was one over.

She came to the rope with her hands on her hips and her chest going, and stood beside him, and watched the steward turn the glass for the next pair. She had not looked at the judges' table once.

"Strange morning," she said.

"Was it?"

"First bout of my life that nobody gets to explain afterward. Nobody's going to say I won it because he was ill, or the floor was soft, or the panel felt sorry for a Copper. It goes on the board, and it stays on the board."

She flexed her strapped right hand and looked at it as if it belonged to someone she was getting to know. "Nobody gets to be surprised by me anymore. I have to actually be this good."

"You are."

"I know," she said, with no flourish at all. Then, because she was Lira: "This morning. Against him. Rooke's got a Wind from a hill house on his list, and nothing next to the name but a question mark. That's the season."

The figure went up on the bracket board a while later, in the rating clerk's chalk. Lira glanced at it once on her way to the wash-house: twenty-six. A strong Iron's figure, with a little Silver at its edge.

Cael wrote the bout that evening the way he wrote every bout, the sentence first and the bill under it.

She took his ground from him twice, the hill and then the wall. Take a Stone's footing away and what's left is a strong man waiting for something to push against.

Cost: four bursts in three exchanges, against a rate of three. One over. She could pay it this once. Ask the next dawn what it charged her.

# Chapter 12 — Page Thirty-One

When the supper was over and the others had gone up, Karis came back down the guest house stair with her lamp and sat across the long table from him, and did not open her notebook.

"Tell me," she said.

The manual lay between them where Gault had left it, with the spill from the stove still marking its page. Cael opened it there and turned it round so that the print faced her. It was a page about thirty in from the front, in the part that set out the panel's duties. Most of it was the dry business of slates and seating and what a judge should do if a fighter fell into the trestle. But near the foot of the page there was a rule in its own short paragraph, set a little apart, as the oldest rules in a book are often set, and under it, in a smaller type, the office's annotation.

He put his finger under the rule and read it aloud.

"*Judges rate the performance demonstrated, not the capacity inferred.*"

Karis nodded. "It's a good rule. Without that rule, none of the rest would be worth having. A judge can't mark a fighter up because he looks strong, or because his house is famous, or because everyone's heard he's dangerous. He can only mark what he sees on the floor." She looked at him. "You've been sitting on this since last night. It isn't news."

"It's the other half of the news." He moved his finger down to the annotation and read that too. "*Performance below evident capacity is not a scoring irregularity.*"

She was quiet.

"The office wrote that note," said Cael, "because somebody asked. At some point, somebody stood in front of a panel, and won, and was rated low, and somebody else protested that the figure was wrong. Like our vain friend in the wool town. And the office looked at it, and wrote this, so that nobody would ever have to look at it again." He sat back. "It's the same ruling. The dry woman at the wool town didn't even have to think. She only had to turn to page thirty-one."

"Last night I told you that you couldn't lean it," said Karis slowly.

"You can lean it."

She looked up at him.

"Not by pushing one leg up," he said. "You were right about that. Nobody can show more than he has. If I tried to make my effect mark read higher than what I did, I couldn't, because the judges only see what happens. It can't be leaned upward. It's too well made." He turned the book back to face himself and looked at the two lines, the rule and the note. "But suppose a man keeps all three marks low together, evenly, well under what he could make them. He isn't breaking anything. He's only showing less. And the rule says the judges must rate what he shows. And the note says that's not their problem. So it leans downward, and never knows that it's leaning, and the people who built it wrote down in advance that it doesn't count."

He kept his voice as level as a clerk's, because he did not trust himself to say it any other way. Whatever else he felt about it belonged in the Log.

Karis did not answer for a long time. Then she reached into the pocket of her coat and took out a small book of her own, and laid it on the table beside the manual. It was not the grey notebook. It was a thin old copybook with a plain brown cover, the kind she bought by the dozen, and she opened it near the back, at a page that held only two lines in her small neat hand.

Cael knew what the two lines were before he read them. On the night of the mock bouts, at the trestle's end on the second training floor, he had watched her tear a strip from her scoring card and lay it in Rooke's old manual, and close the book on it, and say nothing.

"Rooke's copy is old," said Karis. "It's from his own cycle, from when he fought. In that one the rule sits about a third of the way in, in a chapter on the panel's conscience, and the note isn't printed under it at all. Somebody's written it in the margin, in ink, in an examiner's hand, a long time ago." She laid her finger on the two lines. "I copied both out the night of the mock bouts. My strip's still in his book, at the page. He's never mentioned finding it."

"You knew."

"I knew the rule was there. I didn't know whether it would matter." She looked at the copybook and the manual side by side on the table. "I thought it might be the kind of thing that never comes up. Then a vain man protested, and a woman from the continental office ruled on it before noon, and Bracken told Gault you'd want the front third." She closed the copybook. "So it comes up."

They sat with that.

Karis spoke at last. "The fairest instrument on the continent has exactly one blind spot, and you live in it."

He had known she would say it. It was the sentence he had not let himself write since the waystation.

"I was there first," he said. "Long before anybody pointed this at me. At Ardenmere, Vell taught me one rule: give the record what it already holds, and nothing past it. I've kept to that ever since. All that's changed is that the record has learned to measure better." He touched the edge of the manual. "But I'd like it on paper somewhere that I understand the price. This thing looks at me the same way it looks at everybody else. That's the whole point of it, and it's all I ever wanted from it. And I'll be the one person on the continent who meets it with a careful face. It's the one fair measure anybody has offered me, and the doctrine tells me to step up in front of it and not let it take my measure."

"The doctrine's right," said Karis.

"The doctrine's right," said Cael. Then he heard it. He looked at her across the lamps, and saw that she had heard it too. "Note the tense we both used."

Karis did not smile. She wrote it in the grey notebook, the whole exchange, his line and hers and the tense, and underlined the word *is* in the middle of it, once, very lightly. Then she took her lamp and went up.

He sat on with the manual open at page thirty-one. He thought about a woman from the continental office he had never met, and an examiner long dead who had written a note in a margin in ink, and a registrar who had read a whole book in order to tell a boy where to look. All of them had seen the same blind spot, and none of them had done anything about it except write it down.

That, he thought, was the most frightening thing about a really good instrument. It knew exactly where it could not see.

---

The yard-master was the first exhibition of the morning, because he had asked to be.

He had trained the mill guild's yard crews for twenty years, Rooke's sheet said, and had retired from the bracket as an Iron Rank Nine when his knees told him to. But he still came down to the hall every morning at the first bell to run the apprentices, and he had told the steward he would sooner fight the boy then, when the hall was his, than later, when it was everybody's. The steward, who had been one of his apprentices, did not argue.

So the bout was set for the main floor at the second bell, and the hall at that hour was cold and grey and only half full, and the sun was just clearing the hills to the east.

Cael had come in early to look at it.

The main floor was a long rectangle under the crane-beam. Its two long sides, north and south, were roped for the crowd. Its short west end was walled by the guild's tool racks, roped off and declared out of play, a wall of black iron and oak handles that nobody was allowed to touch and nobody could forget. Its short east end was open to the great loading doors, which stood half drawn back on their rails. The light came through them low and flat at that hour and lay across the boards in three long bars, one for each gap between the doors and their frames, and dust turned slowly in it.

High above the floor the crane-beam ran the length of the hall on its iron track. At the east end the track went out through a slot cut in the gable wall, so that the beam could carry a millstone from the hall straight out over the yard. Through that slot, too, the morning came in. It made a fourth bar of light, higher and narrower and brighter than the others, that came down steeply from the gable and fell across the middle of the floor on a long diagonal, from the north-east toward the south-west.

Cael noticed it because it was in his eyes when he faced east. He did not, at first, think anything of it.

The yard-master came in at the second bell with two apprentices carrying his kit and walked straight to the west end, to the chalk mark nearest the tool racks, as if there had never been another mark on the floor. He was a short, wide, grey man with a face like a well-used boot, and he moved carefully on his knees and not carefully at all anywhere else. He shook Cael's hand at the middle of the floor, briefly.

"Heard about the wool town," he said. "The ganger's a friend of mine. He says you don't like to be put anywhere."

"I've heard that too."

"Well." The yard-master went back to his mark. "We'll see what the floor thinks."

"Exchange."

He did not come at Cael. He did not do anything that the gallery would have called an attack. He stood with his back to his racks and stepped forward, a short careful step, and then another, and with each step the floor in front of Cael grew smaller.

Cael understood it within a dozen heartbeats, and admired it within twenty. The ganger at the wool town had herded. This man did not herd; he had no need to move Cael anywhere. He only took ground away, an inch at a time, and every inch he took he kept. The racks behind him were a wall on his west flank, and nothing could come at him from that side. He advanced on the diagonal that cut off the north half of the floor first. Then, when Cael went south to escape it, he was already turning to cut off that half too, with a step that seemed to have been decided before Cael moved. He was not reading Cael. He was reading the floor. He had spent half his life teaching apprentices where a man could and could not go on these boards, and he simply went to the places Cael would need next, a moment before Cael needed them.

He treated Cael's refusals the way a good steward treats an expense. He did not try to stop them, but let each one happen and made sure it cost something.

Halfway through the glass Cael refused once too often toward the south rope, found the floor there already closed by a step he had not seen being taken, and turned back into the middle a quarter-beat late. The yard-master's hand came out hard, a short guild-yard blow with no swing in it, and caught him on the point of the left shoulder.

"Touch. Guild."

The half-full hall made a satisfied noise, for these were the yard-master's own people, early at work.

Cael went back to his mark rubbing his shoulder, and saw that the yard-master had not gone back to his. He had stayed where the exchange had ended, two strides further east than he had started, with the racks still at his back. The floor he had taken in that exchange he meant to keep.

---

The second exchange lasted the whole of the glass, and the gallery began, about halfway through it, to understand that it was watching a siege.

Cael refused, and refused, and refused. He circled out to the open east end, where the loading doors were and the yard-master's floor did not yet reach, and reset there, and came back in on a new line, and found it shut. The yard-master let him go out and let him come back. He did not chase, and he did not hurry. Every time Cael went out to the east end and came back, the old man had taken another step of floor while he was gone, and when Cael came back he found that step already gone from his side of the ledger.

Once, early, Cael burst. The floor had shrunk on the north side until there was only one way out, and the way out was a straight line along the north rope toward the doors, and the yard-master had stepped across it. Cael went over the top of the step instead, in one burst from the middle, and landed twenty feet away in the bar of door-light by the east end with the landing beat holding him still. It was clean. It cost him one. And the yard-master, who had not moved at all while he did it, simply took one more step forward, and the floor Cael had burst out of was his.

*One*, Cael counted. *Of five, on boards this hard. Spend them like a miser.*

The read was giving him everything. It gave him the old man's weight a breath before it moved, and it gave him the next step, and the one after that. It gave him all of it early and clearly and completely, the way it always did. But seeing a man's next purchase did not give you back the floor he had already bought. The yard-master was not hiding his steps. He was showing every one of them, plainly, and taking them anyway, because Cael had no answer to a step that was simply taken.

The glass ran out with nobody touched, and the yard-master stood a pace further east again.

"Even."

Rooke was at the north rope with his arms folded. Cael caught his eye on the way back to the mark and saw nothing in it, which meant Rooke was watching very hard.

When the third exchange began, Cael had perhaps two-thirds of a floor left.

Cael could feel the size of it in his feet. Each time he went round to the left now, the black iron of the racks was there at his blind shoulder, a long stride away, where at the first bell there had been forty feet of boards. The south rope was closer than it had been, and so was the north. The yard-master stood near the middle of the floor now, solid and grey and patient, and everything behind him was his, and everything in front of him was not yet his but would be.

Cael began the third exchange the way he had begun the second, refusing, and partway through it, facing east for the hundredth time, he saw the light.

The sun had climbed. The bar from the gable slot, the high narrow bright one, had moved with it. It had come down off the far wall onto the floor, and it lay now across the middle of the boards on its long diagonal, from the north-east toward the south-west, a hand's breadth wide and painfully bright. As Cael watched, the yard-master took one of his short steps forward, and his leading foot came down in it, and the light went up his body to his face.

Nothing happened. The old man did not stop or turn or put up a hand. He had trained on this floor every morning for twenty years. He had stood in that light ten thousand times, and his body had long ago stopped telling him about it.

But the read did not lie about bodies, and the read showed Cael what the old man no longer knew. For the space of a quarter-beat, as the light crossed his eyes, the yard-master's weight hung. It was the faintest hitch. His eyes narrowed against the glare without his asking them to, and for that quarter-beat his body did not quite know where Cael was, and it waited to be told.

He had stopped seeing his floor's light. Nobody had ever mentioned it to him, because nobody who trained on these boards had ever needed it, and a thing that has always been there stops being a thing at all. It was the one feature of the hall the old man had never priced.

Cael stood in the middle of the third exchange with the answer in front of him, and did a sum.

He could spend it now. If he did, he would have the touch in this exchange and very likely the next, and the bout would be over in four. A touch to one, then two to one: a quick clean win against a respected Iron Rank Nine of the guild, on the old man's own floor, before his own people. Cael could see what a panel would write for that. Execution high, because it would be clean. Control high, because the siege would turn in a single beat. Effect very high, because it would have taken a master's floor away from him in front of his apprentices. He could feel the figure it would make, and it was not a figure the band he had chosen was built to hold. It was the kind of figure the gallery would carry home and talk about, and the talk would reach the next meet before the wagons did, as the ganger's *undersold* had reached this one.

Or he could hold it.

He could let the third exchange run out with nothing in it, and spend the light in the fourth, and take the second touch in the fifth. Five exchanges, hard-won, on a shrinking floor, against a local Rank Nine everybody respected, would rate as a strong Iron performance a sensible distance under par. That was the band. That was the figure a careful boy with good feet would earn.

The cost of holding it was another whole glass on a floor that was getting smaller.

He held it. He went on refusing, on worse ground than before, with the racks at his shoulder and the ropes closing, and he paid for every refusal on the contested boards in a way he had not paid in the first two exchanges. The yard-master's steps came closer together. Twice Cael had to turn hard off his left foot to keep out of the old man's reach, and the second time something in the back of his left calf drew tight, a long thin pull from the knee to the heel, like a cord being wound. He felt it and noted it and kept moving. He did not burst. He did not touch. He gave the panel a third exchange in which a boy kept himself alive on two-thirds of a floor and did nothing else.

The glass ran out. "Even."

He walked back to his mark with the calf telling him about itself at every step, and knew that he had just bought the fifth exchange of the bout, deliberately, at the price of a leg. It was the doctrine's tax. He had paid it at the door, the way you pay a toll, so that the road beyond would go where he wanted it to.

---

In the fourth exchange he spent the light.

He did not try to put the yard-master in it. Nobody could put that man anywhere on his own floor; the old man owned position, and both of them knew it. So Cael went after the one thing the yard-master did not own, which was the rhythm.

He refused at a steady pace for the first part of the glass, steadier than before, the same short sideways step every time, so that the old man's short forward steps fell into time with it, step and refusal, step and refusal, like two men sawing a log. The yard-master did not resist it. A siege had its own rhythm, and he had no reason to break it. Cael watched the light on the floor and watched the old man's feet and counted, and on the fourth beat of the count he made his refusal a fraction short.

The yard-master's next step came forward into the space he had expected Cael to give, and his leading foot came down in the bar of light from the gable slot, and the light went up into his eyes.

His weight hung.

Cael went inside the quarter-beat with the burst he had been saving. It took him across the old man's line on the side away from the racks. The landing beat locked him there, beside the yard-master and a little behind, with the glare between them. Then, before the old man's eyes had come clear of the light, Cael spent the second. It was a short one, a few feet only, from the landing straight in. The landing beat locked him again with his palm on the yard-master's ribs.

"Touch. Halcenvane."

The half-full hall was silent. Then somebody along the south rope, one of the apprentices, said "*Oh*," quite loudly, in the voice of a young man who has just seen something he has walked past every morning of his life.

*Three*, Cael counted. *Of five.* The hip had started its argument, crest to knee, and the calf was worse.

The yard-master stood where he was, blinking, and turned his head slowly and looked up at the slot in the gable where the beam ran out. He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked down at the bright bar on the floor at his feet. Cael saw him understand it, and saw what it cost him to understand it, after twenty years on these boards.

He did not complain; he walked back to his mark.

The fifth exchange was the yard-master finding out whether it had been luck.

He had rebuilt his stance in the interval. He stood quartered now, his left shoulder turned toward the east, so that the high bright bar would fall beside his face and not into his eyes, and he came forward on a new line that kept the light on his left hand. It was a good adjustment. It was a fast one, too, for a man who had stood one way on that floor since before Cael was born.

But a man cannot rebuild a lifetime's stance in the space of a glass, and Cael could see where the old stance was still in him. Every third step, the yard-master's feet forgot what his shoulders had decided, and squared up to the floor as they had always squared, and for that step the light was in his eyes again.

Cael did not need the light, this time. He needed only to know which step would square, and the read told him. He let two steps go by, and on the third he went in along the same long diagonal as before, with one burst, the bout's last, and touched the old man's shoulder as his feet came round.

"Touch. Halcenvane. Two to one. Bout to Halcenvane."

*Four*, Cael counted. *Of five.* He stood where he was after the landing beat released him, letting the calf settle. It did not settle.

The yard-master came across the floor slowly, on his careful knees, and held out his hand. Cael took it. The old man's grip was dry and hard and short.

"You found the beam," he said.

"The light from it."

"The light from it." The yard-master shook his head. "Twenty years. I've had apprentices fall over in that light, and I've told them to stop being soft." He let go of Cael's hand. "You took a fifth exchange you didn't need."

Cael did not answer.

"Don't fret. I'll not tell anybody. It's your bout." The old man looked at him with a kind of grudging respect, as he might look at a beam that had held more than he'd thought it could. "You fight like an auditor. You go through a man's books until you find the one line he never checked, and then you sit there and wait until it's due."

He meant it as a compliment. Cael thanked him for it, as a compliment, and did not say that this particular auditor kept his own books in two hands.

The figure went into the record at the proper interval, to be posted with the others on the last morning: twenty-four. A strong Iron performance, a respectable distance below par. The yard-master's own, beside it, was twenty-three, which was more than the gallery would have guessed and, Cael thought when he saw it, about right.

He sat on the bench at the rope afterward and pressed his thumbs into the left calf until he found the knot of it, deep, just below the bend of the knee. Lira came and sat beside him and watched him do it.

"You held that light for a whole exchange," she said, very quietly. "I saw you see it."

"Yes."

"And that's what that is." She nodded at the calf. "The exchange you held."

"Yes."

She did not say anything else. She took his hands away from the calf and put her own there instead, and worked the knot with her thumbs, hard, in silence, until he had to set his teeth.

---

In the afternoon he fought the two academy Irons, and neither bout asked much of him.

The first was a hill-house fighter who had been told to test the story and who did, earnestly, for four exchanges, coming at Cael with everything his coach had given him and giving it to him in order, like a boy reciting a lesson. Cael gave him a careful bout back and touched him twice. The second was a cautious Shield from the house down the valley who had clearly come to find out what the story looked like close to, and who fought three exchanges mostly from behind his coverage, renewing it steadily and watching. Cael gave him a careful bout too. He spent one burst across the two bouts, from firm ground. That made five for the day. Five free bursts was what these hard boards would sell him between waking and sleeping, however he cut the day into bouts, so whatever the last challenger asked of him would have to be answered on foot.

The figures, when they posted, were twenty-two and twenty-one, and the calf held, though it complained.

The Bronze came last.

He was a Bronze Rank Five, half guild and half academy, as his form had said, a lean cheerful man in his twenties with ink on his fingers and a habit of talking to himself under his breath. He had written *curiosity* in the space for a reason, and as far as Cael could tell, that had been the whole truth. His bout was clever and generous and reached too far. He tried things. He tried a feint off the tool racks that would have been illegal if he had touched them, and a re-entry through one of the door bars that he had clearly worked out from watching the yard-master's bout that morning, and a long dive along the south rope that nearly put him into the crowd. He enjoyed every moment of it, visibly. Cael managed him into a loss over four exchanges, and he seemed to enjoy that too.

Afterward he came straight to the rope, still breathing hard, and leaned on it with both elbows.

"The third exchange," he said. "When I came off the door-light. You weren't there. How did you know I'd come off it on the left?"

"Your weight was on the left before you went."

"Was it?" The Bronze looked delighted. "I didn't feel it go."

"Most people don't. It went about a quarter-beat early. If you load the right before the left, you'll hide it."

"And the second exchange, when I swept low. You stepped over it. Why not burst?"

"It was low enough to step. A step costs less."

"And the burst, when you do burst. They say at the wool town it's twenty feet. Is that the full distance?"

"About twenty feet. That's my documented distance. It's in the record, if you want it in writing."

"And the landing? This morning, against the yard-master, you stopped dead after every burst. Every time. Is that deliberate?"

"No," said Cael. "It's a cost. It happens every time. I can't help it."

The Bronze asked him nine questions in all, standing at the rope with his elbows on it while the hall emptied round them, and Cael answered every one. He answered accurately and completely, and everything he said was true, and every word of it was about the public suite. Every fact he gave was already in a record somewhere: the burst's length, its rate, the landing beat, the read that looked like good timing. He did not lie once. He did not need to. The Bronze went away into the yard with his questions answered and his notebook full, talking to himself, plainly satisfied with his money's worth.

At the guest house window that evening, with the calf propped on a stool, he gave the Bronze a few lines.

*Nine questions, nine true answers, all of it already in the record. At the wool town the mask was a thing I held up. Today I spoke in it. The mask has stopped being a thing I hold up and started being a register I speak in.*

*Quenna's question was about holding it back in front of enemies. Nobody asked the harder one. Today a man who wished me nothing but well thanked me for my honesty, and every word was true, and he went away knowing less than when he came.*

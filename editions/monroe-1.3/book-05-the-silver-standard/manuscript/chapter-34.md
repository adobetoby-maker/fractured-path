# Chapter 34 — The Other Road

Karis was still at the long table when Cael came down from the back room, and she had not lit a second lamp.

Everybody else had gone up.

The guesting-house had settled round her for the night, with the hill house's boots going to and fro on the boards overhead and somebody on the southern house's floor below singing the same three lines of a song over and over, never getting to the fourth.

Brom sat at the far end of the room in the window seat with Marek's notations open on his knees, reading them for what must have been the hundredth time, and he lifted one hand to Cael without looking up from the page.

Karis had laid out her work as she always did. Her notebook lay open at a fresh leaf headed *QUARTERFINAL* and ruled into her four columns. Beside it lay her own chart of the two bouts Ivenne had fought at Norhold, taken from the gallery. Beside that was a thin packet of four pages in a russet paper cover, bought that afternoon from a shop in the delegation quarter. Every house's file on every other house could be had somewhere in this city for the price of a dinner, and Ternhall's was no different.

The four columns were empty.

From the way the pen lay along the edge of the page, dry at the nib, Cael guessed she had been sitting in front of them for an hour.

He took the chair opposite. "Shall I run it? The way we always do."

"Run it."

So he began at the beginning, with the first plain question they always began with, and asked her for Ivenne's tells.

Karis did not answer at once. She looked at him across the lamp, and then she stood up and stepped back from the table into the clear floor between the benches, and set her feet.

"Watch my weight," she said.

He watched.

She brought her right hand up as though to make a contact, and in the half-beat before it went, her weight slid back onto her rear foot and settled there, quite small, like a cup set down on a saucer.

"That's hers," said Karis. "She does it before every ignition. I trained half of them into her." She dropped the hand. "That one we drilled together in our second year, on the long floor at Ternhall, until neither of us could stop doing it. I could tell you which week it set in."

She showed him two more without being asked. A glance at the floor before a planted foot, to a place she did not need to look at; a breath let out through the mouth on the last touch of a run. Both of them were Ivenne's. Both of them, Karis said, had been hers first, or perhaps the other way round, and after so long neither of them could have sworn which.

Ternhall taught Ember in a shape it called a lattice, which had nothing to do with the Lattice Path's invisible lines, and Cael had learned the shape from Karis over two years of watching her work. An Ember fighter's fire came at a single point, only on contact, through the hand. The lattice was what Ternhall built round that one point: three anchors, three planted footholds in a triangle on the floor, laid down in a set order, so that any contact the hand made from inside them had the floor under it. It was a frame and a drain at once, because the heat of an ignition ran down through the planted feet into the boards instead of back up the caster's arm.

And Ternhall paired its students in their first year and taught them to grow their lattices against each other, so that by the end each knew the other's footing better than her own.

Karis had grown hers against Ivenne's.

She came back to the table and sat. "Now ask the other question. The one that's actually useful."

He thought. "And the other way about? What of yours came from her?"

"Every one." Karis picked up the russet packet, held it a moment in both hands, and gave it to him. "Third page. The last paragraph. Read it to me aloud, would you? I've read it to myself. I want to hear how it sounds in somebody else's mouth."

He found the paragraph and read it.

*Subject's method is her signature. Nothing reaches the floor with her that has not been on paper first, and whoever has read her paper has read the bout before it begins. Pressed for time, she goes back to shapes she has already written down. She does not invent well, knows it, and declines to try.*

He put the packet down. The handwriting under the print was small and slanting and very clear.

"That's Ivenne's hand," said Karis. "I'd know it in the dark. It's true, Cael. Truer than anything I've written about myself, and I've written a great deal."

"Standardization is legibility," he said quietly.

"My line, from Greyvane, said about the registry, in triumph." Her mouth moved, not quite into a smile. "Here it is again, about me, in a shop on the river, for two marks."

At the end of the room Brom had stopped turning pages.

Karis looked down at her ruled leaf for a long while, and Cael watched her follow the thing through to its end, carefully, even when the end was plainly going to be somewhere she did not want to arrive.

"Everything I have ever written about an opponent," she said at last, "is worth something only because I know a thing the opponent doesn't know I know. That's all study is. It's a gap between two people's knowledge, and you live in the gap. And there's no gap here. Whatever I could put in these columns, she put in hers two years ago. We each hold the other's key." She stopped. "Except that isn't true either. Hers is the one that's rusted."

"Why?"

"Because she stayed. She's still on the long floor with somebody new at the other end of her lattice, doing what we did, the way we did it. Staying keeps a person in plain sight." She touched the russet cover with one finger. "Ternhall's book on me stops on the day I left. Everything since is a blank page to them." She looked up at him. "And the reason it's blank—"

"Say it," said Cael. "For the notebook."

"The reason it's out of date is you." She said it to his face, and then she turned and said it again down the length of the room to Brom, and then she looked at the ceiling, toward the floor where Lira lay asleep.

"All of you. At Ternhall my method and I were one thing. That's what a doctrine is for. Then I came and sat down at your table, and I've spent two years since writing down people who fight past whatever's written about them." She was quiet for a moment. "I don't know whether any of it went in. I can't see it from in here. Ivenne has never sat at this table. If I'm honest, neither have I, not from the outside."

"Then she'll find out on the floor," said Brom, from the window. "So will you."

For a while longer she sat over the four bare columns. Then she picked up the pen and wrote one word across the tops of all four, in her small square hand. *Unknown.*

*Karis's study died tonight,* Cael wrote before he slept. *She sat in front of four ruled columns and found that whatever she could put in them, the other woman put in hers long ago. I've seen her lose arguments. I've never seen her method lose one.*

*It made me ask who could do that to me, sit across a table holding my key. Three people, and all of them are upstairs asleep, and none of them ever would.*

---

Rooke ran Lira's preparation himself the next day, in a hired practice hall behind the Concourse's east tower, with the door bolted, in two closed sessions, one before noon and one after.

He did not correct her once in either of them. Cael noticed it before the first hour was out. All year Rooke had worked his fighters with a steady trickle of small corrections, a heel here and a hip there and *again*, *wider*, *no*, as a miller keeps adjusting his stones.

This morning there was none of that.

The corrections were finished. They had been finished for a while, Cael supposed, and what was left was quieter, closer to the way Hesk would set one clock beside another and correct neither, only note down the difference between them.

The two reserves stood at chalk marks as gateposts, as they had on the oak in the cold term. And Ephram ran Zerin.

He had come to the hall at first light with Cael's charts of her three bouts copied out in his own hand, which he must have done by lamplight in the room he shared with Brom. He ran them against Lira on the floor a line at a time. He went where Zerin had gone and turned where she had turned, entered off the left as she entered off the left, came out of every turn already moving, as near to it as his legs would take him.

He could not come near her speed.

Nobody in that hall could have, and he knew it better than anybody. But he ran her shape with a fierce, exact patience that Cael had not seen in him before, and whenever he got a line wrong he stopped dead, walked back to his mark without a word, and ran it again before anyone had to ask.

Rooke stood at the side with his arms folded and watched it for most of the morning. Then, between two runs, he spoke to Lira.

"Speed's the one thing no hall can lend you," he said. "I can't give it you in here and neither can he. What you'll get from him, and from this one's pages—" he tipped his head at Cael without turning it, "—is her shape. You'll get her shape. The real thing you'll get once, on the day, with the gallery full." He paused. "So count the first exchange as fees. Pay them. Expect to lose it, and learn everything you can while you do."

Lira nodded and wrote it on the inside of her wrist.

Cael stood at the edge of the floor with the notebook open and did not write anything. A coach was telling his fighter to give away the opening of the biggest bout of her life, and to use it for taking notes. He turned it over and could not think of another coach who would have said it. It was a large compliment to pay a woman he had never spoken to.

In the afternoon Lira ran her corner work against Ephram's Zerin, first at a walk, then at speed, then at speed with every burst counted and the bill added up after each run. Ephram's lungs gave out long before her ideas did. At the end he sat down on the floor with his back to the wall and his head between his knees, and Lira brought him a cup of water and stood over him while he drank it.

"You were a good Zerin," she said.

"I was a slow one."

"A slow good one. That's exactly what I needed."

He looked up at her, scarlet to the roots of his hair. "I owe you a final from the confluence," he said. "I don't mind paying it off a little at a time."

She laughed and pulled him up by the wrist, and the two of them walked the floor together once more at a stroll, while she told him which of his Zerins had been nearest the real one and he argued with her about the third.

Cael closed the notebook and watched them go round. He had seen Lira prepare for fights all year, and he knew the shape of it in her, the restlessness, the extra hour on the oak, the way she ate standing up.

None of that was here today.

She had done her worrying on the quay steps and in the back room, and what was left in her now was a sort of working calm, the steady attention of a carpenter on the last day before a roof goes up, when every beam has been cut and the only question left is whether it fits.

---

In the hour after the second session Brom said he wanted to see Rhagen warm up, and Cael went with him.

The tournament kept one floor open for warming up to anybody who cared to stand at its rail. It was the only place in Norhold where every house could watch every other house for nothing, and most of them, knowing it, sent their fighters there to do as little as possible in front of strangers. Rhagen did not. Rhagen came as a body, eight fighters and two coaches in black and glass-grey, and worked the open floor for a full hour exactly as they would have worked their own hall at home with the doors locked.

They talked the whole time, and that was the first thing Cael noticed and the thing he went on noticing. Every drill on Rhagen's half of the floor had a voice laid over it. A fighter would move and a coach would name the move aloud. The other fighter would answer it and name the answer, while a third stood to one side and wrote it down.

Angles had names and half-beats were counted out loud. A gap in somebody's guard would be spotted, named, argued over by three people at once, and closed, and then the closing would be named too.

Marek worked at the far end of the floor against a tall fighter with a captain's knot on his sleeve.

Cael knew who the captain was.

Rhagen's Shield, Silver of the sixth rank, whose name stood first on the demonstration frame with *Doctrinal interest* written after it. He looked at the man once, long enough to know him again in any crowd, and then he took his eyes away and kept them away.

He had settled it with himself on the stair that morning. Whatever Rhagen's captain did on an open floor, he did there because he had chosen to be seen doing it, and Cael did not mean to carry the man's choice into the ring as if it were a finding.

So he watched Marek.

Marek was a head shorter than Brom and spare, built close to the bone, and he moved the way his notations read, with nothing in it for show. The captain was building coverage against him slowly, a layer at a time, the way a teacher works a sum on a slate for a slow pupil.

Marek was taking it to pieces.

He found a place in it and named it, and the captain closed it, and Marek found the new place the closing had made and named that. Neither of them went above half speed. The coach at their elbow wrote without once lifting his pencil.

Twice the whole Rhagen floor stopped to watch them.

Brom leaned on the rail with his forearms crossed and watched for a long time.

"They're not training," he said at last. "They're peer-reviewing."

"Does it change anything for you?"

"No. It tells me I had it right." He watched Marek name another place. "A house like that makes readers. And a reader believes what's on the page in front of him. He can't help it, any more than I can help being heavy. It's what he's built for." He straightened up off the rail. "So I'll spend my bout handing him things to read."

He gave the far end of the floor one more look, the short spare man in black taking a structure apart out loud while three people wrote it down.

As he looked, Marek glanced up. It was not a long glance. It went along the rail, found Brom, rested on him for the space of a breath, and went back to the captain's coverage. Brom lifted his chin a little, which was as near as he came to a nod in public. Neither of them smiled.

Cael stood a moment longer by the rail after Brom had turned away.

The thing he could not stop looking at was not Marek. It was the noise.

Rhagen said everything out loud. It wrote its whole doctrine down and sold the book in every town on the continent, and on an open floor it argued about its own gaps in front of anybody who cared to listen, and it seemed to lose nothing by it.

Cael had spent a year learning to say nothing, to show a floor exactly what the record could hold and not a hand's breadth more, and he had come to think of silence as the only safe way a fighter could live. Here was a whole house that lived the other way and thrived.

He did not think it would ever be his way. But he walked back across the court behind Brom wondering, for the first time, what it would feel like to be read aloud by people who meant no harm by it.

"Come on," said Brom. "I've seen their library. Time to forge the book."

---

Lira had gone to bed in the afternoon on Rooke's orders and slept through the supper bell. When she came down, the others had kept her place and her plate, and Brom had kept the end of the loaf, which in Brom was close to a declaration.

He passed it to her without a word. Then he filled her cup from the jug, and while he did it he told her a story.

"The first weeks on the oak," he said, "you lost to me every time. Do you remember? Every single time. You'd come at me straight, as hard as you could, and I'd stand there, and you'd bounce."

He set the jug down. "Then one week you stopped coming at me. You went round. You made me chase you all over the oak until I was blowing like a carthorse, and you touched me on the back of the neck, and I sat down on the floor." He went back to his own plate. "You beat me the times you made me play yours. Don't play hers."

Lira looked at him over the bread. Then she ate.

Karis gave Lira a page from her notebook.

She tore it out at the table where everybody could see.

Nobody remarked on it, but every one of them noticed, because in two years none of them had ever known Karis give away so much as a corner of a page. She folded it once and passed it down the board.

It was the panel for the semifinal.

The west tower had posted it at noon, five judges and a clerk, and Karis had sat behind every one of the five from the gallery through the first week. Their names ran in a column down the left of the page, and beside each name she had written one line on the way that judge raised a flag.

At the bottom she had written a finding and underlined it twice.

*These five like to see a thing finished before they rule on it. Three of them run a quarter-beat slow on anything still under construction. Anything you build slowly, they'll wait for. So build slowly.*

"Fight your own fight," said Karis. "They're already sitting in the right chairs to see it."

Lira folded the page again and put it inside her shirt, against her skin.

After supper they stayed at the table for Brom. Nobody called it a study. He only took Marek's notations off his knees, where they had been all through the meal, and spread them among the cups and crusts, and the others drew their chairs in round them.

It was Ephram who knew the doctrine.

Every house on the continent kept a copy of Rhagen's book, and Halcenvane's had come north in Rooke's trunk; Ephram had borrowed it at the second waystation and read it through twice on the tailboard. He found the page he wanted without looking for it.

"First line of the Glass chapter," he said. "*Every structure is its gaps.*" He put the book face down. "Not that it has gaps. That it is them. A fence is only a row of holes with wood round the edges, and if you learn the holes you've learned the farmer, because he decided where every one of them went."

He turned his cup in his hands. "They teach it from the first week. And there's something else, about Marek. His rank came late. Rhagen put his name on the register late, on purpose, and they've never once asked for him to be moved up. They like their best work to sit low on the paper. It makes the doctrine look better when it wins."

"Keeping your best people cheap on purpose." Karis wrote it down. "Institutional banking. It's what Brom does on his own, done by a whole house with a press."

Brom had been listening with his hand on the notations. Now he looked round the table.

"I've been him," he said. "All year. Cheap on paper, and every round somebody across the floor who'd got ready for the price." He tapped the pages. "Now we meet. Two bargains on one floor, and you can't get a bargain off a bargain. So it's his trade against mine, both at the proper price. He finds doors. I charge for them."

"And what is it you want?" said Cael. "Not the title. Under the title."

Brom took his time, as he always did, and nobody hurried him.

"Since the slate country," he said, "every man who's fought me has had my old page three in his pocket. He won't. He'll have read it, and he'll put it down after one touch, and then he'll go looking at what I am now." He gathered the pages together, slowly. "Nobody's looked at me properly since the mending. Plenty of people have looked at the dossier. I'd like to hear what he finds."

*Brom has drawn the best reader on the continent,* Cael wrote afterward, *and the thing he's happiest about is that he'll finally get a proper inspection.*

*He wants to be read. He's mended something and he'd like the best eyes alive to check the work. I sat at that table with my whole year in my pocket, and I couldn't remember one time I'd let anybody check anything of mine.*

---

Rooke had given Lira the whole commercial file on Zerin in the morning, seven pages bound in grey board with the shop's stamp on its cover. She had read the first six at the rail between sessions. The seventh she had kept back for the little room, and for him.

It was not a page of times.

It was the page that every file in that shop's style kept for its end, the page about where a fighter came from, and she read it aloud to him in a low voice, with the window open on the dark courtyard.

*Program entrant at twelve. Kindled at fourteen, within the program. Advanced on schedule, every season.* After that the page went on in the shop's flat, careful manner: the cohorts she had been put in, the coaches and the halls, each step up taken in the very year it was due. Iron Rank Nine at nineteen. Silver assessment pending. And at the very foot, a line that was as close as the shop ever came to praise. *No season wasted.*

Lira laid it on the table and put the lamp on it so the draught would not take it.

For a while neither of them spoke.

"I've been holding this upside down," she said. "Since the quay steps. I've been treating it like a list of punishments." She touched the edge of the page. "That's not what it is. It's an *audit* of the other road."

"Which road?"

"Mine. The one I didn't get." She sat back. "Same Path. Same sort of start. Somebody looked at each of us young and thought there was something there. Fenmark put me in a ledger. Auremont put her in a program. Then Fenmark threw me out, and I went and made myself out of whatever was lying about. A ledger-keeper at Ardenmere. A circuit full of fair-day men. You. Brom. A broom in the common room." She almost laughed.

"And Auremont kept her, and built her properly, every door opened in the right year with the right key. Tomorrow the two of us find out what each way of making a fighter is worth."

Cael sat with that while the lamp hissed. He caught himself trying, without meaning to, to imagine his own other road, the version of him somebody might have been supposed to build. There was nothing there to imagine. No program on the continent had a page for a boy like him, and no shop sold a file that began where his began.

Lira had a road she had not been allowed to walk, and a woman walking it, and she could stand on a floor and look at it. He had never had one. He had only ever had the road he was on, and he had laid most of it himself, a stone at a time, a little ahead of his own feet.

She stood up and fetched her kit bag from the corner, and sat down on the floor with it between her knees, and began to rewind the strap on its buckle, very carefully, the way she did small mending when she was thinking about something large.

Cael looked for self-pity in her, from old habit, and could not find any. Whatever that account had once held, she had spent it a long time ago, and gone back over the spending since, and shut the book.

"It isn't her," she told the buckle. "I'm fighting the version of me they were supposed to build." She pulled the strap tight and tested it. "All right, then. Let's see what the scrapyard version does."

---

She found him later on the stair, on the landing between their floor and the hill house's, where a narrow window looked out over the roofs toward the Concourse.

He had gone there to think, and she seemed to know it, because she did not speak at once. She sat down two steps above him with her arms round her knees and looked out of the same window.

"Do me a chart," she said at last. "Her against me. Properly. No coaching. Not what you hope for me. What the instrument says."

He had known since the second evening that she would ask, and he had been building the answer ever since, the way he would have built it for a stranger. He gave it to her that way, because that was what she had asked for, and because he had never once lied to her about a floor.

"You'll be a tenth late," he said. "On every window, all night. Every time the two of you race for a place, she gets there first, by a tenth. It's not much. But it's every time, and a tenth every time adds up to a bout."

He kept his eyes on hers.

"Everything else is level. Your corners against her starts is the best pairing in that whole bracket. I'd pay to watch it if I weren't already going to. And she's never been behind, not once, and you've spent your whole life behind." He let the rest of the chart finish in his head. "Fight her the way the numbers expect and she takes it on points, in four exchanges, cleanly. If you can find the one place nobody's ever taken her, and keep her there, then I don't know what happens. Nobody knows. That's the only reason to go."

Lira sat quite still on the step above him.

"Behind," she said.

"Behind."

She looked out of the window for a long time at the dark shape of the Concourse against the sky and the lamps burning on the fourth floor of the west tower, where somebody was still at work.

Then she stood up, and as she passed him on her way up she touched the top of his head, once, lightly, the way she used to on the oak when a session had gone well.

"I'll be at the rail with the notebook," he said to her back.

"I know. So will Auremont." She went on up without turning round. "Give them something long to write."

He stayed on the landing until the lamps in the west tower went out. He had given her the true chart and nothing gentler, because she had asked for it and because between the two of them anything gentler would have been an insult.

Giving it had not been free.

He had not known until tonight that honesty with a friend had a price of its own, quite separate from the price of honesty with anybody else, and that part of the price was that the friend had to sit there and watch you pay it.

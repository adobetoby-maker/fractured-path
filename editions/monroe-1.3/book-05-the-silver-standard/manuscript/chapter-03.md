# Chapter 3 — The Second Weight

Rooke signed Ephram's sheet before he said anything else, and he did it in front of everybody.

The squad had come up to the second training floor at the seventh bell of the Sixth-day: a long upper room over the Blade hall, its boards laid on joists that gave a little underfoot like the yard's oak, with tall windows down one side and a row of lamps already lit along the other. The five of them, the two third-year reserves, and Rooke. A trestle table stood by the door with stacks of paper on it under a stone. A board leaned against the end wall with the training calendar pinned to it, five weeks of it, every day ruled.

Rooke took a single sheet from inside his coat, laid it on the trestle, and uncapped a pen.

"Supervised floor time," he said. "Ephram and the enrollee. Tied to the enrollee's evaluation hours under the provision, two sessions in the week, me at the rail. Requested at the end of last term, by Ephram, in writing." He signed it. "Granted."

Ephram, who was strapping a wrist by the window, did not look up. But his hands stopped on the strap, and then went on.

"Next term," said Rooke, "has arrived." He put the pen away and turned to the calendar board, and that was all the ceremony there would ever be about it.

He spoke for perhaps two minutes after that. Cael wrote it down later almost word for word, because Rooke did not waste words and therefore did not leave any spare to be lost.

"Every bracket at every meet is seeded off paper," he said. "Your formal tier puts you in a bracket. Your formal rank puts you on a line in it. The host sets the draw so the top rank meets the bottom rank first and the middle meets the middle, and nobody at the host's table ever looks at you, only at the line on your certificate." He looked at them now, one at a time. "So the paper puts Lira at the foot of the Iron seeding, because she's Rank One, and the first thing the draw will give her is somebody from the top of it. The paper puts Karis in the middle. It puts Ephram near the top, which is where he belongs. And it puts Brom in a Copper bracket, which is where he doesn't."

Lira's face did not change. "Good," she said. "I'd rather start with the best of them. Then I know where the top is."

"You'll know by the second bell of the first day," said Rooke. "And so will they." He turned to Brom. "You're in Copper, and I won't insult you by explaining Copper to you. Learn the sheets. Wait."

"Yes, Instructor."

Then Rooke looked at Cael, and the look went on a little longer than the others had.

"There'll be exhibitions on your card at every meet," he said. "The hosts set them. Volunteers. Whoever puts a name down on the day. Performance scoring, no standings. I can't scout a man who hasn't volunteered yet." He reached back to the calendar and tapped Cael's line, which ran down the full five weeks without a single word on it, only a short dash in every day. "A dash means every day. You'll train against all of it, and you'll be ready for any of it, and you'll find out what it was afterward, the same as everyone finds out everything."

"Yes, Instructor."

"Floor work," said Rooke. "Frames first. Enrollee, you're with Ephram. Feet only. No bursts."

---

Cael had never been hit at Rooke's cohort speed. Ephram had told him so on the oak at the end of last term, with the yard empty, as a courtesy: *Rooke's cohort works at a speed you have never been hit at.* He found out within a quarter of an hour that it was true, and that the courtesy had been a real one.

The frame was simple. Ephram came in with a lath practice blade, straight entries off a chalk mark at four paces, and Cael's work was to be somewhere else when the lath arrived, using his feet and nothing but his feet. There was to be no burst, no hand and no answer, only the evasion and then the recovery to his own mark, ready for the next.

The first six passes were honest work. Ephram had grown quicker over the recess and quieter about it, which made the quickness worse, but quickness was a thing the read could take. The read told him weight and direction before either had finished deciding, a thin early warning along the inside of his attention, like the tug a rope gives your hands before the boat at the end of it swings. Ephram's weight went forward and left; Cael slipped right and back. Ephram's weight went down into the front knee for a low line; Cael stepped up and out. He recovered to the chalk each time with a small hop and came back set.

On the seventh pass Ephram came in the same way, and the lath touched Cael across the ribs while Cael was still halfway home.

It was not a hard touch. It did not need to be.

Cael stood still where it had caught him, with his weight still on its way back to the mark, and looked down at the lath against his side, and then up at Ephram.

"Half a beat," said Ephram. "You were half a beat deep in your recovery." He drew the lath back. "That was the second-year entry. Every house on the continental line teaches it in the second year, and they all teach it the same way, because it works on everybody. Every Iron fighter in the region who came up through one of those houses will throw it at you. You'll see it forty times before the season's out."

"Again."

"Again," Ephram agreed, and went back to his mark.

He threw it again at full speed, and Cael watched for it, and it caught him in the same place. Knowing it was coming did not help. He felt the read take the entry, weight forward and left, true and early, and he slipped right as the read told him to, and started home, and the lath was there.

He stood still again.

"It's not that you're slow," said Ephram, in the voice of a man reporting the weather. "You're early. You're early on everything. It's the first thing anybody on this floor notices about you." He turned the lath over in his hand. "That's what the entry eats."

"Show me."

So Ephram showed him, slowly, the way Rooke must once have shown a boy of fourteen in some other upper room.

He came off the mark at half speed, then at a quarter, then at a walk. At a walk Cael could see it, and once he saw it he could not believe he had missed it twice. Ephram's front foot went forward and took the weight, honest weight, the real thing, enough of it that any read in the world would have reported it as an attack. But the back foot stayed. It stayed planted for the length of one heartbeat after the front had landed, holding a second share of the weight in reserve. Only when the man in front of him had answered the first share, had slipped and turned for home, did the back foot come off the floor and drive. The lath went in behind the evasion, into the space the evader had just vacated and was now coming back to fill.

"Two weights," said Ephram, standing in the finished shape of it with the lath touching Cael's side again, very gently. "The front one is real. It has to be, or nobody bites. You read it, you answer it, you go home. The back one waits till you're on your way home."

"And the read only gives me the first."

"I don't know what your read gives you." Ephram stepped back to the mark. "I know it makes you move before anybody else would. I know this entry was built for people who move first."

Cael stood with that.

The read had never lied to him. He had written it a dozen times, and it was true: weight and direction, early, never what a man meant by them. And here was a man who meant something by the order of his weights, and the read had told him the truth about the first and nothing about the second, because the second had not happened yet. It had reported honestly and he had answered honestly, and he had been hit, twice, by a thing that had been designed by somebody long dead in a house he had never seen to hit that kind of honesty.

From the rail, Rooke watched.

"Again," said Cael. "Half speed. I want to watch the back heel."

They went at half speed. Cael kept his eyes low, on the back foot, and let the read do its work without letting it decide anything. The front foot came, and took weight, and the read reported it, forward and left, and every trained thread in him wanted to slip right and go home. He did not; he let the first weight come and go, and he watched the back heel. It was flat; it was flat; and then it came up off the boards, and *that* was the attack, and he slipped it on the outside and the lath went past his hip into air.

"Yes," said Ephram. "Now at speed."

At speed it was another thing altogether.

The trouble with waiting for the second weight was that the first weight was real. If he did not answer it, it was still coming. Ephram's front foot landed, and the lath came forward on the honest share of the weight, and Cael held his ground to watch the heel, and the lath was very close. The heel came up and he slipped, and the lath took him on the sleeve as he went, a glancing scuff along the outside of the arm, nothing a panel would score, everything a coach would note.

He went back to the mark and set himself.

"Half," said Ephram. "You beat the second weight and nearly ate the first. That's what waiting costs."

"Again."

The next one he took a hair earlier, giving the first weight a quarter-step of ground without committing to it, his own weight on the balls of both feet so that he could go either way. It felt wrong. Everything in his body wanted to answer the first thing it was told. The heel came up and he was already moving off it, and the lath went by a hand's width outside him, clean.

Ephram looked at the space where Cael had been, and nodded.

"That's the correction," he said. "Don't answer the front foot. Answer the back one. You'll get it right one time in three this week and two in three next week and you'll stop thinking about it by the first meet." He tipped the lath at him. "Now watch this."

He came off the mark at full speed with the same front foot, the same honest weight, forward and left; and Cael, who had just been taught to wait, waited, and watched the back heel, and the back heel came off the floor at once, together with the front, and the lath took him clean across the ribs again before he had moved at all.

"One weight," said Ephram. "All of it at once. The plain entry, the one they teach in the first year." He lowered the lath. "Every one of them who throws the second-year entry at you will throw that one too, mixed in, as soon as they see you've stopped biting on the first. That's the other half of the lesson. There isn't an answer. There's a better guess, and a fighter who keeps changing which one you're guessing against."

Cael stood rubbing his ribs, and found that he was not angry and was not embarrassed, and that what he mostly felt was a kind of pleasure he had not expected. He had been shown the edge of a thing he owned. Nobody had ever shown him that before; he had always found the edges himself, alone, in the dark, at the cost of something.

"How many times," he said, "did it take you?"

Ephram considered the question as if it deserved a true answer, which was the most surprising thing he had done all evening.

"To learn it? A term. To stop getting caught by the mix?" He lifted one shoulder. "I still get caught. Less. That's what the cohort's for." He walked back to his mark. "Your verdict, Assay, since you like to keep them. Early is a gift and a weakness, and you've got more of both than anybody I've stood across from. Again."

They went again, and again, until the lamps were brighter than the windows. Cael beat the second weight four times in the next ten and ate the plain entry twice when he waited for a heel that never waited. At the rail Rooke wrote one line on his board, and Cael, passing it on the way to the water jug, read it upside down out of habit.

*Wait for the heel. Cost: the first share. Fine.*

---

The trestle by the door turned out to hold the season.

At the water break the squad drifted to it, and Rooke lifted the stone off the stacks and let them look. Profiles. Forty-one of them, one to a sheet, a fighter to a page: every name entered at the first meet in the Iron and Copper brackets, with a Path, a rank, a house, a record where the house had one, and down the margin in Rooke's hand a verdict of three words or fewer. *Quick hands, slow feet.* *Honest; telegraphs left.* *Lose to her once and she studies you.* *Hits above his rank.* One sheet had only a question mark.

"Coursework," said Rooke. "You learn them like coursework. You'll be tested."

Cael read the Copper stack over Brom's shoulder before he read his own. Halfway down it was a sheet that Rooke had marked more heavily than the others. A fair-day fighter from a market house in the hills, Iron Skin like Brom, Copper Rank Four on her certificate and a record of exactly four bouts, every one of them won, every one of them short. Rooke's margin said: *Rank Four on paper. Fights like a Seven. Buried.* And under it, in smaller letters, as if Rooke had come back to it later: *Somebody's keeping her low on purpose.*

Brom read that sheet twice, which he did with no other. Then he turned it face down on the pile and put his hand on it, and went on to the next one.

Cael read the stack fast and then a second time slowly, and the room around him went quiet, as rooms did when he did that. The read was no use on paper; paper had no weight. But he had a second thing that worked on paper, which was the habit of years spent reading men who wanted to be misread, and it found him more in Rooke's three-word margins than in the long lines of record beside them. A man who wrote *studies you* about an opponent was paying her the highest compliment Rooke owned.

Brom read beside him. Brom read nothing alone if he could help it, and these were opponents, and opponents were documents, and documents were for the table with somebody beside you; so he stood at Cael's elbow and turned the Copper sheets over one by one, very slowly, his lips not moving, his big hand spread on each page as though to keep it from getting up. He read them all without a word.

"Enough," he said.

"Enough what?"

"Five weeks." Brom put the stone back on the Copper pile. "It's enough."

He did not say what it was enough for, and nobody asked, because Brom's sentences arrived finished and did not take kindly to being opened.

Karis had gone straight to the question mark. It was an Iron entry from a house in the hills to the east, a Wind practitioner, rank given, record left blank, and Rooke's margin held nothing but that single mark. She stood looking at it for a long time with her hands behind her back.

"He priced everybody else," she said to Cael, low. "Every sheet on this table has a verdict. This is the only one he couldn't buy."

"Maybe there's no record to buy."

"There's always a record. There's just sometimes nobody who's read it." She put the sheet back where it had lain. "I'd like to know which."

Lira had the Iron stack, and she had stopped at one sheet near the bottom, and she was not reading it. She was looking at the house name at its head.

Fenmark: the house that had Kindled her at fourteen and read her Copper and sent her out into the world with six letters on a certificate. It had a fighter in the region's Iron bracket this year, a second-year, and Rooke had given him four words: *Good. Schooled. Expects respect.*

"They'll be at the meets," said Lira. Her voice had nothing on it at all, which was how Cael knew.

"Some of them."

"Good." She put the sheet back. She did it carefully, square to the stack, and took her hand away from it quickly. "I'd like them to see the board."

Across the room, by the window, Ephram was retying his wrist strap and had very obviously heard every word and was very obviously not going to say so; and Cael wrote that down later, too, on Ephram's page, because it was the second kind thing the man had done in one evening and both had been done in silence.

---

The common room had its fire lit for the first time since the recess, and Lira, who had been quiet all the way across the quadrangle, took the poker off its hook and pointed it at Cael.

"Show me," she said. "The heel thing."

"It's late."

"It's the tenth bell. I'm not asking you to fight a war, I'm asking you to stand on the hearthrug." She weighed the poker in her hand, found it wanting, and put it back, and took up the hearth broom instead, which was about the length of Ephram's lath and a great deal less dangerous. "You came off that floor looking like a man who's been handed a present he doesn't know how to open. I want to see what's in it."

So he stood on the hearthrug, and Brom pulled the good chair round to watch with the air of a man settling in at a play, and Karis sat on the settle under the shelf where the copper crown stood between the lamp and her drafts, and opened her notebook on her knee and did not write in it.

Lira came at him with the broom.

She came badly the first time, all one weight, front and back together, a plain lunge across the rug, and he slipped it without thinking. She frowned and went back to the fender and tried again. This time she tried to keep her back foot planted, and it stuck to the rug for so long that the broom had arrived, been answered, and gone home before the back foot had remembered it was supposed to do anything.

"That's not it," said Brom from the chair.

"I know that's not it."

"You're waiting with your whole back leg. He waited with his heel. The leg was already going."

Lira turned and looked at him. "You watched it?"

"I watched the floor all evening. Ephram does it to everybody." Brom folded his arms. "He did it to me in the first month last year. Three days of it. I thought it was my fault."

"Was it?"

"Some of it." He nodded at the rug. "Go again. Load the back leg. Keep the heel down. Then let it go."

She went again, and this time there was something in it: a front foot that took real weight, a back heel that stayed on the rug half a breath longer than it should have, and then the drive. It was clumsy and slow and a third of the speed of the thing on the second floor, and Cael's whole body still wanted to answer the front foot and go home. He made it wait and watched the heel, and when the heel came up he slipped right, outside, and the broom went past his hip into the coal scuttle with a crash that brought a protest through the wall from the room next door.

"Sorry," called Lira at the wall, not sorry at all.

"Again," said Cael.

They did it a dozen more times, by Karis's count, which she gave afterward without being asked. He beat the second weight seven times and ate the plain lunge four times when Lira, catching on with alarming speed, started throwing it at him as soon as he began staring at her feet; and once, when she threw neither but simply stood there with the broom raised until he lost patience and moved, he walked straight into the bristles, and said, "Oh, *come on*," to the broom in a voice of real grievance.

Brom laughed so hard the good chair creaked.

"Third one," said Lira, lowering the broom. "That one isn't Ephram's. That one's mine." She was flushed from the fire and pleased with herself. "You were so busy waiting for the heel you forgot the person it was attached to."

"That's a fair entry," said Cael.

"I'll take it." She hung the broom back on its hook, and then, offhand, said to the fire: "You liked it. Getting caught tonight. I watched you on the floor. You were happy."

He thought about lying, and could not see the point of it in that room.

"He showed me the edge of something I own," he said. "I've always found the edges myself. In the dark. After they'd cost me. Tonight a man stood there at a walk and showed me where it was, and it cost a scuff on a sleeve." He sat down on the end of the settle by Karis. "I didn't know you could get it that cheap."

Nobody answered that, because nobody needed to. Karis turned a page of her notebook, at last, and wrote something on it; and then she looked up.

"Rooke said the hosts schedule your exhibitions," she said.

"For volunteers."

"Under which line?"

Cael looked at her.

"He didn't say. He spoke of it as settled, and perhaps it is; hosts put on exhibitions, everybody knows they do, nobody has ever needed to ask under which provision of the charter." She tapped the pencil against her teeth. "The brackets live in the fifth part. Fielding lives in the sixth. Between them there's a run of schedules and annexes that I've read for sense and not yet for every word, because I was busy with the bits I was sure we'd need."

"And now you think we'll need them."

"Now I'd like to know which line, before somebody else asks me." She shut the book. "Bed. All of you. Brom, you're on the post at first light, and you'll want to be cross with somebody, and I'd rather it wasn't me."

---

The memorandum came on the morning of the Second-day, four days after the courier, which Karis had predicted to within a day; and it came in a form nobody had predicted at all.

There was no hearing notice, no ruling, no seal and no letter from anybody with a title. There was a single sheet from the regional qualifying registrar's office, printed on a press that was short of ink, headed *Deficiency memorandum: entries returned for correction*, and listing five houses in a column. One had dated its fee note to the wrong year, one had used last cycle's form, one had sent its schedule acknowledgment unsigned, and one had a practitioner whose rank on the entry did not match the registry's extract. The fourth on the list was Halcenvane.

Bracken sent his boy up before the first bell had finished ringing. The four of them stood at the records-hall counter in their coats, still half asleep, and read it in turn.

*Item the third: the enrollment record submitted includes a practitioner holding no registry classification. An unclassified practitioner cannot be seeded to a tier bracket. A practitioner who cannot be seeded cannot be fielded. The roster is accordingly defective on its face. Correct and resubmit within fourteen days.*

Brom read it twice and handed it on to Lira without a word. Lira read it once and handed it on to Karis with her mouth set. Karis read it, and then she laid it on the counter and put both her hands on the wood on either side of it, and Cael, who had seen that gesture in many rooms, felt the morning change shape.

"Three sentences," said Karis. "*Cannot be seeded.* So *cannot be fielded.* So *defective.* It walks like a proof." She tapped the middle of it with one finger. "It isn't one. It's a bridge with a plank missing in the middle, and somebody's painted the gap to look like wood."

"Show me the gap," said Cael.

"*Seeded* is a word from the bracket rules. It's what the host does to fill a draw: it puts a fighter on a line according to his tier and rank. *Fielded* is a word from the sixth part. It's what a house does when it sends its people. The charter has both words. It never once lets them into the same paragraph." She drew a line across the memorandum with her fingernail, between the second sentence and the third. "This joins them so that one seems to follow from the other. So that a house could only send what a host could seed. The charter never says so. Not anywhere. I'd know. I've read it five times."

"Then what does the charter do with somebody who can't be seeded?"

Karis looked at him. He had said the thing she had been trying to say herself.

"I don't know yet," she said. "It must do something. Nobody writes a rule that says *any practitioner* and then leaves no door for the ones they didn't think of." She turned to Bracken, who had been standing behind his counter through all of it with his hands folded and his face doing nothing. "Fourteen days."

"Fourteen days," said Bracken. "From yesterday's date."

"We'll need four," said Karis. "And two more for you to check every citation against the house copies, because I won't have a single page number wrong in this."

"You'll have a single page number wrong," said Bracken, "over my dead body and both my clerks'." He held out his hand for the memorandum.

Karis did not give it to him at once. She read the other four items aloud first, because she would not let anybody leave a document half read. A wrong year on a fee note, last cycle's form, a missing signature, a rank a year out of date. Brom, at the wrong year, had to sit down on the bench by the door.

"Clerical," said Karis, when she had finished. "Clerical, clerical, clerical. Every one a slip of the pen." Her finger came back to the fourth item, which was theirs. "And ours is dressed to look like the others. Same type, same length, same list, so that anybody reading down it would take it for clerical too." She took her finger away. "That's the cleverest thing on the whole sheet. I'd like to meet whoever thought of it, and I'd like a table between us when I did."

Bracken took the memorandum, read it once more, and folded it into the inside of his gown. "I'll take it up to the chancellor myself. She'll want to hear it from somebody who's held the paper."

He was back within the half-hour, which was quick even for Bracken, with one sentence of Withrow's. He gave it to them exactly as he had been given it.

"She says: *Don't argue with the man. Quote him his own charter, slowly, and leave a chair at the table so that he can come round and sit on our side of it.*" Bracken took his pen out of its rest. "She says you're to have the long table in the records hall after the last bell, every night you need it, and the lamps. And she says she's heard the third meet's halls run cold, and she would like the house to get far enough to find out."

Karis looked at Cael, and Cael kept his face still.

"A great many people," he said, "seem to know about the halls at the third meet."

"It's a quarry town," said Bracken. "Everybody knows quarry towns are cold." He began to write. "The long table. After the last bell. I'll bring the charter."

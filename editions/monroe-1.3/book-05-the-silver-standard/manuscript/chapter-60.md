# Chapter 60 — Seven Things

He closed the year that evening in Hesk's book, at the table under the window, with Hesk's steel pen.

It was not the end of a year by anybody's calendar but his own. But it was near enough a year since he had sat at another window, on the last night of the old term, and written down what was in the till. A thing that has been opened ought to be closed, and the ledger had been open all that time. He turned past the inventory at seventeen, and the trial, and the ring, and the minute, and the pole, and the rule, and the warning, to the next clean leaf. He ruled the margin with the edge of the closing ceremony's printed card, because he had no rule with him. Then he sat a moment before he wrote, with the pen lifted. He did that perhaps once a year and had never written it down.

*The year, closed.*

*Seven now, and the one I can't account for.*

*Lira's burst. Six free on the sprung oak; four a day on this city's stone. On finals day I spent nine. The seventh, eighth and ninth were all past six, and the hip sent all three bills the next morning with interest. The landing beat: four years of trying to trim it, and it is no shorter. Since the fourth exchange it doesn't always come when I call it. Sometimes something else comes.*

*The push I took from the Bronze at Ardenmere. Five blows in one sitting, on a scored floor, in front of eight thousand, the first load it has carried in public since the circuit. The right shoulder is still counting the fifth.*

*Brom's read. It never failed. Not once, in four exchanges. This year it learned to read the air as well as a body, and at Gold it will do one or the other and not both. I learned that in the ring, which is a bad place to learn things.*

*Reydan's give. Nobody official had ever seen it. On finals day nine judges, three scribes and a full row of the Compact watched it drink a Gold's wash and then most of a Gold's fire. That's over. It's in the book for good.*

*Karis's spark, by her leave. Three contacts in all, one past the two we allow in public. The first was the touch. The right forearm will shine for a week.*

*The quiet thing from the stair. Sealed through an afternoon with eight thousand watching, and in its last corner, when it offered. Never used, anywhere, by anybody's count. Rent from waking, spent or not. The second year of "nowhere yet" closes tonight, and it is still nowhere. The debt it was sealed for is still owed and still unspoken.*

*Daeva's storm. Taken in the middle of a fight she began, awake, by the old minute's three lines, with the cost on paper as far as paper went. Fifteen feet of it, a third deep. The first notice that ever said Gold. The first that ever warned me what it gave might not hold, and that part stands. Everything else I carry is still shifting to make room for it, slower than after any of the others. Gold is heavy. Hesk taught me to write down what a thing costs the instrument as well as what it does. So: written.*

*And at the foot of the list, the still place from session nine. Looked at again tonight. Nothing to add.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He stopped there, and then went on, because there was one more account owed, to a man who would hear about the match from everybody on the continent but him.

*Reydan will read the accounts. It still isn't the answer. Not yet.*

He dusted it with sand, Bracken's way, and gave it a full minute by Hesk's clock before he turned over.

---

Bracken shut the pouch for the north at the eighth bell to the minute, and had said so at supper, so the letters had a shape to fit.

Hesk's took nearly all of it. Cael began at the roster and did not stop until the referral, and he left nothing soft in it anywhere. There was the clause, and the meets, and the trial, and the form with its one word for a reason. There were all four exchanges, the left side, the stumble on the stone and the stoppage. Hesk would have found the gap if he had smoothed any of it over. He found hidden repairs under new lacquer for a living. Cael copied the eleven words out at the end, every one in its place, because Hesk was owed them as the tower had set them and not as anybody remembered them.

It came to six pages, which was five more than he had ever sent anybody. At the bottom of the sixth he wrote the one thing he was sure of.

*I still don't know what I am. What's changed is that now they don't either, in writing, with a seal on it. I used to be the only one in the room who didn't know. It turns out I like the company.*

Vell's was shorter. She had kept the first honest figure anybody ever wrote down about him, in a circuit ledger at Ardenmere, and she would want the end of the year in the same plain hand. He gave her the season in a page and the match in another. He copied the entry for her too. She would understand better than anybody alive what it cost a keeper of figures to sign his name under *unscorable*. He told her about Umber putting his own boot to a floor he had already signed for, and thought she would like that more than all the rest of the letter. Then he ended it with the only line he had been sure of since he picked up the pen.

*Your ledger rated me before any of theirs could. It's still the one I check the others against.*

He took both down the stair at a quarter to the bell. Bracken weighed Hesk's in his palm, and looked at Cael over it, and put it in the pouch without any remark at all, which from Bracken was a remark.

In the back room Brom had his kit open on the bed and was packing it by a system nobody else understood, the shirts rolled as tight as bandages. The plaque went in last, in its own two shirts, with a letter of one line laid against the back of it. Lira was on the other bed with her boots off. She asked Brom whether he meant to think about the district seat yet, since his summons would be lying there waiting; the notice had said so.

Brom pushed the plaque down into its corner with the flat of his hand until it sat level.

"The recess's problem," he said.

He found Lira on the harbour wall at the powder wharf, sitting on the stone under the two old masts with her legs over the water.

There was no weather, and the masts were quiet. Behind her the city was half dark. Whole streets of it had already loaded and gone, and their windows were black, and between them the houses that were staying another night had lit every lamp they had. She had taken the tape off her wrists. He had not seen them bare since the opening, and they looked thin and white.

He sat down beside her. A lighter was coming across the basin, rowing home, with a lantern swinging at her stern.

"You counted it," said Lira. "Mine. Zerin, in the semifinal, the Wind from Auremont. Don't pretend you didn't."

"Four flags and an exchange."

"Four flags and an exchange." She said it back to him slowly, getting the weight of it. "Nobody had ever taken an exchange off Zerin. Not one, not since she was twelve. I took one." She held up her bare right hand and looked at it. "She beat me. Say that too, whenever you say the other. I'm not going to spend a year turning it into something it wasn't. She was faster and she was better, and I knew it in the first exchange. But I made her pay, and nobody else did."

The lighter's oars knocked in their pins, out on the black water.

"They're all going up," Lira said. "Everybody who made me look slow this year. Zerin, and the big Force from Auremont who put Karis out. They'll be in the Silver bracket next time." She put her hand down on the stone between them. "I'd sooner lose to them up there than keep winning down here and call it a career."

She turned and looked at him. Her face in the lamplight from the wharf was perfectly calm.

"Silver bracket. Next cycle."

He did not say the thing in his head, which was a sentence about official channels, said to him the afternoon before in a room full of clocks. She had heard him tell it at the long table. She knew. There were some things you did not say on a harbour wall, the way Rooke would not put a certain word on a coaching sheet. They sat until the lighter's lantern had gone round the end of the mole, and then they got up and went back through the dark streets to the house.

---

The road home was seventeen days, and on the first evening of it they came to the first waystation.

It was the one they had stopped at on the way down, a low stone house at a crossroads with a long board in its common room and a fire at one end. It was still flying the tournament's flag. The keeper knew the plain colours and would not take full price. The two wagons came into the yard at dusk, and the reserves saw to the horses, and the rest of them went in to the fire.

They sorted themselves round the long board the way they had sorted themselves round every board for a year, without anybody deciding it. Lira took the bench nearest the fire and put her boots up on the next one. Brom went to the kitchen door and came back with bread and a crock of something nobody had ordered. Karis took the corner where the lamp was. Ephram sat at the other end, where he could tell the room about the day's road whether it wanted him to or not. Cael took the wall seat, where he could see the door.

Seln came in last, with the pouch, as he had come in last every evening of the sitting.

He did not go to the end of the board, where the margin was. He did not go to the corner by the door, or stand at the counter with the keeper, or take the stool by the fire where he had sat in other waystations with the pouch on his knee. He came down the room and sat on the bench, in the middle of it, between Lira's boots and Karis's lamp, at the table.

He reached for the bread.

Lira swung her boots down off the bench and pushed the crock along to him without looking, as if he had always sat there. As far as the table was concerned, he always had.

Nobody said a word about it, then or at any board between that one and the bluff, and nobody ever would; but every night, at every board, every one of them counted him. They did it the way you count heads at the door at the end of a long day, to be sure that everybody you care about has come in.

The manifest for the road had been written out the evening before the closing, and Seln had written it himself, as he had written the roster for the road down. Cael saw it on the third night, when Seln left it open on the board to check a cartage figure. Eleven names. Against his own, in his own small upright hand, Seln had put *records and floor scheduling*. It was the line the board in the covered walk had carried on the first morning of the qualifying year, to the letter. Nobody had asked him to change it. It was still true. It had been true on the first morning too, and complete in form, and it had meant almost nothing. Now it meant a seat on a bench, and not one letter of it had moved.

*A line on a form says what a man is listed as,* he wrote in the old volume that night, very small, because the old volume had only a few leaves left. *It has no way to notice when he's become something else underneath it.*

On the fifth night Ephram told the room about the lock-keeper, at great length and with a number of improvements, because the keeper of that waystation had never heard it and Ephram did not believe in wasting a fresh audience on a short version. He got to the part where he had been taught the night-watch song in the lock-house over tea, and said that there were forty verses and he had learned twelve.

"Eleven," said Seln, from along the bench, without looking up from the pouch.

Ephram opened his mouth, and shut it, and looked round the board for help. Everybody was very busy with their bread. After a while he said, "Eleven," in a thoughtful voice, and went on with the story from there.

---

Somewhere to the south and west, at about that time, in a room no window had ever been cut into, a question was put.

What is known of that sitting comes from a few lines of its minute and from one participant's account, given late and to one person. It is not much. The officials who sat in it were senior and, by the custom of such rooms, without names; that was the point of them. A clerk read the question into the record. He had been chosen to read it because his voice had nothing in it.

*Whether the classification [SHATTERED] shall be designated an active threat category.*

The tournament's record was laid before the room. Beside it lay the west tower's entry from Norhold, the same eleven words, which had come the whole way down the post road for this. Then the officer of record was called. He had come in person. He gave his evidence standing, and when it had been written down he signed every line of it with his whole name.

"You are proposing to classify a boy as a threat category because your instruments cannot classify him as anything else. I have evaluated the instruments. The finding is theirs, not his."

They thanked him. His evidence went into the minute. Nobody replied to it. The chair moved to the next item.

Further down the table somebody asked that the file's standing designation be read in. Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4. By itself, the speaker said, it gave all the warrant the room needed to take the matter out of ordinary hands. The minute then asked, for completeness, which office had authorized that designation.

Nobody in the room could say.

A young man from the secretariat answered, after some looking at papers. The office that had tried to follow the designation home had followed it into the registry, he said, to a sub-layer under the registry's own floor, and there lost it; there was no instrument of record behind it, and the attempt had been closed. The room made the small pause that rooms of that height make, and a line went into the minute, and the sitting went on. The designation was useful, and at that height useful things are not asked where they came from. It had sat in the file since the first minutes of his Kindling, and it sat there still: a flag no living hand had authorized.

Nobody called for a vote. The question was *entered for formal proceedings*. In such rooms the words mean the answer has been settled and only the day has not. The officials went back to offices whose names are not written down. The clerk gathered the minute. Somebody put out the lamps.

Then the room was empty, and the file lay where the clerk had squared it, thicker by one entry with no number in it. Nothing had been decided. Everything had been entered.

---

The road took the tournament off them a little at a time, the way a long walk takes a fever out of a man.

For the first days it was still all round them. Waystations had silver-on-grey hung at their gates, and the innkeepers came to the doors to look at the plain colours go by. Twice a rider overtook the wagons with a fresh broadside from the city, and Ephram bought both and read them out on the tailboard in what was left of his voice. The guesses had bred. There were a dozen of them now. The one word was in none.

Cael read the road by its doors. Every waystation had a board beside the door, and at the first few the board still had the finals card chalked on it, every result, as if the innkeeper could not bear to rub it off. A few days on, the boards were selling hay and oats by the bushel. A few days after that a board had nothing on it but nail-holes, and the woman who kept the place asked Gault, quite kindly, what had brought two wagons so far north this early. Gault told her. She thought about it, and said she had heard there had been something on, at the coast, and wasn't that nice for them, and went to see about the soup.

The old volume ran out a leaf at a time as the road did. He gave it a few lines a night, small, and most nights they said the same things. *The Wind at three on yard clay. Came when called, twice of three. Yellow creeping in at the edges of the left side. The flag stands.* Each night the things he carried had shifted a little further toward the new weight, and each night they had not finished. The healers had said a month. His own reckoning said more. The notice's warning sat at the back of his eyes where he had first read it, and did not move.

On the ninth day they reached the district seat.

The column stopped in the square outside the registry while the reserves watered the horses, and Brom got down and went in by himself. He was inside a long while. When he came out he had a folded paper in his hand. He did not look at any of them. He sat down on the wagon's tailboard beside Karis and turned it over in his fingers, the plain side up, and then he said it to the cobbles in front of his boots, all at once, before anybody could ask.

"Mandatory advancement review. Any continental title brings one. A panel, in that building, before ninety days are out. They'll look me over for Iron."

Karis's pen came up. She did not write anything. She waited.

"It isn't a promotion," said Brom. "It's a hearing about whether I get offered one." Then he slid the paper inside his coat, into the pocket where the letter from Velmere was, and pressed the coat down over both. "I know Copper. Every seam of it. I can walk it in the dark. Iron I've only ever watched from the side."

"Ninety days," said Lira, from the wagon bed. "You could map a lot of floor in ninety days."

"I'd have ninety days to decide whether I want one drawn."

Nobody pushed him. Nobody on that wagon had ever needed to push Brom toward anything; he got there, or he didn't, by his own road. Karis capped her pen without having written a word, which Cael had never once seen her do. Brom's coat carried the matter the rest of the way home, buttoned over the summons and the Velmere letter together. Nobody saw either come out again. The year had nothing more to say about it than that.

On the eleventh day, at a waystation where the coast road came in from the south, the coach from the coast pulled into the yard with its horses steaming while they were eating, and stood there blowing while the coachman got down. The coachman put his head in at the door and asked if there was anybody here from the bluff, and held up a letter. Cael knew the hand from across the room.

It was dated four days before. Hesk had not had his six pages. He could not have; they were still somewhere on the hill road, in Bracken's pouch. He had written off the broadsides, which reached the coast faster than any pouch. Then he had worked out which road the wagons would take, and which crossroads they would come to on which day, and sent the letter to meet them there. He had been right about all of it. Hesk's answers had always set out before the questions reached him.

Cael read it at the board with his stew going cold.

*Third on the continent, and the judges wrote "no number goes here." Your grandmother would have had that framed. Seventeen, unmeasured, and true. Come home when the road allows. — H.*

He read it again. Then he slid it in behind the old volume's front board, with the other letters that lived there, and ate the stew cold, and did not mind.

On the twelfth day the watchers came back.

There were two of them, at the far end of a bridge in a market town, standing by a hired horse where the road came off the bridge into the square. Grey coats and good boots. Cael knew both faces. He had counted them for two years from the bluff's walls and windows and the ferry steps. He counted them now from the wagon's bench, and was surprised to find that he was very nearly glad of them. After a city of eight thousand who had all been looking at him at once, two men and a horse were almost homely.

It went in that night next to the warning, because that was where it belonged.

*Two of them, at a bridge. The old arrangement, two and a horse. After Norhold it looks small, and I find I'm fond of it. But Vastin spent an hour telling me the old way is finishing. So: noted, and kept beside his hour. They may be the last watchers I get whose names I could learn.*

On the fourteenth day Karis closed the year's papers.

She did it at a waystation table after supper, and the whole ceremony of it was that she would not have one. She took out everything the year had made, from the cases it had ridden in: the clause and the four pages, the qualifying letters, the circular, the rulings, the trial rules, the nine charts on the backs of old seating plans, the entry. She sorted it all into two piles on the board. She tied each with tape and wrote a contents leaf for each and put the leaf on top, and the bundles went back into the case.

"Two hundred and six sheets," she said, and shut the lid. "Nine of them matter. I've no idea yet which nine. I won't have for a good while. So they all go home."

Ephram spent the last three days working out how to tell it on the bluff.

He tried his versions on the wagon, on the reserves and the horses and anybody who would sit within reach. There was a long one with the trial in it. There was a short one with only the match. There was one that began with the roster and was still in the qualifying year when they reached the next waystation. He threw them all away. Somewhere past the second-to-last ferry he said one more out loud, and stopped, and said it again, and kept it.

"We went down there and they counted us, and the count's on a staff in the middle of the continent where anyone can read it. And one of us they couldn't count." He considered it. "There's a longer version. It comes with my kit, up the bluff, carried by whoever asked for it."

---

The last ferry took them over on the seventeenth day.

On the ferry he wrote in the old volume for the last time, at the foot of its last leaf, until the line ran into the binding and there was no room for a word more. He shut it and put it inside his coat with Hesk's letter. Everything he wrote from now on would go into Hesk's book, and he thought it would be a long while before Hesk's book was full. There had been no letter from Fiske all year. Lira had said she was not counting the days, and had not, and would be in the Silver bracket next cycle whether Fiske watched it or not.

First the river, wide and slow and going copper in the late light; then the roofs of Ostrand, slate and brown, coming up out of the valley along it; and last, above them all, the bluff, with its one long roofline holding the sun after everything below had lost it. The wagons went up the climb with nobody saying much, because it was the last mile of a long year, and every one of them had found that it was enough just to be riding it, together, with the light going off the water behind.

Halfway up, Cael spoke. He had not planned to. It came the way a line comes sometimes at the end of a page, when the page has been working toward it all along without your knowing.

"I have seven things that aren't a Path," he said. He let the wagon roll on a little way. "A match record no instrument can hold, and five people who watched them fail to score me."

He let that sit.

"And somewhere above all of it, a room full of careful people deciding what I am. Again."

"They're behind schedule," said Lira. "You already decided."

"Years ago," said Brom.

Karis, writing: "Noted."

Seln was on the tailboard with the pouch across his knees. He looked up the road toward the gate and said nothing. Cael had learned that this was how he agreed.

*The standard was silver. He wasn't anything the silver could measure. The road home ran through a continent that now knew it — and somewhere behind them, in a room with no windows, the machine began deciding what to do about what it couldn't measure.*

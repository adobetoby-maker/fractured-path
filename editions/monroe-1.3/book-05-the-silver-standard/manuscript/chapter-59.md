# Chapter 59 — Same Build

Vastin went down the tower stair at his ordinary pace and out under the arch.

There was a big young man sitting on the step there with bandaged forearms, watching the court. He looked up as Vastin passed. Vastin knew him from the row: the continental Copper champion, the bluff's wall. He nodded to him, as one nods to a man keeping a door. The young man nodded back, gravely, and went on watching the court.

He crossed it slowly. The copy line still ran along the colonnade, and he walked down the length of it and nobody in it knew him. A woman with a basket of eels. Three boys from some hill house arguing about the fourth exchange with their hands. A grey delegation head in Auremont's blue, standing in his place with his hands folded behind him, who did know him, and inclined his head an inch, and was answered by an inch. At the end of the colonnade, by the north tunnel, there was a frame on a nail with a sheet in it. He stopped in front of it and read the eleven words again, although he had heard them read aloud at dawn and could have said them in his sleep.

He had written a note like that himself, once, on the road home from the bluff. Fewer words, and in a worse light, and signed with his whole name. He had thought at the time that it was the most careful thing he had ever written. He saw now that the west tower had done it better. They had not tried to say what the boy was. They had said only what their instrument could and could not do, and stopped.

He walked on toward the inn under the outer wall.

The summons was in the inside pocket of his coat, where it had been since the post came up the road two days ago. A sitting at the seat, when the houses had dispersed. He was to attend and give evidence as the officer of record. It did not say on what. It did not need to. He would take the post road in the morning. He had already told the inn.

He knew the machine he was going back to. He knew it better than any man alive, he sometimes thought, and without any pride in the thought. He knew which door every letter went through and which clerk's desk it lay on and for how long, and which seal sent it upstairs and which sent it down. He could have drawn the whole house from memory on the back of a form, stair by stair, with the names on every door. And for the first time in forty years he could not have told anybody which way it was going to move. He had drawn it all in his head and there was a room in the drawing he had never seen, and something in that room was deciding things.

He had told the boy that he had stopped meaning to ask who chose which men saw the minutes. That was true. He had not told him why. It was not fear. He had examined it closely on the road down, the way he examined everything, and it was not fear. It was nearer to tact. A guest does not open a door in somebody else's house, however long he has lived there.

That troubled him more than the eight words.

His left hand ached. The afternoon was getting on, and the hand had started early. He put it in his coat pocket, against the summons, and walked on.

He did not yet know what he would say at the sitting. He would know when he was standing in it. That was the only way he had ever known anything worth saying. He knew one thing about it already, and it was enough to be going on with. Whatever he said, he would sign every word of it with his whole name.

---

Cael went down to the rail over the floor at nightfall, alone.

The stewards at the north tunnel let him through without a word, as they had let him through every evening since somewhere in the second week, when they had decided that the boy who walked the empty floors at night was part of the building. He went up into the dark of the lower tiers and along to the rail above the north break, where the five of them had stood the day before. He put both arms on it and looked down.

The ring was still there. It would come up tonight; the crews were already gathering at the tunnel's mouth with their lamps and barrows and long bars, and somebody was laughing. But for the moment it stood as it had stood all day, in the last of the light from the high windows. There was the barrier with its smooth cap, and the four masts with their iron crowns, and the trench grating at the foot of the barrier all round. In the middle was the oak.

He found the mark without looking for it.

It lay along the foot of the barrier, west of the north break, a few strides long. Somebody's broom had been over it and had not been able to lift it. It was a pale line on the grey stone, like the mark a pan leaves on a table, and at its far end there was a scorch the size of a hand where his little fire had cracked. It was the only thing on the floor to show what the city had argued about all day, five different ways, and been wrong every time. The crews would have it up with the stone before morning. Nobody would think to keep it.

He stood and looked at it until the light went off it.

Under the strapping the left side kept up a low steady complaint, like a dog that has decided to go on grumbling all night. The rest of him was not settled at all. All day he had felt the things he carried moving about in him, slowly, the way men move furniture in a house where a great heavy chest has just been carried in. Every one of them leaned a little toward the new weight, and none of them had found its place. Twice on the walk down he had reached for the Wind and got something else. He thought it would be a long while before he could take a step without thinking about it. He had written that in the old volume already, and the healers' account of it, and his own, which was more honest.

He took out Hesk's book and opened it on the rail. There was just enough light. He wrote standing up.

*They didn't score me low. They didn't score me high. They couldn't score me. I have spent three years telling myself I can beat anyone at my tier. Tonight the fairest instrument ever built agreed with the Arbiter, the brackets, and the registry: I am not at any tier.*

He looked at it for a while. The crews had come out onto the floor below him with the lamps, and one of them knelt at the foot of the north mast and began to loosen its cable.

*I keep waiting for that sentence to feel like a wound. It feels like the truth arriving.*

---

The long table had all of them that night. Gault, and Rooke, and Bracken at the end with the pouch, and Withrow at the head with her cup. The two reserves were wide-eyed at the bottom of it. Seln sat at the far corner, on the margin, where he had sat all sitting. Cael told them the hour.

He gave it to them in two piles, as Karis had taught him to give anything that mattered. First what had been said, word for word, as near as he could get it. Then, separately, and saying so, what he had made of it. The telling ran longer than the hour. Nobody interrupted. Karis wrote it all down without looking up once.

When he had finished, Lira was first, because somebody had to argue against and she could do it better than anybody.

"Or," she said, "it's the cleverest thing they've done all year." She put her elbows on the table. "They can't order you off a floor. There's no rule for it. Karis would tear it to pieces in a week. So they send up the one honest man they've got, and he sits you down in a room full of clocks and asks you very kindly to walk off the floor yourself. That's what they want. They can't make you. He can ask." She looked round the table. "Honest men get sent to do that sort of thing. That's what honest men are for, to people like that."

"As a report it's thin," said Ephram. He had almost no voice left, and he used what there was carefully. "One source. No way to check him. And his own house gains if you do what he says." He shrugged. "If I'd brought that to Rooke before a trial, he'd have sent me back out for another."

"So don't grade it as a report," said Brom.

They looked at him. Brom argued perhaps twice in a sitting, and they had all learned to listen when he did.

"Grade the man." He turned his cup round in his bandaged hands. "He came all that way himself, by the post road, with one bag. He sat in that row the whole afternoon. Then he sat all night on a stool by a door in a room with nothing in it for him, and wrote nothing down. You don't spend a night like that on a thing you're only managing." He put the cup down. "That's all."

Karis lifted her pen off the page.

"Then grade it both ways," she said, "and keep both. As a report it's poor. As a man he's good. One doesn't cancel the other. What it means is that the warning is true and we can't prove it, both at once." She looked at the page. "That's the worst kind of warning there is. It's also the only kind anybody ever gets in time to use."

There was a little quiet. Then Seln spoke from his corner, in his counter voice, without looking up from the pouch he had open in his lap.

"Wardens threaten. Politicians hint. That was neither." The pouch's strap went through its buckle under his fingers. "That was an honest man spending career capital to tell you the truth a few months early. Treat the warning as real, because it is."

Nobody argued with that. At that table, nobody arguing was not the same as agreeing because they had to. It meant there was nothing left to say.

Lira let out a breath.

"All right. Strike mine," she said. "I might still be right. It doesn't matter." She took the pot and began to pour, Brom first, as always, and then round to the left. "Say he's honest and on his own. What do we do? We build. Say he's honest and somebody's sent him on an errand. What do we do then?" She filled Brom's cup. "Same build."

"That's the finding," said Karis, and drew a box round it, which was rarer with her than a smile.

Lira filled his cup last.

"Fifty-one minutes," she said, so that only he heard. "That's what mine took. At the station, with the Arbiter." She put the pot down. "Make of that what you like."

The wider table broke up a little after the tenth bell. Rooke and Gault went up the stair arguing about the best week of the year to start a cohort on stone. Bracken took the pouch. Withrow went last. She stood at the door for a moment looking back at the table, as if she were counting something, and then she said goodnight to all of them in one word and was gone.

Then there were four, in the back room, with the door shut and the window open on the courtyard pump.

They had one thing left to do, and Karis had brought the paper for it.

"The minute from the bluff held," she said. "I want that said first, before anything else, because it's true. Nothing was made. She began it. You were awake and you knew what you were doing." She laid the sheet on the rocking table. "And the third line held too, as far as it went. The cost was on paper. Every cost we had ever seen was on paper." She looked at him. "The trouble is we had never seen one at that weight. We had it written down at the weight of a stair, and a yard, and a copper pot. Not at the weight of a Gold, in the middle of a bout, in front of a city."

"It could have gone worse," said Lira quietly. "If she'd been anybody else."

"She wasn't," said Brom.

"No," said Karis. "A rule that only holds when the fighter across from you is kind isn't a rule. It's luck." She took up the pen. "So we put a line in. Not a new rule. The old one, with one more word in it."

She wrote it while they watched, slowly, in the hand she kept for papers that strangers would read long afterwards, and she wrote it twice, a draft and then a fair copy, with nothing crossed out on the second. Then she turned the fair copy round and pushed it across the table to him, and he copied it into Hesk's book under the minute from two nights before, in his best hand.

*The conditions may complete themselves anywhere; the deciding may not. The line holds: directed, open-eyed — and from tonight, costed. Never again in the middle of a fight unless what follows has been priced first, at the weight it will come at. Yesterday the bill fell due with the one person on the floor who could have made it worse choosing not to. We don't get to plan on that twice.*

He blotted it and held the book out.

Karis initialled it first, small, at the right of the last line. Brom put his under hers, large and careful. Lira signed hers with a flourish she had certainly never used on any official paper. Cael put his last. Four sets of initials on a page of a book bound by an instrument-maker, under a rule that no registry would ever see.

"There," said Karis. "Now it's the house's."

They went up one at a time, Karis with her papers and Brom with his cup, and Lira last, and Lira stopped on the stair, and he thought for a moment that she would say something, and she did not. She only knocked twice on the doorframe as she passed, as she had knocked on his head on the night of the party, and went on up.

He sat on alone with the window open, and wrote the night's last entry, slowly, while the city put itself to bed below.

*First honest warning I've ever gotten from inside the machine. He built no case, made no ask, took no note. He just told me the weather. The Compact's best evaluator thinks the next thing that comes for me won't wear a uniform or file a challenge. Write it down. Believe it. Keep competing anyway — but from tonight, we build like people who believe it.*

---

The closing began at the second bell of the morning, and the Concourse filled for it one last time.

It was the oldest thing the tournament did, older than the brackets and older than the Standard, and it was done in the charter's order, which was backward. The presiding official stood at a lectern at the south end of the floor, where the ring had been. The ring was gone. The crews had worked all night, and the stone of the main floor lay grey and plain from barrier to barrier with the oak in the middle of it, as if nothing had ever stood there. Along the north side of the floor stood fourteen bare staffs in a row, one for every house, and at the floor's head, under the champion's gate, three taller ones.

The official began at the bottom. "Fourteenth," he read, and named the house, and its people came out from the tunnel to the mark on the floor in front of him and stood there in their colours while a steward fixed their house's standard to its staff and ran it up.

Then the bowl applauded them.

It did not applaud as it liked. It applauded as the charter said. A steward at the foot of the lectern lifted a white glove, and the bowl began to clap, and when the glove came down it stopped. Cael counted, because he counted everything. Eight. The steward's glove went up and the bowl clapped eight, and the glove came down. Thirteenth. Eight. Twelfth. Eight.

It took nearly an hour to reach the top four. Nobody in the bowl was bored by it. Every house was named in full. Every house stood on the mark under its own colours, and heard the same eight, and walked back. No house was made bigger than its place, and none smaller. It was the fairest thing Cael had seen the tournament do. The fairness went all the way down to the clapping.

"Fourth," read the official. "The Rhagen Institute."

They came out in black and glass, in perfect step, and stood. Cael watched their captain. He was the man who had said that Rhagen could not find the edges of him, a lifetime ago in the second week. He stood in front of his people with his hands at his sides. Their colours went up a staff in the common row, one place short of the honour staffs at the floor's head, by a trial lost on a hill. He did not look up at them. He looked at the floor in front of his feet, as a man looks at a sum he has checked twice and found correct and does not like. Then, as the eight began, he turned his head and found Halcenvane's block in the tunnel mouth, and nodded once, as he had nodded on the crown at the end of his own bout.

"Third. Halcenvane Academy."

They walked out onto the floor of the Concourse in their plain colours, all of them, the five who had fought and Rooke and Gault and Withrow and the two reserves. Seln walked last. Nobody had told him to come out and nobody had told him not to. They stood on the mark.

The steward took the bluff's standard, which was plain and not new and had been mended once at the hoist by somebody's careful needle a long time ago, and carried it past the whole row of fourteen staffs to the head of the floor. He fixed it to the first of the three tall staffs there, the honour staffs. Then he ran it up.

Cael was at the end of the line, where the marshals' list had put him on the first day. He did not look up at the cloth. He looked along the line instead.

Gault had taken the flat box out of his inside pocket, the one with the old blue ribbon in it, and was holding it against his chest with both hands without opening it. Ephram's lips were moving; he was saying the house's name under his breath, over and over, as if he were learning it. Brom put two fingers up to the ribbon at his collar, once, and took them down. Rooke did not look at the standard at all; he was watching the bowl, the way he watched a crowd at a rail, as if to see who had come. Seln stood at the very end, a step behind Cael, looking at nothing, with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting at a counter. And Withrow did not look up either. She looked along the line of them from the near end to the far, one face at a time, and when she came to Cael she stopped, and stayed a moment, and went back the way she had come.

Lira was the only one of them who looked at the cloth. She looked at it with her chin up and her eyes too bright, and said, out loud, so that the whole line heard:

"Eighteen years they didn't come." She did not look away from it. "There's a table-keeper at Fenmark who wrote me off at fourteen. I want him told, in his own ink, what the scrapyard placed."

Karis, beside her, did not write it down. She reached across without looking and took hold of Lira's sleeve, and kept hold of it.

The eight was longer than eight, because when the steward's glove went up and came down the bowl did not stop, and went on, and the sound of it rolled round the tiers and came back off the far side of the oval like surf. He lifted it again, quite gently, and it did not stop. He looked at the presiding official, and the official, after a moment, looked down at his lectern and turned a page that did not need turning, and waited.

Then the banner.

It came down from the champion's gate for the last time in the cycle. It was carried on its staff by the tournament's own colour-guard, two of them, one at either side, as it had been carried in alone on the first day with no house behind it. Everyone in the bowl stood up. They brought it to the oak at the middle of the floor and stood there with it, level, neither raised nor dipped.

The transfer was the oldest procedure in the charter. Cael watched every step of it, because it was an instrument being handed from one keeper to the next, and he had never seen it done.

The presiding official came down from his lectern. Four ties of grey cord held the banner's sleeve to the staff. The charter named the order they were to be loosed in. Bracken had told them at supper that a herald had once been fined for doing it the other way round. He undid the two middle ties first, the upper and then the lower. Then the tie at the head. Then the tie at the foot, last, so that the cloth came away from the staff all at once into the colour-guards' hands and did not touch the floor. The two of them laid it in four, corner to corner, without a crease out of true. It went into a case of plain pine that was very much newer than what was put in it.

Then two registrars sat down at a small table that had been carried out onto the stone, and signed the transfer, one after the other, in ink. Eight thousand people stood on their feet in silence and watched two clerks write their names.

Cael thought he had never seen anything more like the bluff in his life.

At Auremont's place on the floor the pine case was unfastened a second time. Auremont's own people sleeved the banner to Auremont's staff, with the same four ties in the opposite order, and raised it. Then the house's procession took it round the floor once, close under the barrier, slowly, so that every person in the bowl could see it go by at the height of their eyes. Silver on grey. The mark's own flag.

---

Daeva walked at the head of her house's file, two strides behind the banner, where the marshals had put her.

She had walked behind it on the first day, from the tunnel to the gate, and she had felt nothing in particular about it then. It was a flag. Her house had won it in other cycles before she was born and would win it in others after. Her name was on the reason it had come back this cycle, people said, and she supposed that was true. She did not feel it as hers.

She watched the plain colours come up on her left as the procession turned at the north end of the floor.

They were standing in the tunnel mouth under their honour staff, all of them in a line. The big one with the ribbon. The Ember girl with her notebook under her arm. The Wind girl, with her arm held close at her side and her chin up. And at the end of the line the boy, with his left shoulder held a little stiff under his coat, watching the banner go by as she had seen him watch everything since the procession, as though he were reading it.

She had not planned anything. She noticed, afterwards, that she had not planned anything at all. Only once before had that happened to her: at a desk, before dawn, with a filing form.

She stepped out of the file.

It was four steps to the barrier where they stood. The colour-guard's sergeant saw her go and opened his mouth. The banner went on without her. The near tiers saw and made a sound like a wave turning over. She crossed the four steps and stopped in front of the boy, with the barrier's cap between them, and the shadow of the banner sliding away along the stone behind her.

"Rematch," she said.

It was not a challenge. A challenge was a form you filed. It was more like what two craftsmen say to each other at the end of a fair, when one of them names a town and a season and the other nods: an appointment, said once, to be kept. He looked at her and nodded.

She was back in the file before the sergeant had decided whether anything had happened. Four seconds. One of the scribes timed it, from habit. She walked on behind the banner all the way round to the champion's gate, and saw it go up, and did not once look back.

The procession marshal's office wrote her up for it that afternoon. The citation came to her lead instructor in the evening, folded and sealed, with the office's ribbon on it, for the program's file. He brought it up to her room himself and held it out, looking at it rather than at her, with his head on one side.

"You'll want to read it," he said. "For the form."

"Thank you."

She took it. When he had gone she turned it over once in her hands. Then she opened the lining of her travelling case and put it in, still sealed, beside the old exercise sheet from the coast and the marbled notebook, and closed the lining over it. She never did open it. She found, years afterward, that she had forgotten it was there.

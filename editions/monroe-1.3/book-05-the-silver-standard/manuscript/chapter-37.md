# Chapter 37 — The Book

The broadside came up the stair with the bread in the morning, and Ephram read it aloud to the table, standing, in the voice of a town crier with a cold.

The Iron semifinal had the front column.

Somebody had set the four exchanges across the top in large figures, *nought to three, two to one, one to two, one to three*, and drawn a heavy black ring round the second pair, like a feast day in an almanac. Underneath, a writer who signed himself only with a printer's mark had spent most of a column on the second exchange, and a good deal of it was wrong about what had happened, and all of it was right about what it had meant.

Ephram saved the last line for the end, and read it slowly.

"*Auremont went home with the bout. The bluff went home with the first thing anybody has taken off Auremont all week, and there is no column in the standings to put it in.*"

He sat down. "There," he said. "Now you're literature."

"I'm a column nobody can put things in," said Lira, through her bread. She had the heat-wrap on under her shirt and her left arm resting on the table in front of her like a parcel somebody had asked her to mind. "That's worse."

Brom ate his breakfast in silence. He ate all of it, and then he ate the end of the loaf, which nobody contested, and then he sat with his hands on the table on either side of his plate and looked at nothing.

"He'll find every gap," he said at last, to the table in general.

"Rooke said," said Karis.

"I know. I've been thinking about the second half." He looked round at them. "Plan to have fewer. That's not plan to have none. You can't have none. Nobody can. Rooke knows that better than anybody." He stood up and began to clear his own plate, which he never did. "So the question isn't how many I've got. It's which ones he gets to keep."

Rooke, at the head of the table, did not look up from his cup. But Cael saw the corner of his mouth move, very slightly, in the way it moved perhaps twice a year.

On the way out of the dining room Brom caught Cael by the sleeve and drew him aside into the passage by the kitchen door, where nobody was.

"Tell me what you know," he said. "My gaps. All of them. The ones you've charted on the oak."

Cael told him. He did not need the notebook. There was the half-beat after a drive, when the weight had to come back. There was the very start of the left turn, before the guard changed hands. There was the top of the right shoulder, where the coverage went over from arm to back. And there was the way Brom's back heel settled when he loaded a drive, on any floor that gave at all.

"Four," said Brom.

"Four I've seen."

"He'll find more." Brom nodded slowly, like a man hearing the price he had expected. "Good. Four I can charge for. The rest I'll leave to him." He let go of the sleeve. "Don't tell Rooke I asked. He'll think I'm nervous."

"Are you?"

Brom considered it honestly, as he considered everything. "No," he said. "I'm curious. It's worse."

*Brom's final is today,* Cael wrote on the stair afterward, waiting for the others. *All week he's said he wants to know what Marek finds. This morning he said something else. He said the question was which gaps Marek gets to keep. I don't think he meant it as a riddle. I think he's already decided.*

---

The west tower had given the Copper final the main floor.

There had been talk about it in the court all week, and Seln had read the schedule's note out at supper without any comment at all. Copper finals did not get the main floor. They got the third floor or the fourth, and a gallery of coaches and parents and fair-day men with their arms folded. This one got the great grey oval and its four banks of tiers. The tiers were not full; they would not be full until the Gold. But they were fuller than Cael had seen them for anything but the opening, and the people in them had the look of people who had come on purpose.

"Somebody in that tower knows good work when it's coming," said Rooke.

The officials had marked out a square on the grey stone, sixty feet on a side, with white rope on iron stands. At the very centre of it, inside the ring of plugged sockets where the exhibition ring would one day stand, there was a round of old dark oak let into the stone. It was perhaps eight feet across. Karis said it was the oldest piece of floor in the building, older than the dais and older than the tiers, the place where the flags had been planted before anybody had thought to build a hall round them. Every cycle the stone round it was patched, and every cycle the oak was left alone.

So the final had two floors in it.

There was the stone, fast and hard and a little slick under soft shoes, and there was the oak, which gripped and gave. Between them ran a seam in a perfect ring where the one met the other, with a lip on it no higher than a fingernail.

During the formalities, while the clerk read out the names, Marek walked the seam.

He did it slowly and without any show, heel to toe, once round the whole ring, looking down at his feet as a man looks at a page he means to learn. Then he went back to his chalk on the north side.

Brom watched him do it. When the referee called them to their marks, Brom did not go to his chalk. He went to the oak. He set himself just inside it on the south side, with his weight in the middle of the old dark boards and the seam half a stride behind his heels, and stood there.

A low sound went round the craft-readers in the tiers. Cael heard it from three directions at once.

"What's he doing?" said Ephram.

"Making him change floors," said Lira. "Every way in to Brom crosses the seam. Stone to oak. Fast to slow. Every single one." She shifted her bad shoulder under the wrap. "He's charging at the door before the bout's started."

Across the floor, Marek looked at where Brom was standing, and then down at the seam in front of him, and then at Brom again. Cael was watching his face. He saw nothing on it at all, except that the man had read something, and filed it, and gone on to the next line.

High on the east side, over the far rail, the Compact's row was full except for one chair. Ilsev sat in the first seat with her lap-desk on her knees. Havel was in the second. At the end of the row, behind the grey rail-cloth, the twelfth chair stood empty, with its brushed seat and its pale square where a plate should have been.

The flags went up.

---

Marek went to the old seam first, and he went to it only once.

Cael had expected it.

Every man since the slate country had come at Brom's left side with the dossier in his head, and Marek owned the dossier the same as everybody else. He came across the seam fast, changed floors without a hitch, and went in low on the left, at exactly the angle page three described.

The mend held.

Brom took the touch on his forearm, let it run in and stop, and drove through it with the other hand before the absorption was even finished, as Rooke's nineteen exercises had taught him to do. The flag went up for Halcenvane.

One to nothing.

Marek stepped back over the seam onto the stone. He did not look at the left side again for the rest of the morning. One contact had told him everything the dossier was worth, and he had closed that page and put it away, and Cael saw the craft-readers in the tiers see it too. In the space of a single touch, the old edition of Brom's file had stopped being worth the paper.

Then Marek began to read.

It was the doctrine at its full depth, and Cael had never seen anything like it from the rail. Marek did not attack. He surveyed. He came in light and quick and often, at a rate that looked busy from the tiers and cost him almost nothing, and every contact went somewhere different, and every contact was a question.

The first went to the top of Brom's right shoulder, where the coverage of an Iron Skin changed over from the arm to the back. It was very light. The flags did not move. Brom's guard came across a fraction late, and Marek was already gone.

The second went in the half-beat after one of Brom's drives, when the weight he had thrown forward had to come back before he could throw it again. Cael had charted that gap on the oak at home through a whole year of sessions, and he had never once seen an opponent find it live. Marek found it on the second time of asking. The flag went up for Rhagen.

One to one.

The third went to Brom's pivot.

When Brom turned, there was a quarter-beat when his weight was committed to the turn and could not be taken back. Cael had not known it was there. He saw Marek find it, and touch it, and saw the flag go up, and wrote it down with a feeling like missing a step on a stair he had climbed a thousand times.

One to two.

The fourth was light again, unflagged. It went to the very start of Brom's left turn, a hair before the guard changed hands, where the door was still a wall but was about to begin becoming a door. Marek did not try to go through. He only touched it, to show himself where it was.

And the fifth went to a gap Cael had charted only on the oak at home, where the whole floor gave, and had never thought to look for here.

Brom had chosen the oak.

He had planted inside the seam so that every way in to him crossed from stone to wood. But to drive, Brom had to shift his weight back first, onto his rear foot, and his rear foot sat half a pace from the seam on a board that gave very slightly under it. When the weight went back, the board gave, and for a breath Brom's back heel was standing on something soft.

Marek found it.

He found the gap in the ground Brom had chosen himself, the gap in the very position that was meant to cost him. He went in on the stone side of the seam, low and fast, at the instant the board gave under Brom's heel, and touched him on the ribs.

One to three, closed early.

The second floor's whole gallery had stood up for Lira yesterday. The main floor did not stand. It went quiet instead, every bank of it, the quiet of a great many people who had just watched the best defensive fighter in the bracket taken apart in a single exchange in front of them, every piece of him labelled and filed.

Brom walked to his mark without any hurry and stood there.

Cael watched him and could not believe how calm he was. His forearms had taken every contact of that exchange and they would be colouring already under the wraps. But he stood easy, and his eyes were not on the floor and not on the rail. They were on Marek, and they were following where Marek looked.

*Brom spent the first exchange watching where Marek looked,* Cael wrote afterward. *Not the touches. Where the touches went. Five stakes in the ground, and he knows where every one of them is now, because he watched every one go in. So now he knows what Marek's going to build on. Marek read him, and he read the reading, and only Brom knows there were two surveys on that floor.*

When the bell went again, Brom left every one of his gaps exactly where Marek had found it, and put a price on each.

Cael saw it from the first contact, because he knew what to look for. All five of Marek's stakes were still there. Brom had not closed one of them. The half-beat after his drive was still open. The quarter-beat on his pivot was still open. The board under his back heel still gave. But every one of them now had a price on the road in, and the price was paid in Brom.

Marek went to the half-beat after the drive. He found it as he had found it before, and scored, and the flag went up. And as he came out of it, Brom's other forearm was already waiting at the one angle the exit allowed, loaded with everything an Iron Skin could put behind a forearm, and Marek came out of his touch into it. The return was heavier than the touch. Much heavier. The flag went up for Halcenvane.

Nought to one. One to one.

"Toll," said Karis, very softly.

Marek went to the pivot. Brom let him have it. The flag went up for Rhagen, and as Marek stepped clear, Brom's weight came off the turn at him like a door slammed by the wind, and he took the return on the shoulder and the flag went up again for Halcenvane.

Two to two.

Marek knew.

Cael could see that he knew. Every gap he had staked in the first exchange was open, and every gap was going to cost him, and the doctrine said a confirmed gap was the whole fight. *Every structure is its gaps.* He had found them. He had confirmed them. The book told him to take them, and he was the best reader his house had produced in a generation, and the best reader believes the book.

He went to the board under Brom's back heel.

He went in from the stone side, low, at the instant the board gave, as he had at the end of the first exchange. This time Brom's back heel was not on the board. Brom had moved it, by a hand's breadth, onto the oak's harder grain beside it, and had let the board go on looking soft. Marek arrived at a gap that had a price on it and no gap behind the price, and Brom met him there.

Three to two, closed early. Four to five on the bout.

From the tiers it looked as though Brom had simply outscored him. A big man had stood in the middle of the floor and hit harder than a small man, and won an exchange, and the people who did not read craft cheered it as they would have cheered a wrestler at a fair. The people who did read craft were quiet. Rooke kept his own counsel. But Cael, beside him, saw him take his little book out of his pocket and write one word in it and put it away again.

"He's buying them all," said Lira. Her voice was low and fierce. "He knows they're dear and he's buying every one."

"He has to," said Karis. "His book says they're the fight. It doesn't say what to do when the fight sends you a bill."

Brom walked his slow circle in the interval. He held his forearms a little away from his body as he walked, and Cael could guess why. He had taken every contact of the second exchange on them as well, and each had been a strike he had chosen to take so that he could charge for it, and the bruising that redirect work always left behind would be going deep now, under the skin, into the muscle.

He did not look at the rail. He looked at Marek.

---

Marek changed his approach twice in the third exchange. Either change would have won him most bouts at any tier, and Cael thought both were the best work he had seen anybody do at Copper all year.

First he changed his rhythm.

He stopped arriving on the beat Brom had priced and began arriving round it, early and then late, so that the tolls Brom had set were set for a man who was no longer coming at that moment. It was beautiful. It worked once. Marek slipped a touch in between two of Brom's prices, off the beat, and the flag went up for Rhagen.

Nought to one.

Then Brom re-priced.

It took him two contacts. He did not try to follow Marek's new rhythm. He stopped pricing the moments and priced the angles instead, so that it did not matter when Marek came, only where, and on the second contact Marek came out of his touch into a forearm that had been waiting for him at the only exit the angle allowed.

One to one.

So Marek did a brave thing. He put the five stakes aside, every one of them, and went surveying again.

It was the right decision and it was a terrible one.

He went looking for new structure, fresh gaps nobody had priced, in light, quick, unflagged contacts all over Brom's body. But every question asked of a wall that hits back has to be paid for, and Brom was hitting back. Every light contact Marek made bought a heavy one in return. And the heavy ones had been adding up since the first exchange. Cael could see it in him now. He was slower by a hair. His reset came a shade late. His step away was half a step short. All the precision of him, the whole fine instrument, was running now against a cost that rose with every contact, and the cost was beginning to show.

Brom's return caught him on the shoulder as he came out of a survey. Two to one.

And then Marek found something.

Cael saw it happen and did not understand it until afterward. It was a sixth gap, somewhere in the way Brom breathed, a moment at the end of an exhale when his whole frame softened for a breath before it set again. Marek touched it, lightly. It was real. Cael could tell from the way Brom's eyes changed that it was real and that nobody had ever found it before.

Brom's answer to that touch was the hardest thing he threw all day. Brom's forearm came across and took him full on the chest as he came out of the touch, and he went back three strides across the seam onto the stone and very nearly sat down. The flags did not go up for that one; it had landed a hair after Marek's own touch and the panel let both lie. But Marek stood on the stone for a moment with his hand on his chest, breathing, and looked at Brom.

And he did not go back to the sixth gap.

He had found it.

It was real. His whole doctrine said to take it. And he stood there and looked at what it had cost, and added that to everything else the morning had cost, and Cael saw him decide that it was not worth the price. It was the first time all tournament Cael had seen Marek decline anything he had found.

He went instead to the oldest gate he had, the half-beat after Brom's drive, the first one he had ever staked. His touch there came a hair late, a glance off the forearm rather than a strike, and no flag rose for it. Brom's return did not glance. It took the third point.

Three to one, closed early. Seven to six on the bout.

Before the fourth there was a short interval, and the main floor hummed through every breath of it.

Cael spent them watching two men stand still.

Marek stood still on his chalk. Cael knew that kind of stillness; it was the stillness of somebody adding up a column and not liking the total. The survey was spent. The gaps were priced. The cost of the whole morning was in his body now where anybody who knew how to look could see it: a slight roll in the shoulder Brom had hit, a breath that ran half a count too long. And Marek did what Cael had seen him do between every exchange of every bout he had charted. He walked his margins. His eyes went to each of Brom's gaps in turn, one after another, checking each was still where he had left it. The half-beat after the drive. The pivot. The board under the heel. The start of the left turn. The breath.

All of them except one.

He did not look at the top of Brom's right shoulder. It was the first gap he had found, in the first exchange, before anything had been priced.

Marek did not check it. It was the one he was sure of.

Brom stood on the oak and watched Marek not look. Then he breathed out, long and slow, as he did on the oak when a heavy weight came up off the floor and settled at the top.

---

High in the west tower, behind the carved stone screen of the fourth floor's long room, Chief Adjudicator Umber had left his desk and come to the window for the fourth exchange.

He did not often come.

He had a great deal of paper on his desk, as he had every morning of a cycle, and most of the bouts on the floors below would be rated by his panels and posted on his boards without any need for him to see them. He had asked to be told when the Copper final reached its last exchange. The senior clerk had told him. He had put down his pen and come.

He stood at the screen with his hands behind his back and looked down through the carved stone at the square on the grey floor and the two men in it.

He knew the bout.

He had read the panel's slates for the first three exchanges as they came up, as he read every slate of every final, and he knew what the five had marked. He did not need the slates to know what had happened. He had sat on panels for longer than either of those two young men had been alive, and before that he had fought, a long time ago and not well, and he could read a floor.

What he loved about the floor below him this morning was not the touches. It was the honesty of the work.

The big one was building something.

Umber had seen it in the second exchange, and been sure of it in the third. Every gap the small one found, the big one had left open and set a price on, and the prices were exact, a fair charge for every one. Except one. One price on the board had been a fraction low all morning, the first gap, high on the right shoulder. A careless eye would read that as the big one's mistake. Umber did not have a careless eye. He had spent forty years training it out of himself.

It was not a mistake.

It was a structure. It had been set in the first exchange and kept true through the second and the third, under pressure, while the big man's forearms were being beaten black, and not once had he let the price on that one gate drift up to match the others.

Umber thought it was very beautiful.

He had come to think, over the years, that the mark and the craft needed each other more than either side liked to admit. A mark with nothing honest in front of it was only a ruler lying on a table. And craft with no true mark to answer to was a man telling himself stories in an empty hall. The tables in the strong room were worth their seals because, cycle after cycle, somebody walked out onto a floor like this one and did a thing well enough to be worth measuring exactly.

He did not think of it as a trick, though he supposed the tiers might. A trick was a thing done once to a man who did not know. This was a thing built in plain sight, for three exchanges, by a man who knew that the best reader on the floor was reading every line of it. The small one could have seen it. Everything he needed to see it had been in front of him all morning. That was what made it honest, and what made it craft. The whole of the big man's answer had been laid out on the floor where anybody could read it, if only they had read the price instead of the gap.

Umber had spent his life in the service of a mark that rated what was demonstrated. Not what was meant, or felt, or hoped for, or claimed. What a body did on a floor in front of five judges. He had heard young officials call that a cold rule. It had never seemed cold to him. It seemed to him the only rule under which a man like the big one down there could be paid in full for a thing like this, built over an hour, in silence, with his own arms.

The five below him would rate it as it was. Umber did not doubt that for a moment. He had struck a judge from the lists on the eve of the opening for a quarter of a point of kindness, and the five knew it.

The bell went for the fourth exchange.

He watched the small one test two of the dear gates quickly at the start and pay for both. He watched him stand, a point from the end of everything, and look at the floor, and choose. Umber had seen that look on a great many faces at the end of a great many bouts. It was the look of a fighter who needs one thing to be true and has found the one place on the floor where it still seems to be.

The small one went to the right shoulder, the cheap gate, with everything he had left.

Umber watched it close.

He did not move at the screen. He did not smile. When it was done he stood a moment longer looking down at the oak, and at the two men on it, one standing and one getting up, and then he turned away from the window and went back to his desk and his paper. But the senior clerk, who brought him his tea a little later, noticed that he had not picked up his pen again, and that he was sitting with his hands folded on the blotter, looking at nothing in particular, in the way he did perhaps once a cycle.

---

From the rail, Cael saw it the way the tiers saw it, and then the way only a handful of people in the building could.

Marek tested the timing gate and paid for it: one to nothing. He tested the pivot and paid again: two to nothing. Nine to six on the bout, and Cael saw Marek understand the arithmetic as plainly as if the clerk had shouted it. One more point to Brom, and the exchange would close at three, and the bout would close with it, because no fighter on earth could draw level from four points down with a single exchange left. Marek had to score, and he had to score now.

Every gate on the floor was dear. The morning had made them dear. Patience was dear too, because every breath he waited the cost in his body rose.

Except one.

He went to the right shoulder.

He went in full, at last, his first full commitment of the whole bout, low across the seam from the stone with everything he had left behind it, at the gap he was sure of.

The gap shut on him like the lid of a strongbox.

Everything the morning had paid into Brom's forearms came out of them at once, at the one angle he had kept cheap for exactly this. Marek met it with his whole weight committed and nothing held back, and it lifted him off the stone and laid him down on the oak on his back, flat, with a sound the whole floor heard.

The flag went up. All five of the panel's flags went up, all at once.

"Three to nothing," called the clerk. "Ten to six on the bout."

Brom stood over the oak, breathing.

His forearms hung at his sides, and Cael could see, even from the rail, that they were shaking a little. He rolled his right shoulder once, slowly, the shoulder Marek had touched first and touched last, and stood still.

Marek was on his feet before the flags had come down.

He did not lie there. He rolled up off the oak in one movement and stood at his mark through the clerk's call, frowning slightly down at the place on the floor where he had just been thrown. It was the face of a reader who has come to the last word of a sentence and found that the whole sentence meant something else.

The main floor stood up then.

It took a moment to understand what it had seen, because half of it had seen a big man throw a small one and the other half had seen something much stranger, and the two halves had to find each other. But they did, and they stood, for both of them.

Brom and Marek met at the seam.

Marek said something too quietly for the rail. Brom answered, and Marek stood there a moment, and then said something more, and then held out his hand. Brom took it, and they shook hands properly, as men do at the end of a long piece of business, and walked off in opposite directions.

The figures went up eleven minutes later. Brom twenty-five. Marek twenty-five.

"Level," said Karis, looking at the board. She sounded pleased. "The most honest thing that panel's done all week. They rated the bout. Not the result."

Brom came up the steps. He did not unwrap his hands; he held them a little away from him, palms up, like a man carrying two full bowls. Gault took one look at the forearms and went for cold water and a bucket.

"What did he say?" said Lira.

Brom sat down on the bench behind the rail, heavily, and let Gault take his left arm.

"He asked me when I set the shoulder," he said. "I told him the first exchange. He stood and studied the oak. Then he said, *I read it as your mistake.* And I said I knew. I'd written it to be read that way." He winced as the cold water went on. "And he said he'd never had a page forged on him before. He sounded pleased about it. Then he shook my hand."

He looked down at his forearms, which were already going a deep dull colour from wrist to elbow, both of them, under Gault's careful hands.

"He found six," he said. "I knew about four." He let out a long breath. "That was a proper inspection."

Nobody at the rail said anything for a moment.

Then Ephram said, "Brom. You're continental champion."

Brom looked up at him as though this had not occurred to him.

"Am I," he said. And then, slowly: "So I am."

*I sat at the rail today and thought about my own record,* Cael wrote that night, after the ceremony and the letter and everything else. *Every figure on it is a price I set. Every exhibition I've fought this year, I've fought so that the paper would read a certain way. Brom forged one line, once, in the open, for one bout, and then shook the man's hand and told him exactly what he'd done. Mine is the whole file, every day, and I can't tell anybody.*

*Marek said he'd never had a page forged on him before, and sounded pleased. I don't think anybody who reads my file is ever going to get to say that.*

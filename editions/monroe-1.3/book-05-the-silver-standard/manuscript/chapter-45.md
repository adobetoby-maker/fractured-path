# Chapter 45 — Again

He wrote the day in the old volume first, because the old volume was still the one he lived in.

It was nearly full, and he had known for weeks that it would run out somewhere on the road home, and he had been rationing it, a few lines a day, small. Tonight he gave it four. *Rest day. The blue door, all six. Brom's cake, paid three days early. Seln at the table.* He looked at the last three words for a while, and did not add anything to them, and shut the book.

Then he opened Hesk's.

He had not written in it yet. He had carried it back from the harbour under his arm, the whole length of the quays, and up the stair, and set it on the table under the window, and walked round it twice, and had a wash, and come back, and it was still there. It lay open in the middle by itself, as it had at the cookshop. He turned back to the first leaf. The paper took the lamp's light softly and gave nothing back.

Every new book of accounts begins by saying what is in the till. Hesk had taught him that before he could spell *accounts*.

He wrote slowly, in his best hand, with Hesk's steel pen.

*Seventeen. The first page of Hesk's book, on the rest day in the middle of the sitting. Kept at the blue door by everybody who matters, and written here by me, as I was taught to write down the numbers nobody else was keeping. Somebody kept this one. That's the first thing this book knows.*

*What I carry, at seventeen. Six, and the one I can't account for.*

*Lira's burst. Six free on the sprung oak; four a day on this city's stone. Against Rhagen I ran eleven relocations in one exchange and only two of them were bursts. Feet carried the rest. The landing beat is no shorter than it was at the year's open.*

*The push I took from the Bronze at Ardenmere. Flat on two plates on the bluff and one in a felt bag at the mill town. Four blows a sitting, paid in the right shoulder. Not asked for since the mill town's plate.*

*Brom's read. Weight and direction, early. Against both Silvers it was the one thing I never ran short of. It can't see a weave, and it can't see a price. It sees what's heavy.*

*Reydan's give. Not on a scored floor since Ardenmere. Nobody official has seen it. I held it still at a handspan, twice, in a pocket of stone at the north gate with a Silver's hand coming down. It would have worked. It stays where it is.*

*Karis's spark, by her leave, in her hand. Four instants on a copper pot. Two contacts a bout on a public floor, which is our rule, not theirs. Both spent on Rhagen, both on purpose. Four days of a pink hand.*

*The quiet thing from the stair. A year, near enough. Never used, anywhere, by anyone's count. Rent from waking, spent or not. Drifts in the gaps, never in the work, fewer this month than last. Useful where: nowhere yet. Still the second year of that answer.*

*And at the foot of the list, the still place from session nine. I turned it over again tonight, as I do at every opening and every closing, to see whether I can call it back or say why it came. I can't do either.*

*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

*One stamp, renewed. One enrollment, lawful. Half a tournament behind me and half to come. And the entry I wrote at the window after the captain, which has no answer yet and isn't going to get one by being copied out again. This book can carry it from here.*

He sat back and let the ink dry in the tooth of the paper.

It looked right. It looked like a page that would still be there to argue with in a year, and he found, reading it over, that he had already found one line he wanted to argue with, and that the narrow ruled margin was exactly the right width to do it in. He did not write in the margin, not tonight. He shut the book on its first page and put his hand on the cover for a moment, and then went down, because the draw posted at dusk and nobody read a draw alone.

---

The outer court was full by the time they came down into it, and it was still filling.

The Concourse's canvas had come off in the late afternoon, and the city had stretched itself and begun to remember that it was a tournament town. Lamps were being lit along the colonnades. The criers were back in the betting rows under their awnings. On the platform at the court's north side the drum stood on its iron spindle, and the three clerks from the west tower stood beside it, and in front of them, on a board, the trial's printed frame waited with its four lines blank.

Only four houses would field a squad. That had been on the trial rules since the fourth morning, and the standings had settled which four: the houses highest on the count as it stood when the brackets reached their finals. Auremont was first, by a long way. Rhagen was second. Halcenvane, on Brom's title and two semifinals and a quarterfinal over Ternhall's best, had come in third, a long way behind Rhagen and a hand ahead of the river academy.

Four squads, then, and two semifinals. The beaten pair would meet for third place on a later day, and the winners would meet in the final.

The marshal turned the drum.

He did it as he had done it on the night of the brackets' draw, holding each slip up in the lamplight for one long breath before he read it, and the court drew its breath with him every time.

The first slip was Rhagen. The second was the river academy.

Then the third, and Cael heard Lira beside him stop breathing.

"Halcenvane," said the marshal. He reached into the drum for the last slip, which everybody in the court could have told him, and held it up anyway, for the custom. "Auremont."

The clerk wrote it on the board in white. HALCENVANE — AUREMONT.

For a moment the court was quiet in a way Cael had not heard it be quiet before. Then it was not quiet at all. It ran across the square from the board outward, faster than any crier could have carried it. People at the front read it aloud to the people behind them, and the people behind them turned round and said it to the next, and by the time it reached the colonnades on the far side it had been worn down to two words, and then, as he listened, to one, which was *Auremont*, said in a great many different tones of voice.

He watched the houses read it. Rhagen's people read their line and were very obviously relieved, and were far too well brought up to let anybody in plain colours see them be relieved, and went off toward their quarter talking quickly and quietly among themselves. The river academy's coaches read it twice. Then, at the foot of the far colonnade, a cup went up. It rose only a little way and stayed there. Cael followed it down to the hand that held it, and the hand to a russet sleeve: one of two Ternhall Irons, beaten out of the bracket days ago and staying on in the city to watch, who had gone looking through the crowd for plain colours and found them. She did not drink until she was sure they had seen.

Cael knew what that meant. Everybody at a tournament knew. It meant *better you than us*, and it meant *go on anyway*, and both meanings were sincere.

Along the west side of the court a crier's voice went up under an awning, and then another. The little framed sign still hung at the end of the long row of chalked boards, where it had hung all sitting, in neat white paint. *NO BOOK ON THE DEMONSTRATION BOUTS.* Cael read the boards above it as he read anything with a figure on it. Auremont to win the semifinal, at a price so short it was hardly a price at all. Auremont to take the final. How many exchanges the semifinal would run. And two of the criers, who had read the little sign as often as anybody, had begun to cry it as a selling point.

"No price on the fifth man of the bluff! None! The house won't quote him! Come and see the thing we can't price!"

"They've made you a feature," said Ephram.

"They've made me a hole in their board," said Cael.

Nobody else said what everybody in the court was thinking. Auremont had not filed its squad yet. When it did, whatever else the list held, it would hold the Gold, and there was no rule anywhere in the trial's pages to keep her off the floor. A scenario floor had no tiers on it. It had ground, and the people each house sent out to stand on the ground.

Karis had gone back to the guesting-house on the way down from the harbour, for a quarter of an hour, alone. Nobody had asked her why. Now she took her notebook out of her coat, the grey one, and opened it to the page she had shown Cael at this same board on the fourth morning, and spread it open on the rail below the board, under the torch, so that all five of them could read it.

*Squads shall be drawn from the academy's roster.* Its number in the rules. A line drawn down to an older line from the front of the book. *Participating academies shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.* Sixth part. Subsection four. And under both, the one word.

*Again.*

She looked at the place on the rail where Withrow was standing, with Bracken a step behind her and the travel pouch under Bracken's arm. Then she looked at Cael. The torch over the board flared in the evening wind and settled.

"You're on the roster," she said. "The clause does the rest. Again."

Withrow had read the page over Karis's shoulder.

She did not say anything, but took a slip of paper and a pencil from Bracken, and wrote on the slip against the board's frame, in her clear upright hand, five names and the number of a rule. Cael, Lira, Brom, Karis, Ephram. She did not add a sentence. She did not add the clause, or the year's arguments, or anything at all that a clerk might read as somebody expecting a quarrel.

"A filing with an argument stapled to it is a filing that thinks it will lose," she said, to nobody in particular. "This one doesn't think anything. It's a list."

She took it across the court herself to the west tower's night clerk, who sat at a table under the colonnade for exactly this purpose with a lamp and a book, and handed it to him, and watched him enter it, and came back.

"Filed," she said. "Go to bed. All of you. Rooke wants you at the second bell." She looked at Cael. "And happy birthday."

He wrote the second entry in Hesk's book that night, under the inventory, with the window open and the court still murmuring below.

*Seventeen. Six fragments. Tomorrow I find out if the second technicality holds, and if it does — the found family fights together, on a sanctioned floor, for the first time in any of our lives. Whatever else this tournament is for, it was for that.*

---

Rooke did not wait for the second bell.

He sent Ephram round the rooms an hour after the draw to fetch them all to the hired hall behind the east tower. When they got there he had lamps lit along the trestle and five sheets of paper laid face up across it, and the trial's printed rules pinned on the wall above them, and he did not look as if he had any intention of letting anybody go to bed.

"Withrow's filed," he said. "Somebody in the west tower will decide in the morning whether they like it. I'm not going to spend a night waiting to find out what a clerk thinks. We'll plan it as filed, and if they say no, I'll have wasted an evening, and I've wasted worse."

He put his hand on the rules.

"I'll say how it's scored once, so we're all saying the same thing tomorrow. Four exchanges, each by the timekeepers' glass. Three platforms on the floor. While one house stands on a platform alone, the platform marshals count it in quarter-minutes, and the quarter-minutes go in a column they call the objective ledger. The flags count clean touches, as they always do, in the other column. A fighter who's touched goes back to his own gate and puts his hand on the post before he comes back on. Whichever house has more quarter-minutes in an exchange takes the exchange. If the exchanges come out even, the whole objective ledger decides." He took his hand off the sheet. "That's all of it. It's a game about ground, and the touches are how you take the other side's people off the ground for a while."

He turned to the sheets.

"Five names. The ones I've got answers for first."

The first sheet was the lattice-breaker.

"Karis," said Rooke. "He's yours again. You know his sums. He takes things apart for a living, and he's patient about it, and he's very good." He tapped the sheet. "So give him things to take apart. On ramps we don't need. Let him walk into every one of them, and make every one cost him a minute he'll want back."

Karis nodded. Her palms were a week old now, pink and shiny at the heel, and she held them a little open on the table in front of her.

"Your hands," said Rooke.

"Can carry a frame," said Karis. "They can't carry raw contact. Not all afternoon." She looked down at them. "I'll plant. All three anchors, every time, the long way. The frame takes the heat down where it ought to go. I won't be quick." She looked up. "I'll be expensive."

"That'll do," said Rooke.

The second and third sheets were Auremont's two Silver seniors, a man and a woman, and Rooke laid them side by side.

"These two have fought trials for ten years between them. They've won most of them. They play the objective game exactly as it's written, in order, and they don't make mistakes, and they never, ever do anything that isn't in a book." He looked down the trestle. "Ephram."

"Yes."

"They're yours. You'll be calling against them all afternoon. They'll do the right thing every time, and they'll do it on time." Rooke folded his arms. "So you do one thing, once, when it counts, that's not in any book they've ever read. I don't know what it is. You'll know."

Ephram did not answer. He looked at the two sheets for a long time.

The fourth sheet was Zerin.

"Lira," said Rooke.

That was all he said. Lira nodded once, without looking at the sheet. There was nothing on it that she did not already know by heart, and she had already lost to the woman whose name was at the top of it, honestly, in front of eight thousand people, and she was going to spend everything she had learned.

Rooke picked up the fifth sheet.

He did not read it. He turned it over and laid it down again, blank side up, and rested his palm on it.

"I've no plan for her," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"I've watched every minute of her that a man could watch in this city. I can tell you what she does. She finds whatever on a floor is least able to bear her, and goes there, and it doesn't bear her." He kept his hand where it was. "Stopping it isn't a thing any five of you can do, and I won't pretend to you it is."

He looked round at them.

"So here's the only thing I know. That ledger counts the whole floor for the whole of every exchange. Every platform, every minute, all five of us and all five of them. She's very good, and she's one person. She can't be everywhere on a floor at once." He tapped the back of the sheet, once. "We win the places she isn't. As long as we can. That's the plan."

He lifted his palm away. The sheet lay where it was, blank side up, and none of them reached to turn it.

---

The trial clerk brought the list up the west tower at the first bell of the morning, while the Chief Adjudicator was still standing at the window of the long room with his first cup.

Umber had been up since before light, as he usually was during a sitting. Through the gallery screen at the far end of the room he could see down onto the main floor, and he had stood there for most of an hour watching the crews take the exhibition ring down by lamplight. The posts had come out of the sockets one at a time, each eased up out of its hole with a long bar while two men held it, and been laid on the barrows. The planking had gone. The rope had gone. Then the plugs had gone back into the sockets, one by one, and an old woman on an upturned barrow had oiled each of them before it went in, and the crews had begun, at the far end of the oval, to bring in the timber for the trial terrain.

He found he had liked watching it. A floor was a floor. It held whatever stood on it, and the oil did not care who.

"The Halcenvane squad, Chief Adjudicator," said the clerk. He was a tall careful man with ink on his second finger, and he held the slip as if it might go off. "Filed at dusk with the chancellor's signature. Five names and the rule's number. Nothing else."

Umber put down his cup and took the slip.

He read the names, and knew all five. He had watched two of them on the main floor from behind this very screen, and he had read the figures of the other three every evening for two weeks, and the fifth name had been on his desk in one form or another since the fourth morning of the sitting, when the trial rules went up on the board and he had read the rule's single sentence and known exactly what somebody would do with it.

The trial office's two senior clerks had come up the stair behind the first, and his own deputy behind them, and they stood in a row at the end of the long table and waited. Somebody had put the rules on the table already, open at the page.

Umber read it aloud, once.

*"Squads shall be drawn from the academy's roster."*

He let it stand in the room for a moment, as he let every rule stand, so that everybody present had heard the whole of it and nothing but it.

"The rule says roster," he said. "A house's roster is whatever its enrollment book says it is. That book was certified at the start of this year by the house's own registrar. A regional office questioned it, was answered, and printed it." He laid the slip on the open page. "There is nothing to rule on. There's only a list."

"There will be talk," said the senior of the two clerks.

"There's talk now. There will be talk tomorrow whatever I sign this morning." Umber looked down at the slip for a while. "Gentlemen, for two weeks this office has rated that boy under a provision three hundred years old that nobody alive had used before this sitting, on a frame we had to have repainted, by a panel we had to convene. We rated him correctly. I've seen every figure. But every figure was one we built a room for, after he'd arrived in it."

He put his finger on the rule.

"The trial is rated on what a squad does together, against the Standard, on tables this office has used every cycle for longer than any of us has been alive. It has a column for every fighter on the floor. It doesn't ask what any of them are. It asks what they did." He looked up. "For the first time in this sitting, I'd be rating him on a sheet that was already built to hold him, in a column that was already there. I would like that very much. I would like it a great deal more than whatever this office will have to invent if I find a reason to say no."

Nobody spoke.

"Approve it before somebody makes me invent something," said Umber.

The deputy wrote. The senior clerk sealed the approval and took it away down the stair, and the junior clerk followed him, and the deputy went after them both.

Umber picked up his cup again. The tea had gone cold. He drank it anyway, standing at the gallery screen, looking down at the main floor, where the crews were laying out the first long line of the trial's barrier across the stone, from one corner of the floor toward the other, in the early light.

Bracken collected the notice himself from the trial office at the second bell, and brought it up the guesting-house stair to the long table, where they were all eating breakfast, and laid it down beside the bread.

It was one sheet, sealed, and it said that the Halcenvane squad was approved as filed.

"Within the hour of opening," said Bracken. "I'm told the Chief Adjudicator read the rule aloud once and approved it without a hearing." He sat down, which he rarely did at their table, and took a piece of bread, which he more rarely did. "I'm also told he was relieved. The trial office wants it known that it was not relieved, being an office, but that he was." He buttered the bread with great precision. "I don't mind telling you I was relieved myself. I've been sleeping badly on that clause for a year. Last night I slept like a quarry stone." He considered. "That's twice since the bluff. I may be getting used to it."

By the third bell the notice was on the boards in the outer court. HALCENVANE — SQUAD AS FILED.

The broadsides had it by the fourth. The criers had a new thing to shout. And the Concourse's ticket office, which had sold the semifinal's rails in the first hour after the draw, sold its standing room by the middle of the morning and shut its window and put up a board that said *NONE*, which nobody in the city could remember seeing at a semifinal before.

---

They walked the floor at the end of the morning, in the quarter of an hour the charter gave each squad, and they walked it apart, each to the part of it that belonged to them.

The crews had worked all night, and the main floor was no longer an oval of grey stone with a round of oak in the middle. It was country. It ran ninety feet from end to end, west to east, fenced at its edges with low rails, with Halcenvane's gate at the west end and Auremont's at the east. From the north-west corner of the floor clear across to the south-east ran one long barrier of heavy timber, head-high and straight as a ruled line, so that the floor was cut on the slant into two long triangles. Halcenvane's gate opened into the southern one, and Auremont's into the northern.

There were three platforms, each a low square of planking a stride high with a ramp at either end. Two stood in the northern triangle, in Auremont's country, one toward each end of it. The third stood alone in the southern triangle, in Halcenvane's, tucked far down toward the south-east corner. At both ends of the barrier the crews had stacked timber head-high in thick clusters to spoil the view across the corners, and just south of the barrier's middle, untouched, lay the old oak round, a dark disc in the stone.

Lira went first, because she could not stand still. She ran the northern triangle from end to end and then the southern, at three-quarter pace, with the heat-wrap gone from her left arm for the first time in a week and the arm carried a little close. When she came back to the west gate she was hardly breathing.

"North's quick," she said. "Open floor, new boards over the stone, nothing in the way. South's the old patched stuff, and the platform down there is behind a stack with a long ramp each side. It'll take two of us to keep it, and it's only worth what one of the north ones is worth." She wiped her hands on her shirt. "Fast north, slow south. That southern one costs double what it scores."

Rooke had his sheet on his knee at the rail. He looked at Lira over the top of it for a moment, and then, without a word, drew one short line under something he had written there already.

Karis did not run anything. She went and stood on the ramp of the north-west platform with her arms folded and her pink palms tucked into her elbows, and then on the ramp of the north-east one, and then she walked back slowly, looking over her shoulder the whole way.

"From up there you can't see the middle of the barrier," she said to Ephram. "The stacks are in the way, both ends. Any call you make from a northern ramp, you make without seeing where it lands." She considered it. "Auremont's caller has the same trouble from the same ramps. Whoever plans for it before the other one does gets a minute free. Count out loud when you're behind a stack. Then we'll know where you are even if we can't see you."

Brom had gone to the barrier. He did not look at it much. He leaned on it, every few strides, the whole length of it, the way a man leans on a fence to find the post that gives. Twice he stopped and turned sideways and put his shoulder into a place where the barrier was not, and came back out. When he had done the whole length he walked back to the gate.

"Two gaps," he said. "At two of the joins. The first's about a third of the way along from our end. The second's forty feet past it." He held up one big bandaged hand. "One man through either at a run. Not two together. Not if they want to keep their elbows."

He did not say which of the gaps he meant to stand in. Nobody needed him to.

Cael walked it last, on his own.

He walked the triangles and the ramps and the slow southern stone, and he found everything the others had found and agreed with all of it. But the thing he kept coming back to, without any reason he could give, was the barrier.

Everybody who walked this floor, he thought, would walk it as a wall. It was the thing you went through the gaps of, or round the ends of, the thing that split the country in two. He stood at its north-west end and sighted down its face. It ran from that corner to the far one without a break, smooth and flat and straight, ninety-odd feet of it. Nothing else on the floor was that long. Nothing else ran from one end of the fight to the other.

The read had nothing to say about it. The read saw weight, and the barrier's weight was not going anywhere.

He wrote it down all the same, in the column of the notebook where he kept things he could not use yet. *The diagonal. Corner to corner, unbroken. Longest line on the floor. Everybody's reading it as a wall.* He looked at it, and added one more line under it. *The feature nobody has priced.*

Then he put the notebook away. Gault was waving at him from the gate with the tape, and he went.

# Chapter 58 — The Weather

The file reached Ilsev's desk a little before noon, in the observation office's own case, with the west tower's seal on its cord.

She had known it would come. The entry's last word was *Referred*, and the charter gave only one address for a thing shown on a floor outside classification, which was the Compact's observation seat at the tournament. That was her. So she had come up to the office early and cleared the desk and laid out a fresh sheet of the office's paper, and when the case came she cut the cord and took the papers out in order and laid them in a row: the match record; the nine charts, in copy; the scribes' three accounts; the healers' report from the north station; and on top of all of them, by itself, the entry.

She read the entry first, once, as she read everything. She read it a second time for the words alone, as she had been taught to do, long ago, by a woman who read a form the way a jeweller looks at a stone. *Under*, not *by*. She noted that. Somebody had been careful, at the end of a long night.

Then she began on the routing, which was her work and had been her work since before most of the clerks in the building were born: a minute on the front sheet to say what the file was and whom it went to next, and why, and under what article. She knew the article without looking. She wrote it in her small upright hand and blotted it, and reached into the case for the dispatch slip that would send it on up the line.

There was a second packet under the dispatch slip.

It had come in the same case, by the same courier, from a different office altogether. Her own name was on it in a clerk's hand, and she knew the shape of it before she opened it.

The designation in the enrollee's file had been her question for a long while. She had first sent it up from Greyvane, plainly, asking who had entered the mark and on whose authority. Twice it had come back down to her with the same two sentences, *no originating authority of record*, and the second time a third sentence latched on behind them, saying the same nothing more firmly. So last year at Halcenvane, on the fifteenth of Reaping, she had sat at the long table in the emptied hall and filled in a second referral, fuller, every date and reference in its order, and marked it a level higher than the office that had answered. In all her years she had never before filed twice on one matter. A question nobody answered was a question to be asked again, through the next door up.

This was that door's answer.

She slit the packet and took out the return. It was one line, in a registry clerk's round hand, with a stamp she did not know.

*Referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized.*

She sat and looked at it.

Every reader of records learns early that the registry can say no in a hundred ways. *Not of record. Not found. No file.* The registry was full of honest holes, places where something had once been written down and later lost, or never written down at all. She had filed hundreds of those holes in her time without losing a night's sleep over any of them. A hole was only a hole. You marked it, and went round it.

This was not a hole. A hole said, *there is nothing here.* This said, *there is something here, and you may not look at it.* It did not say who had decided that. There was no name under the stamp. There was no office she could write to and ask. It was a drawer somebody had nailed shut and then taken the hammer away.

And it had come to her desk in the same case as a file from the west tower asking the Compact to look closely at the same boy.

She laid the two sheets side by side on the desk. The entry, which asked her to inquire. The return, which forbade it. She looked from one to the other for some time.

Then she did a thing she had not done once in her working life.

She opened the bottom drawer of the desk, where she kept her own things, and took out a sheet of paper that was not the office's. It was a sheet she had bought for herself at a stationer's on the coopers' row on her second day in the city, for letters home, and never used. She laid it on top of everything else, over the office's paper, and she wrote on it. She did not write in her office hand. She wrote in the hand she had used as a girl at her mother's table, before anybody had taught her to make her letters stand up straight.

What she wrote, nobody ever read, because it was not for any file and not addressed to anybody, and it was the first thing she had written in all her years of service that was not meant to be read by somebody else. She wrote it, and read it through once, and folded it twice, and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where she had never in her life carried anything that belonged to the Compact.

Then she finished the routing minute on the front sheet, correctly, under the right article, and sent the file on up the line as the charter required.

---

Havel brought the second round of the dispatch case in at the half after noon, and saw only the paper.

It was not office stock; he knew office stock as he knew his own boots. He saw her fold it twice and put it inside her coat, and pick up her office pen again. He did not look for the words. He did not need to. He had four sheets of his own like it, carried through three postings and now in his kit between the second and third of his manuals, where nobody ever looked.

That night, in his narrow room at the inn behind the east tower, he wrote a fifth. At its head he put the line from the return, which he had read upside down on her desk in perhaps three seconds and found he had entire.

*Referral closed at registry level; no further inquiry authorized.*

Under it he wrote that the senior seat now kept a sheet of its own. Then he put all five back between the manuals and buckled the kit, and sat for a while in the dark, thinking that there were two of them now in one city, each with papers their office had not asked for, and neither going to tell the other.

---

Withrow brought the note herself.

She came up the stair to the back room a little after noon, where the five of them were eating bread and cold meat because nobody had felt like going down to the long table. She knocked, which she did not need to do, and came in, and held out a folded sheet to Cael.

"It came to my office by a tournament page," she said. "It's addressed to you, care of me, which is correct. I've read the outside of it, which is my right as your chancellor. I have not read the inside, which is not." She put it in his hand. "I'll be downstairs."

She went. They heard her go all the way down.

Cael unfolded it. It was a half sheet in a spare upright hand he did not know, though he could have guessed whose it was. There was no seal and no heading.

*The Archmarshal Vastin asks the enrollee for one hour, before the houses leave the city. Nothing will be written by either party. It would be better if nothing were brought to write with. The timekeepers' office in the east tower has lent a room for the purpose; it belongs to neither the adjudication office nor the Compact. He is free to say no; it will count against him nowhere, with no one, at any time; and that sentence is not a formality.*

He read it aloud, because they would have asked him to. Then he laid it on the rocking table, and they all looked at it.

Karis spoke first. She always did when a paper was on the table.

"Nothing written," she said. "Think about what that means for us. This whole house runs on one rule, and it's kept you alive since the bluff. If it's on paper, we can survive it. Every word of the hearing was written down, and that's why we could fight it. The provision's on paper. The roster was on paper and that's the only reason you were on a floor at all." She touched the note with one finger. "He's asking you to walk out of the one place we've ever been safe. Whatever's said up there, afterwards there's his memory of it and yours, and his comes with a grey coat and a seal."

"And friendly isn't a safe word from them," said Lira. "I've been asked into a friendly room by an institution. At Fenmark, the morning they told me I was finished there. Everybody was very kind." She folded her arms. "It was the kindest room I've ever been in, and I walked out of it with nothing."

Brom said one thing.

"You'd be on your own."

Ephram had been sitting on the end of the bed with his bread. He was not of the circle and knew it, and had been asked to stay, and had stayed, and said nothing all through lunch. He said it now, in what was left of his voice. "Look at what you'd get and what it costs you. What it costs is easy. You'd be alone in a room with a senior man of the Compact and no witness. What you'd get is whatever he chooses to say, which you can't check. If it's true it'll still be true in front of a witness. If he won't say it in front of one, you have to ask why."

Cael heard them all out, every one, as the house's custom was. Then he said no to all of them.

"I asked for a witness once," he said, "with a warden across a table from me. I was right to. That man was a piece of the machine, and you don't sit down with a piece of the machine without somebody to say afterward what was said." He looked at the note. "This one's different, and I've had a year to learn how. Eleven questions in a room on the bluff. Three sentences of advisory. A chair held for him that he never asked for, two notes, and a walk out before the figure." He turned the note over. "Every move he's ever made toward me he's made in ink, and signed with his whole name. This is the first thing he's ever asked for that he can't sign. A man like that doesn't give up his ink to set a trap. He'd use the ink." He folded it. "I want to know what's worth that much to him."

Nobody argued further. They did not need to; they had been heard. Karis nodded once, as she did when a figure came out right. Lira let her arms go.

Seln was at the door. Nobody had heard him come up. He had the pouch under his arm and he had evidently been there for some of it, and he did not apologise for that, or come in.

"He has kept every rule of a forty-year career," he said, in his counter voice. "If he means to break one for you, the breaking has been paid for somewhere you will never be shown the bill." He shifted the pouch. "Listen accordingly."

Then he went down the stair to Bracken.

---

Downstairs, while Cael went across the court, Seln wrote his report at the document table in the front room, with the five buff folders lying in their row along the table in front of him.

It was the first report of the sitting he had not had to build.

He noticed that before he had written a word, and it was strange enough that he put the pen down and sat for a moment with his hands on the blotter. Every report he had sent north in a year had been built: true in every line, complete in every box the form asked for, and arranged with great care so that nothing in it led anywhere. He had built them as other men build walls, a stone at a time, checking each. This one did not need building. There was nothing to keep out of it. Eight thousand people had watched the thing. Three scribes had written it down at a speed no clerk could match. The west tower had put it in eleven words and hung them in a frame. Whatever he wrote, the world already held more of it than his form had room for.

He took up the pen again.

*Exhibition, finals day, against the filed Gold challenger of Auremont. Subject fought four exchanges. Capabilities exercised: as in the public record attached, entire. Result and entry: as attached. Observation in this instance is complete, the event having been public.*

He read it through. Every word was true, and every word was all of the truth that was his to write. That had never happened to him before in this work. He found that his hand wanted to go on and write the last line of the form, the line he had written at the foot of every report for a year, the one word that closed it.

He held the pen over the space and did not write the word.

The space stayed empty. He blotted the sheet as it was, and folded it, and sealed it, and laid it on the corner of the table for Bracken's pouch.

Something in him was lighter, as a man is lighter who has set down a case he has carried so long he had forgotten it was in his hand, and he did not look at it closely. He had a long habit of not looking closely at things in himself, and he kept to it now. He sat with his hands folded on the empty blotter, as he sat at his counter in the wing at the end of a day, and let the lightness be there without asking what it was.

Then, because habit is habit, he took the small book from his inside pocket and opened it and wrote in it too, a few lines, in a hand nobody at the wing had ever seen him use. He put it away.

The locked case stood under the table by his chair, where it had stood every evening of the sitting. He had not opened it. He did not open it now.

---

The timekeepers' tower stood at the east end of the Concourse, across the outer court from the west tower where the adjudication office lived. Cael had passed under it every day of the sitting and never been inside.

Brom walked with him across the court, although nobody had asked him to and he had lost the argument about the room an hour before and had not said another word about it since. They went through the people still waiting in the copy line, who turned to look, and under the arch at the tower's foot, and there Brom sat down on the step.

He did it carefully, with the bad shoulder away from the stone, and put his forearms on his knees.

Cael looked at him.

"You said no to the room," said Brom. "So I'm not in the room. Nobody said anything about the step." He looked out across the court at the copy line. "It's a good step. I can see the clock from here."

There was nothing to say to that, and Brom would not have wanted anything said. Cael went in.

The stair went up round the inside of the tower, and the room was at the top of it. A timekeeper's boy was waiting at the door. He had the key already turned. He opened the door and stood aside and went off down the stair without a word, so that nobody would have to let anybody in.

The room was full of clocks.

They stood on shelves round all four walls, and on the long table, and two of them hung over the door. Some were brass and some were wood, and a few were in pieces, with their works laid out on cloths. Each had a card tied to it with a date and a hand. And every one of them was ticking, and no two of them said quite the same time. The noise in the room was like rain on a roof. It was the timekeepers' office for the whole Concourse, where every clock the sitting used was brought to be set against the tower's great one and sent out true, and it belonged to nobody in the sitting at all.


Hesk had written to him in the cold term that in a big city every clock disagrees with every other, and every one of them is certain. Cael had never been in a room that proved a letter so thoroughly.

Vastin was sitting at the long table with his back to the window. He got up when Cael came in.

He was in the plain grey, with nothing at the collar. He had no case with him and no papers, not a sheet, not a pencil. Cael had seen this man in a records hall, in a held chair, across a table of questions, and every time there had been a document somewhere within reach of his hand. There was nothing within reach of it now. The hands hung at his sides. The left one was not quite straight at the fingers.

His eyes went over Cael once. They did it as they had on the bluff, without any hurry and without seeming to do it at all. They rested a moment where the strapping showed under the shirt, and a moment on the wrapped forearm, and on the care with which Cael lowered himself into the chair across the table. Vastin said nothing about any of it. Cael saw him take each thing and put it away, and understood that this was a kind of good manners. In his trade you did not comment on what you found. You kept it.

"Thank you for coming," said Vastin, and sat.

Then he frowned slightly, as a man frowns at a figure he has written down wrong.

"No. Thanks are the wrong instrument. You owe me nothing, and I didn't ask you here for anything of yours. I asked you here for something of mine." He laid his hands on the table, the right flat, the left as flat as it would go. "I would rather we began straight."

"Then begin," said Cael.

Vastin looked round the room before he did, at the shelves of clocks.

"The keeper of this room," he said, "can tell you how wrong each of these is. To the half minute. He doesn't throw them out for it. He knows each one's error, and he corrects for it, and so between them they tell him the time very well." He brought his eyes back. "That has been my work for forty years. Not finding the instrument that is right. Learning exactly how each one is wrong."

He did not say what that had to do with anything. He let Cael carry it.

"I closed my evaluation of you last night," he said. "On a stool by a door, at about the fourth bell. I'll not tell you what it found. You were in the ring when it was found."

A clock on the wall behind him struck the half hour. Another took it up a breath later, and a third, much later, as if it had been thinking about it.

"I'm going to give you eight words," said Vastin. "Four, and then four. I have watched them come across my table this year in letters about your file, the first four in the early part of it, the second four since. I want you to hear them in the order they came." He said the first four slowly, each one by itself.

"*Anomaly. Irregularity. Basis. Review.*"

He waited, and then said the others.

"*Posture. Handling. Exposure.*" And then, after a longer space, and more quietly: "*containment.* I have seen that one twice. Both times in minutes that reached me as a kindness, with nobody expecting me to reply."

Cael put the eight in a row in his head.

"The first four are words you use about a fault," said Vastin. "A thing gone wrong in the record, which a careful office would like to find and mend. You find a fault so that you can take it out." He moved his left hand an inch on the wood. "The second four are words you use about a thing you mean to keep in a box. I went through the Compact's charter for them, article by article, one evening. They aren't in it. You can't write *handling* about a question. You can only write it about an answer you've already got."

"Who sends you the minutes?"

"I can't name him. I'm not certain there is a him. The better question took me a long while to find. Who decides which men are sent minutes, and which men are left off the list?" He said it flatly, as he would have read a line off a form. "I put it on paper once and sent it where such things are meant to go. Within the week it came back. It was very civil and there was nothing in it. I have not asked again." He looked at his left hand. "I've noticed, lately, that I have stopped meaning to. I don't care for that. I tell you so that you'll know it about me."

He was quiet for a while then.

The clocks went round. Down in the court somebody was calling out numbers along the copy line, and the numbers came up faintly through the window, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-three. Cael waited. He did not know what was coming, but he could see that it was coming, in the way the man sat, and that it had been carried a long way.

Then Vastin lifted his eyes to Cael's and kept them there.

"You should stop competing publicly."

Cael let the clocks have a moment of it. Then he asked why.

"Because the people who want this to stop are not going to use official channels much longer."

---

It went into the room and stayed there.

Neither of them hurried it. The numbers came up from the court, forty, forty-one, and stopped. A clock on the long table ran down with a small sigh and was still, and the others went on without it. Cael sat and read the man in front of him, because that was what he had. The read said there was more, and that it would be given at its own rate. Each sentence was being paid for out of something, and the man was paying.

"Who are they?"

"If I gave you names I would be guessing, and I don't guess." He did not say it to be clever. He said it as Gault said a plate would not read past its last notch. "I can tell you what my year has looked like from my chair. A year ago I was asked what you are. This year I am asked what can be done about you. I answer every one of those letters by saying, again, what you are. Each answer goes up the stair. Each one is entered and filed. I am fairly sure none of them is read." He turned his left hand over, and back. "My office still works. It works very well. It has simply stopped mattering. When the questions stop being questions, an evaluator knows the decision has been taken in some room he was not shown."

"There's talk of a sitting."

"There will be one, after the houses have gone home. I'm called to it, and I'll go." The ghost of something went across his face. "I can't tell you what's on its paper. You won't need me to. You were there yesterday when it was written."

Cael asked the next thing slowly. He knew which question it was.

"The designation in my file. The old one, with no office against it. Is that part of what's coming?"

Vastin did not answer.

He did not look away either. He sat with his hands on the table and his eyes on Cael, and the clocks counted for him, and Cael counted with them. One. Two. Three. Four. In the whole hour it was the only silence that pressed on anything. Cael felt it the way he felt the air before a squall, heavy, all along one side.

"I won't answer that one," said Vastin.

That was all. But it was a great deal. He had not said *I don't know*, which would have been true and easy. He had looked for whoever set that line in the file, as Ilsev had, and found nobody; and it troubled him. It troubled him more than anything else in the hour, more than the eight words, more than the warning. He had just said a thing in this room that could end forty years if anybody heard it, and he would not say this. Cael understood that it was not the record he was wary of. It was the blank in it. A man who has spent his life learning exactly how each instrument is wrong does not like an error that belongs to no instrument at all.

"If I stopped," said Cael. "If I went home and never fought where anybody could see me again. Would it change anything?"

"No." Vastin did not soften it. "That is the other half of why I asked for you. What you do now makes no difference to it. A year ago it would have. A thing you do is an answer, and answers are for questions, and they have stopped asking. Nothing answers a conclusion."

---

The clocks went round. Cael let them, for a while. Then he set down the question he had brought up the stair with him, because he did not think the hour would have a better place for it.

"How did you evaluate me?"

Vastin did not ask what he meant. He looked, for a moment, very slightly pleased.

"With the wrong instruments, for three days," he said. "I came up to the bluff with a file and a plate and a set of questions, and all three told me nothing. A man in my trade has one great temptation, and I gave in to it. I thought the nothing was something being hidden from me." He moved the left hand. "It is the oldest mistake there is. When an instrument won't read, you'd sooner believe it's broken than believe you've pointed it at a thing it was never made to measure."

"And then?"

"Then you answered my fourth question. In full, truthfully, every word of it. And I learned nothing whatever from the answer." Something that was nearly amusement moved in his face. "I have done that to other men for forty years. Nobody had ever done it to me. From that question on I stopped measuring what you could do and started watching how you went about it." He looked at Cael steadily. "The method is not a boy's. That is my whole opinion of you. It's in no file. There is no line on any form where it would go."

He glanced down at his own left hand on the table, and then up again.

"You're counting that," he said. "The hand."

"Yes."

"Good. Count it right. It's worse in the evenings now as well as the mornings. Put that down where you put things."

He did not offer protection. He said, in so many words, that he had none to give, because it would have been dishonest to let the boy wonder. Whatever reach his office had, it had through the very channels he had just told Cael would be stepped round. And a warning from a man who could also shelter you, he said, would have been a smaller thing than this, because you could never be sure which of the two he was giving you.

"Do you think I'll stop?" said Cael.

For a moment the grey face did something Cael would spend half that night failing to name in Hesk's book.

"No. I evaluated you." He let it rest a moment. "But you'll know when the channel changes, now. Knowing is worth something. It's the only thing I had to give."

He stood up, and said a word of thanks to the room. He said it to the shelves and the table and the clocks, the way a man pays a small account on his way out of an inn. Then he went to the door, and out, and Cael heard his steps going down round the inside of the tower at the same even pace all the way to the bottom. He was going back down into a machine he knew to the last screw and could no longer account for.

Cael stayed where he was.

He gave it four minutes by the clock over the door. It may have been five by the one beside it. If he got up at once, the hour would have decided when he left, and he meant to decide that himself.

Then he went round the room with his eyes before he went, as he did every room.

The table bare. Not a paper, not a cup, nothing on the wood that had not been there before. The other chair, empty. Forty clocks, near enough, every one of them sure. And on the sill by the window, apart from the rest and ticking a little behind them, a small brass travelling clock not unlike Hesk's. It had a card tied to its handle in a timekeeper's hand. *Slow. Owner informed.*

Nothing to file. No copy, no witness, no line in any book on the continent. The whole hour was in two heads now and nowhere else. One of those heads belonged to a man who had spent forty years making very sure nothing of the kind would ever happen to him.

He went down the stair.

Brom was sitting on the step under the arch with his forearms on his knees, looking at the copy line. He got up when Cael came out into the light, slowly, the bad shoulder first, and stood.

"An hour," said Cael.

Brom tipped his head back and looked up at the face of the tower, at the great clock every other clock in the Concourse was set by.

"Fifty-one minutes."

They went back across the court together. The copy line had moved perhaps twenty yards. Brom did not ask anything, all the way across, and Cael did not offer anything. At the guesting-house door Brom stopped with his hand on the latch and looked at him once, the way he looked at a floor before he stood on it.

"Good," he said, and opened the door. That was the last either of them ever said about the step.

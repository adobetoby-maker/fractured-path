# Chapter 31 — The Mark

Chief Adjudicator Umber sent his clerks home at the ninth bell on the eve of the opening, and then sat down alone with the case.

They did not want to go. They never did, on this night of all the nights in the cycle, and he had known them long enough to let them make their small protests before he sent them.

The senior clerk wanted to stay and hold the lamp.

The youngest, who had been in the tower only since the last cycle and still walked about it as though the floors might give way, wanted to bring him tea, and then a shawl for his knees, because the galleries were cold after dark.

He accepted the tea and declined the shawl, and thanked them both. Then he told them that the mark had managed three hundred years without a shawl and would very likely manage one more night.

They went down the stair together, talking in low voices, and he heard the outer door close behind them, and then the tower was quiet.

The office of the adjudication occupied the whole of the west tower, five floors of it, and on this one, the fourth, it kept its oldest things.

The long room ran the width of the tower, with tall windows at either end. One looked out over the city and the other down onto the main floor through a gallery screen of carved stone.

Down its length stood the clerks' desks in two rows, each with its lamp and its blotter and its stacks of ruled paper squared to the edge, and at the far end, on a table by itself under the best lamp, stood the case.

It was a plain thing to look at. It was a chest of dark wood about the length of a man's arm, bound at the corners in brass gone brown with handling, with two leather straps round it.

Where the straps crossed on the lid, a cord had been passed through both and knotted, and over the knot three seals had been set in red wax.

Umber drew a chair up to the table, and took off his spectacles and polished them, and put them on again, and took out his glass.

He did not need to do this. He knew that perfectly well.

The case had stood in the strong room on the floor below for three years. Two locked doors had stood between it and the stair, and a clerk at a desk who logged every soul who passed them, and not one entry in that log was out of order.

Tomorrow he would carry it out onto the main floor, and open the seals in front of eight thousand people, and every one of them would watch him do it and not one of them would be close enough to see the wax.

If he set his new seal on old wax that had been tampered with, nobody in the Concourse would ever know.

He would know. That was the whole of the matter, and had been for as long as he had held the office.

He bent over the first seal with the glass.

It was his own. He had set it himself, three years ago, on the last night of the last cycle. That had been after the last bout had been rated and the last figure posted and the tables put back in the case for the long sleep.

The office's mark stood clear in the wax, the bar and the ring, every edge of it sharp. The date of the closing was cut beneath it.

And there, low on the right where a careless eye would take it for a flaw in the casting, was the small scratch he made with the corner of his thumbnail in every seal he set, a private habit forty years old that he had never told anybody about.

The scratch was there. His own thumbnail had made it.

He checked the second seal in the same way, and then the third. The second had been set by the senior clerk and witnessed by him, and he compared its impression against the drawing of the matrix in the seal-book, line by line.

He followed the cord with his fingers from the knot along both straps to the brass rings and back, feeling for a cut or a splice or a place where the twist ran wrong.

He went over the lid with the glass, and then the hinges, and then the lock.

It took most of an hour. The tea went cold beside him.

When he had finished, he sat back in his chair and took his spectacles off again and rubbed his eyes.

The seals were whole. The cord was whole. Nobody had touched the case since he had closed it with his own hands three years before.

He had known they would be. He had never once in all his years found anything wrong on this night, and he did not expect ever to find anything.

That was not why he looked. He looked so that tomorrow, when he stood up in front of the city and said the sentence, he would be saying a thing he knew and not a thing he believed.

---

The calibration ledger lay beside the case, and when he had drunk the cold tea he opened it and read the week's last page again.

Every judge on the cycle's lists had been tested that week, as every judge was tested before every cycle.

They had sat in the long room below in fives, behind the same small boards they would sit behind on the dais, and the clerks had shown them old bouts, bouts that had been rated by earlier panels and whose true figures stood in the office's books.

Each judge marked them blind. The clerks took the slates and compared them with the books, mark by mark, and wrote the differences in the ledger.

Most of the differences were small, and wandered on both sides of the line, as honest differences do. A judge might be a shade hard on execution one morning and a shade soft the next.

That was a human eye, and the panel's five eyes and the striking of the high and low were there to absorb it.

One judge's differences did not wander.

He had marked the Blade bouts generous. It was not much. It was a quarter of a point, on effect, never more.

But it was a quarter of a point on every Blade bout he was shown, two days running, and always on the same side of the line.

The clerks had flagged it on the first evening, and Umber had given him the second day to correct it. On the second day he had marked a quarter generous again, without knowing that he did.

Umber had struck him from the lists that morning, and written the letter himself.

He read the ledger's entry now, in the senior clerk's careful hand, and the line under it in his own, and he was sorry.

The man was a good judge. He had a quick eye and a fair mind, and he had sat on Umber's panels for three cycles without a word of complaint from anybody.

He had fought in the Blade bracket himself, a long time ago. Somewhere in him he still loved it, and the love had come out on his slate a quarter of a point at a time.

That was the trouble with a kind eye. It was the best thing a man could bring to most work, and on a panel it was a fault.

Umber closed the ledger.

He had kept this office through more cycles than any of his clerks had been alive for, and he still remembered his first opening. He remembered the weight of the case in his arms, heavier than he had expected, and the way the noise of the tiers had come down on him when he walked out with it, so that for a moment he had not been able to hear his own feet. He remembered his hands shaking as he cut the cord. The old Chief Adjudicator, his master, had stood at his elbow and said nothing at all. Afterward, in the tower, the old man had poured him a cup of something strong and said only, *You checked them last night. So your hands may shake. The seals did not.*

He had not understood it for years. He understood it now.

He got up, stiffly, and walked the length of the long room to the gallery screen, and looked down through the carved stone at the main floor.

It was dark down there. A single lamp burned on the lowest tier for the night porter, and in its light he could just make out the dais on the south side, the five tall chairs in their row.

Tomorrow at midmorning the tiers would be full to the top rows, with not a place left on any bench. The fourteen houses would walk out onto that floor behind their colours.

The crowd would roar for the banner, as it always did, because a banner was a thing people could love.

Then he would carry the case out, and break three seals, and say a sentence.

He said it now, very quietly, under his breath, standing at the screen in the dark.

He had said it at the opening of every cycle he had kept. He had not written it. It was older than he was, and older than any of the men who had said it before him whose names he knew.

Every Chief Adjudicator before him had said it in his turn, and every one of them, Umber supposed, had sat up the night before with a glass and a cold cup of tea, for the same reason he did.

It was a short sentence, and a plain one. There was no ornament in it at all. It said three things and said each of them once, and it had held up the whole weight of the tournament for longer than anybody could remember.

That was what Umber believed in, if he believed in anything, more than in charters or ceremonies or banners. He believed in a sentence that worked.

A sentence worked when it was true every single time it was said.

The first time it was said and was not true, it would stop working. It would stop forever, even on all the days afterward when it was true again, because nobody hearing it would ever be sure which kind of day it was.

So it had to be true every time. And he was the one who said it, so he was the one who had to make sure.

He went back to the table and lifted the case in both arms, carefully, as he would have lifted a child. He carried it down the stair to the strong room, and logged it in with the clerk at the desk in his own hand.

Then he put on his coat and went home through the quiet streets, an old man walking slowly, and nobody he passed looked at him twice.

---

Cael had been told, more than once, that he would stand in a tunnel for a long time on the first day of the new month, and he did.

It ran under the north tiers, wide and dim and lit with lamps, and all fourteen houses waited in it in their files, one behind another, with their colours on staves at their heads.

Somewhere beyond its mouth a band was playing. It played a slow, plain, stepping tune that nobody in the tunnel could name and everybody in it knew, and through the stone over their heads came another sound under the music.

The whole bowl was humming it under its breath, a crowd humming a thing it had heard at every opening it had ever been to.

The houses went out in the order they had qualified. The earliest went first, small houses from far regions blinking at the light, and the great names waited at the back for their turn.

It meant the crowd read the file from its smallest end to its largest, and kept the best of it for last, like a good storyteller.

Halcenvane was eleventh of fourteen.

Withrow stood at its head in a plain formal grey that Cael had seen her wear perhaps twice.

Behind her stood the four in their bracket order, Brom for the Copper and then Ephram and Karis and Lira for the Iron, and behind them, after a gap, Cael.

The gap had taken the marshals most of a quarter of an hour.

Three of them had stood in the tunnel with a list while the early houses filed out, arguing in low voices. Every so often one of them had looked at Cael and then back at the list.

There was no bracket to put him in and no rank to order him by. The procession had a place for everybody who had one or the other and no place at all for anybody who had neither.

At last the eldest of them had taken him by the elbow, quite kindly, and walked him to the very back of Halcenvane's file, a stride behind Lira, and left him.

That was where the list ran out. It suited the marshals' logic. It suited the record, too, though the marshals could not know that.

Then the file in front moved, and they walked out of the dark.

He had stood in front of crowds all year. None of them had been anything like this.

The tiers went up on every side so steeply and so full that they stopped looking like seats at all and became a single sloping wall of faces.

The noise came down off that wall in one long pressure that he felt on his skin and in the bones of his chest, and for the first few strides his feet did not trust the floor under it.

He did what Rooke had taught them to do with weather on a floor. He let himself feel it, and named its price, and walked on.

Halfway across, the noise changed under him.

It did not get louder. It came apart.

A movement ran along the tiers ahead of Halcenvane's file, faster than the file was walking, people leaning to their neighbours and pointing, and inside it the roar broke into separate voices, and some of them came down to him through the stone. *That's the clause house. At the back. The one with no tier. That's him, the last one.*

He heard his whole year said back to him in pieces by strangers, and he kept walking.

The houses wheeled in the middle of the floor and drew up in their blocks along the north side, facing the dais.

Auremont came out of the tunnel last. Forty of them, in their blue and silver, and the order of the procession turned their column round, so that their Gold walked first, at its head.

Behind the last of them, alone, came the banner.

It was carried by the tournament's own colour-guard, four men in grey, two on either side of a tall staff, at a slow march. They had brought it down from the champion's gate at dawn, and it would go back up over the gate when the opening was done. No house walked behind it.

The Concourse got to its feet for it, all of it at once, with a sound like a long wave breaking along a beach, and Cael felt the difference in his chest as plainly as a change of floor.

They had cheered the houses. They stood up for the cloth.

It told him more about this place in a few breaths than the charter had in a year. The people on these benches did not love any house. They loved the thing every house was walking toward.

Then he took his eyes off the silver, and looked for her.

He had been saving his eyes for a week.

He had turned his back on her in the street on purpose so that this would be the first look, and he had known since the orientation what the geometry of the morning would give him.

Auremont's block drew up at the far end of the line, where the north side curved toward the east tower. Halcenvane's stood well down the line from it. Between them lay a hundred yards of polished stone, and no part of the ceremony would bring them any closer.

So he read her at a hundred yards. He gave it everything he had, the read and the compound gaze together, the way he would have read the last exchange of the hardest bout of his life.

The read always asked a body the same four things. Where was the weight? Which way was it going? What did the body do just before it moved? And who was all of it for?

She answered the first two the way any good fighter answered them.

She was tall, and nineteen, and her weight went into the stone and came back out of it cleanly at every step, the honest walk of somebody who had spent her life on floors.

Far behind her, at the tail of the file, the colour-guard kept its slow march, and she kept the same pace, no faster and no slower.

The third question found nothing. He could not find a tell. That was not unheard of at a hundred yards; good fighters kept their tells small, and a hundred yards was a long way.

The fourth question was the strange one.

Every fighter walked out onto a floor with their weight aimed at somebody.

He had read it in a hundred fighters this year. They aimed it at the crowd, or at an opponent, or at a coach in the stand, and some of them aimed it at a mirror they had carried round inside themselves since they were children.

The aim was always there. It was the easiest thing in a body to read, because it was the one thing the body most wanted read.

Hers was aimed at nothing at all.

The noise in that bowl had bent him when he came out of the tunnel. He had seen it bend every fighter in every file that morning, one way or another, into swagger or stiffness or a careful ignoring of it.

It did not bend her. She walked through it at the head of a file that ended in the most loved object on the continent, with eight thousand people on their feet, and her body carried on as though she were crossing an empty yard in the rain.

She was not showing anybody anything, and she was not hiding anything either. There was simply no one it was for.

The music ended. The houses stood in their blocks, and all along the north side the fighters let their shoulders down at last.

Her head turned.

It did not go to the banner, or the dais, or the tiers.

It went straight down the hundred yards of the line to the eleventh block, and moved along Halcenvane from one end to the other and back again, unhurried. It stopped nowhere. Then it went away.

Beside Cael, Lira let out a breath she seemed to have been holding since the tunnel.

"She just counted us," she said, very low. "Didn't she."

Nobody in the block answered her. Brom shifted his weight, once, from one foot to the other, and was still.

Cael put it in the observation notebook that evening, and then into the Log, the same words in both. It wanted a page of its own.

*Daeva, at a hundred yards. The read asks every body four things: where's the weight, where's it going, what happens just before, and who's it for. She answered the first two like any good fighter.*

*The third I couldn't find at that distance. The fourth has no answer. It isn't for anybody.*

*Either there's nothing there to read or she keeps it further in than a hundred yards can reach, and it comes out the same either way: whatever's printed on her sheet is the least of her.*

*When the houses stood, she looked at us. Along the block and back again. Not curious. She was counting what we'd brought.*

---

When the floor was still again, the west tower's door opened, and an old man came out of it carrying a box.

Cael had pictured somebody larger. Umber was slight and a little stooped, in the adjudication office's plain grey, and he crossed the great floor at the pace of a man crossing his own kitchen in the morning.

Two clerks walked behind him with a small table between them. They set it down in the middle of the floor, and he set the case on it, and stood back and looked at it for a moment with his hands folded.

Then he took a glass out of his coat, and bent, and went over the seals.

He did it one seal at a time, close, the glass to his eye. He ran his fingers along the cord from the knot to the straps and back.

Nobody in the tiers could have seen the wax from where they sat. Cael, at the edge of the floor, could not see it himself.

Every person in the Concourse watched him do it anyway, in a silence so complete that Cael could hear the lamps along the lowest tier.

When he had finished, he cut the cord and broke the seals, and lifted his head and spoke.

The Concourse had been built so that a voice at the middle of its floor would reach its highest bench, and his reached it. He did not raise it.

It was a dry, careful, unremarkable voice, the voice of somebody reading out an inventory he has checked himself, and it said the sentence once.

*the mark is the mark of the last cycle and every cycle; no hand has adjusted it; against it all are measured alike*

He opened the case. He lifted the first of the bound tables out of it and laid it on the table, and one clerk held the cord and the other the wax.

He pressed the new cycle's seal down onto the cover with both hands and held it there. Then he lifted it, and bent to look at the impression, and nodded once to himself, as a carpenter nods at a joint.

Rooke had come up behind Cael in the block while it was happening. He bent his head and spoke, low, under the sound beginning to come down from the tiers. "They cheer the banner. They *trust* the seal. Listen."

Cael listened.

It was nothing like the noise for the banner. That had been a wave breaking. This was quieter by half and went on three times as long, a low slow sound like a great many people agreeing with something under their breath.

Very few of them could have told par from a pothole, or read a line of the tables in that case. Every one of them knew exactly what the seals were there to keep out.

They did not love that wax. They leaned on it.

*Before he set the new seal he went over the old ones with a glass, in front of all of us,* he wrote that night, under the line about Daeva.

*Nothing in the charter asks him to. Nobody in the tiers could have seen it if he'd left it out. He did it the way other men wash their hands before a meal.*

*Rooke told me Umber has nothing to want from me. Having watched him, I'd say it differently. Of everybody in this whole machine, he's the one whose want I can read from the far side of a floor, and it has nothing to do with me.*

*He wants the number to be right. It was in every move of his hands.*

---

Next morning the brackets began, on four floors at once. By the end of the first hour it was plain that Rooke's sheet in the long upper room had never been a warning. It had been a forecast.

Lira's first bout was on the second floor, the square hall under the high glass, and it ran long.

Her opponent was an Ash Path fighter from one of the southern houses, a middle seed, a compact quiet man a few years older than her with ash-grey wraps on his hands.

His regional file said he was good and no more. On the board at the end of the hall he was a line of figures that would not have kept a semifinalist awake.

The floor was the pale hard floor Lira had stood on at the orientation, true and fast and honest, and it gave neither of them anything but each other.

Cael was at the rail with Rooke and Brom. Ephram had gone to watch the coast house warm up. Karis was on the next floor, through the arch, fighting the same hour.

In the first exchange Lira ran the survey she had run all year, wide and quick, and it came back wrong.

The Ash fighter's screens were the ordinary kind, the kind every regional Ash fighter used: brief clouds of grey breath let out at the hands and thrown into the air between them, a veil that hung for half a beat and then thinned.

Cael had seen a dozen fighters do it on the circuit.

They threw their veils everywhere, all the time, to look busy.

This man was a miser with them.

Every veil went up exactly where it covered something he meant to do. One went up to hide a step to his left, and one to hide a change of hands, and one to hide nothing at all, so that she would learn to wonder.

Lira had built her whole year on seeing a little more of the other fighter than the other fighter wanted to show. For the whole of that exchange she saw precisely as much as he allowed.

He touched her on the hip at the end of a veil she had walked straight into, and the flags went up. Then he touched her again on the shoulder, out of the next one, and the flags went up again.

The bell went with the third veil still hanging in the air between them.

Nought to two.

Lira walked back to her chalk and stood there breathing, looking at the grey haze thinning over the floor. It was the first opening exchange she had lost all year.

"He's not hiding himself," said Brom, beside Cael. "He's hiding what he's about to do."

"I know." Cael was watching Lira's shoulders. "She knows too."

Rooke kept his mouth shut at the interval. He handed her water over the rail, and she drank it, and handed back the cup, and he took it.

In the second exchange she stopped looking.

Cael saw her decide it, in the first three breaths. If the grey was going to tax her eyes, then she would stop spending them.

She came in at a walk, and then at a run, and then at a walk, the way Ephram did, and every time the grey went up she did not try to see through it. She listened.

The floor rang. That was what she had said about it at the orientation: that it was honest and would give her nothing. It gave her one thing, which was that it was honest.

Every step the Ash fighter took struck the hard pale boards with a clean small sound, and no veil he could throw would muffle it.

Behind the grey Lira could not see his left foot go, but she could hear it, and on the third veil she heard it go and burst to where it was going before it arrived.

Her palm landed on his ribs out of the cloud. The flags went up.

It was not clean. Listening cost her something every time, and Cael could see what it was from the rail. She was a beat late on everything, because she had to wait for the sound before she could go, and a fighter who waits for a sound has given away the first half of every beat to whoever makes it. Twice she went to where his foot had landed and found him a stride further on, because the sound had told her where he had been and not where he was going.

The Ash fighter noticed. He was quick about it. Halfway through the exchange he began to land soft, on the outsides of his feet, and once he put a foot down hard on purpose and went the other way.

She heard that one, and went to it, and he touched her from behind the grey. It was a feint of the foot without the weight in it, and it was a clever one.

Lira stood very still for half a breath after the flags went up. Then she nodded, once, to herself, and the next time a foot came down hard she did not go to it. She waited for the second sound, the one that came when the weight arrived, and went to that instead. She touched him once more, cleanly, a breath before the bell.

Two to one. Two to three on the bout.

---

At the second interval she came to the rail. She did not drink.

She stood with her hands on it and her eyes half shut, the way she had stood on the floor at the orientation, and Cael understood that she was listening to the hall.

"Feet," she said, to nobody. "I'm fighting his feet." She opened her eyes. "It's slow. I'm a beat late on everything, because I'm waiting to hear."

"Then don't wait," said Cael.

She looked at him.

"Price his roads," he said. "The way you did in the yard. You don't need to see him to make the roads dear. You only need to know where they go." He nodded at the floor. "The floor tells you that. It's a square. It's got the same roads with your eyes shut."

Lira looked at the floor for a moment longer. Then she smiled, very slightly, and went back to her chalk.

The third exchange was the corner game.

She did not go at him at all. She burst once, short, to a place on the floor that was nowhere near him, and stood there, and the Ash fighter threw a veil out of pure habit and stepped behind it to his left.

She heard the step. She had already been standing in his left road, so he paid a burst of his own to go round her. She heard that too, and was in the next road before he landed.

He threw a veil there as well, and she walked through it without looking and stood where his feet were about to be.

He had a great many veils. He spent them like a clerk who has found that the ledger no longer balances, faster and faster.

They hung in the air all over that side of the floor, grey on grey. Inside them Lira moved from road to road by the sound of his feet, and made every one of them cost him something.

She touched him three times in that exchange. He touched her once. On the third of hers the flags went up and the exchange closed early, as the rules said it would, and the hall made a noise Cael had not heard it make all morning.

Three to one. Five to four.

At the third interval the Ash fighter's coach leaned over the far rail and said something to him, short and fierce, with one hand making a flat cutting motion. Cael read the gesture without needing the words. *Stop spending. Hold them back. Make her wait.*

The Ash fighter nodded. He stood on his chalk breathing and looked at his own grey hands, and Cael saw him count something on his fingers, very small, against his thigh.

"He's counting what he's got left," said Brom.

"He hasn't got enough," said Cael. "He spent it buying the first exchange. He'll have three, maybe four, and she knows where every one of them will go now."

Lira did not look at the far corner at all. She stood on her chalk with her eyes closed and her head a little on one side, listening to the hall. A broom was going somewhere in a corridor. A man was coughing in the upper gallery. Under all of it, on the far side of the floor, a pair of feet in soft wraps shifted their weight from one to the other, and she was listening to that.

In the fourth exchange there were no more veils. He had spent them. He threw one at the start out of habit, and it came out thin and patchy and hung for less than half a beat. After that he fought her plain, on the hard pale floor under the glass, with nothing to hide behind.

Lira did not need to hear him any more. She could see him. She did not waste the advantage.

Three to nothing, closed early, in a breath under half the bell.

The flags stayed up a long moment after the last touch, and the clerk at the end of the hall called the total. Eight to four.

The fighter behind could not have drawn level with the one exchange remaining, and so the bout was over in four.

The Ash fighter stood on the floor for a moment, looking at his hands. Then he walked across to her and held out one of them, the grey wraps still on it, and she took it.

"You stopped looking," he said. He sounded more interested than anything.

"You made it too dear," said Lira.

He thought about that, still holding her hand, with the grey wraps between them.

"I'll remember it," he said. "Next cycle I'll have something you can't hear." He let go. "You'll have something too, I expect."

"I expect so," said Lira, and meant it, and the two of them walked off the floor in opposite directions like people who would quite like to meet again.

The figures went up on the hall's board eleven minutes later, at the proper interval. Lira's was twenty-six, the Ash fighter's twenty-four.

Karis had won next door in the same hour, and come back through the arch in time to see the fourth exchange from the end of the rail.

She had fought a Rune Path fighter from a river house, a careful, methodical man who laid his marks out on the floor in a slow and beautiful order, like a scribe ruling a page.

Karis had read the order in the first exchange, and in the second she had begun to arrive at each mark half a beat before he finished it. Three and nought; two and one; three and nought.

Eight to one, with two exchanges still to fight and the river fighter unable, even with every point in them, to draw level. The clerk had stopped it after the third.

She came along the rail and stood by Rooke, and he looked at her and nodded, once.

"You two are in different halves," he said. "I'll say it once more, so that nobody has to wonder. Lira's pool and Karis's pool don't meet until the final, and not anywhere before it. So you can stop looking at each other's sheets."

He tapped the program in his hand, open at the two columns. "Solve the bout in front of you."

"I wasn't looking at hers," said Karis.

"You were looking at hers all night," said Lira, coming up the floor to the rail with her wraps still on. "I saw you."

"I was looking at the Ash fighter in her pool. That isn't the same thing."

Lira leaned on the rail beside them and drank half a cup of water at once. Then she turned to the delegation and gave them what she had learned in a single line, as she always did.

"At home they wanted to know if I was good," she said. "Here they want to know if I'm good with my eyes shut."

She put the cup down. "He picked out the one sense I can't do without and sent me the bill for it. In the first round."

Cael wrote her margin that night under the Daeva line and the Umber line, and showed it to her before he closed the book, and she read it and nodded.

*She won by changing, not by being ahead. At the meets she had both. Here there's no room to spare, so the changing has to do all the work. Zerin's figures say Zerin brought her room with her.*

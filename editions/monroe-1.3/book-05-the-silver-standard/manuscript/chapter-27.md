# Chapter 27 — The Corner Game

On the third day out the road came down off the last of the hill pastures into a broad wet valley, and a column passed them.

They heard it before they saw it. There was a jingle of good harness behind the second wagon, and a steady trotting rhythm that did not break when the road went soft. Then it came round the bend: four matched greys pulling a long blue wagon with its sides painted, two more wagons behind it, and six riders spaced along the verge. Every horse was the same height. Every rider sat the same way.

The wagoner pulled over to let it by without being asked. There was no question of not doing so.

"Coast house," said Karis. She was sitting on the second wagon's tailboard with her map of Norhold's provinces folded small on her knee, and she did not need to unfold it. "The colours. And look at the riders' stirrups."

Cael looked. Every rider's stirrup was let down a hole longer than an inland rider would have it.

"Long in the leg," said Karis, pleased. "Everything on that coast is long in the stride. They don't know it, so they've bought their saddles to suit."

The column came level. Its fighters were in the painted wagon, a dozen of them, sitting in two rows with their coats buttoned to the throat against the wet. They looked across at Halcenvane's two plain wagons with open interest. They saw the mud on the wheels and the kit roped under canvas, the reserves on the second bench and Gault's case wedged in among the bags with its handle sticking up. A girl near the back said something to the boy beside her, and he laughed, not unkindly.

Then a rider dropped back from the head of the column and let the painted wagon go on without her.

She was a lean woman of perhaps fifty, in a dark coat with the coast house's colour at the collar, and she rode alongside the second wagon at a walk for a quarter of a mile. She did not look at the kit or the reserves or Gault. She did not look at Lira, though Lira's name was on every Iron sheet on the continent by now. She looked at Cael.

It was not a stare. It was the long, careful look of a person checking a delivery against an invoice. She took in his hands, which were resting on his knees, and his feet on the tailboard, and the way he sat, and then his face. She took her time over each. When she had finished, she was sure of something.

She nodded to him once, a short dip of the head, and touched her heels to her horse and went back up the column at a trot.

"What was that?" said Ephram, from the bench behind.

"Their coach." Rooke had not turned round. He was sitting up beside the wagoner with his hat over his eyes, and Cael had thought him asleep. "She wanted to see the thing she'd read about. Now she's seen it."

"She didn't say anything."

"She nodded." Rooke pushed the hat back an inch. "Between people in this trade, that's a long conversation."

Cael watched the column go away up the valley, the matched greys pulling steadily into the rain. He found that he did not mind the look. A year ago he would have counted it and filed it and slept badly on it. This one had come from somebody who knew exactly what she was looking at, and had looked at it properly, and then gone about her business. She had not wanted anything from him except to know.

He thought of Hesk's letter in the slate country, the line about the ones who measure and the ones who read. The coast coach had done neither, quite. She had checked the cargo, the way a harbour master checks it, and found it was what the papers said, and let it through.

"I've been inspected," he said.

"You've been weighed," said Lira, beside him. She had her collar up and her hands in her armpits. "Get used to it. There'll be thirteen more of her."

---

Brom's snowdrops gave out that evening.

They had lasted three days in his buttonhole, which was longer than anybody had expected. On the third evening, at a waystation with a smoking chimney and a landlord who apologized for it before anybody had complained, Brom took them out and looked at them for some time in his big hand. Then he laid them on the windowsill by his bed, where they could see the road.

Nobody laughed at him. Ephram opened his mouth, and Lira looked at him, and he shut it again.

The road had settled into its shape by then. They rose before light, ate standing in the yard, and were on the road by the first bell. Rooke rode up front and slept, or seemed to. Seln had his corner of the first wagon's tailboard and the travel file on his knees, and he wrote in it at every halt, small and neat, and showed it to nobody. Gault sat with his hand on the case. The reserves took turns walking beside the wagons to keep their feet warm, and the Shield reserve kept his note in his coat, and added to it when he thought nobody was looking.

In the afternoons, when the road ran flat, Cael worked the Log against his knee and charted what there was to chart. Mostly it was other people's traffic. The road was busier every day. There were carters and drovers, and coaches with the mail, and once a whole family moving house with a goat tied on behind. And more and more often, coming up from side roads and joining the main road north, there were other academies.

They were not all as grand as the coast house. One was a single cart with five fighters and their coach in it, and a banner that had been mended so often it was more patch than banner. One was three wagons painted green, with a cook wagon behind. Each of them came onto the road and went the same way, north and east, and none of them turned back.

"Everything's going downhill," said Brom, on the fourth evening, watching another column go past the waystation's gate. "Toward one place."

"Water does that," said Karis.

"Water doesn't know where it's going."

"Neither do half of them," said Karis. "They know where the road goes. It's not the same thing."

---

On the sixth day they lay at a waystation with a walled yard, and Lira asked for the corner game.

It was the best yard they had found. The wall went all the way round, higher than a man, with a stone trough along the north side and an open shed on the east where the carts stood. The ground was beaten clay, old and hard and swept that morning. Cael tried it with his heel while the horses were being watered, and then with one short burst from the trough to the shed door, and came down out of the landing beat feeling the floor's answer in his knees.

Harder than pine, softer than slate. Five a day, he thought, or near it.

Lira was watching him from the trough.

"I want the corner game," she said. "Against you."

He had known she was going to ask. She had been quiet since the first waystation, not unhappy, but turned in on something, and twice on the road he had seen her draw a square in the air with one finger and look at it.

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only person on this road who can watch me build it." She came away from the trough. "Everybody else, I can get into a corner if I'm fast enough. You I can't, because you see me setting it up and you're gone before it shuts. You don't leave faster than me. You leave earlier. It comes to the same thing." She stopped in front of him. "It's the nearest thing I've got to her."

Cael looked at the yard. He looked at Rooke, who was sitting on the edge of the trough with his hat on his knee and a cup of something in his hand. Rooke looked back without any expression at all, which meant yes.

"How many bursts?" said Cael.

"Your five. As many as I like, within Rooke's rule." She was already scoring the clay with her heel. "A square, forty feet, and the corners are a stride in from two lines. If I get a touch on you while you're in a corner, it's mine. If you get to the middle, it's yours. Brom counts."

Brom put down the bag he was carrying and sat on the shed's step and took a spoon out of his pocket.

The others came to the wall to watch. Ephram had his file book. Karis had nothing in her hands at all, which meant she was going to remember every second of it. Seln was at the yard's gate with the travel file, on his way to the stable to see about the horses, and he stopped there and did not go on.

Lira took her line at one side of the square. Cael took the other.

"Go," said Brom, and struck his spoon on the step.

She came hard and wide on the first breath, a burst along the north line that cut him off from the long side of the square. It was a good burst, clean, her landing quick. She had him on the north side now, with the trough behind him and the corner a few strides to his left. One more burst along the short side and the corner would be shut, and he would be in it.

He watched her hips.

He did not have to think about it. The read had been doing it on the oak since long before it had a name. Her weight came down into her right foot and settled. Her hip turned a quarter inch toward the shed. Every Wind fighter he had ever seen did something of the kind before a burst, gathering herself the way a cat gathers on a wall, and Lira's gather was the quickest he knew. But it was still a gather, and the read was still ahead of it.

He left before she went. One step, then a burst of his own, low, along the line she was about to close. By the time she landed on the short side he was past the place where the corner would have been, and walking into the middle of the square.

Brom struck the spoon.

"You saw me," said Lira.

"I saw your hip."

"I know you saw my hip." She went back to her line, breathing quickly. "I'm asking what else."

"Nothing else. That was enough."

She stood on her line for a moment, looking at the clay. Then she nodded, once, and Cael saw her do something to her stance that he could not quite name, a settling lower in the knees.

"Again," she said.

---

The second time she hid the gather.

He could see her doing it, which was the odd part. She had been working on it since the mill town, since Karis had spent her left-hand tell and she had promised to fix it. All through the cold term she had worked on the oak at the far end, alone, folding the gather down into the landing beat of the burst before. She did it now. She came out of her first burst already set for the second, and there was no moment of settling for the read to catch, only a small hard landing and then she was gone again.

She was very good. It was not enough.

The first burst put her down on the north line again, three strides from the trough, and the read did not need her hip after that. Where she had landed told him where she meant to go. There was only one corner on that side worth closing, and only one line she could close it along, and her feet were already pointing at it. The floor she was making said it for her.

"North-east," said Cael, aloud, and stepped out of it.

He was out of the corner before she had finished her second burst. She landed where he had been, and stopped, and stood there with her hands on her knees.

Somebody on the wall laughed. It was the Stone reserve, and he stopped at once.

"That's not fair," said Lira, to the clay.

"It's what she'll do." Cael came back to the middle and stood with her. "You hid the gather. I didn't need it. Where you land says where you're going."

"Then I can't win." She straightened up. Her face had gone very still, which with Lira was worse than temper. "If I hide the set, you read the floor. If I don't build the corner, there's no corner. So I can't win."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to." She looked past him at the trough, at the shed, at the four lines in the clay. "Zerin doesn't need the read. Zerin just goes, and she's there before I've finished going. You've got a warning; she's got legs. It's the same problem from two ends." She was talking quickly now, more to herself than to him. "So the corner can't be something I build. Anything I build, a fast enough person gets out of. It has to be there before I build it."

She stopped.

Cael waited. He had learned, years ago, that this was the part of Lira's thinking that should not be interrupted. It looked like nothing from outside, a girl standing in a muddy yard with her hands on her hips, and it was the most important thing she did.

"Brom," said Lira, without turning round. "How many has he spent?"

"Two," said Brom, from the step.

"And I've spent four." She took her line again. "Again."

This time she did not come at him.

She burst once, short, to a place on the east side that was nowhere near a corner and nowhere near him. It looked like a mistake. Cael felt the read tell him her weight and her direction, and both were wrong for any corner he could see, and he did not move.

Then he looked at where she was standing, and understood that she was standing in his road.

If he went east now, to the middle, he would have to go round her, and going round her would cost him a burst. If he went north, the trough was in his way. The only cheap way out of where he stood was south-west, along the line toward the gate.

He took it. He walked it, because it was free.

Lira moved again, not a burst this time but three quick steps, and stood on the south-west line a little ahead of him. Now that road cost a burst too. The cheap road was north-west, back the way he had come, toward the wall.

He saw it then, or the start of it. She was not building a corner. She was charging him for every road that did not end in one.

He spent a burst to go east round her, because the east was still the best road to the middle. She had expected that and was already there, a stride off his landing, and he had to spend another to get past her. He came down out of the landing beat on the far side and looked for the middle of the square and found the shed in front of him and the east wall at his right shoulder.

He was in the corner. He had bought his own way into it, one fair price at a time, and at no point had he been able to see it being built, because it had not been built. It had been paid for.

Lira's hand landed flat on his shoulder.

Brom struck the spoon, and then, after a moment, struck it again for no reason at all.

Nobody on the wall said anything. Then Rooke put his cup down on the edge of the trough.

"Early," he said.

He said nothing else. He picked up his cup and went into the waystation, and the door swung shut behind him.

Lira stood with her hand still on Cael's shoulder, breathing hard and staring at the place where Rooke had been. Then she took the hand away and looked at it as if it belonged to somebody else.

"Early," she said. "He means I taxed the roads before he needed them. Before you needed them." She turned to Cael. "You couldn't see it."

"No."

"Why not? You see everything."

He thought about it honestly, because she would know if he did not.

"I see weight," he said. "Where it's going and how hard. A corner being built is all weight; you have to put your body where the walls go. That was a bill. You stood in my roads and made them dear, and there's nothing in a hip that says what a road costs." He rubbed the shoulder she had touched. "I can read you building. I can't read you pricing."

Lira was quiet for a moment. Then she took a stub of pencil out of her coat and wrote something on the back of her left hand, small. He did not need to read it.

"It works on you because you pay for your bursts," she said. "You've got five, and you count them, and I can make you spend them." She looked at the hand. "I don't know what she pays. Nobody's ever made her pay for anything."

"Then you'll find out."

"In the first exchange," said Lira. "I know. I know." But she was nearly smiling.

---

They ran it four more times before the light went, and she won three of them.

The one she lost was the last, when Cael stopped trying to reach the middle at all and stood still in the open and let her come to him. She could not price a road he did not mean to take. She stood ten feet away from him for most of a minute with her hands on her hips, and then laughed and said that was cheating, and that Zerin would never do it, because Zerin had never in her life stood still.

"You don't know that," said Karis, from the wall.

"I know it from the times. Nobody with those intervals has stood still since she was twelve."

Cael had spent four of his five bursts by then. The fourth had landed hard, the clay coming up through his heels into the leading hip, and the old line ran from the crest of the hip to the inside of the knee for a few minutes after. That was the floor's ordinary bill. He let it run and did not spend the fifth. The right shoulder had not been asked for anything all day. The other thing, the one with no name on any record, lay quiet where it always lay, costing what it always cost from the moment he woke, spent or not, and asking nothing more.

At supper Lira ate with her left hand flat on the table beside her plate, and every so often she looked down at it.

"What does it say?" said Ephram at last. He could not bear it any longer.

Lira turned the hand over and showed him. It said *Early*, in pencil, already smudged.

"Is that a lesson or a complaint?"

"Both," said Lira. "Pass the bread."

Brom passed it. Then he said, slowly, with the air of a man who has been turning a thing over since the yard, "She'll pay it, though. Zerin. Whoever coached her will have taught her to pay for things early. That's what programs do. They teach you to pay before you're asked."

The table went quiet.

"I know," said Lira. She tore the bread in half. "So it'll work for a while. And then she'll learn it, the way I just did, and then I'll need the next thing." She looked round at them all. "That's fine. I'd rather be beaten by somebody learning than by somebody who already knew."

Cael wrote it that night by the waystation's one good lamp, with the yard dark outside and the clay lines still showing in the starlight.

*Sixth day. Lira's corner game in a walled yard. Two fails and then the version that works: she doesn't build the corner, she prices it. She stands in every road out until every road costs a burst, and lets me buy my own way in. I couldn't see it. The read sees weight; it doesn't see price. Rooke said one word, "Early," and went in to his supper.*

*Four bursts of five on clay. Hip's usual. Nothing else asked for.*

*She thinks it will work on Zerin for a while and then stop working, because Zerin will learn it. She's probably right. She was glad anyway. I've never once seen her afraid of losing to somebody better. Only of losing to the paper.*

---

On the ninth day there was a flag.

It stood on a tall pole in the yard of a waystation at a crossroads, where the north road met another coming in from the west. The waystation was bigger than any they had stopped at, with two courtyards and a smithy, and a board by the gate listing the coaches that called and when. Above it all, on the pole, a banner moved slowly in a wind that was not quite strong enough for it.

It was silver on grey. The silver was a plain bar across the grey, without device or lettering, and the grey had faded at the edges to something nearer white.

Everybody in both wagons looked up at it at the same time. Nobody had told them what it was. Nobody had needed to.

"That's a week off," said Karis slowly. She had the map out now, unfolded across her knees, and her finger was on the crossroads. "We're a full week from the city. More."

The waystation keeper came out to see to the horses, a cheerful bald man with a smith's arms, and Lira asked him about the flag.

"The marshals put it up," he said. "Every cycle. They come down the roads a month before, in a cart, and put one up at every crossroads a week out, and take them all down again when it's over." He squinted up at it. "That's the same one as last time. And the time before. I've been here four cycles, and it's never been a new one."

"Who pays for that?"

"The tournament." He looked surprised that anybody would ask. "Who else? It's their road, while it's on."

Cael sat on the tailboard and looked at the flag for some time after the man had gone.

He had thought of the tournament as a place, a city with a floor in it. He had pictured fourteen delegations going to it, as they would go to a meet, from the outside, and arriving. But here was its colour on a pole a week's drive from its walls, put up by its own people at a crossroads that belonged to nobody, and a waystation keeper who thought it the most natural thing in the world that the road should be the tournament's for a month.

It did not wait for you to arrive. It came out to meet you, a week down every road, and took the road over, quietly, with a cart and a ladder.

"Its writ runs on the roads," he said.

Karis glanced at him and then up at the flag. "Every road in. That's how they've always done it." She folded the map along its old creases. "It's an old charter. Old charters don't stop at walls. They stop where somebody stopped writing."

At the halt that afternoon he took Hesk's travelling clock out of his kit and set it forward an hour, as he did in every tournament week, though the tournament was still nine days off and the clock was already wrong. It seemed to him that the tournament weeks had begun at the flag.

*Ninth day,* he wrote. *Silver on grey on a crossroads pole, a week out. The keeper's seen the same flag four times and thinks the road belongs to the tournament for a month every cycle, and nobody on the road argues. I set Hesk's clock an hour fast this afternoon. It's wrong in the usual direction. It'll be right by accident at the city, same as always.*

*I thought we were driving to a measure. We've been inside it since this morning.*

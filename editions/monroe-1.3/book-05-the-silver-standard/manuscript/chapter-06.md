# Chapter 6 — Par

Gault brought the case.

He came up the stair to the second training floor at the eighth bell of the Second-day carrying it in his right hand, a little away from his body, the way a man carries a thing he is proud of and does not want to seem proud of. It was a plain box of dark oak with brass corners and two brass catches, about the size of a ledger box, and on its lid, riveted through at either end, was a leather handle that had been stitched by somebody who cared about stitching.

Brom, who was sitting on the bench by the wall wrapping his hands, stopped wrapping them.

"That's the handle," he said.

"That is the handle," said Gault. He set the case down on the end of the judges' table, square to its edge, and stood back to let Brom look at it. "Saddler's stitch. Double row. The man who made the frames sent it upriver with a note saying he'd been told I'd been issued a case and he would not have it said that any case of his trade went out with a cheap grip." He lifted the handle an inch, and let it fall back, and it settled without a sound. "I have no idea what to say to him. I've written him three drafts of a letter."

Brom got up, came across the floor, and lifted the case by the handle, once, about a foot off the table, and set it down again.

"Yes," he said, with deep seriousness. "I'd be pleased about that."

"Thank you," said Gault, just as seriously, and the two of them nodded to each other, and the matter appeared to be closed to the satisfaction of everybody concerned.

Rooke had changed the floor. The long room had a ring on it tonight, chalked in a circle about forty feet across, with a rope run round it at waist height on iron stands borrowed from the Crown yard. At the north side of the ring, outside the rope, stood a trestle with five chairs behind it and a lamp at each end, and in front of each chair lay a card printed with a grid of three columns and a pencil. The two third-year reserves were already there, the Shield reserve and the Stone, looking as though they had been told they were sitting an examination and had not been told the subject. Karis sat at the trestle's west end with the grey notebook beside her card. Rooke stood behind the middle chair with a book open in his hands.

It was an old book, thick, bound in a brown cloth that had faded nearly to grey, its spine broken in two places so that it fell open of its own accord.

"The examiner's manual," said Rooke. "My copy. My cycle. The adjudication office issues one to every panel, and every coach who ever fought under the Standard has stolen one." He laid it open on the trestle in front of the middle chair. "Tonight this floor is a meet floor and these five chairs are a panel. Two bouts. Each to two touches inside four exchanges, the way the meets run them. Each fighter rated by all five of us on the three words. Strike high, strike low, average three, add three, and I read the figure out loud where everybody can hear it."

"And the point?" said Lira, from the rope.

"The point," said Rooke, "is that in six days you'll stand in a ring with five strangers at a table, and they'll write three numbers about you that you'll never see them write, and you'll walk out of that ring not knowing why the figure was what it was. I'd rather you knew." He sat. "Enrollee. Ephram. You're first."

He gave them the rules in four sentences, because the meets would give them in forty. An exchange began on Rooke's word and ended on a clean touch, or a fighter out of the rope, or the sand running out in the glass at his elbow, whichever came first. A touch was clean if it landed with intent on the body, not the guard and not the limb that carried the guard, and the panel would say so if it was not. After each exchange both fighters went back to their chalk. "Two touches wins it," said Rooke. "If nobody has two after four exchanges, the panel's figures decide, and you'll find out tonight how little you'll enjoy that."

Gault took the chair at the trestle's east end. Before he sat he opened the case's catches, lifted out a pair of steel-rimmed spectacles he did not normally wear, put them on, and closed the case again.

"I'll mark what the manual says," he said to the table at large, "and not what I happen to know. I mention it so that it's in the room." He picked up his pencil. "I've been an evaluator for long enough to know how hard that will be."

---

They came off their chalk marks at the far sides of the ring, and the first exchange began, and Cael found within a breath that he was fighting two things at once.

The first was Ephram, who knew what Cael had learned in ten days, and had spent those ten days learning what Cael read. He came straight off the mark with the second-year entry, front foot loaded, honest weight, the lath low; and Cael let the first share go by, and watched the heel, and the heel was flat, and flat, and then up, and he was already gone outside it. The lath passed a hand's breadth from his hip. Ephram did not seem surprised. He turned on the miss and came again, and this time the shoulders dropped with the front foot, everything at once, the plain entry, and Cael had to burst to get out from under it.

One burst. He counted it, as he would count every one tonight, aloud in his head. *One.* The leading hip took it, and the landing beat held him, a short locked pause on the far side of the ring, and he used the pause to look.

That was the second thing he was fighting: himself, and what he would let a panel see.

He had six free on the sprung oak. This floor was not the oak, it was the second floor's boards, a little stiffer, a little less forgiving, and he knew from a month of mornings that six here was really five. The record said he had a burst that carried twenty feet and a read that looked like peculiar timing. The record had nothing in it about a burst shut off halfway through its paying, or about a contact that took a blow and did not give it back, or about a plate. So tonight, in front of a panel of five, he would fight with what the record held, at the rates the record held it, and not one thing more. It was the doctrine, and he had chosen it, and it was the first time he had ever had to keep to it in front of people holding pencils.

Ephram came across the ring.

Cael read him: weight forward, left, the shoulders not dropped, so the front foot only, so wait. He waited, and the heel came up, and he went right and outside, and as he went he put his palm flat on Ephram's ribs, a touch, clean, on the line the second weight had just left open, and stepped off.

"Touch," said Rooke, from the trestle. "Enrollee."

They went back to their marks.

The second exchange was long, and it was Ephram's, because he had learned from the first. He stopped throwing the second-year entry at all and began to walk, as he had walked on the morning of the first supervised session, at every angle and every pace, the lath low, never committing. The read told Cael everything about where Ephram's weight was going and nothing about when he would choose to send it. Twice Cael read a step as an entry and moved, and twice it was only a step, and he had moved for nothing, and had spent a little hip on each. *Two. Three.* On the third burst he landed close to the rope. Ephram, who had been walking him there for half a minute without either of them saying so, came in with all of it at once and laid the lath along his forearm as the landing beat held him still.

"Touch. Ephram."

Cael went back to his mark with his forearm stinging and his face doing nothing.

He knew what had happened, and he knew what he could have done about it. There was a thing in him that could take a blow along the forearm and swallow it whole; he had done it once at Ardenmere in front of six hundred people and a ledger. It was not on this house's record. So it had not happened, and the lath had landed, and that was the price of the doctrine, paid in one touch on a practice floor, and he set it down in his head in the column where such things went.

The third exchange he spent learning Ephram's walk. He did not move for any step that did not drop the shoulders, and he let three of Ephram's feints go by so close that he felt the air of the lath. *Four* was a burst that took him clear of the rope and into the open middle of the ring, where Ephram could not herd him. Then Ephram, who had worked out that Cael was now waiting for shoulders, threw a second-year entry with his shoulders held perfectly still. It was the thing he had been practising for ten days in front of the long window's glass. Cael read the front foot, and waited for a shoulder drop that never came, and nearly ate the second weight entirely. He got out of it by a hand. He did not get a touch back. The exchange ran out on Rooke's glass with nobody scored.

"Even," said Rooke.

In the fourth Cael stopped trying to read Ephram's choices at all. He read only his weight, the old way, the first way, early, and answered the first share of everything. Ephram, who had spent the bout teaching him to wait, could not believe he had stopped waiting, and threw the second-year entry three times running into a boy who was simply no longer where the second weight was meant to find him. On the third of them Cael went inside instead of out, under the lath, and laid his palm flat on Ephram's chest.

"Touch. Enrollee. Two to one. Bout to the enrollee."

They stood a moment in the middle of the ring, breathing.

"You stopped waiting," said Ephram.

"You taught me to wait. Then you taught me you'd teach everyone to watch me wait." Cael rubbed his forearm. "So I stopped."

Ephram's face showed, briefly, a craftsman's offence. Then he nodded once.

"That's a good verdict," he said. "I'll take it."

Four bursts, Cael counted, walking to the rope. Four, on a floor where five was his limit, and nothing else: no contact taken. Nothing spent that was not already written down somewhere in a hand the registry could read.

He ducked under the rope, and sat on the bench, and watched the panel mark him.

Rooke read the cards out loud, as he had promised, and Karis did the striking, because she was quicker than any of them.

"Ephram. Execution: eight, eight, nine, eight, nine."

"Strike a nine and an eight," said Karis. "Eight, eight, nine. Eight point three three."

"Control: seven, eight, seven, eight, seven."

"Seven, seven, eight. Seven point three three."

"Effect: eight, eight, seven, eight, nine."

"Eight, eight, eight. Eight." Karis wrote it. "Twenty-three point six six. Twenty-four, rounded."

"Enrollee," said Rooke. "Execution: seven, eight, eight, eight, nine."

"Eight."

"Control: eight, eight, nine, nine, ten."

"Eight, nine, nine. Eight point six six."

"Effect." Rooke paused, very slightly, which in Rooke was the same as a man clearing his throat. "Six, seven, six, seven, six."

Karis's pencil stopped. Then it moved.

"Six, six, seven. Six point three three." She added. "Twenty-three. Exactly."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Ephram, sitting on the bench across the ring with a towel round his neck, looked from the trestle to Cael and back again, and his face went through a short and very private struggle.

"I lost," he said. "And I rated higher."

"By two-thirds of a point," said Rooke.

"I'll take the rating." Ephram put the towel over his head. "He can keep the bout. Fair trade." Then, from under the towel: "I'd still rather have the bout."

Lira laughed out loud at the rope, and the Stone reserve, who had not yet dared to laugh at anything in Rooke's presence, laughed with her and then looked as though he wished he had not.

Cael sat on the bench and did the sums again in his head, though Karis had done them right, because he wanted to see them work in his own hands.

Execution, eight, and that was fair. His feet had been clean and his bursts had been clean and his touches had been flat palms on the right lines.

Control, eight and two-thirds. The panel had seen him own the middle of the ring and keep Ephram's lath out of it, and had marked it high, and he knew why: the read looked like command from outside the rope. It looked like decision. And at the same time, he knew, it had been a kind of trick. He had not decided where the bout would go. He had only known, half a beat early, where Ephram's weight was going, and been there first.

And effect, six and a third.

He looked at the three sixes and the two sevens on Rooke's cards, and understood them, and found that the understanding sat in his stomach like a cold stone.

They were right. Every judge at that table had marked exactly what had happened. He had won a bout without ever once making Ephram do anything Ephram did not already mean to do. Both his touches had been answers. He had not pressed, or driven, or taken ground that was not given; he had read, and arrived, and laid a palm on the space Ephram's own entry opened. The bout had been decided by what Ephram did, and by where Cael was standing when he did it. A panel trained to rate what a performance *did* had looked at that, honestly, five times over, and written six.

And he had chosen it. Every mark in that column was the price of a thing he had decided not to show, at a rate he had decided to keep.

"That's a strong Iron figure," said Rooke from the trestle. He was looking at the cards, not at Cael. "Twenty-three. Every coach in a stand would write your name down for it." He squared the cards. "You'll see it again."

At the trestle's west end Karis was turning the pages of Rooke's old manual, slowly, with one finger on the margin, the way she read anything she meant to come back to. She stopped somewhere about a third of the way in. She read a page there, and then she tore a narrow strip from the bottom of her own scoring card, and laid it in the manual at that page, and closed the book on it, and said nothing at all.

Cael saw her do it, and she saw him see her, and neither of them said a word.

---

"Lira," said Rooke. "Brom."

They had not fought each other on a rated floor since the final, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so. Lira went to her chalk with her wrists strapped and her face entirely calm. Brom went to his with the slow, settled walk he had when a thing was going to be heavy and he meant to carry it anyway. He had his right fist strapped; the knuckles had healed, but he strapped them now from habit, the way some men touch wood.

Cael stood at the rope with his arms on it and watched from outside, and for the first time in his life he watched a bout he was not in with a panel's eyes.

He did not know he was going to do it until it had begun. The first exchange opened, and Lira came off her mark in a burst that took her twenty feet down Brom's left side, and without deciding to, Cael thought: *execution, nine.* It was clean. It was so clean it was almost dull; there was no wasted hand, no wasted step, the landing beat she owned was a sliver of what his own was, and she was moving again before Brom had finished turning. Brom turned, and planted, and took her next burst's shoulder on his forearm at the end of its run and gave it back to her, and Cael thought: *control, Brom, eight. Effect, eight.* Not because Brom had won the moment but because he had made Lira's burst cost her something it had not been meant to cost.

He found he could see the figures forming. Not as numbers in his head, exactly. As a kind of weight on each thing that happened, the way the read put weight on a step before the step was finished. He had spent a month learning what a panel's ten looked like from a table in a map room. Now, outside the rope, he could see par walking about on the floor in front of him, and see where these two came close to it and where they did not.

Lira came close. She came closer than anybody Cael had ever stood across from. In the second exchange she spent three bursts in the time Brom spent one turn, and took him on the shoulder on the third, and walked back to her mark without looking at the trestle at all.

Brom came close in a different place, though he never once looked fast. But in the third exchange, which went long, he did a thing Cael had watched him rebuild across a whole season of Rooke's sheets: he took one of Lira's bursts on the struck side, hard, and did not finish absorbing it before he drove. The seam was gone. The drive came while she was still landing, and the flat of his forearm touched her ribs, and Rooke said "Touch, Brom," in the same voice he used for everything.

Lira stood very still for a moment on her mark after that, and then smiled, briefly, at Brom across the ring. It was not a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a fighter who has been reminded of a thing and is grateful.

She took the fourth exchange. It was not close.

Cael watched how she did it, and for the first time understood it from where a panel would sit. She did not chase Brom. She went to the corners of the ring, one after another, and made him turn to face her at each one. Each turn cost him a little of the ground under his feet, because a man as heavy as Brom does not turn in his own length, while each burst cost her almost nothing. By the third corner Brom was standing on the rope side of the middle with nowhere left to plant. Lira did not even need her fourth burst. She walked in on the third turn's last step, while his weight was still coming round, and laid her palm on his shoulder, very gently. It was the touch a groom gives a horse to tell it he is there.

*Control, nine*, Cael thought, before Rooke had picked up a single card. *Effect, nine. She made him spend his ground. He didn't give it to her; she took it.* It was the very thing his own column had lacked, set out in front of him in four bursts, by the person who had taught him what a burst was.

Rooke read the cards. Karis struck them.

"Lira. Twenty-six point three three. Twenty-six."

There was a small silence along the trestle. The Shield reserve looked at his own card again, to be sure he had really written what he had written.

"Brom. Twenty-three point six six. Twenty-four."

Brom, who had been unwinding the strapping from his right fist, stopped.

"That's the same as Ephram," he said.

"It's the same as Ephram," said Rooke.

"I'm Copper."

"You're Copper on your certificate," said Rooke. "You rated twenty-four tonight against an Iron Rank One with a panel of five, two of whom have never once said a kind word about anybody." The reserves did not look at each other. "The Standard doesn't read certificates. That's the whole of what I've been telling you."

Brom went on unwinding the strap, slowly, and wound it into a neat roll, and put the roll in his pocket. Then he said, to the floor, very quietly:

"That's a number a stranger reads."

Nobody answered him. Lira came across the ring and sat down on the bench beside him, close, with her shoulder against his, and stayed there; and Cael, at the rope, wrote nothing at all, because some things were better left as a space.

---

Afterward the reserves went down the stair arguing about whether they had been too hard on Lira's control, and Ephram went off to the wash-house, and Gault shut the case and carried it away by its handle with the expression of a man walking a good dog. Cael stayed behind and helped Rooke take the rope down.

They coiled it together, in silence, across the width of the ring.

"Twenty-three," said Rooke at last.

"Yes, Instructor."

"With six on effect."

"Yes."

Rooke hung the coil over one of the iron stands. He did not look at Cael. He looked at the chalk ring on the boards, already scuffed half away at the marks.

"In twenty years of coaching I've seen boys rate six on effect because they were frightened," he said. "And because they were tired, and because they didn't know what they were doing yet. I've seen it from boys who were being kind to a friend." He picked up the second stand. "You weren't any of those."

Cael said nothing. It was the nearest Rooke had ever come to asking, and both of them knew it, and Rooke put the stand on his shoulder and went to the stair with it before either of them could find out what would have happened next.

"Six days," he said from the stair. "Rest the hip tomorrow. You spent four on boards that aren't the oak, and you'll pretend you didn't."

He went down.

Cael stood holding the second coil of rope and thought about the question that had not been asked.

Rooke had decided, a long time ago, not to know. Cael had written that down at the end of last year, the first time he understood it: *He never asked what I am.* Rooke put corrections on the sheet and never the cause; he coached the boy in front of him and left the rest alone. A careful farrier shoes the horse he is given and does not ask who owned it before. It was a kind of respect, and it had a cost, and Rooke had paid the cost for a year without once sending Cael the bill. Tonight, with a panel's cards in his hand and a figure on them that said *six*, he had come as near to the edge of it as a man could come without stepping over. Then he had picked up an iron stand and taken it downstairs.

Cael hung the coil beside the other one and found that his hands were not quite steady. He was not frightened of the question. He was frightened of how much, for a moment, he had wanted it asked.

---

Cael stood alone in the long room with the scuffed ring at his feet, and let himself have the thing he had seen, outside the rope, all the way through.

Par was not a number, though he had thought it was one when he sat in the map room and copied out the bands: thirty, twenty-eight, twenty-two. It was a way of moving, a thing you could see from a bench if you knew how to look: clean, and in command, and doing something to the man in front of you. Lira had been near it tonight. Brom had been nearer than his certificate would ever admit. And Cael had been at a table's distance from it all evening, on purpose, choosing his distance mark by mark, while five people wrote down honestly what he had chosen to give them.

The instrument had not been wrong about him. That was what sat in his stomach. It had been perfectly right about everything he showed it. It had measured, to two-thirds of a point, a boy who was not there.

He thought of Karis tearing a strip off her scoring card. He did not know what page she had marked. He thought he would find out, and that he would not like it, and that it would be true.

---

Karis sat over the tournament charter every night that week, late, in the small common room. She always read a governing document before it governed anybody. Cael sat across the table from her with the observation notebook and Rooke's forty-one profiles, which he was learning like coursework.

It was the night after the mock bouts, and five days remained. The fire was down to embers. Brom had gone to bed and Lira had gone to bed after him, threatening to come back down if anybody laughed. Karis had a lamp to herself and the certified transcription open in front of her. She was well into the back of it, past the schedules where she had found the exhibition provision, into the oldest parts of all: the founding articles, printed in a type nobody used any more, in the vocabulary of a century nobody alive had seen.

She read at her usual pace, one finger moving down the margin, even and mechanical, a page every so many breaths. Cael did not watch her. He did not need to; he had sat across tables from her for two years, and he could hear her reading, the turn of a page at its regular interval, the small sound her finger made on the paper.

The page stopped turning.

He did not look up. He counted instead, from habit, while he turned one of Rooke's profiles over and read the back of it, which was blank. Twenty. Thirty. Forty. At forty he heard her pencil.

She wrote for perhaps half a minute. It was not a long passage; he could hear that much from the rhythm of it, a few lines, a note and not a finding. Then the pencil stopped, and he heard the grey notebook's pages turn, back, a long way back, to the front of it, where he knew she kept a certain small family of citations in her neat square hand. The pages stopped. They went forward again. And the charter's page, which had not turned in two minutes, still did not turn.

He looked up then, because there had been a sound he did not know.

Karis was sitting very still with one hand flat on the charter and the other flat on her notebook, looking from one to the other. He had a page on that look. He had been keeping it for two years, in reading rooms and archives, and once at a carrel at Greyvane on an afternoon so dark the lamps were lit by the third bell. It was the look of a reader who has put her foot down on a floor and felt, under the floor, another floor, older, that she had not known was there. From across the table both pages were only grey lines to him. He could see that the charter lay open in its oldest part, the founding articles, older than the schedule where she had found the exhibition provision and older than the clause, written long before the registry's last standardization. And he could see that whatever she had written in the grey notebook, she had written it on the same leaf as something much older.

For a time she sat with it.

Then she closed the notebook on it. She did it gently, the way you close a door on somebody asleep. She closed the charter after it, and stacked the one square on top of the other, and laid her hand flat on the stack.

"Anything?" said Cael.

"A note for the cold term." She stood, and picked up the stack, and held it against her chest in both arms the way she had held the transcription on the day she ran up the stair. "The season comes first. The season has a schedule."

She went up. He heard her on the stair, at her usual even pace, every step the same, and her door, and then nothing.

He sat a while longer with Rooke's profiles in front of him and did not read them.

He did not know what she had found. He did not ask himself to guess. She had put it down, and weighed it against five days and four meets and a roster with five names on it, and the season had won; and of everything he had seen her do since the clause, that was the thing he was surest of. Whatever it was would keep. It had kept a very long time already.

He banked the fire, and put out her lamp and then his, and went up to his own room, and did not go to bed.

The card lay on his desk. Bracken had had a fair copy made for each of the five, on the same cream stock, and Cael's lay square to the desk's edge where he had put it that afternoon, with the fifth line uppermost in Halcenvane's block. *C. Cael, enrolled practitioner, unclassified — scheduled per demonstration-exhibition provisions.* He had read it so many times now that it had stopped looking like writing and begun to look like a shape.

He had not argued for it, or asked for it. Other people's honest words had carried him to it: a clerk's record, a dead board's rule, a girl who read schedules, a registrar's mirror and a chancellor's seal. And somebody, somewhere above all of them, had written an answer to a question and let it go through without a word to anybody on the bluff. He did not know who that was. He would very likely never know. Some silence up there had shaped itself round his name, and he could feel the edges of it, a draught from a door he could not see, and that was all.

And he had an instrument waiting for him now that he wanted honestly, more than he had wanted any number in his life, and that he had already decided, in a cold room with a borrowed card, to manage.

He opened the window. The fountain went on below in the court, the one continuous sentence it had been saying all recess. Beyond the residence roofs the bluff fell away into the dark. The valley lay under no moon, and the town's lamps had gone out street by street until only the ferry lamp was left, small and steady at the landing, with two grey coats under it.

South of the river, where the road climbed out of the valley toward the wool town and the first meet, the road itself showed as a paler seam laid across the black of the fields.

Five days out. It had been a line on a schedule a month ago. Now it was a road he could see from his window, and it was real, and it was going to be walked.

He stood at the window until the bell, and then he went to bed, and for once he took the whole of Hesk's advice.

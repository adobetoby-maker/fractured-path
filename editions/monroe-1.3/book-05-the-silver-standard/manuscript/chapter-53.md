# Chapter 53 — Before, Not After

At the fifth bell of the day before finals, with the guesting-house empty and the light going amber on the courtyard wall, Seln sat down at the document table in the front room and laid his pen on the blotter.

The house was quiet in the way a house is quiet when everybody in it has gone somewhere else at once. The squad was at the hired hall. Rooke was with them. Gault had gone to the healers' corridor at the Concourse to look at the station that would stand at the ring's north break. Bracken was at the west tower with the pouch. On the stair the keeper's cat lay in a square of sun, and down in the courtyard somebody's washing moved on a line.

The table stood under the window. On it, as on every evening of the sitting, the delegation's folders lay in a row, buff card, one to a fighter: renewals of credentials, allotments of floors, protocols for bouts, healers' attestations, the small weather of paper a sitting makes. None was locked; nobody would have troubled to take anything out of them.

He had come back from the west tower an hour before he was expected. He did not account for the hour to anybody, and nobody was there to ask.

For some while he did not write anything. He read, instead, as he read at his counter: the day's last papers first, the healers' attestation for the ring's north station, a revised allotment for the warm-up halls, the tower's note on the hour the ring would be opened for the finals. He initialled each where it wanted initialling and laid it in its folder. A floor allotment in the Ember fighter's folder had slipped a quarter-inch out of true. He squared it.

Then he sat for a time with the pen in his hand and the cap still on it, and looked at the window.

He did not use the delegation's paper. He opened his own case and took from it a packet of plain grey offcuts he kept for notes to the office, and chose one, and laid it on the blotter. With the little knife he kept for sealing wax he cut a strip from it, against the edge of a steel rule, in one stroke. He put the rest of the offcut back in the packet and the packet back in the case.

He looked at the strip.

Then he wrote, in a hand that was not quite the one he used at his counter and not quite the trained hand of any office: a level, middling, careful hand of the sort clerks are taught at the start and grow out of. He did not stop and he did not cross anything out.

*Judges rewatch what they cannot score. Whatever you show tomorrow will be studied frame by frame by people whose profession is categories. Decide tonight what you are content to have studied forever.*

He read it once through. He laid the blotter over it and pressed with the flat of his hand, and lifted the blotter, and tilted the strip toward the window to see that the ink lay evenly in every letter. It did.

He drew the enrollee's folder out of the row.

He went through its papers from the front, one sheet at a time, until he came to the renewal of the delegation credential. Behind that lay the bout protocol for the following day, four pages pinned at the corner. He laid the strip between them, face up. With two fingers he squared it to the protocol beneath, edge to edge, top and side, so that it sat no further in and no further out than any other paper there. He squared the renewal over it. He closed the folder and slid it back into its place in the row, and squared the row with the edge of his palm.

He wiped the pen and capped it. He put the knife and the rule into the case and closed the case and set it on the floor by his chair.

For a little while he sat with his hands folded on the empty blotter, looking at nothing in particular, as he sat at his counter in the wing at the end of a day when the last form had been taken and the copying was done.

The cat on the stair got up, stretched, and went down to the kitchen.

He took the case and went up to his room.

---

At the hired hall Rooke had put on the pads.

He had not held pads for anybody, as far as Cael knew, in the whole time Cael had known him. The Shield reserve had held them all sitting. Tonight Rooke sent the reserve to a stool by the door, took off his coat and laid it over a barrel, and worked the pads onto his own hands a strap at a time, without hurry, frowning slightly at the buckles.

"Hips," he said. "Shoulders. The landing beat, at a walk. You're not learning anything tonight. You're only showing your body where everything is, so it can find it in the dark."

They went at a tenth of anything Cael had. Rooke counted. He counted every touch, out loud, in the flat voice he used at a rail, and moved the pads a hand's width at a time, up and out and back, and Cael followed them, and the hall was nothing but the count and the slap of palm on leather and two pairs of feet on old boards.

Rooke stopped well before Cael would have.

He simply lowered the pads, in the middle of a count, and stood there.

"Eleven short," said Cael. He had been counting too.

"Eleven short." Rooke began to unbuckle. "And you want them. Good. Keep wanting them. Take them to bed with you and don't give them to anybody." He pulled the first pad off. "I've stood beside a lot of men the night before something. The ones who spent the night doing tomorrow did tomorrow tired. That's all the rule is."

He laid the pads on the barrel, side by side.

"I'll be at the rail," he said, and put on his coat. "Whatever comes out of that gate tomorrow. I've made a point all year of not asking what it'll be, and I'm not going to start the night before." He did up a button. "But I'll be at the rail for all of it, and I'll coach all of it, and I'm glad of that. More glad than I expected to be." He looked at the reserve on his stool. "Go and eat, both of you. Early night."

He went out. The reserve looked at Cael with very round eyes and did not say anything whatever, and Cael thought it the best advice he had been given all day.

---

The night before had a form now. This was the third time they had kept it.

The first time nobody had known it was a form. The second had been at Halcenvane, on the eve of Gault's oak, when everybody at the long table had handed him a piece of the next day in place of a present. Tonight it was the back room, after supper, with the shutter closed over the courtyard and paper folded under the table's short leg and the door shut, and the four of them.

Ephram knocked once, early. He had a covered dish in each hand and the cider jug in the crook of his arm. He did not try to come in. He passed it all across to Brom at the door and looked past him at the three of them and at the bare wall Karis had cleared.

"Eat," he said. "Whatever else you do in there. Eat."

He went down the stair whistling the lock-keeper's song, badly, on purpose, and Brom shut the door.

Karis had a rule for the wall, and she said it before she pinned anything.

"Nothing goes up there I can't say where it came from. If I can't, it goes on the door." She pinned a blank sheet to the back of the door at eye height and wrote across its top, *We don't know.* "That's the honesty sheet. By the end of tonight I expect it to be longer than the wall."

It nearly was. Auremont let nobody copy the girl's bouts and never had. The Storm Path kept its workings sealed with the registry like a guild with a secret glaze. Every file Karis had bought on her in three weeks was ceremonies and adjectives. By the time she had written down all the things they did not know, the door had eleven lines on it and the wall had none.

"Then we start from the trial," she said, "and from what we saw. Not what we'd like."

The first model was a pipe.

Karis drew it plainly. The lane was a long narrow pipe, cleared of air all at once from end to end, and Daeva went down it the way a pea goes down a blowpipe, with nothing in front of her to slow her. It explained the speed. It explained the crack at the lane's mouth, the snap of air coming back in behind. She pinned it up and stood back and looked at it with her head on one side.

"No," said Brom, from the door.

They all looked at him.

"Nothing in it to lean on," he said. "I've spent my life taking things on these arms. You can't change your line without something to push off. In the trial she bent. Once at the north-west end and once coming at Karis, a little, at the end of the lane." He did not uncross his arms. "A pea in a pipe goes straight."

Karis studied the chart while she counted to some number of her own. Then she unpinned it herself, one pin at a time, and laid it face down on the table, and wrote on the door: *A pipe. Dead. Brom: she bent.* And then, under it, *Most of an hour.*

"That's good," she said, and they could all hear that she meant it. "That's an hour we didn't spend finding it out tomorrow."

The second model was a river.

The lane was a fast narrow current of air laid down along a line, and she rode it as a barge rides the ebb out of the basin. It let her bend. It let her keep her pace without seeming to work. Karis liked it better than the first, and so did Cael, and she had just finished pricing it on the wall when Lira, flat on her back by the stove with her eyes shut, said, "No."

"Why?"

"I was close enough to touch it." Lira did not open her eyes. "At the north-east ramp. I've stood next to Winds going past since I was smaller than the reserve. You feel them in your teeth before they arrive. You feel the air go by." She opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling. "My hair didn't move."

Karis put the pen down.

"Nothing," said Lira. "Not a hair. A river that fast, I'd have felt from the gallery."

The river went face down on the pipe. Karis wrote it on the door, and then stood at the bare wall with her pink palms pressed together under her chin and looked at nothing for so long that Brom eventually sat down on the floor.

"Cael," she said. "Go back in. Slowly. From before she moved."

He shut his eyes and went.

It was not free. Going back into a charted exchange at a tenth of its speed cost what it always cost, a dull hold behind the eyes that he would still feel in the morning. He went back to the barrier's south-east end and the girl standing at its foot, and the read catching something wrong along the timber, and he stayed there, in the second before the second, and made himself look.

"The air was wrong before she was," he said. "All along the barrier. Heavy where nobody was standing."

"Heavy, and then what?"

"And then it creased." He kept his eyes shut. "As if somebody had folded it. A long fold, all down the timber, a couple of her strides out in front of her. Then the lane, in the fold. Then her." He opened his eyes. "She's last. The fold, then the lane, then her. She arrives third."

Karis wrote *she arrives third* on a clean sheet, and *trial, read live* in its corner, and pinned it alone in the middle of the wall. Then she stood and looked at it.

"Not empty," she said slowly. "Not moving. So the air's still there, and it's still. It's only stopped being in the way." She turned round to them. "It's allowed. She doesn't run. She walks along a line the air has agreed to stand back from. Every instrument in this city has been timing her as if she were running, and she isn't. She's going through a door at a walk." She picked up the pen. "*Permitted.*"

She drew it: the fold, the lane in the fold, the girl last, and at the lane's far end the fire, riding the place where the air had been asked hardest. It went up on the wall, and nobody could take it down.

She tried. She argued against her own model for half an hour, which was the part of the night Cael would remember best, Karis at the wall with her arms folded, pulling at every joint of what she had built. Where does the noise come from? From the fold, before anything moves; that was the pressing in his ears. Why does her stride show nothing? Because it is not her legs doing the work. Then why, said Karis at last, very quietly, did she stop?

None of them had an answer. Karis wrote it on the door.

"That one's hers," she said. "Not the lane's. I can't chart a person off ceremonies." She capped the pen. "You'll have to take it out there with you as it is. You'll be the only one in that building close enough to read it."

Then she drew a box on the wall, round one line, and drew it twice.

"The fold comes first," she said. "Half a beat ahead of the lane. Her body won't show you anything. Don't look at her body. You did it this morning, with Lira, in the hall. You put Lira down and listened past her." She tapped the box. "Do that. At her. Half a beat is all I've got for you. Everything on this wall comes to half a beat."

"Half a beat's a fortune," said Cael, and saw her shoulders come down half an inch.

At the end she wrote it on a card, the whole night, small, in her tightest hand. *Fold first. Half a beat. Listen past her to the air. The lane ends where the leave ends. What she owns, she bought ahead.* He read it four times. Then he gave it back, because a card could not go into a ring, and she opened the stove door and laid it on the coals, and the four of them watched it go.

Brom had been standing by the door again for an hour, since the second model. He spoke when the card was ash.

"You've never fought anyone where survival was the win condition."

He waited until it had gone all the way round the room.

"There's always been a number," he said. "Something to come out under, or over. The ganger. The grappler. The captain. You were always steering, right up to the bell, whatever it cost." He moved his weight. "Tomorrow there's no steering. There's being upright at the end, or not. Make every choice out there against that one. Score is for the judges."

He looked at his own bound forearms.

"And if she takes the upright away from you, and she may, go down well. Chin in. Arms in. Loose, not stiff. Nothing left out for her to have. Going down well is a skill," he said. "I've been putting it in you a bit at a time since the circuit, and you never knew. Tomorrow you'll find out if it took."

Lira stood up off the floor and went to the door.

She had her hand on the latch when she turned round. At Halcenvane, the night before Gault's oak, she had said a thing like it, in other words, and it had been true then. She said it now in the words it had become.

"The person who walks onto that floor has to still be you."

She went out into the passage and shut the door behind her. They heard her go to the end of it and stand there for a while, and then come back with a fresh candle, because the night was not finished and she had only gone out so that the line could be the last thing said before the business.

The business had a form of its own, Karis's, and they had kept it every time.

Four present, nobody else. No record but the log. Nothing decided until the other side had been argued, properly, by somebody who had once believed it. And the minute written down before the thing was done, never afterwards, because a minute written afterwards was a story about a decision, not the decision.

Karis argued first, before anybody had proposed anything, because she was the one who had held the other side.

"Here's the case for keeping the bank shut," she said. "You go out tomorrow inside your rates, as you've gone out to every floor this year. She stops you, early. First exchange or second. You lose a bout in which you were never supposed to win anything, in front of a city that already thinks you're brave for walking out. Nothing goes on the record that wasn't there before. Every file stays wrong in the way we've built it to be wrong." She laid her pen down beside her hand. "That was my side, the morning the filing went up. I sat with it the whole day."

"What moved you?" said Brom.

"The word *early*." Karis looked at the candle. "An early stoppage under that woman isn't a line in a record. It's a healers' report. I read the rider. I read the protocols. She crossed his ground in the trial without looking at him, and he had half a second. Give her all her attention and keep him inside his rates, and the cheap loss isn't cheap. It might not be a loss you get up from." She picked the pen up. "That's my side argued. I've left it. I'm for spending."

"Then I'll say it," said Cael.

He named the bank, every piece of it, because the minute had to have the names.

"Wind past six, if the fight wants a seventh, and the bill comes in the morning. The push I took from the Bronze at Ardenmere, at a fighting load, past the four blows a sitting we've held it to; it hasn't carried a load anybody could see since the circuit record. Reydan's give, on a scored floor in front of a crowd, which it hasn't been since the bout at Ardenmere, and that bout's the only paper it's on. Karis's spark past the two contacts we allow in public. And the read, as far down as it'll go." He looked at them. "All of it, at whatever she asks for. Ceilings, not rates."

"And the price," said Lira.

"And the price." He did not look away. "Every evaluator who's ever set a plate in front of me measured me against those rates. Every shop that's sold a file on me wrote it against them. They're the reason I've been left alone as much as I have. After tomorrow they're gone, and whatever I show is on the record, and nobody's ever going to measure me against less again. There's no shutting it once it's open." He spread his hands on the rocking table. "A rate you have shown is a rate you have shown forever."

"Spend it," said Lira. "With the line from the door. All of it but him."

"Spend it," said Brom. "I've held pads for you inside the rates since I've known you. Tomorrow I'll see what I was holding them for." The corner of his mouth went in. "So will you."

"Then it's agreed," said Karis, and wrote.

He wrote the minute into Hesk's book while they watched, under the ring, in his best hand.

*The night before. The four present. The bank is spent, from the flags: Wind past six; the push at a fighting load; Reydan's give on a scored floor; the spark past two; the read to the bottom. The other side argued by the one who held it, honestly, and left honestly. Agreed by all four. Written before, not after, so that whatever tomorrow's record says, this page says first that it was chosen, at this table, by the four who made the bank.*

"And the rest of it stays where we put it," said Karis, when he had blotted the page. "The minute from the year before. Nothing made to happen. Only inside a fight somebody else begins. The cost on paper first." She looked at him steadily. "That hasn't changed because the fight's bigger."

"It hasn't changed."

He did not put the pen down.

"There's one more thing," he said, "and it has to be said in here or nowhere." He looked at the candle, not at them. "The quiet thing stays sealed."

The room went still. Then Lira leaned forward on the rocking table, because the form said somebody must argue and she was the only one of them who could make it hurt.

"Then price the seal," she said. "Out loud, so the room has it." She held his eyes. "It's a thing that makes you hard to find, in a fight against a woman whose whole art is knowing where you'll be. It's a thing that hides your going, against a woman who lays her road before you've gone. Of everything in that vault, it's the one that might have been made for tomorrow. And you're leaving it in the vault." She sat back. "Say why."

"Because of who it came from," said Cael.

He did not say the name. They all knew it. It would never be said in this room or any other while any of the four of them could stop it.

"Tomorrow's going to be written down three times over and read for years by people who do nothing but sort what they read," he said. "If I use it, one of them will ask one question. Where would a practitioner with no tier ever have seen that sort of work done, well, and long enough to take any of it? And the question will get up and start walking. And every road it can take ends at a quiet man at a counter whose whole life is not being looked at twice." He breathed out. "The debt doesn't stop because the bout's big. He doesn't get told. He never gets told. That's all of it."

"That's all of it," said Lira, very quietly, and wrote nothing, because nothing about it was ever written.

Nobody wrote it down, and nobody ever would. What the four of them owed the man at the counter would go on being owed, as it had been since a bad night on a stair a year ago, near enough: kept off him the way you keep rain off somebody by holding a coat over them without saying you are holding it. They had only ever had one coin to pay it in. They paid it again tonight.

---

Cael found the strip at midnight, because he could not sleep and went down to read the protocol again, and the protocol was in his folder.

It lay squared between the renewal and the protocol's first page. He read it standing in the dark front room by the light that came down the stair. He read it twice. Then he went to the stove.

The poker hung on its hook. The fire had sunk to a low red bed behind the iron door. He opened the door, and stopped with his hand still on it.

Somebody had already broken the ash.

It had been done lately, and done thoroughly: the whole bed raked fine, the grey on top of the red broken down small and even, as though a hand had stood where he was standing a little while before with the poker and got the stove ready for something. The poker was back on its hook. The room was empty. The stair was dark all the way up.

He did not let himself think about whose hand. He laid the strip flat on the red. It browned at one corner and blackened across its middle and caught, and was flame for a breath, and was nothing. He watched until there was nothing. Then he took the poker down and broke the new ash into the old until no one could have told the one from the other, as Karis had done on the common-room coals at Halcenvane on the only other night.

There had been one before. Now there were two. Nobody had ever said one word about the first, and nobody ever would about this.

He wrote it in the old volume, in its very last leaves, in the smallest hand he owned.

*Second one. He knows the bank's open tomorrow. He wasn't in the room and nobody told him. He read it off the day the way Karis reads a page of a charter, from what's there and what isn't.*

*The first told me how to be measured. This one says I'm going to be, for good, and that the only choice I've got left is which of me goes in their books. He doesn't know we made that choice an hour ago. It's the same choice. He got there from the other side of the counter.*

*It cost him a strip of grey paper. He'll never once hear that I know what it cost him to write it.*

---

The house did not sleep. It lay down in its separate rooms and pretended, all of it at once.

Cael lay in the dark with the shutter open a crack on the harbour and listened to it pretending. Above him, on the bare boards of the room under the roof, Brom was doing his nineteen, very slowly, with a soft thump at the end of each that he plainly thought nobody could hear. Cael counted them to nineteen. There was a pause, and they began again. Across the passage Karis's pen went and stopped and went, the stops much longer than the going. Next door Lira was humming the turn of the lock-keeper's song under her breath, over and over, while something in her kit clicked shut and open and shut. And at the foot of the stair the keeper went to bed past every door, as he did every night, and Cael heard him count them softly, from habit, to five.

He got up and ate what was left in Ephram's dish, standing at the window, cold. It was easier than lying there.

He went through it once more, in his head. Five cleared, from the flags, as far as each would go. One sealed, for a man who would never hear why. The still place from session nine, untouched in its three words. Karis's half beat. Brom's way of going down. Lira's line at the door. A strip of grey paper in the stove, broken fine.

After that he did the ordinary things, the ones that change nothing and are done anyway. He wiped Hesk's steel pen and laid it in the book's gutter. He stood his boots by the door with the laces already loosened, and folded the morning's shirt over the back of the chair.

Last of all he took Hesk's travelling clock down off the sill. It had been wrong every single day he had owned it. He wound it and pushed the hands an hour ahead, the tournament-week habit, so that his first act on waking would be to mend a mistake he had made on purpose.

He opened Hesk's book under the lamp, past the ring and the minute, to the next clean leaf, and wrote slowly.

*Quenna asked if I could not-do it in front of people who wish me harm. I learned to. It's under everything else I know how to do. It's held in front of wardens and panels and men with plates, and in front of a bowl of eight thousand people on their feet, and I never once had to wonder.*

*Tomorrow's question is whether I can afford to keep not-doing it in front of someone who can kill me by accident.*

*I don't think I can.*

*Write it down before, not after.*

He blotted it and closed the book and laid his hand flat on its cover for a moment. Then he put the lamp out and lay down, with the harbour moving under the window and the clock on the chair ticking an hour ahead of the whole city, and waited for the morning, so that he could put it right.

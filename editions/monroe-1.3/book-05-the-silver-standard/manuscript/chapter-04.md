# Chapter 4 — A Mirror

The long table in the records hall had been built for forty clerks, and on the first night of the drafting it held one, with a lamp at each elbow.

Karis had spread the charter's three copies down its length in a row, open at three different places, with the certified transcription at the head of the row like a magistrate at the head of a bench. Beside them lay a stack of clean foolscap, four pencils sharpened to the same length, the memorandum with its short-inked type, and the grey notebook, open at the page where the clause had been copied out on the afternoon before the recess with one word beside it. Bracken had brought the copies down himself after the last bell, had set them out in the order she asked for, had told the night porter that the hall was in use and why, and had gone home. He would come back, he said, when there was something to check. He did not like to watch people draft. It made him want to correct them before they had finished being wrong.

Cael sat at the far end with the observation notebook, because he had told her at supper that he would read every version, and she had said that he would.

It was not a request for approval. Karis had been plain about that, over the bread. A document about his enrollment, going out of the house under the house's seal, that he had not read from the first word to the last, was a kind of thing that neither of them would allow into the world. So she would draft, and he would read, and he would say nothing at all unless something in it was untrue.

"Not unwise," she said. "Not unkind. Untrue. Those are the only notes I'll take."

"Agreed."

"Don't agree. Read."

So he read. He read the first page of the first version, which was the clause, set out whole in the middle of a sheet with nothing round it but its citation: the charter's edition by its printing year, the sixth part, the fourth subsection, the line. *Participating academies shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.* Below it Karis had written a single sentence, and struck it out, and written another, and struck that, and then written nothing, so that the clause stood alone at the top of the page like a post in an empty field.

"You took your sentences off it," he said.

"Everything I put under it made it weaker." She was writing the second page and did not look up. "Anyone who reads that line and doesn't understand it won't understand it any better with me standing next to it explaining."

At the eleventh bell she had three pages in rough and none she liked. At the twelfth she had four, and liked one. The lamps had begun to smoke. The stone hall beyond the long room was dark except for the night porter's candle at the door, moving now and then as he shifted on his stool. Once, very late, the porter came in with two cups of something hot that neither of them had asked for and set them down at the end of the table without a word and went out again. Karis looked at the cups for a long moment as if she had found a clause she had not expected.

"The bracket rules," she said, at last, into the quiet. "By the memorandum, no seeding means no fielding. The fielding's ours, and the seeding's the host's. But it isn't enough to say the host's problem is the host's problem. That's a door slammed. I want a door held open." She laid her pencil down. "Somewhere in this charter there's a line that tells a host what to do with a fighter it can't seed. There has to be. People don't write *any practitioner* and then leave no road for the ones they didn't think of."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then I'll write four very good pages about a road that isn't there," said Karis, "and they'll be the best four pages the regional registrar has ever been sent, and he'll still send them back." She picked the pencil up again. "Go to bed. You've read everything I've written tonight, and nothing in it was untrue, and I'm going to sit here for an hour and be angry with the fifth part."

---

She found it on the second day, in the middle of the afternoon, and she came up the residence stair at a run.

Cael heard her before he saw her. Nobody on the bluff had ever heard Karis run; she walked everywhere at the same even pace, as though the distance between two places were a sum she had already done. But there were feet on the residence stair taking it two at a time, and a door banging on the floor below, and Brom's voice on the landing saying "What—" in the tone of a man flattening himself against a wall. Then she was in Cael's doorway with Bracken's transcription held against her chest in both arms and her hair come half out of its pins.

"Schedules," she said. "The third schedule. Not the fifth part at all. It's tucked into a schedule at the back, between the fee table and the rules for travelling surgeons, where nobody has read it in forty years because nobody has needed to."

Brom had come up behind her and stood in the passage, enormous and alarmed.

"Is somebody hurt?" he said.

"Somebody," said Karis, "has been very clever, a long time ago, and then forgotten." She came in, laid the transcription open across Cael's desk on top of his ledger, and put her finger on a line about halfway down a page so close-printed it looked grey. "Read it aloud. I want to hear it in somebody else's voice."

Cael read it aloud.

"*Practitioners for whom no tier seeding is applicable shall be scheduled per the demonstration-exhibition provisions.*"

He stopped, and looked up.

"Keep going," said Karis. "Two paragraphs down. The provisions themselves."

He found it. "*Demonstration performances are rated against the Standard for performance rating only.*"

Lira had arrived by then too, in her stocking feet with a boot in one hand, drawn by the noise. The four of them stood round the desk in the small room with the window open and the fountain going below, and looked at a page of grey type that had been waiting there, unread, for longer than any of them had been alive.

"It's a fossil," said Karis. She had got her breath back, and her voice had gone level again, the voice she used for findings. "Before the registry standardized its classifications, there was an older way of evaluating practitioners who didn't fit the tables: you put them on a floor and watched what they could do. Demonstration. The charter was written in that era, and it kept a door for those people. Then the registry tidied everything up, and every practitioner on the continent got a tier and a rank, and nobody needed the door any more. So nobody ever used it." She tapped the page. "But nobody ever struck it out, either. It sat here. Unrepealed and unfashionable and perfectly good, just like the assay clause sat in Greyvane's charter until somebody needed it."

"So a fighter who can't be seeded—" said Brom slowly.

"—can be fielded. The charter answers its own objection. It says where to put him. The host doesn't seed him into a bracket. The host *schedules* him, under the exhibition provisions, and the panel rates what he does against the Standard, for a performance rating and nothing else." She took her finger off the line. "There's my door held open. The registrar doesn't have to swallow anything. All he has to do is turn three pages."

Lira put her boot down on the floor, very carefully, as though it might go off.

"So he fights," she said.

"So he's scheduled," said Karis. "Under a provision forty years older than any of us. Which means somebody at the first meet has to read it, and work out how to run it, and find out what a demonstration performance is in this century." She closed the transcription. "That's their problem. I've just solved ours."

Cael asked her what a demonstration had looked like, in the years when it was still done.

"Nobody knows exactly. There's a paragraph in the provisions about the floor, and another about the panel, and a third about how the rating's to be entered, and none of it's been used in living memory." She pushed a pin back in, uselessly. "The panel watches. The practitioner does what he does. They write down how well he did it against the mark. That's all a demonstration ever was, I think: being looked at properly by people who had agreed beforehand what *properly* meant." She glanced at him. "It's not so different from your provision. It's just older, and nobody's angry about it yet."

Brom looked from her to the closed book and back.

"You ran," he said. "Up the stair."

"I know."

"I've never seen you run."

"I've never found a schedule before." She pushed her hair back into its pins, without much success. "Don't write it down."

Cael had already written it down. He kept that to himself.

---

On the third night the four pages lay on the long table in a row, each in its final hand, and Bracken checked them.

He did it standing, with his spectacles on the end of his nose and the three charter copies open in front of him, and he did not read the pages as arguments. He read them as a list of claims about where things were. Every time a page said *sixth part* he turned to the sixth part. Every time it said *third schedule* he turned to the third schedule and laid his finger on the line and read the line aloud, in a low voice, and then read the page's quotation of it, and then said "Yes," and moved on. When a page gave a year he asked Karis where the year came from, and she told him, and he went to the shelf and found the volume and checked it. He took an hour and three-quarters over four sheets of foolscap. He found no error.

"Good," he said, and took his spectacles off.

Page one was the clause, standing alone at the top of its sheet with its citation, as she had left it on the first night.

Page two was its history. Forty years ago a house with a long name and a short temper had kept two of its strongest practitioners off a qualifying side, both fit, both enrolled, to sweeten a bargain about postings with a richer house. The tournament's board, finding out, had written the fourth subsection into the sixth part the following year in language meant to make sure it could never be done again. *Shall field.* Not *may*. The duty ran against the house, and the house was performing it.

Page three set the memorandum's own sentences beside the charter's, line against line, so that the gap between them showed. Seeding was the host's business under the bracket rules. Fielding was the house's business under the sixth part. The two words never met. And for the practitioner whom no seeding could reach, the third schedule had already laid down a road: scheduled per the demonstration-exhibition provisions; rated against the Standard for performance rating only. The charter had foreseen the objection and answered it before the objector was born.

Page four was one paragraph long, and it was Bracken's.

He had asked for it. That was the only time in two years Cael had known him ask for anything. On the second night, while Karis was still fighting the fifth part, he had stood at the end of the table and said, "If there's to be a last page, I'd like to write it," and Karis had looked at him and handed him the pencil.

It said that the enrollment record was true, and had been certified so, without omission, as the charter required. It said that the house stood ready to comply with the charter's mandatory language in every particular. And it said that if the regional registrar should determine that a true enrollment record must be altered before it could be accepted, the house respectfully requested that instruction in writing, over the registrar's name.

Cael read it twice. Then he looked up.

"That last line's a trap," he said.

"It's a mirror," said Bracken. He was cleaning his spectacles on his cuff, and he did not stop. "A trap catches you moving. A mirror just stands there. Let him sign an order to make a true record false, if he likes. The mirror will keep his name on it for as long as there are people left to read it." He hooked the spectacles back over his ears. "He won't sign it. Nobody signs that."

"Then what will he do?"

"What any careful officer does with a rule he doesn't like and can't break." Bracken gathered the four pages, knocked their edges square against the table, and slid them into a clean folder. "He'll write a short note asking for guidance, and send it to somebody senior, and go home early with a clear conscience. Then it's somebody else's afternoon." He tied the folder's tape. "Mind you, I don't blame him. I'd do the same, in his chair."

"Would you?" said Karis.

Bracken considered it honestly, which was the most alarming thing about him.

"No," he said. "I'd have read the third schedule first. But I've had twelve years at this counter to get into bad habits." He put the folder under his arm. "The chancellor signs tomorrow evening. I'll take it up at the sixth bell. She's asked for the enrollee to be there."

---

Withrow's office was at the top of the old range, up a stair that turned twice, and it looked just as it had looked a year ago, which Cael found he had been relying on.

A long desk bare of everything but the work in hand. A wall of grey file cases, each labelled in the same hand. Above the cases, framed and glazed and hung at the height of a person's eyes, the house's own charter, the founding one, its ink gone brown, hung there not as an ornament but where she could point at it. One window looked down the bluff to the river. The lamp on the desk was already lit, and the folder with the four pages lay beside it, open.

"Sit," said Withrow. "I want a witness who isn't on my staff."

He sat in the chair across the desk from her. Bracken stood by the door. Withrow read the four pages through, slowly, at her own pace, initialling each margin where her initials already stood on an earlier draft. She read the fourth page twice. Then she put the pen down, squared the pages, and folded her hands on top of them, and looked at him across them.

"When this seal goes down," she said, "the house stands behind you fighting this season, in front of everyone, start to finish. You should know why. I'll say it once, and then you need never wonder. A great many people this year are going to offer you their opinion of my reasons. Most of them will say ambition, and most of them will be about half right." Her face did not change. "I want this house at Norhold. I've wanted it since before you came up the road. Your enrollment helps, and I know it helps, and I'm not going to pretend to you or to anybody else that I don't know it."

Cael waited. He had learned that Withrow did not need encouraging.

"Now look at the sequence. It's the only part that matters." She lifted one finger from the stack. "First there was a house. I spent a good many years making it a house that does difficult things in writing, in front of witnesses, and gets them right." A second finger. "Then there were rules: their charter, written by their own board, forty years before you were born. Nobody here wrote a word of it." A third. "Then there were people who keep the rules as written. A registrar who signs true records because he can't bear to sign any other kind. A girl in a reading room who reads the whole of a thing, including the schedule between the fee table and the surgeons." She did not glance at Bracken by the door. "And last of all, there was you."

She let the fingers lie flat again.

"Take your line off that roster," she said, "and every other sheet in this folder reads exactly as it reads tonight. The clause would still be there. The record would still be true. The schedule would still be where Karis found it. That is why it will hold. A thing built round one person comes down when that person slips. A thing built straight stands whoever stumbles in it." She picked up the pen. "I learned that last term, with an Archmarshal sitting in my records hall for eleven days. I'd been told it before. I'd never been shown."

She signed. She signed in full, her whole name, slowly, and Bracken came forward from the door with the seal and the wax, and the house's mark went down beside her name.

"Bracken," she said.

"Chancellor."

"The morning courier, please. Not the noon one."

"The morning one," Bracken agreed, and took the folder, and went.

Cael sat in the chair with his hands on his knees and found he had nothing ready to say. He had expected, he realized, to be told he was valued, or to be told he was a risk, and he had readied himself for either. He had not readied himself to be told that he came last in the order of things, after a house and a set of rules and the people who kept them, and that this was the reason the thing would stand. It was the least flattering account of himself anyone had ever given him to his face. It was also, he thought, the safest place in the world to be standing: at the end of a line of other people's honest work, where nobody could pull him out without pulling all of it down.

He looked up at the framed charter above the file cases, the brown ink and the old seal, and Withrow saw him look.

"The house's," she said. "Somebody wrote that a long time ago, too, for reasons of their own." She did not smile. "I read it once a year. It still says what it says."

Withrow sat back in her chair. The lamp made a small bright room of the desk and left the file cases in shadow.

"Go and train," she said. "If their charter holds, you've a season starting in a little over three weeks. If it doesn't, you've a quarrel starting rather sooner, and I'd like you fit for that too." She took her spectacles off, which in Withrow was the same as a door opening. "Thank Karis for me. Don't let her refuse it. Tell her it's from the chancellor and she has no standing to decline."

---

The first of Ephram's sessions fell on the morning after the signing, at the second bell, with Rooke at the rail of the second training floor and nobody else in the room.

It was the floor time the sheet had asked for, and it was not what Cael had expected. He had thought Ephram would come at him with the second-year entry until one of them dropped. Instead Ephram spent the first half-hour doing nothing but walking: walking at him off the chalk, at every angle the floor allowed, at every speed from a stroll to a lunge, with the lath held low and never once thrown, while Cael stood and called the weight aloud as it came.

"Front," said Cael. "Front. Both. Front. Front. Both."

"Both," Ephram agreed, on the third. "You called that one before my back heel knew."

"I called it off your shoulders. They drop when you're bringing everything."

Ephram stopped walking.

"Do they?" He tried it, standing still, loading the front foot and then both feet, and watched his own shoulders in the long window's glass. "They do," he said, in a tone of real annoyance. "Nobody's ever told me that."

"Nobody else reads shoulders that early."

"Rooke does."

"I didn't say anything," said Rooke from the rail, without looking up from his board. "I've been waiting a year for somebody else to."

Ephram looked at the rail, and then at Cael, and for the first time since Cael had known him his face did something that was very nearly a laugh, quickly mastered.

"Then that's mine to fix," he said, "and the heel's yours. Fair trade. Again."

In the second half-hour they worked at speed. Cael beat the second weight four times in ten, waiting for the heel or, when the heel was too quick, for the dip of the shoulders that told him it was coming. He ate the plain entry three times. Once he ate nothing at all, because Ephram, now thinking about his shoulders, threw the entry so badly that it went a yard wide and both of them stood looking at the lath in reproach.

Rooke wrote on his board. At the end of the hour he turned it round so that they could both read it, which he had never done before.

*Enrollee: heel, one in three, nearer two. Ephram: shoulders. Good trade.*

He turned it back.

"Again on Fourth-day," he said. "Same hour. Bring the shoulders."

Then the silence began, and the house went on preparing for a season with its eyes turned very slightly toward the road.

The response had gone down the bluff on the morning courier of the Sixth-day. After that, nothing came back up. The regional registrar did not write. The memorandum's fourteen days went on running down on the calendar behind Bracken's counter, one day a morning, each crossed off in his small neat stroke. The coats at the ferry landing and the road's foot changed on the bell, two and two, and Karis's marbled book got a line a day, and every line was the line above it.

Nobody spoke to Cael about any of it. He put it together the way he put everything together, out of the things people were careful not to do.

Rooke did not post the first meet's bout targets. Every other week on the calendar board had its targets pinned beside it in Rooke's hand, and the first meet's week had a clean space the size of a sheet where they would go. Bracken's clerk opened a travel file for the delegation and wrote out its cover in full, every name and wagon and stable fee, and left the date line blank. The wagoner came up from the town to look at the axles and went down again without being told which morning to come back. And at the counter on the Fifth-day, when Cael brought the week's coursework, the teaching assistant took it, read the head of it, and laid it in the tray face down, square to the others, and said "Mm," and that was all, as it should have been.

Five people's seasons sat on one line of a roster, and the line was his. If it failed, Lira's first season at Iron would open at a table instead of on a floor, and Brom's first chance to be priced by strangers with it, and Karis's, and Ephram's. He could not stop doing the sum. It came to meals with him and sat in his mouth instead of food.

The floor, at least, did not wait for anybody. On the Fourth-day Ephram came to the second session with his shoulders held so still that he looked like a man carrying a full tray across a crowded room. He threw the second-year entry at Cael twenty times in an hour, and Cael beat the second weight eleven of them by the heel alone, because there was no longer any shoulder to read. On the ninth throw he ate the plain entry clean across the ribs and said nothing. On the fourteenth Ephram's shoulders dropped again from tiredness, and Cael called it aloud before the heel had moved, and Ephram muttered something at his own shoulders and went back to his chalk. Rooke's board, at the end, said only *Better. Both.* Cael read it upside down on his way to the water jug, and carried it back up to the residence with him like a coin in his pocket, and turned it over at supper when the sum came back.

Hesk's answer came up on the evening coach of the Third-day, the fourth day of the silence, and it was four lines long.

Cael took it from his pigeonhole in the cloister and read it standing there, under the lamp by the porter's lodge, with second-years going past him in both directions on their way to supper. He had stopped trying to wait until the stair with Hesk's letters. He had never once managed it.

*A list is an honest thing if the man who keeps it is. Sounds like yours is. Mind your feet on strange floors, and pack wool; a hall is always colder than the man who booked it says. Glad about the handle. Every man should have one thing he's foolish about. — H.*

He read it three times. Then he stood in the cloister holding it and laughed, once, out loud, so that a Current girl going by turned to see what was funny and found nothing she could see.

*Pack wool.* Two men now, in a fortnight, had told him to pack for the cold, one across a counter in front of a queue and one across the whole width of the country in a hand that grew larger every year. Neither knew the other existed. One of them could not know what he was saying and the other could not say what he knew. Cael did not put them together in his head, because there was nothing to put together; quarry towns were cold and grandfathers were careful and that was the whole of it. But he noticed that he had noticed, and he wrote that down that night, because noticing was the only part of it that was his.

The letter went behind the front board of the observation notebook with the other two that lived there: the folded note that had come in the pine case with the pen, on the eleventh of Sowing, and the sheet that had said *Sixteen suits you.* Three now, in the same hand. He sat with the notebook shut on all three for a moment before he put it away, and did not write in the Log what it cost him to have them there together, because some lines in a ledger were better left as a space.

---

On the sixth evening of the silence Lira found him on the wall behind the second quadrangle.

She did not sit down at once. She stood with her back to the valley and her hands in her coat and looked at him, the way she looked at an opponent's mark before a bout, measuring where he would be standing when she arrived.

"Who signed it?" she said.

"What?"

"The response. Who signed it?"

"The chancellor."

"Who wrote the last page?"

"Bracken."

"Who found the schedule? Who sat up three nights in a stone hall with the porter bringing her tea? Who certified the record, and sanded it, and put it in a wallet, and watched the courier take it?" She took one hand out of her coat and held it up, empty. "Who decided to put Halcenvane in a tournament in the first place, a year before you'd ever heard the word *clause*?"

He did not answer, because she was not asking.

"Every one of them chose," said Lira. "In ink. With their names. And you've been walking round this house for a week carrying all of it on your own back like they'd dropped it on you in the dark." She sat down on the stone beside him at last, hard, with her boots hanging over the drop. "It's an insult, if you want to know. To them. You're counting their choices as your debts."

"If it fails—"

"If it fails, we fight it. We've fought paper before, the four of us, at Greyvane, with less than this, and we won. And if we lose a week of the season doing it, every house on the circuit learns that Halcenvane doesn't lie down when somebody sends it a sheet of short-inked type." She shrugged. "Withrow could tell you to the half-point what that's worth in standings. Ask her. She'd like being asked."

Below them the ferry lamp came on, and two grey coats stood under it.

"Brom," said Lira, "has started eating your bread without asking. That's how worried he is. Karis wrote me a card." She took it out of her pocket and handed it to him, a folded square in Karis's small hand, three numbered lines.

*1. It isn't his weight. 2. He'll carry it anyway. 3. Make him list who chose.*

Under it, in heavy pencil, in a hand that could only be Brom's: *Tell him the floor's still the floor.*

"I followed most of it," said Lira. "I left out the floor. I didn't know what it meant."

"It means whatever happens with the paper, the oak's still there in the morning, and so are the frames, and so's Ephram's second weight."

"Oh." She considered that. "That's better than mine. Don't tell him."

He gave her back the card, and she put it away carefully, a thing she meant to keep.

"You rehearsed it," he said.

"Since the second day of drafting. You went quiet at supper that night, when Karis came in with her hair down." She stood and brushed off her coat. "Come and eat your own bread."

She went off along the wall toward the lit windows, and after a moment he followed her.

He wrote it that night, at the window, and found he was smiling while he wrote.

*They have a procedure for me now. They watch for the quiet. They choose who'll say the thing, and one of them writes it down for her on a card, and somebody adds a line at the bottom in pencil, and she's sent out to say it where I'll be sitting. I've been handled. By three people. With a card.*

*I'm entering it on the wealth side of the ledger.*

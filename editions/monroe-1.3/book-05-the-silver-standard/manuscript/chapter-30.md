# Chapter 30 — Cheaper Than Deciding

The orientation began at the second bell on the fifth morning, at a side door of the Concourse that Cael would not have found in a week, and it was conducted by a marshal who had plainly given it many times before and had decided long ago to enjoy it.

He was a short broad man with a grey beard cut square and a voice that carried without being raised.

He met them at the door with a list, ticked them off one by one, and looked at each of them as he did it, so that by the end he had every face matched to a name.

When he came to Cael he looked a little longer than at the others, and ticked the list, and said nothing about it.

When he had finished he tucked the list away. "Fourteen houses, and every one of them gets this walk, and the draw at dusk. I'll show you every floor you'll fight on and every door you'll need, and I'll hand you the rules at the end, and if you've any sense you'll read them tonight and not on the morning of your first bout. Questions at the end, please. Follow me."

They followed him.

He took them through the Concourse from the inside, along corridors and galleries and up and down stairs, and the building that had looked from the rise like a range of hills turned out, close to, to be a town.

There were warm-up halls with sprung floors, dressing rooms with numbered doors, a kitchen the size of the house's long hall, a surgeon's corridor with clean beds in a row.

Brom looked down the row of beds for some time as they passed, and said that he hoped to see a great deal less of it than of the kitchen.

In one of the warm-up halls a coast house's Copper was running drills on a sprung floor while his coach counted, and he stopped in the middle of a drill to stare at Cael going past the open door.

His coach, without breaking the count, reached out and turned the boy's head back to the floor with one hand, as you would turn a tap. The marshal saw it and did not smile, quite.

There were four lesser floors.

They saw the second of them last, a square hall under a high roof of glass, with a pale hard floor that rang a little under their boots.

Lira stopped in the middle of it and stood there with her eyes half shut while the others went on, and Cael saw her rise very slightly onto the balls of her feet and settle back, testing it.

"Fast." She had seen him waiting for her. "Very fast, and very honest. It won't give you anything." She looked up at the glass. "I like it."

Then the marshal took them through a last pair of doors and out onto the main floor, and for a while nobody said anything at all.

It was a long oval of grey stone, polished by three hundred years of feet.

Round it, on every side, the tiers went up in four great banks, stone benches rising one above another so steeply that the topmost rows seemed to lean over the floor.

They were empty now, except for a few cleaners working their way along with brooms, small as insects, very far up.

At the east and west ends stood the two towers Cael had seen from the rise, and on the face of each, high above the floor, hung a great board, painted black and ruled in white, with nothing written on it yet.

On the south side a long dais held five tall chairs in a row and a clerk's desk below them.

"The panel," said the marshal. "Five judges. You'll know how they mark by now, I expect; every house does."

He pointed east, then west. "The boards are for the figures. The east tower is the timekeepers'. The west tower is the adjudication office."

He paused, and something in his voice changed, very slightly. "That's the Chief Adjudicator's tower. You'll see him at the opening. You won't see much of him otherwise. He doesn't come down unless the mark needs him."

Cael looked up at the west tower.

There were open galleries along its upper floors, and in them, far off, he could make out figures moving about. They were clerks in grey, going between desks with papers, at a pace that did not hurry and did not stop.

In the middle of the main floor the stone had been cut in a circle of square holes.

They were about the size of a man's two fists, sunk deep into the floor, perhaps two dozen of them in a ring, and each was stopped with a plug of dark wood worn smooth and level with the stone.

Ephram pointed. "What are those?"

"Sockets," said the marshal. "For the exhibition ring, when there's call for it. It goes up in the second week if there's been filing, and not before." He glanced at Cael, very briefly, and away again. "There's been a fair bit of talk about that this cycle."

---

The rules were handed out at the end, at the side door, in a printed program bound in grey card, one copy for each house's coach and one for each fighter.

Karis had hers open before she had stepped back into the street.

She read walking, with Lira steering her round the posts by her elbow, and by the time they reached the guesting-house she had found the page she wanted and was reading it aloud, at the long table, before anybody had taken off a coat.

*Bracket bouts at the continental finals are scored by the exchange. A bout is fought over five exchanges. In each exchange every clean touch the flags give is one point, and the exchange ends at the bell or as soon as either fighter has three points in it. The points of all the exchanges are added together. A bout ends before the fifth exchange if the fighter who is behind could not draw level even by taking every point that remains. If the points are level after five exchanges, the figures decide.*

She put the program down.

Every one of them had spent a year fighting to two touches inside four exchanges, and every one of them was turning that year over in their heads and finding it did not fit here.

Lira spoke first, slowly. "So it isn't first to two."

"Nothing's first to anything." Karis had taken a pencil out and was writing figures in the margin of the program. "Every exchange is money. You can lose one and buy it back in the next, and the next, and the next, if you've got the legs."

She drew a line under her figures. "Or you can lose one three-nothing in the first breath of the bout and spend the other four buying it back."

"Five exchanges," said Brom.

Everybody looked at him. He was sitting at the end of the table with the program open on his knee at the same page, and he was frowning at it as though it had said something about his family.

"I've never fought a fifth. Not once. Not at any meet. Four's the most anybody ever asked of me." He turned the page back and read the paragraph again. "That's a long time to keep a door shut."

Lira shrugged. "Then shut it for five."

"I intend to." He did not look up from the page. "I'm only saying I've never tried."

Rooke had been standing by the window with his own copy unopened in his hand. Now he came to the table.

"Read it again tonight, all of you. Every word. Then think about the third point in an exchange. A man who's two up in an exchange with the bell coming will stop and hold, because the third point ends it early and he'd like to rest. A man who's two down will throw everything at you, because he's got nothing to save it for. You'll see both, and you'll feel both, in yourselves."

He tapped the program. "Exhibitions keep their own old rules. That's yours." He nodded at Cael. "Five exchanges at most, two touches ends it, a clean throw a touch. Nothing about it changes. The tournament doesn't touch the category. It never has."

"And third place?" said Ephram. He had found something at the foot of the page. "It doesn't say."

"Because there isn't one." It was Gault who answered, from the end of the table, quietly. "There's never been a bout for third at Norhold. The two beaten semifinalists stand in the bracket's standing by their figures, and that's all."

He turned his cup round on the table. "That's how I came to be fourth. On the figures. I never fought for it. I sat in the dressing room and a clerk came and told me."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Lira leaned forward. "Was that worse? Or better?"

Gault considered it with great care.

"Better. I'd have lost the bout."

They sat over the program through the middle of the day, all five of them, at the long table with the shutters open on the street.

Karis had worked out every way a bout could end and written them on a single sheet: three-nothing, three-nothing, three-nothing, and done in three; two-one every exchange and a long tired walk to the fifth; a three-nothing lost in the first breath and four exchanges spent buying it back.

She laid the sheet in the middle of the table and they argued over it like a family over a will. Lira wanted to know how fast a three-nothing could happen, and Karis said faster than anybody liked.

Ephram wanted to know whether a fighter two up with the bell coming could simply stand off and run out the time, and Rooke, from the window, said yes, and that anybody who did it in front of the five judges would see what the judges thought of it on the board eleven minutes later.

Brom did not argue.

He sat with his elbows on the table and the program open at the page and read the paragraph once more, slowly, moving his lips a little. Then he closed it and put his big hand on the cover.

"Five," he said, to himself. "All right."

---

Rooke took Cael back to the main floor rail at the end of the afternoon, alone, and stood beside him there for some time without speaking, looking up at the west tower.

The cleaners had gone.

The great oval was empty and quiet, and the light came down through the high windows along the top of the tiers in long slanting bars that lay across the grey stone and across the ring of plugged sockets in the middle of it.

In the west tower's galleries a lamp had been lit, and the grey clerks were still moving between their desks.

"Umber," said Rooke at last.

Cael waited.

"I'll tell you about him once, here, because you're the one of us most likely to end up across a table from him."

He kept his eyes on the tower. "On the opening day this floor will be full of people on strings. Fourteen houses of them. The house pulls one string and the coach another, and the money and the index and the folks at home pull the rest. I answer to Withrow. Withrow answers to a board and a charter and eighteen years. You answer to the four of them, and to whatever it is you write in that book."

He did not look round. "That man answers to the mark. Nothing else. He doesn't care who wins. He doesn't care who's watching. He cares whether the number on that board is the true one."

"You trust him."

"I trust him more than anybody I've met in this trade, and I've met most of it."

Rooke was quiet a moment. "Everybody else here will want something off you. A story, a figure, a bout, a look at what's under your coat."

He shook his head slowly. "Not him. He can't want anything from you, because there's nothing he wants in the whole world except for the measure to be right."

He pushed himself away from the rail. "If you ever find yourself in front of him, keep that in mind. It'll be the only conversation in this city where nobody's selling."

He went back across the floor toward the side door, his footsteps loud in the empty bowl. Halfway there he stopped and turned.

"And Cael."

"Yes?"

"If that day comes, don't try to sell him anything." Rooke's face did not change. "Not a story, not a figure, not a look. He'll know at once, and he'll think the less of the house for it." Then he went on, and the side door shut behind him.

Cael stayed at the rail.

Rooke had never once asked him what lay under the record, and he had not asked now. But he had come as near as Rooke ever came to saying that he knew there was something there, and that it was Cael's to carry and not his.

Cael stood at the rail and looked up at the lamp in the west tower.

He thought of Vell, in Ardenmere, who kept a circuit ledger and would not write a false line in it for anybody.

He thought of Bracken at his green table, certifying a list without omission because a true record was the only kind there was.

He thought of Hesk in his cold workshop, warming a barometer and waiting for it to come right.

Now there was a grey man in a tower who answered to a mark. They were the people who held a pattern and would not let it slip for anybody's convenience.

*Another for the short list,* he wrote that evening, *of people who'd sooner be right than be on anybody's side. Vell. Bracken. Hesk at his bench. Now a grey man in a tower. Every one of them has done me more good than all the people who ever wanted something from me put together, and not one of them was trying to.*

---

Before the light went he slipped back into the Concourse by the side door the marshal had shown them, alone. Nobody else on the roster would ever have to know that ring, and he meant to know it before anybody built it.

The bowl was nearly dark.

A few lamps had been lit along the lowest tier for the cleaners, and the long bars of light from the high windows had climbed the tiers and gone. He walked out across the polished stone to the ring of sockets and stood in the middle of it.

Then he paced it.

He walked it twice, from socket to socket straight across, once north to south and once east to west, and wrote both figures in the notebook. It was fifty-odd feet, a little longer one way than the other, and wider than any floor he had fought on all year.

He knelt and put his hand flat on the stone inside the ring, and then on the stone outside it.

They were not the same.

Outside the ring the stone was old and even, worn smooth by centuries of feet and brooms. Inside the ring it had been patched, and patched again, in a dozen different stones of a dozen different shades.

Some of the patches were nearly as old as the floor, and some looked to be from the last cycle or the one before.

The ring had been taken up and put down again more times than anybody had counted, each time to whatever size the bouts of that cycle had asked for, and every time it went up it left a little more of itself behind in the stone.

He stood up and looked round at the plugged holes in the half-dark. Whole cycles must have gone by without anybody fighting on this ground at all.

And still, between every cycle, somebody had come out onto this floor with an oilcan and a rag, and kept every plug sound, and never once needed to ask why.

"Volunteer category, are you?"

The voice came from behind him.

It was a steward on her closing round, a tall woman with a lantern and a great ring of keys at her belt, and she had crossed the floor so quietly that he had not heard a step.

She held the lantern a little higher and looked at his credential, and then at his face, and nodded. It was not really a question. The printed sheets had seen to that.

She did not move him along.

She stood for a moment inside the ring with him and looked round at the plugged sockets, with the patient attention of somebody looking at a piece of furniture that has been in the family a long while.

"My grandmother worked the floor crews," she said. "Forty years. She put this ring up three times in her whole working life, and took it down three times, and the rest of the time she oiled the plugs."

She touched one with the toe of her boot.

"She always said this ring was like an old doorway somebody's walled over with brick. You don't pull the brick out, or you'd have to decide what to do with whatever came through. And you don't knock the frame down either, because one day somebody might want a door there." She shrugged. "So you leave the bricks where they are and keep the frame painted. It's cheaper than deciding."

She nodded to him again and went on up the floor with her lantern and her keys, checking the doors as she went, and he heard each one close and lock behind her.

He stayed in the ring a little while after the last door had shut, until the dark had come right down the tiers and there was nothing left to see but the faint shine of the plugs at his feet.

He found he was not thinking about whoever might file.

He was thinking about the steward's grandmother, oiling plugs for forty years in a floor she had put a ring on three times, and about how many people like her it had taken, one after another, to keep a door open that nobody walked through.

Then he went out by the side door, and found the porter there waiting to lock it behind him, without the least impatience.

Her sentence went into the Log whole that night, under the line he had written in the first week of the year about Bracken's forms, that every phrase in one was a scar from some old fight.

*Cheaper than deciding. Every clause I've ever stood on is a walled-up doorway somebody kept painting.*

---

The draw was made at dusk that same day, in the Concourse's outer court, by lamplight, in front of everybody.

The court was a great paved square under the Concourse's north face, and by the time the light began to go it was full. All fourteen houses had come, every fighter and coach, and behind them half the city.

On a platform at the court's head stood a long table with a drum on it, a barrel of polished wood on an iron spindle, and behind the table sat three clerks from the west tower with their ledgers open.

It took two hours.

The seeded names were laid in first by their bands, read out one by one from the clerks' ledgers and chalked up on boards behind the table.

Then the drum was turned, and the rest came out of it one slip at a time, each read aloud in a marshal's carrying voice, each chalked into its line.

The marshal who turned the drum had a way of pausing with each slip held up in the lamplight before he read it, just long enough for the whole court to draw breath, and Ephram said afterward that he must have been practising the pause for a month.

Every name that came out of the drum went through the court like a stone into a pond.

Fourteen delegations stood in their knots and worked the brackets in their heads as the lines filled, and the court was full of the sound of it, a dozen accents doing arithmetic aloud at once.

When it was done the clerks carried the ledgers away, and printed boards went up along the court's east wall by torchlight, twelve feet of them, the whole tournament set out in black type.

The crowd moved to the boards. Nobody read the draw alone. That was the custom, Rooke had said: you read it with your house round you, so that whatever it said, you were not by yourself when you found it.

Withrow had come with them, in her dark coat, and Bracken with her, and Seln with the travel file open on his arm, already copying each of the house's lines into it as the boards went up.

At the far end of the court Auremont read its boards in a knot of deep blue, forty strong, very quiet. Cael kept his eyes on his own wall.

Halcenvane read it in the order it had always read things. Karis went fastest, from the top. Cael went slowest and read everything. The others went to their own names first.

Lira found hers inside a minute. She read her pool from the top line down: an Ash Path fighter from a southern house in the first round, a middle seed, and a second round that would want looking at.

Then her eyes went on down, past the quarterfinal, to the line she had worked out on the back of her hand on the bluff.

*ZERIN — AUREMONT — WIND — IRON 9.*

She looked at it for a long time with her hands in her coat pockets. Her face did not change.

"Good." It came at last, quite calmly. "Nobody hiding behind a door, then. Plenty of time to be ready." She did not move away from the board. Her eyes stayed where they were, on a name three lines of type below her own.

Brom's Copper bracket was a long one, and it read, Karis said afterward, like a meeting of the circuit's mistakes.

Half its names were men and women whose ranks had come late or never, and a good many carried a compiler's advisory in the index.

At the top of it, on the first seed's line, stood the name Brom had pinned above his bed for ten weeks.

*MAREK — RHAGEN — GLASS — COPPER 1. FIRST SEED.*

Brom read it twice, and his broad face eased into something very like contentment.

"First seed at Copper One. Somebody in the seeding office reads past the paper."

He looked down the rest of his own line, the rounds stacked under it. "Early rounds this week. Quarters in the second. The final's before the rest day." He nodded to himself. "Good. I'd hate to wait."

Ephram read his pool twice, and then once more with his finger on it, and Cael read it over his shoulder. The first round was the coast house's second entry, the house whose coach had nodded on the road.

There was a likely meeting in the round of sixteen with the lake house's methodical Shield, and nothing of his own house anywhere in his quarter of the draw before the semifinals at the earliest.

"Nothing free," said Ephram. "Nothing unfair." He took his finger off the board. "Correct tournament."

He looked at it a moment more, and then went on in the voice he used for reading out a bill. "The round of sixteen's what I'm paying to get in. Anything after that, I'm trading on."

Karis had gone quiet.

Cael caught the change before he had turned his head. She had been reading fast and talking as she read, telling Lira where the southern houses had landed and Rooke where the lake house's Silvers sat.

Then she had stopped talking, and stopped reading, and stood quite still in front of the Iron board with one finger resting on a line.

It was her own line. *KARIS — HALCENVANE — EMBER — IRON 3.*

Her finger was not on her name. It was two lines below it, on the row for the quarterfinal, where the draw said she would meet whoever came through the other side of her pool.

Cael read the line under her finger. The house was Ternhall. Next to it, in type no larger than any other on the wall, stood *IVENNE*.

"You know her," he said.

Karis did not take her finger away. "We shared a floor. At Ternhall. All the time I was there." She spoke very evenly, and it cost her something to.

"She took the place I left. The top of their column. We learned the lattice side by side, and we kept notebooks on it, and for a long time we traded the notebooks every week."

She was quiet. "She can read me the way you read me. Better than you do. She knew it while it was forming."

Cael noticed that she had not said the name. She had read it, and touched it, and talked about it for a minute, and not once said it aloud.

Karis took out her notebook and wrote the line in it, house and name and round, though it was printed on a board in front of her and would be printed in every gallery sheet in the city by morning.

Cael understood that the writing was the point. Then the notebook went back into her coat.

"Quarterfinal. Well. Everybody was always going to be measured."

Withrow had seen it too.

She was standing a little behind them, and Cael saw her read Karis's line and the line two below it, and look at Karis's back, and then turn her eyes back to the brackets with a face that had seen nothing in particular.

She did not say a word. She moved, very slightly, so that she stood between Karis and the crowd behind.

Karis went on reading the board where she had left off, at the same speed, as though nothing had happened.

Cael read last, and longest, and he read all of it.

Below the last bracket, almost down at the paving, hung one more frame.

It was older than the boards round it, dark wood gone nearly black, and the card in it was printed in an old plain type that nobody used any more. It was the demonstration category's card.

At the top it set out the category's rules, five short lines that had not changed in living memory, and the size of the ring, and the provision for rating against the Standard.

Under them, where any other frame would have held a bracket, ran a column of ruled lines.

They were blank. The category did not draw. It waited for filings, and nobody had filed.

He stood in front of it a long while.

The crowd moved round him, reading and talking, and after a time he became aware that the people nearest him had stopped reading the brackets and were reading him reading the blank lines.

Lira was at his elbow. So were the others. He had not heard them come.

"Everyone else knows who they're fighting," he said. "I'm about to find out who volunteers."

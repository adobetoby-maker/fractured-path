# Chapter 26 — The Melt

Karis built Norhold from its provinces in the top-floor reading room, at the long table under the window, with the door shut.

The card was still in its brass holder on the door, in Bracken's small upright hand: *Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.* Nobody had ever taken it down.

Inside, the room had changed a good deal since the recess. Karis had pinned a map of the whole continent to the long wall, a cheap printed one from a stationer's in Ostrand, and over the weeks it had disappeared under paper.

There were slips pinned to it in every quarter, each with a house's name and a few lines in her hand, and threads of coloured wool running from the slips to places on the map.

Cael came up on a Third-day afternoon in the fifth week with two mugs of tea, because she had not been down to the common room since breakfast. He found her standing in front of the map with a pin in her mouth.

"Fourteen," she said round the pin, without turning. "Fourteen delegations, and I have thirteen. The fourteenth is a lake house that qualified at the year's turn on a recount, and nobody's printed anything about it yet. I've written to their registrar. Bracken says he'll answer, because registrars always answer registrars' friends." She took the pin out and stuck it in a slip. "Look."

He looked.

She had not pinned up fighters. She had pinned up places. Every slip was a house, and every house was pinned where it came from, and under each house's name she had written the same few things.

There was the floor it trained on, oak or stone or plank or something stranger. There was the weather of its country, and what its people did for a living, and which way its rivers ran. There was what a fighter from there would think was normal before he had ever been told what normal was.

"The coast houses," she said, and touched three slips along the western edge. "Every one of them trains on wet boards, because everything on that coast is wet. They're all a little long in the stride, every fighter in every house, because a long stride on a wet floor is how you don't fall over. They don't know they do it. Nobody's ever told them, because everybody they've ever fought does it too."

She moved her hand inland, to a knot of slips in the hills.

"Stone. All of them. They warm up for an hour because the floors are cold, and they never stand still between exchanges, and they've never in their lives been told to walk a circle. They just do." She looked at him sideways. "You'd have saved a shoulder if you'd been born up there."

"I'd have been a different fighter."

"Everybody is. That's the whole point." She moved east, to a single slip with three threads running out of it, and her voice changed a little. "Auremont. A program, not a place. They take children at twelve and give them a floor that's the same in every room and a schedule that's the same every year. Their fighters don't have a country. They have a curriculum." She touched the slip once and took her hand away. "That's what makes them hard to read. You can't read the ground a fighter grew up on if somebody built the ground."

Beside Auremont there was another slip, with one thread.

"Rhagen," said Karis. "Doctrine. Everything they do is written down first and taught second. Marek's from there. So is whatever they send to look at you." She considered the slip. "Rooke's right about one thing. If a doctrine house files on you, it'll be to find out what you are, not to beat you. They won't care about the touches. They'll care about the survey."

He drank his tea and looked at the map for a long time.

He told her it was very good. Karis said, "It's a draft," in the voice she used when she was pleased and did not intend anybody to know.

She needed a host rule about delegation quarters. She went to the shelf for the certified transcription of the tournament charter and took it down and opened it at the back, where the schedules were, and turned pages until she found the rule.

Cael watched her do it.

He watched her hand go past the schedules into the oldest part of the book, the founding articles in their old type. It stopped there.

A narrow slip of paper stood up out of the binding at that place, one of her own, with a line of her writing on it that he had seen her write by the common-room fire at the beginning of the year and had never read.

She did not open it. She did not take the slip out. Her hand lay on the page beside it for perhaps the space of a breath.

Then she turned the pages back to the schedules, found the rule she wanted, and copied it into her notebook.

"After the season," she said, to the book rather than to him.

"I didn't ask."

"I know you didn't. I said it to me." She shut the transcription and put it back on the shelf. "I keep deciding it, every time I go past. It's very tiring. The season first. Then the other thing." She came back to the map and picked up another pin. "It isn't going anywhere. It's been in that book longer than the house has been on this bluff."

Below the window, at the foot of the bluff road, two coats stood in the snow in the lee of the wall, and two more stood at the ferry landing on the far side of the river, small and dark against the white, where Cael could see them from here as he could see them from every high window on the hill.

It was a little before the sixth bell, and at the sixth bell they would change.

Karis did not look at them. She took the marbled log out of her satchel, opened it at the day's page, and when the bell went she wrote the time, and the number, which was four, and the line, which was the same as the line above it.

She had written that line every day since the recess. She had never once had to change it.

"All this snow," she said, closing the log. "And not one man added. Whoever pays them must pay them by the year."

---

Hesk's letter came up on the evening coach in the sixth week, with snow on the satchel.

It was not a long letter. Hesk's letters in the cold months were never long, because his workshop was cold and his hands were stiff and he said so. This one was written in the same hand it always was, a little larger every year, bearing down hard on the down-strokes.

*Your circular came down the coast road on the same coach as a new belt for my lathe, and I am not sure which of them I was gladder to see. The belt runs true. So, I gather, do you.*

*It is very cold here. I have had three barometers brought in this month by men who swear they are broken, and none of them is. A glass read in a cold room reads short. Warm it, and wait, and it comes right. I tell every man this. Most of them look at me as though I had picked their pockets.*

*You will be going to a big city. In a big city every clock disagrees with every other clock, and every one of them is certain. Do not set yours by the loudest bell. Pick one clock you have reason to trust, and keep to it, and let the rest argue among themselves. The travelling clock I gave you will be no help at all. It never has been. Set it an hour fast as you always do and you will be right by accident, which is how that clock has always been right.*

*Tell the Wind girl the workshop is still open. Tell the big one I have heard about his line in the Copper and that a flat line is the hardest thing in my trade to make. — H.*

He read it twice at the common-room table. Then he took it across to the fire, where Lira was sitting with her boots up and Brom was mending a strap, and read them the last two lines aloud.

Lira said, "Tell him I know," and went back to her book. She did not turn a page for some time.

Brom put down the strap and looked at the fire.

"The hardest thing in his trade," he said slowly. He seemed to turn it over, as he turned over every true thing anybody told him, to look at the underside. "Then I'll keep it flat."

The letter went behind the observation notebook's front board, with the others.

He thought about the clocks afterward, sitting by the fire with the notebook on his knee. Hesk had never once in his life given advice that was only about the thing it seemed to be about. In a big city every clock is certain, and you keep to one you have reason to trust. Cael knew which one he had. It had four hands, Lira's and Brom's and Karis's and the Log's, and between them they had kept better time this year than any bell on the continent.

The block's inventory went into the Log that night, with the ferry landing white and empty below except for its two coats. Most of it said what it always said, so he wrote those lines short: the oak's six free a day and the landing beat no shorter; the right shoulder unasked for anything since the mill town's plate; Compression shown nowhere, not even for the barge-master's fall; Shadow's rent from waking, and one drift on the stair. Then he wrote the two lines that were new.

*Ember: two contacts, the circle's rate, on the caravan captain at the confluence, in front of a hundred and forty people. It was on paper already, at the back of the Greyvane transcript, and it's on a seat's report now as well. Two a round on the Shield reserve since, on a floor with no crowd, because a habit kept only in public is a costume.*

*Six. The record holds two of them public, and has been shown a third at its documented rate. Nothing on any floor this year has been news to any paper. The doctrine holds.*

---

Seln wrote the block's report at the copying table behind the wing's counter, on the last Fifth-day of the term, after the counter had closed.

The wing was quiet. The day's forms lay in their trays, and the term's last coursework folders were stacked for return with their slips torn off.

Outside the high window the light was going blue over the snow, and the ferry landing on the far side of the river showed two dark marks against the white, as it had shown every evening for ten weeks.

He wrote the report in the office's standard form, slowly, and read each line back as he went.

*Subject in residence for the term. Training per house schedule, in preparation for the continental finals. Capabilities exercised in training consistent with those on record. No undisclosed capability observed. Travel per the attached roster. Schedule continues. Routine.*

He read the first line again. The subject had been in residence for the term. The wing's own counter sheet said so, and the subject had brought his coursework to the counter every Fifth-day and said *Thank you for the form*, and Seln had said *Mm*, and taken it.

He read the second. The subject had trained per the house's schedule. The schedule was pinned to a board in the long upper room in the coach's capitals, and Seln had read it on his way to somewhere else.

He read the third. The capabilities exercised were consistent with those on record. The subject had spent two ignitions a round against the Shield reserve on the oak, every second day.

The record held the ignition: a thin exhibit at the back of a public transcript, a reading on the wing's own vessel, and now the confluence seat's report. Consistent.

He had watched one of those sessions from the rail of the oak, on his way to somewhere else, and counted. Two a round. Every round. With nobody there to count but a third-year reserve and a clerk on his way to somewhere else.

He did not ask himself why the boy kept to two where nobody was looking. He noted that he had noticed it, and set the noticing aside, in the place where he kept things that were not the office's business.

He read the fourth line. *No undisclosed capability observed.* That was true. It was true in the way his reports had been true all year: every word of it would bear any weight anybody cared to put on it, and none of it carried more than its own.

He had observed nothing undisclosed. He had arranged, for most of a year, to be on his way to somewhere else whenever there might have been anything to observe, and that was not a thing a report was required to state.

He read the fifth. *Travel per the attached roster.*

He had written the roster himself that afternoon, in fair copy, for posting on the board in the covered walk and for the regional office and for Norhold. Eleven names. Five competitors, the coach and the evaluator of record, two reserves, a wagoner.

On the last line, in the same hand as the rest, *S. Seln, assessment office: records and floor scheduling.*

He had looked at that line for some time before he blotted it. It was the second roster this year with his name on its last line. Nobody had asked him to put it there either time. He had simply been the person who wrote the roster, and the roster had to be complete.

He signed the report, sealed it with the office's plain seal, and put it in the pouch for the north. The pouch would go down the bluff on the morning coach, past the two coats at the foot of the road. They would not look at it. Nobody ever looked at the pouch; that was what a pouch was for.

Then he took up the fair copy of the roster and went down the wing's stair to the covered walk with it, and pinned it to the board under the crooked frame, square to the edge, with four tacks.

There was nobody in the walk to see him do it. It was nearly dark, and snowing again, a little. He stood looking at the board for a moment with his hands in his coat. Then he went back up to the wing to put out the lamps.

True. Complete in form. And, this once, not quite empty: there was a roster attached.

---

The roster was on the board in the morning, and by the first bell the whole house had read it. By the second bell the provisional seedings had gone up beside it.

They came from the continental index, the commercial one the scouts and the coaches bought, printed in the capital and sent out by coach to every qualified house.

Rooke had paid for an early copy, as he had paid for the extracts in the slate country. He pinned the Iron page next to the roster without a word and went away to the oak.

It was not the draw. The draw would not be made until the delegations were in Norhold, at the orientation, by lamplight, in front of everybody. But it was the seeding the draw would be made from, and every house on the hill knew by now how the circuit made a draw from a seeding.

Lira read it at the board with her hands in her pockets. She read it once quickly, and then took a pencil out of her coat and did the draw herself, on the back of her hand, foot against head, as it would be done. Then she did it again, to be sure.

Cael, standing behind her, did it in his head. He came out where she did.

If the seeds held through two rounds, Lira's semifinal line was Zerin's.

She did not say anything at the board, or at breakfast, or on the speed line in the afternoon, where she ran her intervals a hair shorter than she had the day before and did not mention it.

At supper she ate what Brom put in front of her. Afterward she went up early, before the fire had properly settled, and said good night to the room.

The lamp under her door was still burning when Cael went up at the tenth bell. It was still burning when he woke a little after the second hour of the night with the cold on his face and did not know what had woken him.

He got up and put on his coat and went out.

She was on the wall above the river, where she always went, sitting on the coping with her coat round her and her knees up, looking north.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared and was full of stars, and the river below was loud and dark and fast, with ice going down it in plates that knocked together in the current with a sound like somebody far off moving furniture.

He sat down on the coping beside her, not too close. She did not look round.

"I've built her for ten weeks," said Lira, after a long while. "Her distances and her intervals and her turn. Everything on the page. I can do all of it now, nearly. Not as fast. Nearly." She was quiet. "And I still can't find the middle. Ten weeks, and there's a hole in the middle of her the shape of the one thing I need."

"You'll find it in the first exchange."

"Or I won't."

"Or you won't," he agreed. "And then you'll know."

She laughed, a little, under her breath.

"You said that at the beginning of the block," she said. "I hated it then too." She pulled the coat closer. "I'm not frightened of her. I want you to know that. I've been frightened of a great many people in my life, and some of them were behind desks, and none of them is her." She watched the ice go down the river. "I'm frightened of finding the middle and finding it's something I can't do. That I've been right all year that I'm good, and she's simply better, and there was never anything I could have done about it except be born at twelve in a room with the right floor."

He did not say anything. There was nothing true he could have said that was also comforting, and she would have known the difference.

They sat for a long time. The stars went round a little. Somewhere down in Ostrand a dog barked, and stopped.

"Put down that I'm not glad tonight," said Lira at last. "In the Log. I said I was glad, on the quay steps. Put down that tonight I'm not." She stood up and brushed the snow off the back of her coat. "I'll be glad again in the morning. I usually am." She looked down at him. "Go to bed. You've a Shield at the second bell, and he's started making his foot smaller."

She went in. He stayed on the wall a little longer.

He put it down when he went back up, by the light of a candle end, in four words. *Not glad tonight. Morning.*

---

The melt came the next night, at the very end of the tenth week, all at once, in the dark.

Cael woke to the sound of water. It was running everywhere, off the roofs and down the gutters and along the covered walk, and in the morning the snow on the bluff had gone grey and soft and was sliding off the tiles in long wet sheets. The river under the wall had risen a man's height overnight and ran brown and heavy and very fast, carrying whole trees down past Ostrand toward the confluence, and the bluff road was a stream for a day, and then deep mud, and then, on the morning of the Second-day, a road again, or near enough.

They left on the Second-day at first light.

There were two wagons, as there had been all year, loaded the night before under Rooke's eye, with the reserves riding on the second and Gault's case wedged in among the kit where he could reach its handle.

Seln had a corner of the first wagon's tailboard with the travel file on his knees.

Withrow came down to the gate in her dark coat to see them off. She and Bracken would follow in a week with the hired carriage, by the faster road.

She did not make a speech. She shook Rooke's hand, and then, to everybody's surprise, each of theirs. She went down the line of the five of them in order, and then the reserves, and then Gault, and then, after the smallest pause, Seln.

"I'll see you in the city," she said, to all of them.

The wagons went out through the gate and down the bluff road in the thin early light, with the mud sucking at the wheels and the whole valley below them steaming in the first sun.

At the foot of the road the two coats stood in the lee of the wall, as they had stood every day of the year.

Cael watched them as the wagons went past. They did not move or turn their heads, but stood where they had always stood, on the bell, and watched the wagons go by as they would have watched a cart of turnips go by on any market morning of the year.

At the bridge into Ostrand there were people.

Nobody had arranged that either. It was market day, and the town was out, and somebody had seen the wagons coming down the bluff. By the time they reached the bridge there were perhaps two hundred people standing along it in the mud, with more coming down the lanes.

There were the baker's boys and the ferry crew and the women from the fish steps, and the man who sold hot chestnuts at the bottom of the hill. There were children on their fathers' shoulders. A great many of them had never set foot on the bluff in their lives.

They did not cheer, exactly. They stood along the parapet and called out names as the wagons went by, all five, and some of them the reserves' names too, which the reserves had not known anybody in Ostrand knew.

The Shield reserve went pink and waved with both hands.

Somebody threw a bunch of early snowdrops into the first wagon. Brom caught it, and looked at it in his big hand, and did not know what to do with it, and in the end put it in his buttonhole, where it stayed for three days.

On the far side of the bridge the road ran north between the flooded fields.

Cael sat on the tailboard of the second wagon with Lira beside him and looked back. The bridge was still lined with people, getting smaller.

Above it the bluff stood up dark against the morning, with the house on top of it, and the wall where Lira had sat a few nights before, looking north, in the starlight.

At the ferry landing, the other two coats were still standing on the bell.

"They're not coming," said Karis, behind him. She had the marbled log open on her knee, and she had written the time, and the number, and then, under it, a line she had never written before.

"No."

"They don't need to." She closed the log. "Norhold prints a program. We'll be in it every day, with the hour and the hall. Anybody who wants to know where we are can buy it for a copper." She put the log away in her satchel. "We don't need watching any more. We've been published."

The watchers grew smaller behind them, two and two, standing in the melt on the bell, and then the road bent and the bluff went behind a line of willows, and they were gone.

They lay that night at the first waystation on the north road, a long low inn with a yard full of mud and a fire nobody could get near.

The soup was very good, which Brom said was an omen. Ephram said that was not how omens worked, and Brom said he had never seen Ephram eat a bad bowl of soup and call it an omen either, which was fair.

Cael wrote the Log at the end of the long table with a candle, while the others argued.

*Out. Seventeen days to the city, if the roads hold. Two wagons, eleven on the roster, and the chancellor in a week. Ostrand on the bridge. The watchers on the bell, not following, because we're printed now wherever we go and nobody needs to stand in the snow to find us.*

*In the file on Rooke's knee: a city we've never seen, two sheets we haven't solved, and a Shield I think I have.*

*We'll see.*

# Chapter 35 — Behind

The morning of the semifinal went the way their fight mornings always went, and nobody remarked on any of it, because remarking was not part of the observance.

They ate early, at the end of the long table, before the house's other guests came down. Nobody tasted anything.

Lira ate what she always ate before a bout, bread and an egg and half a cup of milk, and she left the other half of the cup as she always did, and Brom reached over and finished it for her without being asked, as he had done on every fight morning since the slate country.

Then she checked her kit on the bench under the window. Wraps and spare wraps, the tape, the soft shoes and the hard ones, the strap of the bag she had rewound the night before. She went through it once, in her own order. Then Brom picked up the bag and went through it again in his.

"You've done that," said Lira.

"I know." He put the tape back exactly where it had been. "It isn't for you."

They walked to the Concourse through the delegation quarter in the order the city had learned to know them by: Brom in front, Lira and Cael in the middle, Karis and Ephram behind, and Rooke last with Gault and the coats. People stepped aside for them at corners.

Twice somebody called Lira's name from a doorway. Once a girl of about ten ran alongside them for half a street, shouting *Bluff! Bluff!*, until her mother caught her by the collar and hauled her back.

Lira did not seem to hear any of it. She walked with her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes on the cobbles a few yards ahead. Cael had walked beside her onto more floors than he could count, and he could read nothing on her this morning but a great evenness. She had done her thinking. Nothing was left in her that still wanted deciding.

On the back of her left hand, very faint, was the ghost of one word in ink. She had scrubbed at it and not quite got it out.

The second floor was full before they reached it.

It was full to the rails and past them.

The square hall under the high glass had been built to seat perhaps six hundred on its tiers, and this morning there were more than that on the benches and more again in the passages behind, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the archways.

The broadsides had been pricing the bout for two days, and Cael had seen them pasted on the walls of the delegation quarter all the way in, one on top of another where the bill-stickers had run out of wall. Some were about Auremont's perfect product and some about the girl Fenmark had thrown out. The best of them simply printed the two names side by side in large type and left the reader to do the sum.

The dais stood against the south rail with its five tall chairs and the clerk's desk. Morning light came down through the glass in long pale panes that lay across the western half of the floor and had not yet begun to move.

On the far side the Auremont block sat in its blue, very still. In its front row were the two scouts Cael had charted all year. The older man had his book shut on his knee. The younger woman had hers open.

Halcenvane had the near rail. Rooke stood at the centre of it with his arms folded, and Gault beside him with the coats and the heat-wrap, and the rest of them along it in a row. High up behind them, in the first row of the upper gallery, Cael caught a glimpse of Withrow's dark coat and Bracken's grey head beside it. She had not said she was coming. She had not needed to.

Zerin came out to her chalk on the western mark, with the light behind her.

Cael had seen her fight three times and had never been this close. She was compact and fair, a little shorter than Lira, with her hair tied back hard and a face that seemed to keep its feelings somewhere else for later. She reached her chalk neither early nor late. She had walked through every bout of the tournament like that, like somebody coming into a room to fetch a thing she had left there.

The referee called them to the centre flag for the formalities.

They stood a stride apart. Zerin looked Lira over from her feet to her face with plain professional attention, as a buyer looks at a horse she has already decided is worth the price. Then her eyes went down to Lira's left hand and rested a moment on the grey ghost of a word.

Cael saw her try to read it. He saw her fail.

The flags went up.

---

The tiers saw speed. Cael, at the rail with the full gaze on her, saw something harder to look at.

Zerin opened at her walking pace.

Her walking pace was everybody else's sprint, and she came across the floor at it with no change in her body at all. There was no gather, no breath, no setting of the hips. She simply went from one place to another place very fast.

Lira read her. She read her well, every line of it, as she read everybody.

And every read was a tenth late.

Cael saw it on the first contact and on every contact after.

Lira would read the line, and the answer would form in her, and it would be the right answer, every time, the answer he would have given her himself from the rail; but by the time it had formed, the moment it would have fitted was already gone. It was as though every window in the fight had been cut a tenth narrower than the windows Lira had grown up fighting through, and nobody had told her the new size.

The first touch came on the shoulder. Zerin entered from the left, and Lira read it and turned to meet it, and turned to meet it a tenth after it had arrived.

Lira spent her one survey burst straight after that, as she and Rooke had agreed she would, not to score but to measure. She burst across the line Zerin was running and let Zerin close on her, and Cael watched her body take the measurement as it happened: how far, how fast, and how little warning. It came back the way the extract had said it would, and a little worse, because the extract had been written by a clerk with a clock and Lira was finding it out with her ribs.

The second touch came low on the hip, from behind, while Lira was still landing from the burst.

The third took longer. Lira began to put a corner together against the north rope, a half-step here and a feint there to close the roads. Zerin went through it before it was done. She did not go round it. She went through, like a draught through a door that somebody is still in the act of shutting, and touched Lira on the ribs as she passed.

Three flags. Clean ones, and not one of them close.

On the far side, the younger Auremont scout wrote something short in the gap after each flag, quickly, without looking down at the page. The older man did not write at all. He sat with his book shut on his knee and watched Lira, and Cael understood that he was waiting to see what she did next, because what a fighter did after a clean loss was the only thing about her that could not be bought from a shop.

"Nought to three," called the clerk, and the exchange was closed.

The gallery let out its breath. It was a long, soft, falling sound.

Several hundred people had paid to see a story, and had watched it go wrong in the first two minutes. Behind Cael a man said, not unkindly, "Well. That's that, then."

Lira walked back to her chalk.

Cael watched her shoulders, because her shoulders were where she kept the truth, and they did not come up. She did not tighten. She stood easily on her mark with her hands loose and looked across at Zerin as she had looked at the extract in the back room. She was not looking at the woman. She was looking at the shape of her. Then she took three slow breaths.

"Fees," said Rooke, very low. "She's paid them."

Cael had the whole exchange charted in his head, the windows and the tenth and all three touches. None of it was any use to her now. She had felt every piece of it from the inside, and she knew it better than he did.

But he could see something forming in the set of her weight as she stood there. It was an adjustment. He knew its name before she made it, because it was older than either of them had words for and it belonged to both of them.

*Stop racing her. Sell her the floor.*

---

When the flags went up again, Lira had given up answering.

Zerin came across the floor as she had before, and Lira was not there to be come at.

She had moved before the entry arrived. She had not moved away from it but sideways, a half-step and a turn that cut the east approach in two and left the half by the rope belonging to her. Zerin went through the place where Lira had been and found it empty. She turned in her stream-round-a-stone way and came back, and by then Lira had moved again and closed a diagonal, and Zerin had to go round it.

"North," said Ephram quietly at the rail. He had both hands on it and his eyes on the floor, and he was calling it the way he called the bench floor for the squad. "North's gone. East half."

Lira was buying floor.

She was not buying it from Zerin. She was buying it from the square, a yard at a time. Each yard cost her a step or a feint or a turn, paid at the moment it was cheapest, and every yard she bought she kept.

She did not burst. She had burst once in the first exchange, to survey, and she did not burst again, not when a road opened in front of her that a burst would have taken, not when Zerin came close enough to touch; and Cael, counting at the rail, understood that she was saving it.

The gallery began to mutter. From the tiers it looked like retreat. A fighter who had lost the first exchange three to nothing was refusing every entry she was offered and backing round the floor with her hands low. A few people on the benches began to say so aloud.

"West," said Ephram. "Gone. She's got three sides."

Zerin's floor was shrinking round her. For the first time in the tournament it was beginning to cost her something to move through it.

The first corner cost her nothing. Lira closed it on her against the north rope, all three roads sold at once, and Zerin stepped out of it with a small change of line and a turn, as easily as a woman steps round a puddle.

The second cost her the turn.

Cael saw it. The older Auremont scout saw it too, because his head came up. Zerin's line bent. For the first time since the opening, the fastest fighter in the bracket had to change where she was going because of where somebody else had already been. The bend was small, and sensible, and plain to anybody who knew what to look for.

The third corner came with its bill already inside it.

Lira had banked a burst. She had held it through two openings where she might have spent it, so that Zerin would learn that this girl did not burst out of her corners. She only walked out of them.

Zerin learned it. At the third corner her line bent the same way it had bent at the second, a small, sensible, predictable bend, because the floor had just taught her that bending was enough.

Lira spent the burst. She spent the whole of it, at the one angle the bend had made certain.

For half a beat Zerin was behind.

Cael could never afterward say how long it lasted. He only knew that it was the shortest thing he saw all week and the largest.

Zerin arrived where she had meant to be, a tenth early as always, and Lira was already standing there. Every piece of Zerin had been built to arrive first. For half a beat she had arrived second, and her body did not know what to do about it. Her weight hung.

Her angle, made for a start that was hers, pointed at a start that belonged to somebody else.

Lira touched her on the shoulder.

It was not a quick flag. It was the kind of contact that comes at the end of a long build, and three of the five judges on the dais were, by Karis's page, a quarter-beat slow on anything still being built. For a moment the flags stayed down and the hall held its breath. Then they went up, all on Halcenvane's side.

The second floor came apart.

It was not a cheer.

It was bigger and messier than a cheer.

For two days the broadsides had told this gallery that nobody in the tournament had taken an exchange off Zerin. Now they had watched somebody take a point off her the hard way, and every one of them understood at the same moment that the exchange was open, and they stood up and shouted at each other about it as people shout at a race when the horse nobody backed puts its head in front at the last turn.

On the far side, the older scout was writing. He was writing in the middle of an exchange. Cael had charted the man at three meets and never once seen him write before the bell.

Zerin took one back at once, without fuss, a clean touch on Lira's hip as she landed from the burst. The noise hardly changed.

She had cut her loss quickly and with no waste, Cael thought, as she must have been taught to, and even the cutting was a kind of tell, because it had been drilled into her.

Then she went back into the floor Lira had sold her, carefully this time, testing each corner before she trusted it.

Lira let her test. By her own count and Rooke's she had one burst left for the bout, and she kept it in her pocket. She closed one more corner against the south rope under the dais, on foot alone. Zerin went out of it the long way, along the rope. There was just room at the end of the long way for Lira to be standing there with a hand out.

Two to one, at the bell. Two to four on the bout.

Brom let out a breath beside Cael. "There it is." He sounded like a man who has watched the first course of a wall go down dead level.

Rooke had his little book out, the one he kept for nobody but himself, and his pencil was moving.

High in the upper gallery, Withrow had not stood up with the rest. Cael looked for her and found her sitting perfectly still with her hands in her lap, looking down at the floor with an expression he could not read at that distance and did not try to.

---

Zerin spent the interval studying.

She stood on her chalk in the light from the glass with her hands at her sides, and her eyes went round the floor, corner to corner, rope to rope. Cael knew exactly what she was doing. She was reading the second exchange back to herself, the way he read the notebook. It was the first time all tournament that he had seen anybody's study run in her direction.

She had come to Norhold with everybody's files in her head and had needed none of them. Now she had found something she had no file for. So she was writing one, standing there, in the space of a single interval.

She came into the third exchange with the corner game half solved.

Lira's corners wanted Zerin to bend late. So Zerin bent early.

She spent speed she had not needed to spend all tournament, taking every corner Lira offered from an angle that got there while the selling was still going on, paying the toll a beat before Lira could set it.

It cost her, and Cael could see exactly what it cost her, a breath that came a little harder at the end of every corner and a line that ran a little wider than she would have liked; but she had more to pay with than Lira had to charge, and she knew it, and she paid.

It was the best fight Cael had ever seen Lira fight. And it was going the wrong way.

For most of that exchange the two of them ground at each other across the pale boards. It was long and close and quiet, and almost nothing in it was easy for the tiers to follow.

Lira sold her yards and Zerin bought them early. Lira closed a corner and Zerin was already through the gap she had meant to shut. So Lira began to sell the gaps as well, and Zerin began to pay for those in advance too.

Cael's chart showed him the thing he had been afraid of since the stair.

Each corner Lira built was still working. But each one was working a little less well than the one before. The yield was falling. Lira's bursts were a store, and the store was going down. Zerin's speed was not a store at all. It was only what she was.

Cael watched one corner go from start to finish and could have drawn it afterward with his eyes shut.

Lira began it against the west rope, in the long light from the glass, with a step to close the north road and a feint to sell the south. The feint was meant to come a beat after the step. Before it came, Zerin was already in the corner and out of it again by the south road, going through the place the feint would have sold, at an angle she had chosen while Lira was still choosing hers. It had cost her a burst of speed she would not have spent an exchange earlier, and Cael saw it cost her, a slight hardening of the breath. Then she was gone along the rope, and the corner was empty, and the feint arrived into nothing.

Lira scored once, from a corner against the east rope that Zerin went into a fraction too soon and had to correct from inside. To close that corner Lira spent the last burst of her rate. Zerin scored twice. Both times it came from an angle that had opened and closed before the corner round it was done.

One to two, at the bell. Three to six on the bout.

"She's paying early," said Karis. "Brom said she would."

"Brom said she'd been taught to."

"For a program that's the same thing. It's what a program is for."

Lira did not come to the rail. She stood on her chalk with her right hand on her left shoulder, as she sometimes did after a long session on the oak, and rolled the left shoulder under it slowly. Once. Twice. It was the shoulder she led her bursts with.

Gault had seen it before Cael had finished seeing it. He already had the heat-wrap in his hands.

At the very end of the interval Lira turned her head and looked across at Rooke. It was not a long look, and nobody else in the hall would have known it for a question. Rooke held up three fingers against the rail, low, where only she could see them, and folded them down again one at a time. That was her rate for a bout. She had spent it.

Lira nodded to him. Then she turned back to her chalk.

---

In the fourth exchange Zerin went to her full speed.

Nothing in the three bouts Cael had charted had asked it of her, and so he had never seen it.

The extract had promised it. The examiner's mark beside the closing figure had promised it. Now she gave it, and the floor changed state round her the way water does when it freezes. It was the same forty feet of pale board under the same glass. It was not the same stuff at all.

Cael felt it go wrong in his own instrument.

For three bouts and three exchanges he had kept her in the full gaze by setting his eyes a tenth ahead of her and letting her walk into them. Now she arrived before the tell.

Entries he still had forming in his head were already finished on the floor. Turns he had placed a beat ahead were complete, and she was coming out of them, while he still watched the spot where they ought to have begun. It was like looking at Hesk's clock while somebody spun the long hand round with a finger.

All year he had trusted his eyes to see a fight a fraction early. For one exchange they could only follow this one from behind. He stood at the rail and watched a friend be outrun by something he could not keep up with either.

Lira knew it before he did.

She had built her corners all through the second exchange and the third, and in the first minute of the fourth Zerin went through every one of them. She did not solve them. She went through faster than they could close, as if they had been painted on paper. Every yard Lira had bought, she lost again in a breath.

Lira cut one diagonal.

Of everything she did that morning, it was the finest.

She cut it fresh, on foot, with the last of her floor-sense and three hard strides, across the middle of the square from the north-west corner to the south-east. Then she held it for half a beat while Zerin came at it. For half a beat it stood. Zerin had to go round, and Lira was waiting at the far end of it, and touched her on the forearm.

One to nothing. The gallery came to its feet again.

After that it was arithmetic, done at a speed only one of them could vote on.

Lira spent everything. Cael watched her do it and counted every coin.

She spent the burst after the last, the one she did not have, which Rooke's rule did not allow and which her own body had never once let her spend on any floor before, and Cael felt the cost of it go into her left shoulder from across the hall. She spent her reads to buy position, and her position to buy time. Nothing she gave up was given. All of it had to be taken.

Zerin took it anyway.

She took the first touch off an entry from the left. She took the second off the turn after it.

The third came at the end of a long, close, fierce run along the north rope, with Lira a single stride behind her all the way and gaining, a hand's breadth and then another, so that for a moment the whole hall thought she would get there. She was almost there. The flag went up as her hand closed on the air where Zerin's shoulder had just been.

"One to three," called the clerk. Then, after a moment: "Four to nine on the bout."

Nobody needed the sum explained. Lira could not have drawn level with every point of a fifth exchange, so there would be no fifth exchange. It was over in four.

Zerin did not press.

She had not pressed past what the bout required all morning, and she did not do it now. She stopped where she was when the flag went up, a stride from the rope, and let her hands drop, and turned to face the girl who had just spent everything she owned trying to catch her.

There was no malice in her anywhere. Cael had looked for it all morning and found none. There was nothing in her at all, he thought, except the work. And the work was done.

Lira stood bent over on the floor with her hands on her knees, breathing hard. The second floor got to its feet.

It stood for both of them.

It had come for a story, the girl who was built against the girl who had rebuilt herself, and it had been given a measurement instead. It stood up anyway, every bench of it and the archways too, and it went on standing for a long time.

Cael had seen galleries stand for winners all year. Once or twice he had seen one stand for somebody brave who lost. He had never seen a gallery stand like this, for two people at once, because it had just watched somebody's ceiling and somebody else's floor meet in the same square, and both of them hold.

Lira straightened up slowly. She held her left arm a little stiff at the shoulder. Cael could see the shoulder setting already, tightening round the burst she had not had. She did not look at it.

He watched her face, because he was afraid of what he would find there. What he found was not what he had expected.

There was no performance in it.

She was not being brave for the gallery. She was working. Behind her eyes the bout was already turning into something else, into numbers and corners and the tenth and the half-beat, and he could almost watch her filing it, one exchange after another, in the order she would want it later.

She walked across the floor to Zerin and held out her hand, and Zerin took it.

Whatever the tiers had hoped for, tears or a fist thrown in the air, they did not get it. Two working fighters shook hands on a piece of business. Their grips were firm and their faces were level, and both of them would be going over the figures for weeks.

They said something to each other, too quiet for any rail to hear. Then they let go and walked off in opposite directions, and the gallery went on standing until both of them had gone.

The figures went up on the board at the end of the hall eleven minutes later, at the proper interval. Zerin's was twenty-nine. Lira's was twenty-seven.

Cael stayed at the rail looking at the two numbers after the others had turned away.

Twenty-nine was a Silver figure.

Every person in that hall who could read the board knew it. Zerin had posted a Silver figure in an Iron semifinal, as plainly as the Standard could say anything. And two places below it, two points short, stood a girl whom Fenmark's ledger had once written down as Copper.

He thought about the half-beat in the second exchange, when the fastest fighter in the bracket had been behind and had not known what to do there. Nobody had ever studied Zerin from behind, because nobody had ever got her there. So when it came, there had been nothing in her to stand on.

He thought about his own file, the one nobody had the whole of. Nobody had ever got him there either. He was not sure, standing at the rail, whether that was a strength or only a place nobody had been.

Gault was at the top of the steps with the heat-wrap. Lira came up them with her left arm held against her side, and let him put it on her without a word, and only then, when it was on and tied, did she look round for Cael.

"Long enough?" she said.

He knew what she meant. He looked across the floor at the Auremont block, where the older scout was still writing, steadily, page after page.

"They're still going," he said.

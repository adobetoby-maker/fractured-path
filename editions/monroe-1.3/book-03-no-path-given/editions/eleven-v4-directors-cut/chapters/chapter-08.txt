By the time he crossed the yard that afternoon, the rumors had found a face to hang themselves on, and it was his.

He had first noticed Edran three days earlier, from the rail of the open floor, where an observer was allowed to stand and watch as long as he did not step over the chalk. Edran had been the easiest person in the hall to pick out, because everyone else on the floor moved around him like water around a post, without seeming to think about it, and because he fought with the settled ease of someone who had been at the top of a board for so long that he no longer checked whether he was still there. Glass Path. Iron Rank Two. First on the standings.

Cael had watched him for most of an hour, filling a page. Glass was not what he had expected; he had always thought of glass as the thing that broke. Edran's declarations broke, certainly, and Cael watched a bright, hard shell of something like crystal form along his forearm to take a strike and then burst into a glitter that hung for a heartbeat in the air before it vanished. But the shell did not break because it failed. It broke because breaking was what it was for: the burst carried the strike's force away with it in a hundred directions at once, and before the glitter had faded a new shell was already forming in the next place Edran expected to be hit. By the end of that first bout Cael had crossed out a page of his own notes about brittleness and started again.

Somewhere in the middle of the second bout, between exchanges, Edran had looked up at the rail. His eyes had gone first to the open binder, Cael was nearly sure, and only afterward to the person holding it. He held the look a beat longer than a glance needed, his face showing nothing at all, before he turned back to his opponent and finished the bout a little sharper than he had begun it, as if he had tightened a screw. Sharper with an audience, Cael had written under the page, and then, because the log was the one place he did not let himself be generous: Or he minds being taken down in ink. Find out which.

Now Edran was waiting near the yard's north gate, as a man waits when he has decided where another man will have to walk.

He was not alone. Three or four students were nearby, doing other things very slowly; one was retying a boot that had not come untied, and two were talking with the particular attention of people listening to a different conversation. Cael saw the shape of it before he was close enough to see faces. It was not an ambush. It was an audience.

"Observers don't demonstrate."

Edran said it to the yard in general, in a clear, level voice that carried without being raised, but he never once took his eyes off Cael, and Cael stopped walking.

"That's not what the category is for. I've read the provision. I've sat beside observers in lectures since my first year. Observers watch. They take notes. They don't get a panel and a closed hall and a training partner at first bell." He tilted his head very slightly. "So either the faculty spent a morning watching you do nothing, which I don't believe, or somebody wrote a form that says one thing and means another."

The boy retying his boot had stopped pretending.

"What exactly are you?"

It was not hostile, and that was the hard part. Cael had faced hostile questions all his life, and hostile questions were easy in their way, because they told you what the person wanted and you could refuse to give it. This was something else. Edran sounded genuinely curious, almost academic, like a student who has found a hole in a rule and would like to know how deep it goes. Anger burned out. Curiosity kept a notebook.

Cael felt the yard around him the way he felt a crowd before a bout, the loose half-ring of listeners, the boy with the boot. He knew that whatever he said in the next breath would be in every common room by the seventh bell, word for word, and improved on by the eighth. He went through what he had: a form that said a thing its signers knew was not so; a binder in his pack that no one here would ever read; and nothing between the two that he could say out loud and keep. He would not lie. He had decided that long ago, on a different road.

"I'm enrolled."

Every word of it was true, and none of it was the thing Edran had asked.

Edran looked at him, weighing it as Cael had watched him weigh an opponent between exchanges. Cael saw him consider pressing and decide against it, not out of kindness but out of something more like respect for a guard that had been put up properly.

"That isn't an answer."

"No."

"It's the one you're giving, though."

"Yes."

Edran nodded once, slowly, and left for the training hall without saying anything else. The boy with the boot went after him, and the others drifted apart, already talking. Cael stayed where he was by the north gate, his own shadow stretched long beside him on the packed earth, and understood that the question had not been answered. It had only been put down, carefully, where both of them could see it.

He told Brom that evening in the stable, over barley, because by now it would have felt stranger not to. Brom heard it out as he heard out any fight, which was what it had been, and asked two questions — where the other students had been standing, and whether Edran had raised his voice — and then ate without speaking, turning it over.

"The yard was the point," he said finally. "Not the question. He wanted a dozen people to hear you not answer."

"It isn't you he's cross with." Brom pointed his spoon toward the window, at the dark yard and the training hall beyond it. "It's the standings. He's sat at the top of them since he came, and he thinks they're true. Then this morning the faculty spent first bell on somebody who isn't on them at all, and nobody will say why." Brom considered that with great care. "Watch him. Don't fear him. Whatever he does next, he'll do it by the book, in daylight, with his name signed at the bottom, and you'll see it coming a week off." He paused. "That's the useful part. The other part is that he'll never cheat you. Remember which is which."

Lira, listening from the end of the bench with her chin on her fist, said, "I like him."

They both looked at her.

He wrote the day up that night by the narrow window with the lamp turned low, in the four headings, because the four headings were where it belonged.

First assessment. Function shown: Wind framework, four passes at panel speed, one with the count broken; surface read, four calls, eyes closed on the last. Benefit: assessment satisfied. Wray's words, on the record: Unorthodox architecture. Consistent execution. No safety concerns. Cost: one strike taken, left forearm, third pass. The Compression fragment woke for it and I gave it no road. It let go cleanly. Bruise, about the size of a hand.

Integration note. Today was the first time I have ever chosen not to use a fragment that was already reaching. Not failed to reach. Chosen. It went quietly. I think that is worth more than the quarter-push.

He sat with that. It was true, and it frightened him a little, and he could not entirely say why.

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper, because he had told Hesk he would write again after the first assessment and he had meant it. The second letter was shorter than the first. He told Hesk the assessment had passed, that three people had watched, that one of them had asked a good question and he had answered it with a limit in it and the man had looked pleased. He did not mention the strike or the fragment that had reached for it; some things were for the binder. He signed it, folded it, and set it ready for Lira's next trip down the hill.

His supervised hour on the third day of the second week was the one he would remember, though nothing much happened in it.

It was section four again, and Quenna again, standing at the chalk with her slate, and nobody else. He ran the framework against no one at half pace and then full, supplying his own strikes out of the air, and did Lira's circuits, and at some point in the first quarter of an hour he realized he had stopped shaping any of it for the slate. He had not decided to stop. He had simply forgotten she was there. The work had become his own again, plain and repetitive and a little dull, the shoulder rolls slow and careful, the footwork patterns walked through over and over until his legs stopped asking his head for directions. Twice he glanced up and found her pen still, and once he found her not looking at him at all but out of the high window. He understood that she had stopped needing to watch him closely, which might have been the most useful thing that had happened to him all week.

At the end she signed the session record, and then, instead of handing him only the strip, she took a second slip from inside her folder and held it out between two fingers.

It was small and stiff, printed on a thin grey card, with a date stamped across one corner in purple ink and a set of initials he did not know in the margin. The printing said Received for Notation and beneath it, in a clerk's hand, his name, and the name of the academy, and a long reference number.

"It came back up the hill yesterday," Quenna said. "The regional office acknowledges what it's sent. They always do."

He turned the card over. The back was blank.

"So someone has it now."

"Someone has entered a line in a book, and initialed a card, and put the card in the post." She took it back from him and slid it into the folder. "Whether anyone reads the line is another question, and not one the card can answer."

"Usually," said Cael, "a clerk files it."

She looked at him, and he could not tell whether she was amused.

"Usually." She closed the folder. "You're learning the language. Keep your assessments clean, Cael. That's the whole of our part. Theirs is theirs."

He walked back across the yard with the date on the card fixed in his head, a purple stamp on a grey corner, the first thing that had come back up the road the brown envelope had gone down. It did not frighten him. It was more like hearing, from inside a quiet house, a door close somewhere at the far end of it, and knowing that someone else was home.

In every other free hour of that second week, he watched.

That was what an observer was allowed to do, so he did it from the rail of the open floor, from the edge of the formal yard during standings bouts, and from the back of the defensive branch's drill hall, with the binder open on his knee and his pen moving. He could not fight anyone, and he was not eligible for the open bouts where most of the real learning of the place seemed to happen, the unscheduled matches between friends and rivals that filled the late afternoons. But nobody had ever said he could not stand at the edge and look. By the fourth day he was building a page format without having decided to. Across the top went the declaration structure, as far as he could see it from outside — what came up, where, how fast, how it faded — and down the left margin went the tells, the small things a body did before its Path did anything, and the middle held timing, and at the bottom of each page, after some days of trying other headings, he wrote a single line under the words What this Path believes. He had come to think that every Path held an opinion, not only a set of abilities but a settled view of what a fight was for and how one ought to be won, and that you could see the view in how its people stood long before they threw anything.

Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose.

The Stone Path page was the broad student's. Cael had watched him three times now, on Brom's floor, across a chalk ring at first bell, and once more on the fifth day of the second week, drilling alone in a corner of the defensive hall while Wray worked with someone else. Alone, with no one to be furniture for, the boy moved differently — slower than Cael had expected, and lower, his feet barely seeming to leave the floor, though every shift of his weight went down through his heels and came back up a fraction stronger, as if the ground were lending him something each time he touched it. Watching, Cael realized that the boy had been solving the same problem Brom solved, how to take a strike and survive it, from the opposite direction entirely. Brom gave and borrowed. This boy refused to be moved at all, and let the ground do the arguing.

Stone believes that if you refuse to move, the other person has to.

The defensive branch posted its drill pairings on a board outside the hall. Cael had walked past it every day for a week without reading it, because it had nothing to do with him, and that afternoon he stopped and read it. Brom's name was there three times in the week, and each time, paired beside it in the same clerk's hand, was a single short name.

Hobb. Stone. Fourth year.

He said it under his breath once, to fix it. Hobb. It was a plain name for a plain, patient person who stood where he was told and hit exactly as hard as he was asked. Cael did not go and find him. A thank-you shouted across a drill hall would be worth nothing, and he did not want it to look like an observer currying favor with the partner who had been lent to him. He wanted a quiet moment, and he thought he would know it when it came. He wrote the name under the Stone Path page and left the debt where he could see it.

That evening he told Brom. "His name's Hobb."

Brom put his hand up flat, like a man stopping a cart. "Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't tell me any more. I'm going to ask him myself." Brom looked faintly embarrassed, and then faintly stubborn. "It's been two weeks. He's hit me harder than anyone in my life. The least I can do is hear his name from his own mouth."

"You already know it now."

"I'll forget it," said Brom firmly. "Watch me."

Lira's standings bout fell on the sixth day of the second week.

It was not an assessment, not exactly. The re-certification track required its candidates to fight a set number of recorded bouts each month against students from the standard roster, with the results entered in the candidate's file beside an assessor's initials, and it was meant to be routine. The clerk who scheduled it had paired her, by the luck of the roster, with a third-year standing high on the Copper side of the board: a Force Path, tall and long-armed, Copper Rank Eight by the board, with a clean record and a reputation, Cael learned from the rail, for pushing opponents out of the ring before they had properly set their feet. He had no dot beside his name anywhere. He came into the formal yard with the easy, half-bored air of somebody keeping an appointment.

Twenty or so students had drifted in to watch, not for him but for the dot.

Cael stood at the rail. Gerda came and stood two places along, arms folded, her ash-pale hair bright in the cold sun, and nodded to him without speaking, and a minute later Brom arrived, still moving carefully on his right side, and leaned on the rail between them.

"She's going to go hard," Brom said quietly.

"I know."

"She shouldn't."

"I know. She's going to anyway."

Gerda glanced at them both and said nothing, and then said, to no one in particular, "The assessor will initial it either way. The procedure doesn't care how she wins."

"Lira cares," said Brom.

Gerda considered that, and something passed over her face that Cael could not quite read.

"Yes," she said. "I used to."

The ring in the formal yard was wider than the training-hall sections, eight long paces across, and the chalk around its edge had been freshly laid, white against the packed brown earth. The Force Path took his mark on the north side with his back to the benches. Lira took hers on the south with the low sun behind her, which Cael suspected she had arranged by being first to the ring.

The first exchange went the way a procedural bout was supposed to go. The Force Path opened at the textbook distance and tested her with two straight pushes, each carrying a thickening of the air in front of his palm, a shove made solid. Lira slipped the first and let the second brush past her shoulder without committing to anything. She was measuring him. Cael could see it in her feet, which never quite settled, and in the way her eyes went to his hips and not his hands. The third push she let come all the way in before stepping off its line at the last instant, the Wind in her moving her a half-pace sideways as easily as a door swinging, so that the push went past her into empty air and the Force Path's weight followed it a little too far. She did not punish it. She stepped back to the centre, and the crowd made a small, satisfied sound, because that was what a procedural bout looked like: careful, polite, a testing of edges.

Then the second exchange began, and Lira stopped being polite.

She came off her mark before he had finished setting his feet, not at the textbook distance but inside it, fast, the way she had fought on the Ardenmere circuit when the rent was due, in a style no academy had ever taught her and Fenmark's records had never once managed to describe. The Force Path threw a push to drive her back out, and it was a good push, a real one, the air in front of his palm going thick and hard as a wall. She could have slipped it. Cael saw the space where she could have slipped it.

She didn't. She took it on the left shoulder.

It knocked her a full step sideways, and Cael heard Brom draw breath beside him. But she had turned with it, so that the step sideways became a step around, and before the Force Path had brought his arm back she was inside his reach on the side he had just opened and had hit him twice, short and hard, once under the ribs and once on the point of the shoulder that had pushed. He gasped and went back a pace, and the yard went very quiet. Cael had seen this before, on the circuit, many times: Lira had paid for those two strikes with a hit she could have avoided, deliberately, in front of everyone, so that everyone would see the price and see that she did not care about it.

The Force Path was not a fool, and in the third exchange he tried to take the fight back to where he wanted it. He reset to the textbook distance, five paces, and set himself properly, rear foot planted, and when she came in again he was ready for her and threw a push low and wide at her legs to take away her footing. She jumped it, the Wind lifting her a hand's width higher than her legs alone could have managed, and landed already moving, and he had read that too. His second push was waiting for her landing, high this time, aimed at the place her shoulders would be when her feet touched down. It caught her half-turned and drove her back across the ring toward the south chalk, three paces, four, until her rear heel came down a finger's width from the line and she stopped there with her arms out like someone on a wall.

The crowd drew a breath. Gerda's arms came unfolded.

Lira stood on the edge for a heartbeat, breathing, and Cael watched her look not at the Force Path's hands but at his feet. He saw what she saw, because he had been watching the same thing from the rail. Before every full push, the tall boy planted his rear foot. It was a small thing, the heel going down flat and the knee softening, the body asking the ground for something to push from, and it came a clear half-beat before the palm opened. She had found the tell. The next push came, and his rear heel went down. She was already gone, stepping off the line on the plant rather than the palm, so that the push went through the space where she had been and out over the chalk into empty yard. She came back along the edge of the ring and in at his side, and he had to turn to find her.

He learned. Cael saw him learn it, and liked him a little for it. For the rest of the third exchange the tall boy stopped planting, pushing instead from wherever his feet happened to be, short, quick shoves with less weight behind them, which she could no longer read early but which no longer had the weight to move her far. The exchange ended with the two of them back near the centre, his chest heaving and her shoulder beginning to stiffen, neither ahead.

The fourth exchange was the one Cael drew afterward in the binder, as a map, with arrows.

She did not let him have distance. A Force Path's push needed room between palm and target to gather itself, the way a door needs room to swing. She simply refused him that room: she came in and stayed in, inside his elbows, so close that any push he threw had nowhere to travel before it reached her and arrived as little more than a shove. He stepped back to make space. She followed, and slightly to his left. He stepped back again. She followed, a little more to his left. Each step he took to find room for his arm she turned into a curve, so that without ever once hitting him she walked him in a slow arc across the ring. Cael, at the rail, watched the chalk come up behind the tall boy's heels a foot at a time and saw that he could not see it, because he was looking at her.

At the last he understood where he was. Cael saw it in his shoulders. He planted, once, hard, for one big push to clear her off him and buy back the ring — and she ducked under it, so that his own weight carried him forward over his planted foot. She put two fingers flat against his breastbone as he came, not a strike, hardly a touch, and he stepped back to keep his balance as anyone would.

His heel came down on the chalk.

The assessor called it.

He made the week's inventory on the last night of the second week, cross-legged on his bed with the binder open across his knees and the lamp turned low, and he counted the Path pages first.

Glass, from Edran. Stone, from Hobb. Shield, from Wray, from the way she stood and the way she changed one condition at a time, though he had never once seen her use her Path. He stopped there.

He had walked through the gate two weeks ago thinking of the observer category like everyone else, as a patch over a hole in the rules, bars with a hinge on them, something to put up with until somebody could argue him into a better one. He had thought the narrowness was the whole of it and that his work at Greyvane would be pushing at the bars. It was not the whole of it. The category had shut him out of every open bout on the grounds; it had refused him a partner, in writing; it had put him in a room full of thirteen-year-olds and at the far end of every desk. And because it had shut him out of everything, it had left him standing at the edge of everything with nothing to do but look. He had never had so much to look at in his life.

He took his time over it, using the slow square hand that meant he expected to come back to the page.

Greyvane, end of week two. Eleven Paths seen closer than the circuit ever let me. They built the observer track to hold me still.

He looked at the line, and then wrote the rest of it.

Holding still turns out to be the best place to see from. It isn't a cage. It's a vantage point.

He read back over the two weeks before he closed the binder, as he did on nights that felt like hinges. Brom had a teacher, a woman who wanted to be proved wrong and had said so out loud to twenty people, who had found the soft place in him and offered to mend it. He was still holding his right side when he sat down and was happier than Cael had ever seen him. Lira had put something down at an intake desk and picked something else up on a formal floor. She was carrying both now, the signed page and the third-year's heel on the chalk, and neither of them had won; she was letting them share the bench. He himself had a name on a public board in the main hall, in the same ink as everyone else's, and a hollow circle on his tag, and a refusal in writing behind the stranger's note in the back of his binder, and a grey card with a purple date on it in a folder somewhere in Quenna's keeping. He had a first assessment satisfied and a second coming in a month. He had a question asked in the open by the best fighter in the building, and an answer that was true and was not an answer, and a strong feeling that the question had only been set down, not dropped. And he had a name he owed a thank-you to. Hobb.

He closed the binder.

Across the corridor, quite clearly, in his sleep, Brom said, "Hobb," and then grunted, and turned over.

Cael lay back in the dark and laughed silently at the ceiling until his ribs hurt. Then he lay still and listened to the building, and knew every sound in it now — the stair, the latch, the pipe, the bell far off saying it was late.

He knew where he was. He fell asleep knowing it.

Chapter 31 — Twenty-Two

[Patient, lucid narration; make repeated failure feel cumulative rather than repetitive]

There were eleven people at the rail for Gerda's supplementary bout, six more than had come to her review, and Cael counted them as he came through the gate, because he could not help it, and because he thought that Gerda, who counted hours, would want somebody to have counted this.

Lira was at the front, with her shoulder unstrapped at last and a purple-yellow bruise showing at the edge of her collar. Brom was beside her, and Karis, with her hands in her pockets and no notebook. Hobb was at the far corner, as always, with his hands in his pockets too. The other seven Cael had not expected. Two of them were Current Path second-years from the movement floor. One was the girl whose footwork never stopped, who had lost to Gerda at the review and seemed to have come to see what she had lost to. Three were from the heat-Paths floor, Ash and Ember first-years standing together. They had the shy bright look of children who have heard that somebody from their own floor is going to do something, and do not want to miss it. One was the second-year tout, who had no wager on it, because nobody was taking wagers on a re-certification candidate's supplementary bout at first bell, and who had come, he told Cael, out of professional interest.

The last was Edran. He stood by the standings board with his arms folded, as he had stood for Lira's review. He did not look at the board.

The panel sat at the north end: Quenna in the middle with the review ledger open and Gerda's request beside it, Wray on her left in the brown coat, the heat-Paths instructor with scorched cuffs on her right. The same panel, as the provision required.

Gerda came through the gate as the first bell finished. She did not stop to look at the clock above the main hall door. Cael watched her walk past it with her chin level and her stride unbroken and her eyes on the ring, and he saw her not look, and he knew that it had cost her something and that she had decided to pay it.

"For the record," said Quenna. "Supplementary recorded bout, elected by the candidate under the reconsideration clause of the re-certification provision. The candidate: Gerda, Ash Path. Recorded tier, Copper. The bout will be considered together with the candidate's formal review, and advancement assessed against the originating record. The candidate takes the south mark."

The opponent was the Blade fourth-year with the very fast hands. Cael did not know whether the panel had chosen him on purpose, and suspected Wray had, and suspected that Gerda had been told and had said nothing but Good. He took the north mark and stood light on his feet with his fast hands loose and his face listening to whatever it always listened to.

Wray lowered her finger.

Gerda laid her haze. It went out from her across the swept earth, low and grey, two paces in every direction, as it always had. The Blade fourth-year stopped at its edge and waited, as he had waited before, light and patient, outside the slowness, with nothing to make him go into it.

Gerda came out.

She came out at once, before he had settled, two long strides out of her own grey onto clean ground. She went straight at him with nothing slowing anybody down at all, and his fast hand flicked out and caught her on the shoulder, exactly where it had caught her at the review, and she took it. Cael saw her take it. He saw her see the hand coming, and see the space where she could have turned from it, and not turn. She let it land on the point of her shoulder, where everyone at the rail and all three chairs could see it land.

"Touch," said Wray. "Opponent."

Gerda went back to the south mark with her shoulder held a little away from her side and her face white and absolutely calm. She did not look at the rail. She did not look at the clock. She looked at the Blade fourth-year, and Cael, who had watched her for two months, saw something in her face he had never seen there before. It was the look Lira wore on a circuit floor after a hit she had chosen. That one cost me. Now you owe me.

"She took it," said the tout behind Cael, in a reverent whisper.

"She bought it," said Lira, without turning round.

The second exchange she won with Hobb's lesson.

She laid the haze again and came out of it again. This time when the fast hand came she took it on the forearm and turned from it, and the turn put her in under his arm, close, closer than a Blade fighter ever let anybody get. She put her shoulder into his side with the whole of her weight and the whole of her height, the way she had put it into Hobb on the fourth evening. A Blade fourth-year was not a Stone fourth-year. He went three steps sideways and nearly off his feet, and her two fingers were on his breastbone before he had them back under him.

"Touch. Candidate."

At the far corner of the rail Hobb took his hands out of his pockets.

The third exchange went by slower. He had learned; she had shown him twice now that she would come out, and he met her at the edge of the haze instead of waiting outside it. For a long moment the two of them circled on the border of the grey, his fast hands against her height, neither committing. Cael held his breath. He could see the old weight on Gerda's back foot, the pull toward the haze, the relief that waited for her two paces behind her. He saw her feel it, and he saw her leave it there. She stepped forward onto clean ground, took his next strike on the outside of the forearm without slipping it, and turned under it. Her fingers were on his breastbone. Wray called it, and called the bout.

Two touches to one.

The yard did not cheer, because it never did. But the three heat-Paths first-years at the rail made a sound of pure delight they could not keep in. The Current Path girl who had lost to Gerda at the review clapped twice before she remembered where she was and stopped. Lira, at the front of the rail, put both hands flat on the cold wood and leaned on them and let her head drop between her shoulders, and Cael heard her breathe out for what he thought must have been the first time since the first bell.

The panel did not go to the far wall. They talked at the table, low, for no longer than it took the Blade fourth-year to walk across the ring and shake Gerda's hand, which he did, formally and in silence, as he did everything. Then Quenna wrote, and lifted her pen.

"Supplementary recorded bout. Won by the candidate, two touches to one. The panel considers it together with the formal review." She read off the ledger in the slow flat cadence of the record. "The candidate came out of her own declaration under live opposition and committed fully, at cost, twice. The recommendation of reconsideration is withdrawn. Continued track."

She looked up from the ledger at Gerda, across the frosted earth.

"Initials," she said. "And the candidate's signature."

Gerda walked to the table and signed, carefully, every letter separate. Then she stood and read the line Quenna had written, as Lira had stood and read hers, and Cael saw her lips move once.

She came off the ring toward the rail. She stopped in front of Lira, who had straightened up and folded her arms and arranged her face into something she clearly hoped looked unconcerned, and did not.

"Answered," said Gerda.

"Correctly?" said Lira.

Gerda considered the word, as she considered every word.

"Correctly," she said. "And the right way round, this time."

Then she did look at the clock above the main hall door. Her eyes stayed on it. And she laughed, a short, surprised, rusty sound, as if it had not been used in a long time and had had to be found at the bottom of a box. Lira, who had kept her face still through two bouts of her own and one of Gerda's, did not manage it through that, and put her good arm round Gerda's shoulders, bruise and all, and held on.

Hobb passed them on his way to the gate. He did not stop. He slowed, as he had slowed at the rail after the exhibition, and looked at Gerda once.

"Didn't go back," he said, in his deep slow voice, and went on out of the yard.

The twenty-second session was that evening, and all four of them came, because it was the last one written into the plan in the back of Karis's ledger and none of them meant to hear about it afterward. Lira had said at the midday meal that she was not going to let a thing end without being in the room for it, especially a thing that had produced nothing.

Lira sat on the floor by the door with her knees up and Gerda's laugh still visible somewhere at the corners of her mouth. Brom sat beside her with a heel of bread he did not take out of his coat. Karis stood at the far end of the whitewashed room with the ledger closed on the boards at her feet. Cael stood three paces from her, as close as the room had ever brought them except for the one morning at a single pace.

"I consent," said Karis, "to this session," and wrote the hour.

She had told him at breakfast what the last session would be. It would not be a variable; it would be all of them, everything the twenty-one before it had tried, put into one hour at the nearest distance and the longest duration she could sustain without her points going dirty, and she would run it fresh. He would reach every way he had ever reached, in turn and then all at once, with the read open on his skin and every channel of attention he owned turned toward her like lamps.

"If it doesn't close in that," she had said, "it doesn't close in this room. And then we'll know that, which is the finding."

So she began, and he reached.

He knew her now. That was what he understood, standing three paces from her in the last hour of the series while the first point bloomed at the height of her shoulder and held. He knew her better than he had known anybody he had ever watched, better than he had known Reydan at the top of the bout and better, in this one narrow way, than he knew Lira. He knew where she would lay the next point before she laid it. Twice in the hour, with the read open, he felt the air at that place grow faintly heavier before the light came, as it had on the long afternoon. He could have shut his eyes and rebuilt any one of twenty-two sessions from the sounds alone, the small hiss of a point, the kettle-song of a channel, the click of her pen. He had learned her past memory, down into the place below memory where Lira's footwork lived in his feet.

And every way he reached, and all of them at once, nothing came.

There was no settling, and no space being filled. Nothing arrived anywhere in him, and no line appeared in anybody's handwriting. There was a boy standing three paces from a girl in a whitewashed room, wanting, and the girl's small exact fires blooming and going out, and the light in the high window going from grey to dark. Lira and Brom by the door did not move once in the whole hour.

At the end Karis let her last point go and watched its shimmer hang and fade, and then she bent and picked up the ledger.

"Null," said Cael.

"Null." She wrote it, in the same careful hand, at the same size, as she had written the first. Session twenty-two. All conditions of sessions one to twenty-one, combined. Result: null. Then she did not close the ledger. She looked at the page, and drew a line across it under the last session, straight from one side to the other, as a person rules off a column of figures before writing the total.

Under the line she wrote one more sentence. She did not read it aloud. She turned the ledger round and held it out across the three paces so that he could read it under the fourth clause.

Twenty-two controlled sessions. Twenty-two nulls. We are missing a condition.

"I don't know what it is," said Karis. Her voice was perfectly level. "The pattern's real. I'd stake both notebooks on it. You watch, and then something comes. But watching isn't closing it, not at demonstration intensity, not at any distance or for any length of time I can sustain, not however hard you reach. Whatever closes it is something else, and it isn't here with us." She lowered the ledger. "I can't think of anything I could bring into this room that would be it, without breaking the line at the bottom of our page."

Cael said nothing. He stood with his hands at his sides and the binder under his arm, and he felt Lira's eyes on him from the door, steady, and did not look round. It was the hardest thing he had done in twenty-two sessions, harder than any of the reaching.

Karis looked at him. Perhaps she knew there was something behind his silence. She had a page, after all, called Not asked. She did not ask.

"So that's the end of the plan," she said. "But I want to say one more thing, with all three of you here, so that it's witnessed."

Brom took his hand out of his coat.

"Whatever it is that closes it," said Karis, "someday there may be a way to find out that doesn't break that last line. An honest way, that nobody has to arrange in secret. I don't know what it would look like. I've asked Quenna once whether there could ever be anything more than watching, inside the rules, and she said not as the provision's written, and I asked her to keep the question." She held Cael's eyes across three paces. "If that day ever comes, I want it to be me. I've done the hard part already. I've stood in front of it twenty-two times on purpose, knowing what it might do. If it's going to be tested, I'd rather it were tested on someone who knows what she's standing in front of, and has written down everything she knows about it first."

The quiet room was very quiet.

"You don't have to decide that," said Lira from the door. Her voice was not quite steady. "Not now. Not ever, if you don't want."

"I know I don't." Karis did not look away from Cael. "I've decided it anyway. I'm only telling you, so that it's on the record somewhere honest, which is this room, with the three of you in it."

For a moment Cael could not say anything at all. He thought of the line at the bottom of the second terms, if anything at all happens to the source that the source did not choose. The girl who had written it to keep herself safe from him was standing in front of him now with her ledger in her hand and her face perfectly calm, choosing to stand in front of it anyway.

"Noted," he said at last, because it was the only word he had that was big enough and it was hers.

Something moved at the corner of Karis's mouth, and she did not correct it.

Then Brom cleared his throat.

"Is there," he said, "now that it's over, any chance at all of a clause about barley?"

Karis looked at him, and then at the ledger. Then she uncapped her pen and wrote, under the line, under the sentence about the missing condition, in her small upright hand, and turned it round so that Brom could read it from the door.

Witness's barley clause: granted, after the fact, in recognition of service.

Brom read it twice. He sat back against the wall with his bread in his hand and an expression of such deep and peaceful satisfaction that Lira, beside him, laughed until she had to put her head down on her knees.

After supper he sat down at the desk by the narrow window and took out a clean sheet of paper. The third-day post went down the hill in the morning. Hesk had written that he read both kinds of letter the same, and this week had been mostly the second kind.

Hesk —

Nothing happened this week. I'm writing because you said to.

He looked at that, and decided it was true in the way that mattered to Hesk and not true in any other way, and went on.

I've been working at something in my training that won't come. I tried it twenty-two times over three weeks and it failed every one of them. A girl here helped me try. She wrote down every failure as carefully as anybody ever wrote down a win, and the word null the same size every time. I didn't know you could be failed at that carefully. It's better company than you'd think.

Lira had her review. The academy wrote down, in its own words, that her old school's paper about her doesn't add up. She's keeping both papers. She'll want to tell you the rest herself one day, so I won't.

Brom found out from his teacher that there's a gap in the way he turns a strike that only somebody very fast could use, and nobody here is fast enough to teach him to close it. He took it the way he takes everything, by eating more barley.

There's a woman on Lira's track who fought a bout today and came out of her own corner for the first time in a long while. She laughed afterward. I don't think she'd laughed since she came here. I wanted you to know that, though you've never heard of her.

The shoulder still complains. The bench sounds good.

Cael

It was not long. Nothing in it was untrue, and a great deal was left out: the whole push, the quarter of a strike, a mark in a ledger beside a page he never added to. Some things were for the binder. But he had told Hesk about the ledger and the word null and the woman who had laughed, and he thought that was nearer to the whole of the week than it looked. He folded it, wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the corner of the desk for Lira to take down in the morning.

Then he went out to the wall, because Lira would be on it, and she was, alone, in the dark.

The frost had come back after two days of rain, and the top of the wall was rough with it under his hand. Below them the hill town's lamps were coming on down the slope, along the street worth walking and then the street worth avoiding. On the corner stood the grey building with its board over the door, where Oona would go down in a little under six weeks with her mother's hand on her shoulder. Lira had her knees drawn up and her coat round her. Fenmark's four paragraphs, he knew without seeing them, were folded inside it against her ribs.

"Gerda's asleep," she said. "Before the eighth bell. I watched her go in. I don't think she's been asleep before the eighth bell since she came here."

"You did that."

"Hobb did that. And she did. I only stood at the rail and shouted." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Quenna's sent you a note. It'll be under your door."

"How do you know?"

"Karis has one. She showed me at supper. She held it out at arm's length so I'd know she wasn't hiding it." Lira looked out at the yard. "She's very proper about notes."

They sat without saying anything. Somewhere down the hill a dog barked twice and stopped.

"You didn't tell her," said Lira. "Tonight. When she wrote the line."

"No."

"It was in your face the whole time. I could see it from the door."

"I know."

"She told the three of us she'd stand in front of it," said Lira, "and you were holding the one thing that would tell her what she'd be standing in front of."

He did not answer, and she did not ask him to. She looked back at the yard.

"Whatever Quenna wants, after the sitting," she said, "you'll tell me the day you know it."

"The day I know it."

"Same rule."

"Same rule. Heavier load."

"It'll hold," said Lira, and stood, and put her hand on his shoulder, light and brief, and took it away, and went in.

The note was under his door when he came up, folded once, with no envelope and no seal, delivered with the ceremony of a laundry list.

After the fourth sitting. My room, the third bell. Bring Karis. She has the same note. Q.

He read it standing in the doorway with one hand on the latch.

The fourth sitting was on the calendar in the main hall, posted a week ago in the clerk's small even hand: Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track. Fourth sitting. Training hall, first bell. Eighth day. It was a routine sitting, and he meant to keep it routine. Hobb would be there, and Wray would say something with nothing new in it, and Quenna would write Assessment satisfied.

It was after that sitting that something would happen.

He put the note in the back pocket of the binder, with the papers that proved things: the written refusal of a training partner from his first week, the stranger's note from Ardenmere, Vell's sheaf, and the fair copy of his own third sitting. It did not prove anything yet, but he put it there anyway.

Then he sat down at the desk with the lamp turned low and wrote, slowly.

End of week eleven. Twenty-two sessions with Karis as source, every way I know how to reach. Twenty-two nulls. No fragment. No notice. Under the last one she ruled a line: we are missing a condition.

Session nine at Ardenmere is kept, marked real, unexplained, keep. Compression-adjacent: the whole slow push once, a quarter of a fast strike once. Wind-adjacent lead held, narrowly.

Quenna wants to see us both after the fourth sitting.

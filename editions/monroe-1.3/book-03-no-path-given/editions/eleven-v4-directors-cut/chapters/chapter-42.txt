The seal was new to the gate clerk, and her face could not settle on what to do about it.

It was the middle of the morning on the Monday of the nineteenth week, between the second bell and the third, and the yard was full of the ordinary traffic of a school between lectures. Students crossed with books under their arms and kit over their shoulders, a cart of flour sacks was being checked in at the kitchen gate, and a second-year was being shouted at by somebody for leaving a door open. Cael was sitting on the low step of the lecture wing with the observation notebook on his knee, halfway down a page about Edran's off arm. He had watched the third structure come up whole four times in five that morning at the high windows, and he was trying to write down why, when something at the gate pulled his eyes off the page as a wrong note pulls a listener's head round.

There were two travellers at the clerk's booth. One was young, with a satchel on each shoulder and a case in each hand, standing the half-step back that an aide stands from the person he carries for. The other was older, in plain travelling clothes the colour of the road, and he was holding out a small stamped seal on a card, and waiting.

The clerk had it in her inky fingers and was turning it over. She looked at it much longer than she looked at anything that came through her booth, and Cael watched her look and knew what the look was. It was the look of somebody checking a thing against her memory and finding nowhere in her memory to put it. She said something. The older man answered, patiently, and took a second paper from a document case under his arm, folded once: a careful traveller's spare proof. The clerk read it, and put her finger on her list, and found something on the list, and the gate opened as it opened for anybody the list expected.

The second paper he never needed to see.

He had sat across a borrowed desk from that patience once, in Ardenmere, with his hands pressed together on his knees and a paper of his own that he had found in a district archive in two days. He had learned its shape that afternoon without meaning to: a man who wrote everything down and never seemed to hurry, and never once, in the whole afternoon, wrote anything that was not so. The shape was older now, and more travelled, and there was more grey in it. It was built the same way underneath.

He watched Coss cross the yard.

That was the part he would turn over afterward and never get to the bottom of. He did not stand, or call out, or move at all. He sat on the step with the open notebook on his knee and the pen loose in his fingers, and watched the man who had carried his file since Denvash cross the middle of everything Cael had made for himself at Greyvane. Coss walked it at the unhurried pace of somebody whose authority was so complete that it never needed showing. He did not look round the yard. He did not have to. Somewhere in that document case, Cael knew with a certainty that went cold all the way to the bottom, the next thing was already written down with his name at the top of it, and had been written long before the clerk turned her key.

Coss and the aide went in at the door of the old building, the one that led to the worn stair.

Cael looked down at the notebook. He had written third structure, four in five, whole and then nothing. He did not write anything else. Whatever had just happened was real already; the notebook could add nothing to it.

Naveth's runner found him in the archive twenty minutes later.

He had gone there because he could not think of anywhere else to go, and because Prynn had been in the middle of explaining to him, the evening before, the way she numbered the slips she left in place of a pulled volume, and he had asked her to finish. He had asked only so that his hands would have something to do. She had seen that at once, and finished anyway, in more detail than the subject strictly needed, and he had been grateful.

The runner was a first-year with a cold nose who put his head round the iron-banded door and said, all in one breath, "Provost wants you in his office. Now, he says, not after," and was gone again before Cael was fully on his feet.

Prynn looked at him over her spectacles from the high stool.

"Whatever it is," she said, "the shelves will still be here."

No other comfort was to be had in that archive. She had meant it that way, and he knew it going out. It did more work in him on the way up the stair than it had any right to.

The walk took four minutes, and he spent every one of them counting, because counting was easier than looking at the one thing he was not ready to look at. Fourteen students crossing the yard. Three crows along the kitchen ridge. His boots loud on the flags and then quiet on the trodden earth, and the wind cold on one cheek. He had rehearsed this day since Denvash in more versions than he could number, and in every one of them it had come as a knock: a courier on Hesk's front step on the second morning, one knock, a polite wait, a satchel. In none of them had it come as a patient man in road-coloured clothes holding up a seal at a gate, and the gate swinging open for him because nothing about him was wrong.

That was the lesson about the machine he seemed to need teaching over and over. It did not kick doors in. It never had to. Whoever had hung every door he had ever sheltered behind had hung it with the machine's leave.

The provost's door was open.

He saw it from the bottom of the last flight, the worn stair dipping at the centre of every step under his boots, and he slowed on the landing. Naveth shut his door on the conversations he meant to keep and left it wide on the ones he did not mind anybody hearing, and Cael had learned in the fourth week what a wide door meant in this building. A shut door meant something private. A wide one meant a procedure, with nothing in it the room was ashamed of. And Quenna had taught him, across a dozen sittings and without once putting it into words, that you fought a procedure differently from a secret.

He stopped on the threshold for the length of a breath. Over the visitor's shoulder Quenna looked straight at him, and there was no comfort in her face at all, only an order as plain as chalk: say little, hear everything. He had been taking that look across a panel table since the first sitting.

He went in.

Coss was already speaking when Cael came in, and he finished his sentence before he turned round.

"...which is why I'd rather the provost heard it from me than from the regional channel." Then he stood, as a courtesy, and looked at Cael, and sat down again.

Cael counted the room, because counting was what his mind did with a place before it let him feel anything about it.

There were four chairs and three of them were taken: Naveth behind the desk with both hands flat on the blotter, Coss in the chair angled toward it with a document case open on his knee, and Quenna not in a chair at all but at the tall window, her grey folder pressed under one arm. The fourth chair was empty and set a little apart, facing all three of them. The aide was not in the room; Cael had passed him on the landing below, sitting on the bench with both satchels on his lap like a boy waiting for a coach.

So it was that kind of meeting, small and on the record.

"Cael," said Naveth. "Warden Coss. I'm told you know each other."

"From Ardenmere," said Coss, before Cael could open his mouth. He did not say it to take anything from him; he said it because it was exact. "Two days in a borrowed office and a good many questions, and I asked them as gently as they would go."

He was older; that was the first thing Cael's eyes took in after the counting. The grey had come further up from the temples, and there were lines at the corners of the eyes, the kind lamplight gives a man who reads late. The hands on the document case were the same hands, still and patient and tidy, and they did not seem to belong to anybody in a hurry.

"This concerns your enrollment," Naveth said. "That makes it a proceeding on your status, and you sit in on those by right. Not because anybody's being kind to you." He turned his head toward Coss, and there was something hard in it that Cael had never seen him show. "I asked that it be said before anything else was. The Warden agreed."

"Readily," said Coss. "When a subject hears a thing at second hand, the second hand gets it wrong, and then somebody appeals, and we all do it twice."

Quenna's eyes went to the empty chair, and Cael sat.

His voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "You've come a long way from your district."

"I have. That needs explaining first." Coss took something small from the case and held it so the room could see it. It was a card, stamped and plainly printed, not much bigger than a playing card, and Naveth did not look at it. He had seen it already, Cael realized, alone, before anybody went to fetch the observer from the archive.

"'Warden' was never a town title," said Coss.

He let it settle in the quiet office.

"Districts hear the word and think of a man with a lantern and a list of lost dogs. The Compact lets them think it, because a district opens its gate to a Warden and has to be talked into opening it for a field agent." He tapped the card once against his knee. "That's what I am, and have been for a long time. I'm not a local officer who took an interest. Your file came to my desk in the week you Kindled. I've held it ever since, from as far off as the assignment wanted me to hold it." He put the card face down on the case. "Greyvane changed the assignment."

Cael did not answer straight away.

He was not surprised, and that was the strange part, because he had always half known it. He had counted watchers on the circuit, and he had sat across a market table from a junior assessor who wrote everything down, and the quiet that came back from his file every season had never been the quiet of nobody. Somewhere there had been a desk, and he had simply never had a face to put behind it.

Now he had one, tired from the road, and it had known him at fourteen.

"You've come to file something," he said.

"I have." Coss brought out a second paper, a thick packet tied crosswise. "The enrollment is invalid as categorized. The category's own gloss reserves it for a candidate who has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment. You were assessed, at the station in Denvash. The registry holds the date, and I hold the entry. The provision's drafting records show what it was written for, and it was not written for you." He set the packet on Naveth's blotter and squared it to the edge. "That is the case against the category. The case that makes it urgent is this one, Provost. Keep an enrollment on your rolls that you know to be invalid, and a chartered academy exposes its own charter to review. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you why this can't be left alone."

Naveth did not move his hands, and Cael, watching him, saw nothing at all on the long face, and understood that this was the most he had ever seen it hide.

He had watched Naveth slow a records request to the last legal hour, one requisition slip at a time. Naveth would have tried every slow and lawful road against this morning in his head already, and none of them went anywhere, because you could keep a request waiting but you could not keep a challenge waiting.

"You're filing a challenge," said Naveth.

"In this room, now, on the record." A third paper came out of the case, already signed. "It compels a public adjudication, with a magistrate of the Compact presiding. It sits on the academy's own ground, and the record goes into the permanent registry. None of this pleases me. I'd ask you to believe that. And I'd ask you to believe as well that my being pleased or not was never part of it."

"Forty years," said Quenna.

Everybody looked at the window; it was the first word she had spoken.

"Forty years this provision has sheltered students here," she said, in a voice so level that it had plainly been chosen. "Late Kindlers. Disputed assessments. Children whose papers your own registry botched, set straight by this house. If the category falls, every one of them is a precedent somebody can pull on."

"I've read the alumni ledger," said Coss. "I mean this: it does the academy credit." There was no heat in his voice at all, and somehow that was worse. "But it doesn't answer me. Every one of those students was what the provision was written for while it held them. None of them was a registered classification. Nobody had ever thought to ask it to cover one." He paused, and chose the next word. "Nobody had been inventive enough. This won't be a hearing about forty years. It'll be a hearing about a single student."

The room went still round the packet on the blotter, and when Cael looked at Quenna he saw only the blank, careful face of somebody deciding which of several things to do would actually help.

"How long," said Naveth, "until it's heard?"

"Three weeks. That interval is the magistrate's office's to set. It isn't mine, and I'd not want it." Coss closed the case, and the clasp made a very small sound. "I've watched this provision put under strain before, in other districts. Three times. I have never seen it survive."

At the door, with the case under his arm, Coss paused and looked back, and that once he spoke to Cael rather than over him.

"You were fourteen," he said. "You came into that office in Ardenmere with a paper you'd found in two days, and it was right, and I had to concede it. I was as gentle that afternoon as the work allowed. I hoped that was the part you'd remember." He did not look away, and he did not soften. "This is also the job. Hear that as plainly as I can say it. It isn't easier for being true. It isn't less true for being hard."

Then he went, and they heard him on the worn stair, unhurried, and his voice on the landing, saying something to the aide, and then nothing.

Naveth let out a breath. "Counsel," he said, to nobody in particular, and reached for a pen and a clean sheet, as if the morning could still be put into an order.

Quenna walked Cael down, saying nothing along the corridor and nothing on the first flight, and he knew her well enough by now to know that a Quenna without a sentence ready was a Quenna who was frightened. On the half-landing, under the narrow window, she stopped.

"Here's how it will go," she said, "so we don't waste a day of it pretending. It'll be the assembly room, on the ground floor of the lecture wing. The record's public, every word. Our counsel gets to see their documents on a timetable, and they get to see ours. Either side can enter witnesses of fact, by name." She counted the points off on her fingers, as Lira counted rumors. "Counsel will fight the timetable and the scope and the order of documents, and every one of those fights buys us days. None of them buys us the result. He went straight for the wording that sheltered you. That means he read our file as carefully as I wrote it."

She looked out of the narrow window at the yard, where the second bell's crowd was thinning and a clerk was carrying a ladder toward the standings board.

"Five sittings," she said. "An exhibition. A match. Three signatures on every page." She said it like a sum she had checked twice and still did not like the answer to. "That was the whole plan, you know: be on the record so thickly that nobody would bother looking. He bothered. He's read every page I wrote, and he's decided the category weighs more than all of them." Her voice did not change, but something in her face did; it was not fear, it was nearer a debt being owned aloud. "It was my name that put you in that chair across from him. So the next three weeks can have whatever they want of me, and I mean that as a promise and not a comfort."

He remembered her in the empty training hall after the first sitting, slate face down. Today proves you're real.

"After the first sitting you said our job was to get on the record before somebody hostile read it," he said. "Somebody has. What's the job now?"

"After that, the job is to be right," said Quenna.

She went back up the stair, and he went on down it alone, one hand on the cold wall.

Coss had meant every word, and that was what Cael kept turning over on the way down, because it was the hardest part to hold. He knew how to be angry at cruelty, since cruelty told you where to aim, but nobody in that office had been cruel. A decent man had climbed a hill so that a boy would hear it from the hand that wrote it. The whole long road from Denvash had needed no cruelty at all; it had only needed people like Coss to be good at their work.

He could not hate that cleanly, and he did not yet know how to fight it either. He stood at the bottom of the stair with both of those, and then he went to find the others, because the rule was the day you knew a thing, and he had known since the gate.

The aide was in the yard when he crossed it, standing at the clerk's booth with a satchel on each shoulder, asking the way down to the draper's in a voice that carried. He saw Cael and stopped in the middle of a word.

He was perhaps nineteen, with a new coat and ink on his cuff, and he plainly had not been told whether he was allowed to speak to the subject. He decided, visibly, that nobody had told him he wasn't.

"He read your file on the coach," he said. "Both days. Every page, and then from the front again. He does that with all of them, they told me at the office, but I thought you'd —" He stopped, and went red to the ears. "I don't know what I thought. He's fair. That's all. Everybody at the office says he's the fairest man on the floor."

"I know," said Cael.

The aide opened his mouth and shut it again, and then the clerk said something sharp from the booth about the draper's, and he turned back to her with both satchels swinging.

Brom was stacking cups, one on top of the next, slowly, and Cael knew from it before he sat down that some of the news had already climbed the stair ahead of him, and that Brom was waiting to have the rest of it given to him properly.

Lira had her boots up on the bench. "All of it," she said. "From the beginning. Not the quick version."

So at the end of the scarred table, under the bricked-up hay door, he gave them the slow version, as plainly as he had ever given them a fragment: the seal at the gate and Naveth's open door; the card, and the word Warden, and what lay under the word; a file kept on him since the day he Kindled; the gloss, and Denvash, and the charter; a magistrate, the assembly room, three weeks. I have never seen it survive.

At field agent Lira lifted both boots off the bench and set them down on the floor together, without hurrying.

She said nothing, but he watched her face go back two years, to a girl being handed nineteen words from Fenmark that had already been used against her before anyone let her read them. It was the face of someone who had learned early how far the machine reached above any office with a door you could knock on.

Karis had left her notebook shut, and both her hands lay flat on the wood.

Brom set another cup on the stack. "Three weeks," he said. "In public. On paper." The next cup went on with great care. "And who speaks for Greyvane?"

"The academy's counsel, I think." Cael rubbed his face with his good hand. "That can wait for tomorrow. Tonight I just wanted you to hear it from me."

"Coss," said Lira.

She said it quietly, in the voice she kept for things she meant to get exactly right on the first try.

"You were fourteen, and he sat down across a table from you. Then he wrote you down in a file, and followed you round the country with it, and waited for you to put a foot wrong." Her eyes came up to his. "And that's his job."

"He meant it."

"Of course he meant it. I can hear that in the way you tell it." She leaned in. "So be angry. Only point it somewhere." One finger came down on the scarred wood between them. "Coss isn't worth the fuel. Point it at what stands behind him, the thing that took a good man and made this his work. And hold on to it. If his being kind puts it out now, you'll walk into that room with no fire in you at all."

Karis lifted her hands off the table at last. She opened the notebook to a clean page and uncapped her pen, slowly, the way a fighter takes a blade down off a rack.

"What do you need?" she said.

"I don't know yet."

"Then I'll begin without you." She was ruling columns already. "A war fought on paper goes to the side with the better index. I've kept indexes since I was eleven."

Brom put the last cup on, seven high, and it did not lean.

"I've done some counting as well," he said. "Three hundred people in the formal yard, and a panel of three, and not a single one of them could name what you did, and the record held regardless. And today one man with one card climbs the hill to tell us the paperwork can't hold you." He lifted the whole stack in both hands, as though weighing the argument itself. "If you ask me, it's the paperwork that should be worried."

Lira nearly laughed, and Karis wrote something down, but Cael gave none of them an answer yet, because the real one was still taking shape somewhere under his breastbone, and he was not going to hand them a piece of it until he could see what it would be whole.

Lira was on the wall afterward, and he went out and sat beside her, because there was nowhere else in the building he wanted to be.

The night was clear and very cold. Below them the hill town's lamps were scattered down the slope as they always were, along the street worth walking and the street worth avoiding, and going out one by one as the town went to bed. One of them did not go out. It burned in an upper window above the draper's shop, at the back, a steady small square of yellow, long after the windows round it had gone dark.

"That's him," said Lira.

"How do you know?"

"The draper told me on Saturday. She's a room over the shop she lets to people's mothers. Oona's mother had it." Lira drew her knees up. "She told me this morning she'd let it again. To a gentleman from the Compact and his clerk, for three weeks, paid in advance." She looked at the lit window. "He's in the mothers' room."

Cael laughed before he could help it, and it came out of him in a way that hurt his chest, and Lira did not laugh, but her shoulder knocked against his, once, the old knock.

"He's reading," said Cael, when he could. "He reads everything from the first page. Every time. He'll have the file open on the bed."

"You know that about him."

"I've sat across a desk from it." He watched the small yellow square. "He read my paper three times, in Ardenmere, before he conceded it. I watched him do it. He didn't skip a line."

Lira was quiet.

"Then you'd better read his," she said. "Three times. Without skipping a line." She stood up off the wall and shook out her coat. "And then you'd better read the thing he never read at all, because if there's anything in that whole building he hasn't read from the first page, that's where you go."

She went in. He stayed on the wall until the lamp above the draper's went out, which was a long time, and then he went in too, and lay awake thinking about a case at the back of the archive, floor to ceiling, as deep as an old woman's arm.

The quiet room had not changed. It was the smaller of the two rooms off the training hall, whitewashed to the ceiling, with a scrubbed board floor and one narrow window high on the end wall. The slate on the nail beside the door still said K. — declaration practice in Karis's careful hand, because the sixth bell was still hers. He stood in the doorway now with his heart going hard, and he made himself notice that, and name it, and go in anyway.

Brom had found the post. It was a length of old oak about as long as a man's forearm, grey with weather on one side and split along the grain on the other. He had stood it upright in an iron bucket of sand from the yard, so that it rose out of the sand like a stump out of snow. The water bucket stood beside it. Lira had set both in the middle of the floor, well away from every wall, and had then moved them a pace further from the window without explaining why.

"Stand where you can reach it," she said. "Right hand. Not the arm."

He had not meant to use the arm. The burn under the loose linen had stopped beating and begun, over the last two days, to itch, which Wray had said was the good sign and Lira had said was the worst part. He kept it close against his side and knelt by the bucket so that the top of the post was level with his chest.

Karis stood three paces off, by the end wall, with her hands at her sides and no notebook anywhere on her. Brom stood at the sand bucket with a scoop. Lira stood by the water. Nobody spoke, and the room was very quiet, the noise of the training hall coming through the narrow door like weather through a shut window.

"Tell us what you mean to do," said Karis. "Before you do it."

He had thought about this for three days, in between the misfires and the columns and the clean mornings, and he had decided that it ought to be said out loud, the whole of it, in order, so that afterward there would be something to measure what happened against.

"I'm going to put my palm flat on the top of the post," he said. "I'm going to turn toward the new one, the way I turn toward the others, without reaching. Then I'm going to ask it for one point, the size of my thumbnail, at the middle of my palm, and let it go. And then I'm going to take my hand away and look."

"The size of a thumbnail," said Lira.

"That's what I mean. I don't know what it'll do."

"Nobody does," said Karis. "That's why we're here."

He put his right palm flat on the top of the post.

The wood was cold and dry and rough, the grain running under his hand in long low ridges, and a splinter caught at the heel of his thumb and he ignored it. He turned inward toward the warmth. It was there at once, low and patient and faintly familiar, and there was something in it that he had begun to think of, since the Sunday, as the feel of her: small commitments kept exactly, and no fuss made about any of them. He did not reach for it. He only attended, as you look at a coin in your palm before you decide to spend it, and then he asked it, as plainly as he knew how to ask anything inside himself, for one small point under the middle of his hand.

Nothing glowed.

He had expected a coin of light under his palm, a warmth climbing into his skin as Karis's points warmed the air above her hand, something he could feel gathering and hold for a count before he let it go. There was none of that. There was no gathering at all, and no holding, and no light that he could see through the cracks between his fingers. There was only a single instant, so short he nearly missed it, when something in him that had been warm was suddenly not warm, as a candle is not lit and then is, with nothing in between. Then it was over, and his hand was lying on a post, and he did not know whether anything had happened.

He took his hand away.

Under it, in the middle of the grey oak, there was a ring of char about the size of a copper coin, black at the centre and brown at its edges. From the centre of the ring a thread of smoke was going up, thin and pale, straight as a plumb line in the still air of the room. As he watched, the black centre showed a bead of yellow, then a small true flame no bigger than a barleycorn. The flame was not dim, not faint, nothing like Karis's patient coins; it was an ordinary small fire, the colour of any candle, and it was eating along the grain of the wood toward the split edge as if it had somewhere to be.

"That's bigger than a thumbnail," said Lira.

"Put it out," said Cael.

Brom put it out. He did it with one scoop of sand, without hurry, as he did everything. Then he pressed the sand down with the flat of the scoop and held it there for a count of five, and when he lifted the scoop there was a grey crust over the char and no smoke at all.

Then the cost came, and it came in a way he had not prepared for, because none of the others had ever sent their bill like this.

It began under his breastbone, a sudden cold drop, the feeling of putting a foot down in the dark for a stair that is not there. He caught his breath on it before he could stop himself. Then the cold ran out from his chest and down his right arm, into the hand that had been on the post, so that by the time he looked at the hand the fingertips had gone pale and faintly grey and the palm was cold through. It was not pain. It was more like being emptied of something he had not known he was full of, all at once and without any warning. And the warmth that had sat in him since Thursday, so faintly and steadily that he had stopped noticing it, was gone. Where it had been there was the old space, the shape he had learned in the quiet room by failing to fill it, and it was cold.

"Cael," said Karis.

Lira had come round the bucket and taken his right hand between both of hers without asking, and was chafing it hard. She was looking at him and not at the post.

"I didn't see anything," she said. "You know that? I was watching your face the whole time, and I didn't see anything at all. You looked like you were thinking about your dinner."

He could not do it twice, not at once.

When the cold had gone out of his hand, which took longer than he liked, he put the palm back on the post a finger's width from the crusted ring and turned inward, and there was nothing to turn toward. The space was there, but it was empty and cold, and asking it for anything was like asking an empty cup to pour.

"It won't," he said.

"Then wait," said Karis. "I'll count."

She counted aloud, slowly, in her even voice, the numbers going up through the quiet room like a clock with nothing else to do. Lira sat down on the scrubbed boards with her back against the whitewash. Brom leaned on the scoop. Cael knelt with his hand on the cold oak and attended, and at sixty there was nothing, and at eighty nothing, and somewhere after ninety he felt it come back. It did not arrive all at once. It crept in from the edges of the empty space until it was faintly and steadily there again, low in his chest, a stone in a wall in the evening.

"Now," he said.

"A hundred and four," said Karis. "Write that in yours. A hundred and four, the first time. It might change."

This time he did what he had learned to do with Compression on Brom's floor, which was the only thing he had learned in his whole life about doing something dangerous small. He chose the size first. Before he turned inward at all he fixed it, a thumbnail and not a hair more, as you might decide how much of a loaf to cut before you lift the knife, and he held the size in his mind. The same instant came, the same candle that was not lit and then was. He took his hand away.

On the Wednesday and the Thursday they went back to the quiet room at the sixth bell, and on both days he did nothing but choose sizes.

He came to understand, over those two afternoons, that this was going to be the whole of the work for a long while. It was the same work he had done with every fragment he had ever been handed. Finding out what it could do was the easy part, and could be done in an hour. The work was finding out what he could make it do on purpose, every time, with nobody holding his hand. The Wind framework had taken him the better part of a year in Ardenmere before it would stay at the speed he asked for. Compression had taken him the whole of the autumn to get from a quarter to a slow push. He did not expect this one to be quicker because it was newer, and it was not.

On the Wednesday he made five marks. The first two came larger than he chose and the third came smaller, so small that the bead of flame went out on its own before Brom could reach it. He sat looking at the little ring with real pleasure until Karis pointed out that a mark too small was exactly as wrong as a mark too large. The fourth and fifth came near enough to a thumbnail that Lira, measuring them with the end of her own thumb, allowed them. Between each the warmth went away and came back, a hundred and six, a hundred and twelve, ninety-eight, and the cold came after every one of them. By the fifth his right hand had stopped warming up between turns. It stayed grey and stiff until he had chafed it against his thigh for a quarter of an hour.

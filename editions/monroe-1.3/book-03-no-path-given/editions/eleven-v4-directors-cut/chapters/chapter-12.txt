Chapter 12 — Borrowed

[Curious, analytical narration; keep consent, records, and trust sharply distinct]

Quenna read the terms standing up.

She read them in the small room off the main hall that the assessors used between sittings, with its one window, its two hard chairs and its long table stacked with slates, and she did not sit down to do it, though both chairs were empty. Karis stood on one side of the table and Cael on the other.

Quenna held the page at reading distance and went through it from the first clause to the four signatures at the bottom, and then turned back to the top and went through it again, and the second reading took longer than the first. Cael began, somewhere around the fourth clause, to wonder whether she was looking for a reason to refuse.

She set the page down at last and looked at Karis.

"Where do the notes live?"

"In my room, in a box with a lock on it," said Karis. "His copies live in his."

"Not in the archive. Not in any academy record. Not attached to anything with a seal on it."

"No."

"Good." Quenna turned the page a quarter-turn on the table, so that it faced Cael. "And you've read the sixth clause properly. You understand that it gives you every word before anyone else sees one."

"Yes."

"Then I have one condition, and it's mine, not theirs." She laid a finger on the margin. "The sessions happen inside your supervised hours, in section four, with me at the chalk. Not on the open floor, and not in somebody's room after the eighth bell. Every hour she watches you is an hour the record already shows you were supervised. If anyone ever asks what an observer was doing with a Ternhall transfer and a notebook, the answer is already written down, in my hand, with a date on it."

Karis was nodding before Quenna had finished. Cael saw what the condition was for, and saw that Karis had seen it too. It was not a restriction at all. It was a coat Quenna was putting over the whole arrangement, cut in advance to the shape of a question nobody had asked yet.

"There's one more thing," Quenna said. "I won't read her notes, and I don't want to be offered them. If I've read them, I can be asked what they say." She said it without any weight at all, and then she picked the page up once more and looked at the bottom of it, at the seventh clause in Karis's small upright hand.

"Whoever drafted this last clause," she said, "has a future in provision law."

Karis did not quite manage to keep the pleasure off her face. It was a small failure, a flicker at the corner of the mouth that she corrected almost at once, and Cael enjoyed it more than he would have admitted.

Across the table the two of them looked at each other, and Cael had the odd sense of watching an assessor and a student, a lifetime apart in age and standing, find the same appetite in each other: a taste for words that would bear weight.

"Thursday," said Quenna. "Fifth bell. Bring your own stool. Section four doesn't have one."

Karis laid two pens side by side on the boards next to her stool, nibs pointing the same way, in case one of them failed her. The stool was not hers. She had borrowed it from the lecture wing, because section four had none, and she had set it on the far side of the chalk, as far from Cael as the section allowed. Her notebook lay open on her knee. Quenna stood at the chalk with her slate, as she stood at every one of his hours.

The bell finished ringing. A moment later Lira came in.

Nobody had told her the time. Nobody had needed to; the second clause said she could come to any session without a word of warning, and Cael suspected that trying the clause out was half of why she had come to this one. At the rail she folded her arms and settled in, and said nothing to anybody.

Karis lifted her eyes, gave Lira a single nod, and went back to her page. She did not sit straighter. She did not shift her notebook or change where she was looking. Cael watched her not change, and thought it was the right answer, and very likely the only one Lira would have let pass.

"Framework," he said. "Three passes."

It was the evasion framework, the Wind-adjacent pattern Lira had put into him in Ardenmere when neither of them knew what she was teaching or what he was learning. By now it lived somewhere underneath thought. He ran it three times through.

He took the first pass slowly, with loose joints and every change of direction drawn out, so that the bones of it showed through like a sketch under paint. He took the second at working speed, the speed that had carried him through Reydan and the dozen circuit fighters before Reydan, fast enough that an untrained eye would have seen motion and no plan at all. From the chalk, Quenna asked for the third to be slow again. That way the same structure could be laid beside itself at two speeds, and anyone watching could see what changed and what held.

Karis wrote the whole time.

He could hear how she worked even with his back to her. Her pen ran in long unbroken strokes while he moved, and the instant he stopped, it stopped too, and lay quiet on the paper until he went again. She was not describing him. Anyone with eyes could have described him. She was following something that only ran while the framework ran, and when the framework halted there was nothing left for the pen to follow.

She never asked for a movement again. She never broke in to check a detail. Twice he saw the pen come up off the page and hang, as if a question had landed on it, and then settle back without a sound, as if she had answered it on her own.

There was something close to rest in being watched like that. No one was marking him. Whether his feet were pretty, whether the pattern belonged on any Path's chart, whether he ought to be allowed to stay, what word went in which box: none of it seemed to concern her at all. She was taking all of him down, every piece of it worth her trouble.

About twenty minutes into the hour, he caught himself.

He had come out of a turn a shade squarer than the turn needed, and kept his shoulders set on their line a beat too long, so that the shape would read cleanly from where she sat. It was not the circuit itch to make a crowd lean in. It took him the rest of the pass to find what it was instead.

He wanted her to see it right. Impressing her had nothing to do with it. For once in his life someone was really trying to understand him, and he wanted the understanding to be correct.

On the third pass he made himself quit it, because shaping the movement for her page would make the page worth less. That night it went into the binder as a finding of its own. I wanted her to get it right. Not to be impressed. To have it understood. Those aren't the same, and I couldn't explain why to anyone who hasn't been misread for years.

He finished the third pass and stood still in the middle of the section, breathing a little faster than he liked. Karis shut her notebook and put the cap on her pen. It went on with a small, firm click, and he had already learned what the click meant: a thought finished all the way, not just set down.

"One question, if I may."

"That's the arrangement."

She looked up at him from the stool. "Does it feel borrowed, when you use it?"

His breath had not settled, and he simply stood there with the question. It had not been asked lightly. He could tell that at once.

In all the hundreds of times Lira had watched the framework, she had never wondered aloud what it was like to be inside it. Brom read him through the skin better than anyone alive, and even Brom had never come at it from that side, never suggested that a movement might carry a memory of its owner, or that doing it might feel like wearing someone else's coat. Quenna's questions, for all her years of studying what he was, always had answers a person could watch. This one had no outside at all. Only one person could ever stand where its answer was. Karis had walked straight up to that door on her very first afternoon and tapped on it, politely.

And she was nearer than she knew. Every fragment in the binder had a name written beside it, and the first of those names belonged to the girl at the rail with her arms folded.

He did not look toward the rail. He kept his eyes on Karis, and he did not reach for an answer, because he had none, and he had learned long ago not to build one just to get out of an awkward silence.

"I don't know," he said.

That was true. He already suspected he would be turning the question over for a long time.

Karis nodded. If she was disappointed, it did not show; she looked like a person who had predicted this result and was logging it. She wrote nothing. There was nothing to write. She got up, folded the borrowed stool under her arm, thanked Quenna, gave Lira a nod at the rail on her way past, and went out, and the question stayed behind in section four like a seed she was content to leave in the ground.

Quenna made a single mark on her slate, signed the record, tore the strip off and held it out to him without a word.

Lira was waiting in the corridor. She fell in beside him, matching his step, as she had on the way home from more fight-halls than he could have counted. She had heard the question from the rail, and she had heard the answer, so she asked about neither. She went underneath them, to whatever they were sitting on.

"Something happened when she asked you that," she said. "You went off somewhere behind your eyes, and I couldn't come." There was no blame in it. "Tell me when you're back."

"I'm back."

"Mm." She let it go, and he knew that was a gift too. They crossed the yard without talking while the light stretched out long over the chalk.

At the residence wing door she stopped with a hand on the frame, and kept her eyes on the wood.

"For what it's worth," she said, "from outside it doesn't look borrowed. I've watched you run that thing since before either of us knew what it was, and for a long while now it's looked like it was never anybody's but yours." Then she was inside, and he was alone on the step with it, which he guessed was also on purpose.

It was the only thing from the whole day worth writing, and he wrote it.

Does it feel borrowed? Don't know. Lira: it doesn't look it. How a thing looks and how it feels are two different readings, and I'm leaning on hers because I haven't got mine.

Below it, smaller:

Someday I'll find out. Wanting to won't come into it.

The pages came the next evening, as the sixth clause said they would.

Karis found him in the archive after supper, at Prynn's long table, and set a folded sheaf of paper beside his elbow without sitting down. There were four sheets, written out fair in her small upright hand, and a fifth folded inside them that was a copy of the four.

"The session," she said. "Everything I wrote, put in order. The copy is yours to keep. I'll wait, if you want to ask anything, or go, if you'd rather read it alone."

"Stay," he said, and then, because it seemed important to be exact with her, "please."

She sat across the table and opened her own notebook and did not watch him read.

He had never in his life read a page about himself before anyone else had read it. He understood that only as he unfolded the first sheet, and it made his hands slow.

Every page anyone had ever written about him had been written somewhere else and read somewhere else, and had reached him, when it reached him at all, long afterward, in pieces, through someone who had seen it first. The registry's word. The certification clerk's form. Whatever the patient official had been writing all this time. He had always been the last to know what he was on paper.

This page he was the first to know.

It was dry. That was the first thing that struck him. There were no adjectives in it at all.

It described the three passes of the framework in order, with timings in the margin, and the transitions that changed between the slow passes and the working pass and the transitions that did not. Where she had watched something she could not account for, she had written not accounted for and left it there, without a guess.

Nowhere did it say remarkable, or strange, or irregular. Nowhere did it say what he was, or should be, or might become. Nowhere did it say how. It said what a careful person with a pen had seen in section four on a single afternoon, and nothing else, and it said it so plainly that reading it was like looking at his own footprints in fresh chalk.

He read all four sheets twice. On the second reading he found it.

"Here," he said, and turned the third sheet toward her, with his finger beside a line. "You've written that the third pass was the same in structure as the first."

"It was."

"Not quite. On the first pass I came out of the second turn with my shoulders square for longer than I needed to. On the third I didn't." He felt his face go warm, and made himself go on. "I was doing it on the first pass so that you'd see it clearly. I stopped on the third because it wasn't honest. So they weren't the same. One transition was different, and it was different because of me."

Karis studied the line. Then she opened her own notebook, found the page from the session, and ran her finger down a column of timings he could not read upside down.

"You're right," she said. "There's a difference here, in the second turn. I took it for noise." She did not sound embarrassed. She sounded, if anything, interested. "I took it for noise because I didn't know there was a reason for it."

She uncapped her pen. She did not rub out the line or scrape it away. She drew a single straight stroke through it, so that it could still be read, and wrote beside it in the margin, small and exact: Error, observer's. Pass 3 differed from pass 1 at the second turn; subject reports deliberate change. Corrected on the subject's reading. Then she wrote the date, and did the same on his copy, and pushed it back across the table to him.

He looked at the struck line with the correction beside it, and something moved in his chest that he did not have a ready word for.

She had told him in this room, on the first afternoon, that she would be right about small things until it became evidence, and that when she was wrong she would show him the page. He had believed her, more or less, as a person believes a promise that has not yet been tested.

Now it had been tested. She had been right about forty small things on four sheets, and wrong about one, and when he showed her the one she had not argued or explained it away or made it smaller. She had drawn a line through it where anyone could see it, and written down who had caught it.

"Thank you," she said. "That's the most useful thing anyone's said to me since I came here."

"It was one line."

"It was one line I'd have built on." She capped the pen. "Things get built on wrong lines. That's how most of the second notebook happened."

He folded his copy and put it in the back of the binder, with the refusal and the stranger's note and Vell's sheaf, among the papers that proved things. It was the first page in that pocket written about him that he had read before anyone else in the world.

Oona was waiting for him at the assessment board the next morning, which by now he had come to think of as her office.

She did not pretend she had come for any other reason. She stood in front of his line on the calendar with her slate and her tag on its string, and when he came up she turned and looked at him.

"Is the Ternhall girl studying you?"

"Yes."

Oona blinked. "Everybody says she is. They said you'd say she wasn't."

"I told you I wouldn't lie to you."

She considered that. She had a way of considering things that involved her whole face, as if she were working out a sum on the inside of her forehead.

"Can I study you too?"

"No."

"Why does she get to?"

"She asked first," said Cael. "And she wrote down all the things she wouldn't do, and signed it, and let three people argue with her about it for an hour."

Oona weighed this. "I could write down the things I won't do."

"What wouldn't you do?"

"I wouldn't ask you anything boring." She paused, and then added, with scrupulous honesty, "I might ask you some things that are rude. I don't always know which ones are rude until afterward."

Cael was trying very hard not to laugh, because he suspected she would not forgive him for it. "Ask me again after your Kindling," he said, "if you still want to. You'll be busy with your own things then."

"I'll still want to," said Oona, with absolute certainty, and wrote something on her slate, and underlined it twice, and went off to the lecture wing.

Brom had news at supper that evening, and he delivered it with the solemn pride of a man reporting a difficult summit reached.

"I asked him."

Lira put her spoon down. "Asked who what?"

"The broad one. My partner. Wray's furniture." Brom set his bowl down and folded his hands on the table. "After the fetch drill this afternoon. I'd decided it was time. I said I'd been hit by him more than by anyone in my life and I'd like to know what to call him."

"And?"

"He looked at me for a long time." Brom paused, honouring the memory. "Then he said, 'Hobb.'"

"That's it?"

"Then there was another long time. Then he said, 'Brom,' as if he were giving mine back to me so we'd be even." Brom picked up his spoon. "Then he pointed at my side and said, 'Mended?' And I said, 'Mostly,' and he nodded, and that was the end of it."

Lira stared at him. "That's four words."

"Four words," said Brom, with deep contentment. "Best conversation I've had at this school."

Cael laughed so hard he had to put his own spoon down, and Brom looked at him with the mild, injured dignity of a man whose triumph has been misunderstood, and then laughed too, carefully, holding his side, which was very nearly mended now and no longer purple but a fading yellow at the edges.

Cael thought of the name on the drill board and the thanks he still owed. Brom had paid his own debt first, as he had said he would, in his own way and from Hobb's own mouth. Cael's was still waiting for the right moment. He found he did not mind waiting for it, so long as he did not let the waiting become a habit.

Later, alone in the narrow room with the lamp turned low, he did something he had not done since the road. He opened the binder at the front, where the four sections began, and read only their first lines.

Wind-adjacent. Lira.

Pressure-adjacent. Feryn.

Iron-adjacent. Brom.

Compression-adjacent. Reydan.

Four fragments, four notices copied out word for word as they had arrived, and beside each one a name. He had written the names on the day each notice came, because it had seemed only honest to record where a thing had been when it arrived, as a man records the field where he found a stone.

He had never thought of the names as owners. He had thought of the fragments like weather, which came without asking and without explaining and belonged to nobody, and which a sensible person learned the shape of afterward.

Does it feel borrowed.

He tried it, sitting there. He thought about the framework, and how it moved him half a pace into the space beside a strike, and tried to feel whether the moving was his or Lira's. It was his. It was also, unmistakably, the way Lira moved; he had watched her do it across a hundred rings, and his own feet did it now with her timing in them. Both of those were true, and neither of them was an answer.

He thought about the read, the pressure along the skin that told him where a body's weight was going, and it had Brom's patience in it, the long listening wait before anything happened. He thought about the Compression, the hot bar across his collarbones, and it had nothing of anyone in it at all that he could find, only the cost.

He did not know. He had told her so, and it had been the truth, and it was still the truth now, with the binder open on his knees and the four names looking back at him.

What he found, sitting there, was that he did not mind not knowing as much as he would have a month ago.

A month ago, I don't know had been a hole in the wall that anybody could look through. Tonight it was an answer he had given out loud to someone who wrote down only what she could see, and who had not written down a theory after it. It was a question left open on purpose, waiting for evidence. That was a different thing entirely. He was not sure yet whether it was a better thing. He suspected it might be.

He wrote one more line before the lamps went out.

First session. One question asked, one question unanswered. Brom learned Hobb's name from Hobb. Oona wants to study me and promises only to be rude by accident. Lira says the framework doesn't look borrowed. I believe her about the looking.

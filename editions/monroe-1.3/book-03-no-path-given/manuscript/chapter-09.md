# Chapter 9 — Two Notebooks

The rumor arrived at breakfast on the first morning of the third week, carried in by Lira along with half a loaf she had certainly not paid for.

"There's a transfer."

Brom did not look up from his porridge. "There are always transfers."

"Not like this one." Lira sat, tore the loaf in three and pushed the pieces across the table like a dealer laying out cards. "Ternhall. She came in at the gate yesterday afternoon with a trunk and a letter with the Ternhall seal on it, and the clerk at the standard desk read the letter twice and then looked at her rank for so long that the girl behind her in line started to worry the clerk had died."

"What rank?" Cael asked.

"Iron Rank Three. Ember Path." Lira let that sit for as long as it deserved. "Sixteen years old. She walked out of a first-rank academy nine days before its autumn term began, and came here, and nobody can work out why. I've heard three reasons already and I'm not happy with any of them."

"Let's have them," said Brom, who pretended not to enjoy Lira's market and had never once asked her to stop.

She counted on her fingers. "One, she struck an instructor and Ternhall let her leave quietly instead of throwing her out. Two, she lost one bout at Ternhall, just one, and couldn't bear to stay. Three, her family lost their money and Greyvane is cheaper." She frowned at the third finger. "That one's mine. I made it up on the stairs to see if it would spread, and it already has, so now I don't trust any of them."

Brom considered the matter seriously while he ate. "Ternhall doesn't lose people. People leave other schools to go to Ternhall."

"Exactly," said Lira, pleased. "So it's a mystery. I like a mystery before the first bell."

Cael saw her for himself at the fourth bell, when he crossed the formal yard on the way to the stable and found someone standing in front of the standings board with a notebook.

She was dark-haired and neat in the way of a person who had decided long ago what she would wear and had not given it a thought since, and she was copying the board. That alone would not have made him stop, because new students read the board all the time; he had done it himself on his second day.

What made him stop was the order she did it in. She had started at the bottom. The last names on the board, the Copper first-years with two recorded bouts and a loss beside each, were already copied out in a small, upright hand. She was working steadily upward, one row at a time, without once glancing at the top, where Edran's name had sat for longer than anyone could remember.

He stood at the yard's edge for perhaps half a minute, watching her pen. She did not look round. If she knew she was being watched, she gave no sign of minding, and she did not hurry or slow.

That evening, in the binder, under the page for the day, he wrote one line.

*Ternhall transfer. Ember, Iron 3. Copies the standings from the bottom up.*

He looked at it, decided it was the kind of fact that meant nothing yet, and let it be.

The rest of the third week belonged, more than he liked, to Edran.

Since the afternoon at the north gate the question had not come up again, not in words. Edran had not repeated it in the yard or the stable or the lecture wing, and he had certainly not gone to the provost about it. Brom had been right about that, as Brom was right about most things that involved people standing still.

But something had changed in the way the best fighter at Greyvane moved through the building. He had begun, quite openly, to watch back.

Cael noticed it first at the rail of the open floor. He was filling a page on the Storm fourth-year who left the yard smelling of rain. When he looked up between bouts, Edran was at the far rail, not watching the Storm fighter at all, but watching Cael watch. He did not look away when he was seen. He simply held the look for a breath, without any expression, and then turned back to the floor as if he had been looking at the floor all along.

It happened again two days later in the stable, at supper, and again at the formal yard during a Copper standings bout. There Edran stood at the end of the benches with his arms folded and his attention not on the ring but on the binder open on Cael's knee.

By the end of the week Cael had begun to feel it before he saw it. It was the faint pressure of being read, the way he felt a crowd turn without needing to look up.

"He's charting you," said Lira, when he mentioned it on the wall.

"I know."

"How does it feel?"

He thought about it honestly. "Fair," he said at last. "I've been charting him for a fortnight. I've got three pages on him. He's allowed one."

"Have you got a line for him? The bottom line. What his Path believes."

"Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose."

Lira considered that. "And what does he believe?"

Cael did not have an answer. He wrote the question at the bottom of the Glass page that night, under the line about breaking, and left a space beneath it for whenever he found one.

---

The rumor reached Karis Dellenmoor by way of a joke, which was how most interesting things reached Ternhall.

Master Marlowe lectured the second-years on Path history, and he liked an audience of second-years better than any other, because they still laughed in the right places. He was a dry, clever man who collected stories from the provinces as some men collect bad coins, for the pleasure of showing how bad they were. One afternoon in late winter he paused between the founding of the registry and the reform of the station fees, and told them about the south.

"Down in Ardenmere, I'm told, the circuit has a boy who beats Iron fighters with no Path at all." He let the room settle. "I expect by spring he'll be doing it with no arms. The provinces are tremendously ambitious."

The hall laughed. Karis, in the third row, wrote *Ardenmere. Circuit. Unranked boy. Beats Iron. No Path* before the laughter had finished. Underneath it, smaller, she added: *Marlowe heard it third-hand and improved it at least once.* He was an excellent lecturer and a very poor witness.

She wrote it in the second notebook, not the first.

She had kept two since the winter she turned eleven. The first was the one everyone expected a serious student to keep: bout logs, timing tables, declaration notes, the slow honest record of a girl becoming very good at something the world had agreed to measure. When she Kindled at fourteen and her Arbiter named her Path as Ember, it simply grew a new section and went on.

By sixteen she was Iron Rank Three, early for anyone and unheard of at Ternhall for a student two years past Kindling. Her name sat at the head of the standings in a hand the clerk had stopped bothering to rub out between terms.

The second notebook was the reason she kept the first.

It held everything the standard model got wrong. A Current Path courier who ran faster downhill than any table said a Current Path could. A Shield instructor whose shell held a third longer against cold than against heat. A long procession of small refusals, each with a date and a source and, wherever she could get one, a measurement.

Every instructor she asked put them down to careless counting. Karis had never met a careless count that explained itself so consistently. What she believed in was models nobody had finished.

The first entry she could not explain was her own off hand. It went still a quarter of a breath before every ignition she laid, as reliably as a clock, though nothing in Ember doctrine said it should. She had charted it for two years and still did not know what it meant. She had stopped minding that, which she counted as the most important thing the notebook had taught her.

Rank, she had understood at thirteen, was not the point. Rank was access. The system paid its best students by letting them stand closer to the works, and the works were what she wanted to see, so she was excellent on purpose.

Ternhall trained its Ember students in pairs. It paired them at the first drill and kept them paired, on the theory that a fire learned against another fire learned faster, and the theory was correct; her curve proved it. So did the curve of the person across the paired floor, which had climbed beside hers week for week for three years.

Karis did not write that person's name in either notebook. She had found, quite early, that there was a small amount of data she would rather carry than file.

---

It took her three weeks to find a real thread behind Marlowe's joke and four months to stop finding contradictions along it.

She ranked her sources by their distance from the event. Anyone who had stood at a rail came first, then anyone who had talked to someone at a rail, then taverns, and lecturers last. She tagged every claim with who had made it and what they had gained by making it. A cousin of her mother's who traded wool through Ardenmere sent three paragraphs of carters' gossip, which she ranked third and kept.

Then she paid a copying house, at a price she left out of both notebooks, to transcribe the circuit's public bout ledgers. The copies came incomplete and out of order and worth more than everything else together, because a ledger did not exaggerate. It only disagreed with the world in ways someone had to explain.

The rumor was wrong about everything that did not matter. His name was spelled two ways, and his age ran anywhere from thirteen to sixteen. It was stubbornly right about the things that did: an unclassified fighter, a string of recorded wins against ranked opponents, and, as far as she could rebuild them, more than one distinct combat signature in a single body.

She made a page titled, with a restraint she considered a professional duty, *Candidate explanations*. Then she spent the summer killing the entries one at a time.

A concealed second Path in the family died first; the signatures sat too far apart for any two Paths she knew, at least three families and possibly four. Fraud died next, because too many strangers agreed on too many small details, and because the ledger keeper's ink changed colour twice at exactly the right entries.

Exceptional range within one Path died hardest. She pieced together a bout he had lost, to a Bronze-tier Pressure fighter, out of four incomplete accounts. The timing chart lay on her desk for a week and seemed to sneer at her, because nothing in any single Path's grammar did what it said he had done in the last three minutes.

Nothing survived. That was the finding.

The last ledger copy came in the first days of autumn, sent on at her standing order, and its newest entry was barely a week old. An Iron Rank Eight, a fighter of real standing, had been beaten in front of something like six hundred people by a boy with no rank on the board at all.

Karis set the entry beside the others and went for a long walk around the Ternhall reservoir. When she came back she wrote the sentence she had been trained for five years never to write.

*The model is missing a category.*

She did not underline it. She considered underlining a form of shouting, and she did not shout in either notebook. She only sat and looked at it, as a person looks at a door that has been painted over for so long that everyone has forgotten it opens.

---

Ternhall's registrar received a weekly digest of new enrollments from every chartered academy in the region. It was meant for recruiting, and almost nobody read it. In the spring Karis had asked the junior clerk to set aside any digest that carried a certain name, and had not explained why. The clerk was used to that.

The digest came on a grey morning nine days before Ternhall's autumn term, not a week after the reservoir. The name was on the third page, under a minor-tier academy she had to find on a map. Beside it, in the column for category, someone had written *observer*.

She laughed aloud in the library, once, and a second-year at the next table jumped.

It was the word that did it. A boy who had beaten an Iron Rank Eight in front of six hundred people had been enrolled, in ink, under a category built for children who had not yet Kindled. Either a clerk had made the worst mistake she had ever seen in a digest, or someone at that small academy had looked at the same gap she had been staring at since the winter and decided to use it.

She wanted, more than she had wanted anything since her Arbiter first named her Path, to know which.

She filed the transfer request before the midday bell.

Her advisor took a full hour to talk her out of it, with the door shut and the weary patience of a man who had talked many promising students out of many things. He set out the sums, as though sums were what stood in her way: where Ternhall's graduates went and where a minor academy's went, and which guilds would read which name on a letter. At the end of the hour he folded his hands.

"A first-rank student at a minor academy," he said, "is a good lamp lit in a cellar."

Karis admired the sentence. It was compact, and quotable, and certain to be repeated to the next student in that chair. It was also wrong in a direction he had no way of seeing, because a cellar was exactly where a person carried a lamp when she meant to find something. She thanked him for his time and did not change the request.

Her parents were harder, because their worry was about her and not her prospects, and she had no arithmetic that answered it. Her mother sat with her at the kitchen table on the last evening and asked one question.

"And if he turns out to be only a boy, and the whole mystery is in the paperwork?"

Karis gave the honest answer, because her mother deserved nothing less. "Then I'll have spent a year proving it properly, with everything documented. It would be the best negative result I ever produced, and I'd come home and be proud of it."

Her mother looked at her steadily. "You've already decided."

That was true. If Karis was strict with herself, it had been true since the walk around the reservoir. Her father said nothing at all that evening, and in the morning he packed her trunk with great care, folding each thing as a man folds what he expects to see again.

There was one person at Ternhall she told after the papers were filed rather than before. She did not record, in either notebook, why she had chosen that order.

The coach south took four days. She spent most of them doing something she had never done for any subject in either notebook, which was writing a list of the questions she would not ask.

She began it as an exercise and found by the second morning that it was the most important page she had written all year. Every subject in the second notebook had been a thing, a timing or a curve or a shell that held too long against cold, and none of them had been able to object to being written down. This one could. If he was what the ledgers said, he had spent his whole life being written down by people who never asked his leave, and she had read enough of those people's work, at second and third hand, to know exactly what it looked like from outside. She did not intend to add a page to it.

So she wrote, in a column, the questions she would not ask him unless he offered first. Where it came from. How it worked. Whether it hurt. What the registry had called him, if it had called him anything. What he could do that he had not shown on the circuit. She wrote eleven of them, and then a twelfth, which was simply *anything he hasn't chosen to show*, and she drew a line under the column and wrote beneath it the only question she would ask.

*Whether I may ask.*

Then, because she was honest with herself in both notebooks, she wrote one more line under that.

*If he says no, the finding is no. Record it and go home.*

---

Greyvane, when she came over the brow of its hill on the last afternoon of the second week of its term, was smaller than its letters and better kept than its reputation. The gate clerk had a list, and Karis's name was on it, entered in the same plain hand as every other name, and she found she liked the academy a little for that before she was through the gate.

She went to the standard desk, because transfers went to the standard desk. The brisk young clerk there read the Ternhall letter twice, then looked at Karis's rank in the margin for long enough that the girl behind her shifted her feet. Karis waited. She had been looked at in exactly that way at every desk since she was fourteen, and she had learned that the look passed sooner if one gave it nothing to push against.

She was given a room in the residence wing, a schedule, and a block on the training floor at the fifth bell in section two. She unpacked that night. She registered for lectures the next morning, including the declaration theory block, which she did not need and attended anyway, because the way a school taught its basics told her more than its prospectus ever had.

On the second afternoon she demonstrated for the floor instructor who took the heat Paths, Ember and Ash together, a lean old man with scorched cuffs. He watched her lay six points on the boards and let them die on his count, and said only, "Ternhall," in the tone of a man identifying a bird by its song.

She showed him the standard set at the standard size and nothing more. A transfer demonstrated to be placed, not to be admired.

It was harder than she had expected, and not for the reason she would have guessed. The points themselves were easy; she had laid ten thousand of them. What was hard was the space across the section from her. For three years another set of points had always answered hers there, a breath behind or a breath ahead, so that every ignition she made had been half of a conversation.

Here there was only chalk and a lean old man with a slate. She laid her six and let them die on his count. She noticed, because she noticed everything about herself, that she was laying them a fraction early, the way a person speaks a fraction early into a silence where someone has always replied.

She corrected it on the fourth point. She did not write the correction down in either notebook. She only stood a moment afterward with her off hand very still at her side, and then went to sign her slip.

The next name on the instructor's list was a tall, narrow woman of perhaps twenty with ash-pale hair and a training coat patched at both elbows, who had been waiting by the wall throughout without watching. Her tag carried the academy's crest and, beneath it, a small raised dot. Karis had read the floor roster by then and knew what the dot meant.

She watched the woman lay a low grey haze across the boards that slowed everything passing through it, as an Ash Path should. Then she watched her sign her own slip at the desk afterward with great care, checking the hour against the clock above the door before she wrote it.

Karis filed her under *re-certification track; handles procedure as if it might bite.* It was a thing she recognized. She had not yet decided whether it was a thing she liked.

She catalogued the grounds in a single afternoon. She listened to the rumor market for three days.

There were four theories about the observer and, by the second evening, three about herself, and she ranked them all as evidence and found that every one was worthless about its subject and very useful about the academy.

Nobody's theory about the observer was frightened. They were curious and silly and competitive, a third-year insisting on a northern scandal and a first-year insisting on poetry, but not one of them was afraid of him. She wrote that down. It told her how this place held him, and it was more than any interview would have told her.

She saw him on the second day and twice more after that.

The first time he was at the rail of the open floor during the fifth bell, with a binder open on his knee, writing while a Blade pair drilled in front of him in absolute silence.

He did not look up when people looked at him, and people looked at him a great deal. The tag on his coat was not a crest but a hollow circle. She noticed, because she could not help noticing, that his pen moved in long passages while the pair worked and stopped when they stopped, and she recognized the habit with a jolt, because it was her own.

The second time he was at the edge of the formal yard during a standings bout, watching a third-year Force Path lose. The third time he crossed the yard with a stocky boy the roster listed as Iron Skin and a girl with a dot on her tag, the three of them walking in step without seeming to arrange it. The girl said something that made the stocky boy put his hand flat over his own face.

Each time, Karis wanted very much to cross the floor and introduce herself. Each time, she did not.

She had decided on the coach that she would spend one full week at Greyvane as an ordinary transfer before she spoke to him. She would unpack and register, demonstrate for her instructors and fight her first scheduled standings bout, and do all of it with her own name, as a student among students.

Later, when anyone asked, and she was certain someone eventually would, she wanted the record to show that Karis Dellenmoor had come to Greyvane as a student and not as a hunter with a question. It would matter which. In her experience a difference kept carefully enough, for long enough, always came to matter in the end.

Her first standings bout fell on the fifth day, against a Copper Rank Nine who had heard the rumors and came at her far too hard. She finished it in two exchanges and did not look at the rail to see who had been watching.

On the last afternoon of the week she went to the archive, because she had a rule about reading an instrument before she studied what it held. The observer was enrolled under a demonstration provision, and she had not yet read it.

The archivist was a small, straight old woman with spectacles on a cord, and she held the door open at an angle so exact that Karis noticed it and filed it at once.

"I'd like to read the demonstration provision," Karis said. "The academy's copy."

The archivist's eyes went past her shoulder, into the room, and then came back.

"It's out," she said. "Somebody's reading it."

---

He had read the provision three times that week already, and he was reading it a fourth when the door opened. The second sitting was coming, and Prynn had left the statutes out on the long table for him and it seemed ungrateful not to use them.

He heard the archivist's voice at the door, low and dry, and another voice answer it, and he did not look up at once. Then he felt someone cross the room. Students usually came into the archive drifting and uncertain, peering down aisles they did not mean to enter. Whoever this was walked as if she had already measured the floor and counted the steps. He looked up.

It was the girl from the standings board. She stopped at the end of the long table, not at his elbow and not across the room, but at the one distance that was neither crowding nor caution, and stood there with her hands at her sides and nothing in them.

Behind her, at the high desk by the door, Prynn had put her spectacles on. She was watching the newcomer like an official who has just seen someone walk straight past the checkpoint and is deciding whether to mind.

"You're Cael," the girl said.

It was not a question, and he noticed that before anything else.

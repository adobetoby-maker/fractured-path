# Chapter 34 — Pick Faster

The sack turned out to be Brom's.

Lira had gone to the kitchen and come back with a flour sack and a bag of dried peas the cook had given her for reasons nobody ever learned. Brom took one look at them on the stable table, sighed, and went to his room for his leather needle and the waxed thread he kept for boots. By the sixth bell on the first night there were a dozen small heavy bags on the table, each the size of a fist, each sewn shut along two sides with stitches so small and even that Lira picked one up and turned it over and looked at Brom with real suspicion.

"Where did you learn that?"

"Velmere," said Brom. "Boots are expensive. Feet aren't."

They took them out at dusk to the training yard, the open square of packed earth below the wall where nobody went after the sixth bell in winter because there was nothing out there but frost and a pump. Lira drew a ring on the earth with a stick of chalk the size of her thumb, eight paces across, the size of a formal ring. Then she stood in the middle of it with the chalk in one hand and a bag in the other and explained the rules as if she were reading them off a board.

"I'm her," she said. "I'm also her floor. When I say *point*, I draw a circle." She drew one, a hand's width across, by her foot. "That's a point. It's lit. You don't stand in it. Every time I say *point*, there's one more, and the ring gets smaller."

"And the bags?"

"The bags are channels." She tossed one to Brom, who caught it without looking. "Brom throws them. Flat, along the ground, from one circle toward another, whenever he likes and without telling anybody. If a bag touches you, you've crossed a channel, and I count it out loud, and I don't feel sorry." She looked at him. "And I'm also me. I'll be pressing you the whole time. Making you turn. Because she won't just stand there laying coins and waiting for you to trip. She'll be making you choose."

"She never touched Hobb."

"She didn't need to touch Hobb. Hobb was walking forward. You won't be." Lira set her feet. "Framework. Panel speed is for panels. Go."

It was the worst hour of practice he had ever had, and it was not close.

She came at him from the first breath. She did not strike, not really, but she moved as if she would, short sharp feints off the front foot that made the framework wake and read and want to slip. Every time it slipped him she said *point* and bent and drew a circle on the earth where he had been about to go. Within a few minutes there were six, and the ring that had been eight paces across was full of small chalk mouths. Every step he took had to find the space between them, and every slip the framework offered him had to be checked against the floor before he could take it.

Then the first bag came, low and flat along the frozen earth from Brom's hand at the edge of the ring, skidding from one circle toward another, and Cael never saw it because he was looking at Lira. It caught him on the ankle with a soft heavy thump and fell over.

"One," said Lira.

The second caught him on the shin while he was choosing between two gaps. The third he saw, and jumped, and landed in a circle.

"Three. And that was a point, so four. You stood in her fire."

He did not argue; he set his feet again.

By the end of the hour she had drawn sixteen circles and Brom had thrown more bags than Cael could count, and eleven of them had touched him. He stood in the middle of a ring that had become a forest of chalk, breathing in hard white clouds, his ankles sore and his legs gone heavy in a way the framework never made them, and understood exactly what Lira had meant.

"Eleven," said Lira, who was breathing hard too, and bent with her hands on her knees. "That's your number. Tomorrow we beat it."

"Was that what it'll be like?"

"No," said Lira, straightening. "She's better than me at this, and she won't be throwing peas."

---

Karis timed him on the Thursday in section four, as she had timed him since the first session, and when she read back his recovery between passes she frowned at her own page.

"Five counts," she said. "It was four on Tuesday. Before that, three." She looked up at him. "You haven't been doing whatever it was in the eleventh week. Quenna would have said."

"No."

"Then something else is making you tired." She held her pen above the page, the sixth clause sitting between them in the cold air of the section: he read everything she wrote about him before it existed anywhere else. "Cause?"

He looked at Quenna, who stood at the chalk with her slate and her face doing nothing at all, and then back at Karis.

"Lira," he said.

Karis's pen did not move for a moment. Then it did, and she turned the notebook round so that he could read what she had written, as the clause said she must.

*Recovery slower by one count. Cause stated by subject: Lira.*

"That's all it'll say," said Karis. "I'm not allowed to write what she's doing, and I don't want to know." She closed the notebook. "I'd guess, but I'd be guessing about my own match, and that would be in poor taste."

At the chalk, Quenna made a very small sound. It might have been a cough.

They did the post work after that, with the felt on and Brom leaning on the far side, and the read gave him four in twenty, his best yet, which Karis wrote down and read back in his own words. When the hour was done she folded her stool and stood with it under her arm, and did a thing she had not done since the terms: she hesitated.

"It's strange," she said, "writing down how tired you are and knowing I'll be across a ring from it in a fortnight."

"You could stop. The clause doesn't make you keep coming."

"No. The clause makes you read it." She tucked the stool more firmly under her arm. "I keep coming because I said I would, and I keep writing because it's true. It would be stranger to stop." She looked at him. "And you're reading every line I write about your recovery. That's the most even thing about this whole arrangement. I know exactly how tired you are, and you know exactly what I know."

"Symmetry."

"Symmetry." She went to the door and turned in it. "Tell Lira she's very good at her job."

He told Lira at supper. Lira went pink and stole his bread and said nothing about it for the rest of the meal, which was how he knew she had been pleased.

---

On the Friday he stopped at the rail of the defensive floor on his way to the archive, because Brom was there with Wray, and Brom had said at breakfast with a careful face that Wray had a new idea.

The new idea turned out to be Hobb, and a Storm second-year Cael did not know, and Edran, one after another. Wray stood at the edge of the chalk with her slate and called them in at Brom in turn, each faster than the last, Hobb slow and heavy, the Storm boy quicker, and Edran the quickest of all with his ready pair built at his mark. Brom took every strike and gave and waited and turned. Cael watched the wait. He could see it now every time, the small held instant between the read and the turn that Wray had shown him on the seventh strike, and Edran came closest to it, and Edran never got in.

"Again," said Wray, and wrote.

When it was over Brom came to the rail, wiping chalk off his forearms, and took his number sheet out of his coat and showed it to Cael without being asked. Under the old column of fetch numbers he had begun a second one, in the same square capitals.

*WALL. SEEN EVERY TIME. NOT CLOSED. — B.*

"She says seeing it is the first half," said Brom. "She says the second half needs somebody faster than anybody here, and she's going to keep looking." He folded the sheet away. "I don't mind. It's an honest wall. I'd rather know where it is than walk into it with somebody's money on me."

Wray had come along the rail behind him. She stopped beside Cael and looked at him with her level measuring eyes, as she had looked at him across the formal yard after the exhibition.

"You've taken on the Ternhall girl," she said.

"Quenna has. I said yes."

"You said yes." Wray looked out at the section, where Edran was letting his last structure go in a pale spray. "I've a thing my Path believes, and you wrote it on my page once without ever seeing me use it. Nothing should be spent that doesn't have to be." She tucked her slate under her arm. "You'll spend a great deal at the sitting. Everybody does, against her. Spend it on purpose. Know what each thing costs before you lay it down, and lay it down anyway if it's worth it." She looked at him. "That's all. It's the only advice I give anybody, and most people don't take it."

She went back to her chalk, and Brom looked after her with something like affection.

"She likes you," he said.

"How can you tell?"

"She gave you advice," said Brom. "She's given me advice twice in three months, and I'm her student."

---

On the second night Lira added a rule.

"You're watching me," she said, before the first point. "I saw it all last night. You watch my feet and you watch my hands and you trust Brom to throw where you can see it. That's wrong." She drew the first circle. "She isn't going to be in one place. Her floor's going to be everywhere. So you're going to watch the floor, and let me be something you read out of the side of your eye."

It was much harder, for the first quarter of an hour, because the framework had always lived on bodies and the floor was not a body; it did not lean before it moved, and a chalk circle did not drop its shoulder. But Lira's hand did, when she bent to draw one, and after a while Cael found he could see the circle coming before she drew it, because he could see where she was looking when she bent.

"That's cheating," said Lira, when he stepped out of a place before she had finished drawing in it.

"She looks where she's going to lay. Her off hand goes still."

"Then you can cheat." She drew another. "Point."

Nine bags touched him on the second night.

On the third night Lira was tired. She had been on the open floor with Gerda and Hobb at the sixth bell, as she still was most evenings, and she came out to the training yard in the dark with her shoulder strapped again under her coat and her temper very short. Instead of saying so she came at him harder, and when she saw his legs begin to go in the second half of the hour she aimed at them. She pressed him toward the edges of the ring where the circles were thickest and called *point* at the exact moment he was too tired to take a slip at full width, crueler than any lattice could ever be, because a lattice could not see him getting tired and Lira could.

Brom threw from the edge of the ring without a word, flat and unhurried, without any wasted motion, every bag going exactly where he had meant it to, and twice Cael heard him say under his breath, "Sorry," as a bag hit.

"Don't say sorry," said Lira, without turning. "She won't."

"I'm not her," said Brom. "I'm a man with a sack."

Six touched him on the third night. He stood at the end of it with his hands on his knees in the middle of a ring that had nineteen circles in it, having done what he had not believed possible on legs that had stopped being his own somewhere around the fortieth minute.

"Six," said Lira. She was sitting on the frozen earth with her back against the pump, holding her shoulder. "That's half."

"That's nearly half."

"I'm not counting the one where you fell over. That was the ground's fault." She looked at him in the dark. "You're picking better."

"I'm picking. I don't know about better."

"You're picking." Lira got up, slowly, using the pump. "Last week you'd have stood there deciding until the bag made the decision for you. That's better. Better's just less standing there." She went to collect the bags. "Tomorrow we find out if it's real, or if it's only my shoulder making me slow."

He found her on the wall an hour later with the strapping off and a cold cloth from the pump held against the shoulder under her coat. He sat down beside her and held out the second cloth he had brought, because she had come out without one for her neck and he had seen her rubbing it all through the last quarter-hour. She looked at it, took it without a word, put it round the back of her neck and closed her eyes.

"You're supposed to be the one with the cloth," she said. "That's how this goes. I've a whole system."

"I know. I've read it."

"You've *lived* it." She opened one eye. "Don't make a habit of this. I don't know what I'd do with my evenings."

They sat. The yard below them was dark, the chalk ring still faintly pale in the middle of it and the pump standing up out of the frost beside it like a man waiting for somebody, and down the hill the town's lamps were going out one at a time.

"Why are you doing it?" he said.

"Doing what?"

"This. Four nights. Your shoulder. Being a lattice." He looked at the ring. "You never asked me once whether it was worth it. Whether the fight mattered more than the other thing. You just found a sack."

Lira did not answer for a long time, only moved the cloth on her neck.

"At Fenmark," she said, "I knew the right foot for the third turn, and I said so, and they wrote me down in nineteen words and put me out." She was looking at the ring. "I've spent two years knowing things about floors that nobody let me teach. I taught you, in Ardenmere, but I didn't know I was doing it. You were just a boy in a yard I was trying to keep alive." She shifted on the stone. "This is the first time I've stood on a floor and known exactly what I was doing to somebody, and why, and that it was right. Nobody asked me to. Nobody's going to sign it or write it in a ledger. There's no panel." She almost smiled. "It's the best teaching I've ever done, and it's in a frozen yard with a sack of peas, and the only people who'll ever know are you and Brom."

"And Karis. She'll know on Thursday."

"Karis will know on Thursday." Lira took the cloth off her neck and folded it in her lap. "She'll know somebody taught you to stand on a floor that isn't there. She won't know who. That's all right." She looked at him then, in the dark. "I said in the stable once that we were a family and she wanted data. I was being clever. I meant it, but I was being clever about it." She stood up and shook out her coat. "This is what the sentence costs, when it stops being a sentence. A sack and a shoulder and four nights. I'd pay it twice."

She went in. He sat on the wall a while longer with the cold coming up through the stone, and found that he had nothing at all to write down about it, because it had already been written somewhere better.

---

The next evening, at the sixth bell, before the fourth night's chalk, he went to Brom's room. He had told Lira at breakfast that he would, and she had made him promise in the eleventh week to tell her before and not after. So she was already there, sitting on Brom's bed with her back against the wall and her boots on Brom's blanket and a wet cloth from the kitchen pump folded in her lap. She looked at him as he came in as she had looked at him on the stair the morning after the fast catch.

"Tell me what it is," she said. "Before."

He knelt on the boards and set his left forearm across his chest, braced, as he did for everything in this room.

"Hobb said something," he said. "About crossing her channel. He said it shoves first. Hard, like a stove door. And then it's hot. The shove's what puts you back where you started, and the heat's what you remember."

Lira went very still on the bed.

"I've never been able to do anything with a strike except catch it and send it home," Cael went on. "And sending it home costs. You saw what a quarter fast cost." He did not look at her. "But there's the drop. I gather it and let it go and give it no road. I've done it four times, slowly, on a push. It never cost anything I could find. I've never tried it fast."

"You want to know if you can drop a strike," said Brom slowly.

"I want to know if I can drop a shove." Cael made himself say the rest. "If I had to go through something that pushed. So that it didn't put me back where I started."

Nobody said anything for a while. A cart went along the street worth walking far below the window.

"It won't take the heat," said Lira. Her voice was very level. "Will it? Nothing you have takes heat."

"No. Nothing I have takes heat."

"So if you ever did it, in a ring, you'd be choosing a burn."

"If I ever did it. Yes." He looked up at her then. "That's why I'm telling you before. I'm choosing the size first. A quarter. Fast. Woken before Brom moves, the way the catch worked, and then let go instead of sent. If it costs what the catch cost, I stop, and I never think about it again."

Over the wet cloth Lira studied him a long moment.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll know I could. That's all. Knowing isn't doing."

"No," said Lira. "It isn't." She held up the cloth. "Go on, then. I'm here."

Brom brought up the Iron Skin along his right palm, the faint dull sheen coming over the skin, and drew his hand back and looked at Cael, and Cael woke the fragment.

He did it as he had learned to on the night of the fast catch, before anything moved: the gathering begun low in his chest and held there half-ready, like a breath drawn and not let out, with the road laid open in front of it. He held it, and held it, and Brom struck.

The strike came into the bone and the fragment closed on it in the same instant, a quarter of it, and there was the road, wide open, back along the line it had come in. Every part of him wanted to send it home. He did not. He opened his hands on it, inside, as you open your fingers on something hot, and the gathered quarter spilled out of him and went nowhere at all.

He waited for the bill.

It came, but it was small: a tightness across the breastbone, like a strap pulled one notch, for the length of one breath, and then it eased. His knees were under him, his hand was his own, and he could feel the floor.

"Well?" said Lira, with the cloth half-raised.

"One breath," said Cael. "And a strap. That's all."

Brom looked at his own palm. "I didn't feel anything come back."

"Nothing did. I dropped it." Cael looked at his forearm, where the strike had landed; it would be a bruise and nothing else. "It's the sending. The catch doesn't cost much. Sending it home costs. Letting it go almost doesn't."

He did it twice more, because one was luck and two was the floor, as Wray had told Brom. The second was the same, one breath and the strap. The third he woke too late, so that the strike landed before he had the road open and the fragment found nothing to close on, and he took it the ordinary way, and nothing went into a joint.

"Two of three," said Brom.

Lira put the cloth down on the blanket unused.

"Write it," she said. "All of it. The part about choosing a burn as well."

He wrote it kneeling on Brom's floor, with the binder on the boards.

*Compression-adjacent. Brom's room, L. present, told before. The drop, fast: a quarter of a quick palm strike caught and let go, unsent, with the fragment woken before the strike. Two of three. Cost: one breath, a tightness across the breastbone. The cost lives in the sending, not the catching. It takes the shove. It does not take heat. Nothing I have takes heat. If I ever used it to cross something hot, the burn would be a thing I chose.*

He read it back, and Lira read it upside down from the bed, and neither of them said anything more about it that night.

---

On the fourth night the number stopped.

He had thought it would go on falling. Eleven, nine, six: the arithmetic seemed to promise a night when no bag touched him at all. Lira's shoulder was rested and she was fast again, and Brom threw as he always threw, and Cael watched the floor and read Lira out of the side of his eye and picked and picked and picked, faster than the night before, and four bags touched him. The next session, after a rest, it was four again.

He knew why before Lira said it, because he could feel it in his own legs in the instant before each slip. The framework read the threat and offered him a way out of it, as it always had. Then, in the half-beat while he was still taking it, something in him checked the floor and the floor said *not that way*, and he stopped and began a second way, and the second way was always a hair too late, and a bag that would have missed him found him.

They sat down together in the dirt of the training yard at the end of it, because neither of them had the legs left to stand. The last light had gone out of the sky, the pump dripped, and Brom was walking slowly round the ring picking up bags and putting them back in the flour sack.

"You're still deciding," said Lira, "whether to trust the slip or throw it away. And you're doing it in the same half-beat you're meant to be doing one or the other. You can't do both in there. There isn't room."

"I know."

"So pick faster." She said it flatly, to the frozen earth between her boots. "It doesn't matter which, at first. Pick the wrong one fast and you'll get further than the right one slow. Speed the decision. The correctness comes later, once your feet have learned the floor."

"That isn't how Vell taught it."

"With Vell it was always one opponent. One rhythm, one body, one strike to read. You got very good at it." Lira leaned back on her hands. "This isn't one rhythm. It's twenty, all coming due at once, and she's the only one who knows the order. You can't read twenty rhythms the way you read one. You've got to stop asking whether it's right and just go, and trust the floor to have been read already."

He thought about it, sitting in the dirt.

"Watch the quiet part," he said. "Brom said. Everything she does early is where the fight is."

"Brom's right. Brom's always right about the floor." Lira got up, slowly, and held out a hand, which he did not need and took all the same. "You'll get there. In the end you always do. Only I've never watched you do it against anything with so many moving parts, not before you'd even—"

She stopped.

She stood with his hand still in hers, in the dark, her mouth a little open on the next word, and did not say it. At the far side of the ring Brom had stopped with a bag in his hand. Nobody moved for a moment.

"Before you've even had a chance to learn it properly," said Lira, and let go of his hand, and went to help Brom with the sack.

Nobody had said the word all week, not on the wall or at the scarred table or in Brom's room with the wet cloth folded on the blanket, not once in four nights of chalk and peas and Lira's voice saying *point* in the dark. Still it came to every meal, a fifth chair drawn up to the table that none of them would look at. Lira had nearly sat in it just now and caught herself, and the three of them walked back across the frozen yard to the residence wing in a silence that had the word in the very middle of it.

He wrote that night at the desk by the window, with his legs aching so badly he could feel his pulse in his calves.

*Lira's lattice. Eleven, nine, six, four, four. Stopped at four. I'm still deciding in the half-beat where I should be moving. Lira: pick faster. Speed the decision. The correctness comes later.*

He looked at that for a long time, and then wrote one more line, small, at the bottom.

*Nobody has said it all week. I haven't either. I don't think I've let myself think it.*

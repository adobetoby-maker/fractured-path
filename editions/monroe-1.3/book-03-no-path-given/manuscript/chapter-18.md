# Chapter 18 — Odds

By the next evening the whole academy had decided the exhibition was the best thing that had happened to it all term.

Cael had braced himself for something else. He had spent the night after the posting lying awake in the narrow room, building the week ahead the way he had once built the reader under the lamp, carefully and out of every unkind thing he remembered. He had expected the low, sidelong noise of the first week, the conversations that stopped on the stair as he passed, the people who looked at the hollow circle on his tag and then very carefully did not look at his face. None of it came. What came instead took him most of three days to recognize, because he had not been on the receiving end of it since Ardenmere.

People asked him things, plainly, to his face, in corridors and at the meal hatch.

A Storm Path fourth-year he had never once spoken to stopped him on the stair outside the lecture wing and asked whether he meant to fight Edran close or at range. A pair of Copper first-years from the movement floor asked, very politely, whether observers were allowed to wear gloves. A girl from the hardening family asked if it was true that he had fought on the Ardenmere circuit, and when he said it was, she said, "Then you've done this before, with people watching," and went away looking reassured on his behalf.

On the second day, in the line for barley at the seventh bell, a second-year from the movement floor fell in beside him and began, without any introduction at all, to talk odds.

He was a thin, quick boy with ink on his thumb, and he talked the way circuit touts had talked on the stairs of the Ironyard, fast and pleasant and entirely without shame. He had grown up, he said, in a river town with a fighting pit behind the tannery, and he had been taking wagers on things since he could count. Not money, he added quickly, because Greyvane didn't hold with money, but crusts and kitchen duty and the good seat by the stable fire.

"Edran's the favourite," he said, as if this were news. "Three to one, on the stairs. Five to two in the hardening hall, because they like an underdog in there. The Edge floor won't take a wager against him at all." He lowered his voice. "I'd be very grateful for anything you could tell me. Not much. A word."

"What sort of word?"

"Whether you've got something you haven't shown."

Cael looked at him, and the boy looked back, bright and hopeful, with his bowl held out for barley he had forgotten about.

"No."

"No, you haven't?"

"No, I won't tell you."

The boy considered this, and then, to Cael's surprise, his whole face split into a delighted grin.

"That's the right answer. That's exactly what a real fighter says. I'll put a crust on you myself." He took his barley and went off down the long room toward the fire. A moment later Cael heard him announcing to a table of second-years that the observer had refused to give inside information, and that this was the best sign yet.

Lira, at the end of the bench, had watched the whole thing with her chin on her fist.

"You've made his week."

"He wanted me to tell him what I can do."

"He wanted you to refuse. Touts never want the answer." She pushed half her bread across the table to him. "Three to one on the stairs. I've been asking. It was four to one this morning, and then somebody found out you fought on the Ardenmere circuit and it came in."

"You've been asking?"

"I've been listening. That's different." She looked down the long room at the fire, where the second-year was holding up fingers. "It's nice, isn't it."

"What is?"

"This." She waved her bread at the room. "Nobody's whispering. They're betting crusts. A week ago they were making up stories about rivers and sealed courts." She ate. "It's a fight now. Everybody knows how to talk about a fight."

He wrote it down that night, because it had taken him three days to find the shape and he did not want to lose it.

*Sport. That's how they're taking it, not as a verdict. I keep bracing for the dread, and every time it's a boy wanting to know whether I'll fight close or at range instead. I think this is what a question looks like when a school decides to ask it out loud, instead of letting it go round behind my back.*

He read it over and added one more line.

*Three to one. Lira says that's fair.*

---

He spent the six days on Edran.

He had done this before, many times, in a city where the cost of getting it wrong had been a split lip and a long walk home. He had spent six days on Feryn once and lost to him anyway, and the losing had taught him more than the six days had; he had spent longer than six days on Reydan. He knew how to do it. The only new thing was the size of the price, and he wrote that down on the first night so that he would not pretend otherwise.

*Ardenmere, losing cost me a bruise and some pride. This costs the provision. Study accordingly.*

Edran trained on the open floor every morning before the first bell, in the far corner by the high windows, exactly as he had said he would, and he did not change anything for the rail. He nodded to Cael on the first morning when Cael came in, and after that he never looked at the rail at all, because he did not need to: Cael was always there.

The first three days taught him the Path before they taught him anything about the man.

Glass ran on discrete commitments. Cael had known that in outline since his second week, when he crossed out a page of notes about brittleness and started again, but now he watched it close enough to see the grain. A Glass declaration did not grow or bend. When Edran called a shell along his forearm it came up all at once, bright and hard and finished, like ice that had decided to be ice everywhere in the same instant. Once it was there, it could not be changed. He could not thicken it on one side or thin it on another, or turn its edge a little further round as a strike came in. If he wanted a different shape, he had to let the first one go and call a new one, and letting go meant bursting: the shell broke outward in that glittering spray that carried force away with it, and then it was gone.

It was the exact opposite of Lira's Path. Wind could be feathered; Lira could start moving one way and change her mind halfway through the motion, and the wind in her went with her, adjusting as she went. It was the opposite of Brom's, too, because Iron Skin could be laid on late and pressed harder as the blow arrived. Glass did neither. A Glass fighter chose before he moved, committed everything to the choice, and lived with it until it broke.

*Choose first,* Cael wrote in the margin, and then stopped and looked at the words, because they were his own rule, the one the Compression fragment had beaten into him on the road and Lira had beaten into him again before the first sitting. Glass was a whole Path built on the lesson he was still learning.

There was a second rule underneath the first, and it took him the whole of the second morning to see it, because it was a rule about the inside of a person and he could only watch the outside.

Edran could hold two structures at once, one on each forearm, and no more. He always went into a sequence with both already up: a shell on his left forearm for taking a blow, and on his right an edge, a thin hard ridge along the outside of the bone, so that a strike with that arm was also a cut. Glass sat in the Edge family with Blade, and now Cael saw why. The taxonomy instructor had refused to finish his story about a broken bottle, but Cael thought he understood it now. A thing that broke well still cut.

What he could not do was build a new one quickly while another still stood. Each structure took the whole of his attention to make, and Cael watched him make the opening pair every morning, slowly, one and then the other, before he moved at all, with his eyes half shut, like a man threading a needle. A Glass fighter could not give his whole mind to a new structure while some of it was still holding up an old one. So the pair he started with was ready and waiting, built at leisure, and everything after them had to be made fresh in the middle of the fight, from nothing, after the last one had gone.

Edran ran his sequences in sets of two and three and four, against the empty air, without a partner, and every one of them was clean. Cael began to understand the shape of the training, and then the shape of the person who had chosen it. A Glass fighter could not waste a motion, because every motion was a commitment he could not take back, and a Glass fighter who trained sloppily would fight sloppily, with no feathering to save him. So Edran did not train sloppily, ever. He had come to Greyvane at fifteen and drilled this way, Cael was willing to bet, every single morning for three years, until there was nothing left in him that had not been chosen.

*Glass doesn't forgive,* he wrote on the second night. *So neither does he. Not himself, anyway.*

On the third day, in the afternoon, he went to the archive and asked Prynn for the standings record.

"Which year?"

"All of his."

Prynn looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment, then climbed down off her stool without a word and walked him to the end of the nearest aisle, where the academy's own records were kept. She took down a tall narrow book with a cloth spine and laid it on the long table.

"Standings bouts. Every one, every term, as the assessor entered them. Public record. Anyone may read it."

"Does anyone?"

"Almost nobody." She set a weight beside it. "Tea?"

"Please."

She went away between the shelves, and Cael opened the book.

It was a plain record, kept the way Vell had kept her ledger, in columns: the date, the names, the assessor, the number of exchanges, the result, and a short line in the assessor's hand. He found Edran's first entry near the front of the section three years back, a Copper first-year of fifteen beating another Copper first-year in five exchanges, and beside it the assessor had written *Glass. Over-commits. Will learn.* The next entries said the same thing in shorter words and then stopped saying it, and by the end of the first year the line beside Edran's name said only *Clean*.

He followed the name forward through three years. It climbed the columns as it had climbed the board, steadily and without any great leaps, through the Copper ranks and over the threshold into Iron, and it lost only four times in the whole record. Every one of the four losses was in a bout that went past the third exchange.

Cael sat up.

He read the four entries again, and then went back and read every bout Edran had won in more than three exchanges, and there were only eleven of those. Edran won early, in two exchanges or three; he pressed from the first count and finished things while they were still going his way. When a bout ran long, the record began to say other things in the assessors' small hands. *Late in the fourth.* *Lost his line after the third.* And once, in Wray's square hand, *Tires in the sequence, not the body.*

Prynn came back with the cup and saucer and set it at his elbow, well away from the book.

"You've found something." It was not a question.

"I don't know yet."

"Then you've found something worth not knowing about properly." She went back to her stool. "Don't spill it on the standings."

He drank the tea, which was as bad as ever, and copied Wray's line into the binder exactly. *Tires in the sequence, not the body.*

---

Karis found him that evening on the wall outside the residence wing, where he had gone to sit with the binder and the cold.

She did not sit down. She stood at the end of the wall with her notebook shut under her arm and her other hand in her coat pocket, at the distance she always chose, neither near nor far.

"May I ask you something that isn't covered?"

"Go on."

"The exhibition is public. Anyone may watch, and anyone may write down what they see." She paused. "I'd like to chart it. The way I charted your sessions. I don't need your permission, because it isn't a session and the clauses don't reach it. I'm asking anyway."

In the half-light the yard had gone grey behind her, and the hill town's first lamps had come on below.

"Why ask, if you don't need to?"

"Because you'd know I was doing it, and you'd wonder whether you'd agreed to it." She said it simply. "I'd rather you didn't spend any of the eighth morning wondering about me."

"Chart it. Clause six still applies. I read it first."

"Of course." She nodded once, and he thought that was the end of it. Then she did not go.

"There's something else. I'm going to tell you that I won't tell you something. That's an odd thing to say, so I'll be exact about it." She looked out at the yard. "I fought him, not a week ago. I've charted him, and I have pages on him you've never seen. You haven't asked me what's on them."

"No."

"I'm glad." She seemed to mean it. "I've been waiting all week to find out whether you would. If you'd asked, I'd have had to decide whether answering was fair to him. He doesn't know what's in my notebook. He'd never agree to me handing it across a table to the person he's fighting." She turned the notebook over under her arm. "It would be a little like reading his declarations to you."

"That's why I didn't ask."

She looked at him properly then, and he saw her take that and set it down somewhere careful, the way she set down a timing that agreed with a timing she had written before.

"Noted."

She went in. He sat on the wall and watched the lamps come on down the hill, and wrote one line before he lost the light.

*K. has pages on him. Didn't ask. She was glad I didn't. That's the second thing this week somebody has been glad I didn't do.*

Oona was at the board the next morning, in front of the answer, reading it for what he suspected was the fifth or sixth time. She had her slate under her arm and her tag on its string, and when he came up beside her she did not look round. She only said, as if they had been in the middle of a conversation, "They keep asking me."

"Who does?"

"The second-years. The ones with the crusts." She frowned at the posted sheet. "Because I'm on your track. They think I'd know if you had something."

"What do you tell them?"

"I tell them to ask you." She turned at last and looked up at him, with the serious, whole-faced attention she gave every sum. "I'm not telling anybody what you told me. At the board. About the second page."

He had not asked her to keep it, and he realized, standing there, that he had not even thought to. He had told a thirteen-year-old the most important true thing he owned and then walked away to assembly, and she had carried it for two days through a building full of people who were trying to find out exactly that.

"Thank you."

"It's yours," said Oona, as if that settled it. "You get to say it. Not me." She looked back at the board. "Are you going to win?"

"I don't know."

She weighed that, the way she weighed everything.

"That's a true one. You'd have said yes if it wasn't."

"Would I?"

"Grown-ups always say yes about winning. To make you feel better." She tucked her slate in tighter. "I'm coming to watch. Everybody is. My uncle says you should watch a good fight even if you don't know anybody in it, and I know somebody in this one."

She went off toward the lecture wing before he could answer. He stood in front of the board with the answer and the petition side by side and found that he was smiling in a way he would not have wanted Edran to see.

---

He found the seam on the fourth morning.

He found it the way he had found every seam that mattered, not by watching for a mistake, because Edran almost never made one, but by watching for the place where the precision itself cost something. It took him a long time to see, because it lived in a gap so small that only the shape of the whole sequence showed it.

Edran ran a set of four. The shell on his left forearm and the edge on his right were both up before he moved, waiting like a held breath. The first two went one after the other, the shell bursting on an imagined blow and the edge sweeping through an imagined strike, so fast that the two seemed to be one long motion. Cael had watched that a hundred times now and it never stopped being beautiful.

The third did not come so fast.

It came a sliver later: not much, a tenth of a breath, perhaps less. But in that sliver, after the edge had burst and before the third structure had formed, both of Edran's forearms were bare, and his weight sat on the commitment that had just ended with nothing new yet to carry it.

Cael watched him run the set again, and the sliver was there again, in the same place, between the second and the third. He watched the set of three, and the set of five, and the sliver sat in every one of them, always after the second, always before the third.

He understood it on the walk back across the yard, with the binder held flat against his chest in the cold. The first two were the ready pair, built at leisure with the whole of Edran's attention before the fight asked anything of him, and they cost him nothing to spend. The third had nothing behind it. It had to be built from nothing in the middle of everything, and building a full structure took the whole of a Glass fighter's mind, which was not free until the last of the ready pair had gone. Every Glass sequence of three or more had to pass through that one moment when the ready structures were spent and the next was not yet made.

It was not Edran's mistake. Any Glass fighter alive would carry that sliver in a sequence of three or more, and the better trained they were the smaller it would be, but it would always be there, because it came out of the Path itself. Edran had narrowed it as far as three years of perfect mornings could narrow it. He could not close it, not without paying for the closing somewhere else.

The record had said it too, in Wray's square hand. *Tires in the sequence, not the body.*

He wrote it in the binder that night in the plainest words he had.

*The gap sits between the second and the third. He didn't put it there; the Path did. There's nothing in him to fix. Patience, not correction.*

He did not sleep much.

On the fifth and sixth days he confirmed it from three directions, because one direction was a guess and two was a coincidence. He watched Edran drill alone in the mornings, and the gap held. He watched him spar in the afternoons against two partners Wray put across from him, a Blade fourth-year with very fast hands, and Hobb, who stood like a wall and made Edran come to him, so that every sequence had to run long. Against the Blade student the gap hardly mattered, because the bouts ended early. Against Hobb, who refused to be moved, Edran's sequences ran to four and five, and the gap opened after the second every single time, and Hobb, who was not looking for it, never used it. Hobb nodded to Cael at the rail afterward on his way out and said nothing at all, and Cael was almost certain that Hobb knew exactly what he had been watching.

The third direction was the record. He went back to the archive on the sixth afternoon and read the four losses again with Prynn's terrible tea at his elbow, and this time he knew what to look for. Every one of the four had been lost to a fighter who outlasted the ready pair in an exchange, and every one had been lost after the third exchange, when Edran was pressing to finish. The gap held from every direction. It was structural, not incidental, and it was there whether Edran was fresh or tired, alone or watched.

He did not train against it.

He wanted to, very badly. He wanted to go into section four in his supervised hour and run the framework against an imagined Glass sequence, timing a step into the sliver over and over until his legs knew it. He did not. Edran had said he would not change anything for the rail, and Cael did not doubt it. But Wray would be in that hall for one of his hours and Quenna for the other, and a seam that anyone could watch him rehearsing stopped being a seam the moment it was seen. He had learned that on the circuit, from the wrong end, more than once.

So he ran Lira's circuits in his supervised hour, and the framework against nothing, and the slow shoulder work for the Reydan joint, and Quenna wrote less than usual on her slate. Once she looked up at him across the section with an expression he could not read at all, and then looked back down.

The one thing he did train, those six nights, had nothing to do with Edran at all.

"Compression," he said on the fifth night, on Brom's floor after the eighth bell. "Quarter only. I'm not reaching for half this week."

Brom lowered himself onto the boards opposite with care; his own left wrist still remembered the night a half push had knocked it back a hand's width. "Why not?"

"Because half costs four breaths and a locked shoulder, and I want both shoulders on the eighth." Cael set his left forearm across his chest and braced it. His own wrist, the one he had misrouted a fortnight ago, had gone from swollen to stiff to nothing, and he flexed it once to make sure it stayed nothing. "And because it gets worse when I leave it. Two days without practice and the timing slides. If I let it slide this week, it'll wake on the eighth with bad manners."

"It'll wake anyway. If he puts a shell on you."

"I know. I want it to wake the way it's used to waking. Quietly. So I can tell it no."

Brom looked at him for a moment, and then set his left palm on Cael's forearm without any further argument. Iron Skin came up under the skin like frost forming on a pane, and he leaned.

They did ten. Each time Cael waited for the force to settle into the bone and chose the road before the fragment could choose one, out and back along the line it came in, a quarter's width and no wider. He did not let himself imagine the channel any broader than that. Five of the ten caught. Each of the five went home into Brom's wrist with the small solid knock of a door shutting in another room. Each one cost exactly what the quarter had always cost, the hot bar across the collarbones and two breaths he could not take, and none of them went into a joint.

On the sixth try he did something new, and he did it on purpose.

The force arrived, and the fragment woke and gathered and reached for it, and the road was laid and open. He let it gather all the way, until he could feel the quarter held in him like water in cupped hands, ready to go home.

And then he let it go.

He did not send it anywhere. He opened his hands, as it were, and let it spill out harmlessly, as he had at the first sitting with three pens moving, and his forearm took the rest of the push the ordinary way, with a dull flat ache.

Brom eased off and looked at him.

"You had that one."

"I had it."

"Then why—"

"Because on the eighth I'll have to have it and not use it. In front of everybody. I've only done it once in my life when it counted, and that was half by surprise. I wanted to do it when I'd decided to."

Brom sat back against the leg of his desk and looked at him for a long while, and then he made the low sound in his chest that did him for a laugh.

"Most people practise catching. You're practising dropping."

"Lira says I've always done everything backward."

"Lira's right." Brom turned his left wrist over and looked at it, as if it might have something to report. "Again. Catch four. Drop the fifth. I'll count."

They did ten more. Four caught and went home, and two did nothing, and four times Cael gathered a quarter and held it and then let it go. The fourth time it went so quietly that Brom, with his palm on the forearm, could not tell from his side whether anything had been caught at all.

"That last one. Did you have it?"

"Yes."

"I couldn't feel it go out." Brom took his hand away. "That's either very good or very strange."

Cael wrote it up that night under the four headings. *Compression-adjacent. Quarter only, by choice. Five of ten caught. Then four caught and dropped on purpose, after the gather, before the send. The last one so quiet Brom couldn't feel it leave. Cost of a drop: none found yet. Integration: partial. Learning to put it down.*

---

Lira found him on the sixth night in the stable after supper, when the long room had nearly emptied.

He had the binder open on the end of the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, and he was not writing in it. He was reading the Glass page from the top for perhaps the tenth time. She came down the long room with a mug of something hot in each hand, set one by his elbow, read the page over his shoulder without asking, as she had read every page of that binder since Ardenmere. Then she sat down across from him and wrapped both hands round her own mug.

"You've had it since the fourth day."

It was not a question.

"I wanted to be sure."

"You're always sure by the fourth day." She blew across the top of the mug. "You've just stopped believing yourself that early. You used to. In Ardenmere you'd come home on the fourth night and say, *it's his left foot*, and you'd be right, and we'd go and eat."

"Being wrong on the fourth day used to be cheaper."

She did not argue with that. She drank, and looked at him over the rim, and he could see her decide not to say three different things before she said the fourth.

"It's a good seam. I read it. It's real."

"I know it's real."

"Then stop reading the page." She reached across and closed the binder for him, gently, with two fingers on the cover. "You've read it ten times. I counted from the door. There's nothing on it tonight that wasn't on it at supper."

He let her close it, and sat with his hands flat on the cover and the hot mug steaming beside them, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. The fire at the far end of the room was down to its last coals, and two second-years playing tiles beside it had very carefully not looked up when Lira came in, and somewhere upstairs a door closed. It was the kind of quiet the two of them had got good at, on more nights like this than either of them could have counted, across a table in some building that did not belong to them.

"Brom's done his numbers," Lira said eventually. "Fetch. Eight today."

"Eight."

"He told me at the hatch as if he were reporting a death in the family, and then went away looking pleased." She smiled into her mug. "He's started carrying the sheet in his coat. He takes it out when he thinks nobody's watching and looks at it."

"Like Edran with his shells."

"Like you with that." She nodded at the closed binder. "Everybody in this building has a thing they take out when they think nobody's watching. Mine's the dot." She drank. "Karis has two notebooks. Gerda checks the clock."

"What does Edran take out?"

Lira thought about it with the same care she gave an opponent's feet.

"The board. He goes and looks at the board."

They sat until the coals were nearly black and the tile players had packed up and gone and the lamp at the end of the table had burned down so far that there was no reason left to sit on. Then Lira stood and took both mugs, and put her free hand on his shoulder for a moment as she passed, the way she always had.

"Sleep. Tomorrow's the seventh. You get one more day of being sure."

He slept better than he had all week.

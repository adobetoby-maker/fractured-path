# Chapter 39 — Three Clean Days

Lira's door had shut and the corridor had gone quiet, and it was late on the Saturday, and the binder lay open on the desk by the narrow window under a lamp he had turned down almost to nothing.

For two days the match had been sending him its bill, and the bill had come in three different hands.

Quenna had written hers in the record's language. Her page would stand only until someone unfriendly watched him do it a second time, and under it sat a question he had no answer to. *Whether you can not do it.* Karis's had been the stranger and the kinder, for she had spent two days searching herself for a cost, as patiently as she would search a table for a bad figure, and where the cost should have been she had found something she wanted more of. That was dizzying in its own way, because it meant that whatever lived in him did not only take; it joined, the join ran both ways, and none of them had a word for it yet. The third hand was his own and the oldest, the binder's: two misfires, with the date and the hour, a price charged in half-seconds of nothing for a thing he had done without ever being told what it cost.

So he put it in columns, because a frightened mind will lean on a sentence and cannot lean on a column, and he needed something that would not let him lean.

*Known. A directed acquisition is possible. Once. Under six weeks of close watching, and then a match where she was trying with everything she had, against me, and I had nothing left.*

*Known. It costs the others. About a day. Two misfires in the Wind, this time, and then clean.*

*Known. The panel saw an effect and not how. The record is drafted. It will hold unless I give somebody a reason to open it again.*

*Unknown. Whether I can not do it, if the same things ever line up again without my wanting them to. In a real fight. Against somebody who means it. In front of exactly the people Quenna is afraid of.*

*Unknown. Whether the cost grows, and with what. Whether some later one asks for something the older four can't pay.*

The last column stayed empty a long while, and when he did write in it, he wrote slowly.

*Unknown. What it is for. Whether it's for anything.*

Round that line he drew a small box, his own mark for *not yet*, which he had used before: round the session-nine page at the back, round the line he had copied out of Vell's archive in somebody else's hand, round every door he had found before he had the key to it. He sat back and considered it; for what it held, the box looked very small.

The sentence had been gathering in him since he knelt on the frozen ground of the formal yard. He picked up the pen again and wrote it down plainly, because he knew no other way to hold a thing this big.

*I need to understand what I'm actually doing before it gets someone killed.*

He read it back, and it was the truest line he had put in the binder since the week of Karis's hypothesis, and it was only half finished; he could feel the rest of it waiting. So he sat with the pen lifted until he could make himself say the rest exactly instead of bravely.

*It isn't me I'm afraid for. I can carry my own bill; I've decided that. It's the three people who keep standing close enough to be in the room when I find out what the next one costs.*

Some while after the corridor had gone silent, Brom let himself in without knocking and lowered himself onto the floor across from the desk, put his back to the wall, and stretched his legs out in front of him.

For a long time he said nothing, sitting as he had sat in the Ardenmere market square, the judge of interesting problems settled at his post, with nowhere else to be and nothing that needed saying yet. The lamp sank a knuckle's width while somewhere beneath them the old building ticked and settled.

What he finally talked about was not the misfires, and not Quenna's question, and not the soft-cornered page in the binder's back pocket.

"I said a thing to you once in Ardenmere," said Brom. "That the word for you would end up yours. Not something handed down to you by somebody else." His chin went toward the wet ink on the open page. "You just started choosing."

Cael looked down at the page.

Nothing about it felt like winning, but he had never before put a true number to something heavy, and he found that the honest weight sat easier on him than a vague and comfortable one would have. Thursday had taught him that the reach was there. The two days since had taught him its price, and whose shoulders the price might fall on, and that there was only one way of carrying it he could bear to live inside: the slow way, counting and dating, with no glory in any of it. Taking was something anybody could do. The choosing happened in the ledger.

"It's a heavier word than I expected," Cael told him.

"Most of the good ones are," said Brom. He stayed where he was, on the floor against the wall, until the lamp had burned so low that nothing was left that either of them needed to say.

---

On the Sunday morning, before the first bell, Karis came out to the training yard with them.

She came out with her coat collar up and her hands in her pockets and no notebook, and stood at the edge of the frost a little way off from the pump while Lira and Cael squared up, and she did not say anything until Lira looked at her.

"May I count?" said Karis. "It isn't a session. There aren't any terms for this one. I'd only like to know the number."

"You're in," said Lira. "You don't have to ask."

"I know I don't." Karis did not move. "I'd like to anyway. For a while. Until I've got the hang of not."

Lira considered her a long moment. Then something in her face gave, very slightly, and she nodded at the frozen earth beside the pump.

"Stand there, then. You'll see his feet."

They ran the old patterns at half speed, Lira coming at him with the sequences she had taught him before either of them had known what he was learning, slip and turn and step, and he called on the framework for every one of them and every one of them came. It was there before he had finished turning toward it, quick and light and his, as it had been every morning of his life before the formal yard. The left turn where it had failed on Friday came clean, and the third sequence, and he ran forty and every one of them answered.

At the end Karis said, from beside the pump, "A quarter of a breath. The lead. It's back to where it was before the eleventh week."

"Is that good?" said Lira.

"It's what it was," said Karis. "I'd call that good. I'd write it down as unchanged."

"Then write it down as unchanged." Lira picked up the rope from the pump's nail and coiled it. "That's two. Saturday was clean and today's clean. One more."

"Saturday wasn't counted," said Cael. "You didn't say."

"I counted it. I didn't say it. I'm saying it now." Lira hung the rope back on its nail. "Two."

At breakfast there were four bowls again, and this time somebody said something about it, and it was Karis.

"I've been thinking," she said, to her porridge, "about how this is supposed to work."

Lira looked up from the loaf she was tearing. "How what's supposed to work?"

"This. Being in." Karis turned her spoon over. "At Ternhall there were rules for everything. Where the first rank sat in the refectory. Who spoke first at a paired drill. Whether you were allowed to borrow somebody's notes without asking in writing." She looked round the three of them. "I've been watching the three of you since the autumn and I can't find any rules at all. I've looked."

"There aren't any," said Brom.

"There must be some."

"There's one," said Lira. She tore the loaf into four, not three, and pushed the pieces round the table like a dealer laying out cards, and the largest piece went to Karis. "I take everybody's bread. That's the rule. Everybody's. Yours too, from tomorrow."

Karis looked at the largest piece.

"You've given me the most."

"That's the other half of the rule," said Lira. "I take it, and then I give most of it back. Nobody knows why. Don't ask."

Karis considered this with the whole of her attention, the way she considered a timing that did not agree with anything she had written before. Then she picked up the bread, broke it in half, and held out the smaller piece across the table to Lira.

"Then I'll start practising," she said.

Lira stared at the bread, and then she laughed, really laughed, the sound that came up out of her only a few times a term, and took the piece and ate it.

"She's learning," said Brom.

"She's very quick," said Lira, with her mouth full. "I'd forgotten that about her. Watch your bowl, Brom."

Brom moved his bowl a hand's width further from Karis with great dignity, and Karis watched him do it with an expression that was trying very hard not to be delight and failing.

Cael ate his porridge and did not say anything at all. He was watching Karis, because he could not help watching, so he saw her look once, very briefly, at the corner of the binder showing in the inside of his coat, and then look away, at once, and not look back at it for the rest of the meal. She did not ask, and he had not thought she would. It was a long promise, and she had made it in a stable in the fourth week at a table with a clean sheet on it, and she was keeping it as she kept everything, exactly.

---

Oona found him at the board in the main hall on the Sunday afternoon, in front of the calendar, which had been her office far longer now than it had been his.

She was not looking at the calendar but at his arm. He was wearing his oldest shirt, with the sleeve rolled loose above the linen so that nothing touched it, and she looked at the linen gravely for a long moment before she looked up at him.

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

He thought about it, because she would know.

"Like a very bad kettle. Less every day."

She nodded, as if that was a good answer and a precise one, and looked round the hall, which was half empty on a Sunday afternoon with nobody near, and lowered her voice anyway.

"I was on the front bench," she said. "I was close. Closer than the panel. When you went down on one knee." She looked at his right hand, the good one, and then back at his face. "There was a light under your hand. Not hers. Yours. I saw it. Nobody believes me. I told the girls on my landing, and they said it was hers and I didn't know where to look." Her chin came up. "I know where to look."

He stood in front of the calendar and remembered a morning in the first fortnight when he had told her, without knowing what it would cost him later, that he would not lie to her. He had kept it, and he was not going to stop keeping it now, in a half-empty hall, because the truth had become heavier.

"It was mine," he said.

Oona did not move.

"It's new," he said. "I don't understand it yet. I don't understand most of what I am yet, and that's the truth too." He held her eyes. "When I understand it better, I'll tell you what I can. I'm not going to tell you everything. Some of it isn't safe to say out loud yet, even to people I'd tell anything. But I'm not going to tell you it was hers, because it wasn't."

Oona considered this, the way she considered every sum, with great care and all the way through.

"Is it the strange kind? Like yours was?"

"It's part of the strange kind."

"Will mine be?"

"No," said Cael. "Yours will be ordinary. You decided that in the first week, and you've been right about everything else."

Oona looked at him for a long time, and then nodded, once, briskly, and wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice, and he did not try to read it.

"Six days," she said.

"Six days."

"The hand goes on the other shoulder. Not the one by your arm." She tucked the slate under her own arm. "I worked it out. My mother's going to stand on the left, and if you come down too you'd have to stand on the right, and your right arm's fine." She was already walking away, and she turned back. "I won't tell the girls on my landing. They wouldn't believe it, and it's not theirs."

She went up the hall toward the residence wing with her slate held very tight against her chest.

Gerda was at the floor desk in the training hall when he passed it on his way back from the archive, signing a slip for the sixth bell. She wrote the hour on it in her careful square figures and blotted it, and then, instead of looking up at the clock above the door, she looked at him.

"I didn't look at it," she said. "On Thursday. I watched the three exchanges. Not the clock once."

"That was your advice."

"It was. I thought I'd better take it, if I was giving it." She folded the slip once along a crease that was already there. "I was by the gate. When you crossed the short one, I could feel the heat from where I stood." She looked at the linen on his arm. "That was a hit you took on purpose. Where everybody could see you chose it."

"Lira says that's the only kind worth taking."

"Lira's right." Gerda tucked the slip into her coat. "I'm sparring Hobb again on Tuesdays. Nobody asked me to. I asked him." She said it in her procedure voice, every word set down separately, as if she were reading a clause aloud. "I lay the haze and walk out of it on purpose, every time, before he's even moved. It's the opposite of what I did for a year." She nodded once, formally. "I wanted somebody to know. It isn't in any record."

"It's in mine now," said Cael. "If you don't mind."

Gerda considered that.

"I don't mind," she said, and went off toward the open floor, and did not check the clock on the way.

Lira had the stories at supper, all six of them, and she counted them out at the scarred table between the four bowls on her fingers.

"First, Karis threw the match on purpose so that the faculty would stop watching her. That one's mostly the Edge floor, and it's very stupid, and they love it." A second finger. "Second, the observer's secretly Ember and has been hiding it all term. That's near, so I've been squashing it." A third. "Third, Karis's lattice failed on its own because she'd held it too long, and she laughed because she was embarrassed. The heat-Paths second-years are telling that one, and they're very sure, because they understand sustain cost." She looked at Karis. "You're not offended?"

"No. It's a reasonable hypothesis from the evidence they had," said Karis. "It's wrong, but it's reasonable."

"Fourth and fifth are boring. Sixth." Lira held up the sixth finger, which meant the thumb of her other hand. "The observer made fire. Out of his own hand. On one knee." She put the hand down. "That's the true one. Nobody believes it. Oona's been telling it all afternoon on the second floor, and they're teasing her about it."

"I told her it was true," said Cael.

Across the table Lira studied him a long moment.

"Good," she said at last. "You'd promised her." She took his bread and gave him back the larger half. "Four stories pulling different ways and two true ones nobody believes. Worry when they start to agree."

Hobb passed the end of the table on his way to the hatch with his bowl, and slowed, as he slowed at rails. He looked at the linen on Cael's arm.

"Shortest one?" said Hobb.

"Shortest one."

Hobb considered the arm with a kind of professional sympathy.

"My shins say you should've listened," he said, and went on to the hatch.

---

He went to the archive after supper, because it was where he went, and sat at the far end of the long table with the observation notebook shut and the bad arm resting on the wood, while Prynn sat at her high desk with her ledger and her spectacles on their cord and did not look up when he came in.

At the eighth bell, when she would ordinarily have come down off her stool and put out her lamp and stood by the door with her keys until he took the hint, she came down off her stool and went to the pot by her desk instead and poured two cups. She brought one to the long table and set it at his elbow, and then she did a thing she had never done in fifteen weeks, which was to sit down across from him with the other.

He did not say anything; he had learned that much about Prynn.

"I heard the yard," she said, after a while. "On Thursday. From here, with the windows shut. I've heard a great many yards in sixty years. I've never heard one go that quiet." She drank her tea. "I'm not going to ask you what made it go quiet. I don't ask readers what they're reading."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've told you before." She set the cup down square to the edge of the table, exactly, as she set everything. "I'll tell you something instead, since you've been sitting at my table every evening for a term and pretending not to notice a thing."

He waited.

"I have not forgotten to lock that door," said Prynn, "once in sixty years." She looked at him over her spectacles. "Not once. Not on the night of the long frost, when I couldn't feel my own hands on the key." She picked her cup up again. "So you may stop being so careful never to mention it. I'd hate you to strain yourself."

He looked at her, and she looked back, perfectly composed, and drank her tea.

"Why?" he said at last.

"Because the shelves want reading," said Prynn, "and they don't much care what hour it is. Most of the people who come in here want one page and go away again. You read the page next to it." She looked at her cup. "Drink your tea. It's getting cold, and I don't make it twice."

He drank his tea and she drank hers, and when the cups were empty she took them both back to her desk and climbed onto her stool and opened her ledger, and left the lamp burning, and did not look at him again.

He wrote to Hesk on the Sunday evening, at the desk by the window, because Hesk had said to write when something happened, and the last letter had said nothing had.

*Hesk —*

*Something happened this week.*

*I fought the girl from the bigger school. The one who asks before she looks. It was a match in front of the whole academy, with three people writing it down, and she was the best I have ever stood across from. She had been showing me her best for six weeks and it turned out it was only her careful. The real thing was faster. I got burned on the arm doing something stupid that I chose on purpose, and it will be a while healing, and I would choose it again. It was called a draw.*

*I found out something about myself in it. I can't put it in a letter. I'm not keeping it from you; I'm keeping it off paper. I'll tell you the whole of it one day across your bench, if you'll have me on it.*

*She has asked to be one of us. We said yes. Lira left her bread.*

*Brom says I've started choosing. I think he's right. It's heavier than I thought.*

*The shoulder still complains. The arm complains louder. Both fine.*

*Cael*

He read it back. There was nothing in it that was untrue and nothing a stranger could have made anything of, and the whole week was in it all the same, folded small. He wrote the Denvash direction on the outside and set it on the corner of the desk for Lira to take down on the third-day post.

Then he sat a while longer with the lamp low, and let himself turn, carefully, toward the new one.

He did not reach for it, because Lira's rule held until the third day and the third day was not yet; he only attended to it, as you look at a coin in your palm without spending it. It was where it had been since Thursday, low and warm, not doing anything, and it still felt like her, the precision still there in the warmth, and the patience, the small commitments kept exactly. He had stopped being startled by that and had begun, instead, to be glad of it, in a way he did not yet know how to write down, and perhaps never would. Some things you carried before you filed them.

He thought of all of them, the five, and the people in them. He thought that whatever else it was, whatever it was for, the thing in him had always kept the company it came from.

---

On the Monday of the seventeenth week, at first light, the frost was so thick on the training yard that their boots left prints in it like prints in snow.

Lira came at him at half speed and then, because it was the third day and she had promised herself she would test it properly, at three-quarters. Karis stood by the pump and counted. Brom had come out too this time, without saying why, and stood beside Karis with his hands behind his back and his breath going up white, planted like a gatepost.

The framework answered every time.

It answered on the left turn and the right, on the third sequence and the eleventh, and on the double that Lira threw at the end, two pushes one on top of the other, the way Wray had broken the count at the first sitting, and it read both off her shoulders and slipped him out of both, and there was no empty pocket anywhere in it. It was quick and light and his, the oldest thing he owned, and the quickness in it was Lira's and always had been.

Lira stopped and stood in the frost with her hands on her hips, breathing white, and looked at him for a long moment.

"Three," she said.

"Three," said Karis, from the pump. "A quarter of a breath. Unchanged."

Brom nodded once, the slow nod.

"Three clean days," said Lira. "That's the rule, and it's met." She took the rope off the pump's nail and began to coil it, and did not look at him while she did it. "You can touch the new one now. Carefully. Small. With somebody in the room." She hung the rope back. "Tomorrow. Not today. Today you've earned a breakfast."

They went in across the frozen yard together, the four of them, leaving four sets of prints in the frost behind them.

He wrote it at the desk by the window before the first bell rang, in the plain hand he kept for the mornings when something had been counted and come out right.

*Monday, week seventeen. Third clean day. The framework answered every call, at three-quarter speed, double included. Lead a quarter of a breath, unchanged. K. counted. Lira's rule met. The new one may be touched from tomorrow, small, with somebody in the room.*

He looked at the page above it, where the misfires were written, and the columns, and the small box round the line he could not answer, and at the back pocket, where a consent and a soft-cornered paragraph sat side by side, and he thought of Quenna's question, which was not written anywhere, in any record, in anybody's hand, and was the heaviest thing he had.

Then he wrote one more line, small, at the bottom.

*Five fragments. One of them chosen. A question I can't answer yet, filed nowhere. Four bowls at the end of the table. Oona Kindles on Saturday.*

He closed the binder, and went down to breakfast, where Lira had already taken his bread.

# Chapter 38 — Inside

Karis woke on the Friday with warm hands, and the first thing she did, before she had sat up or looked at the window, was check whether she was still all there.

She held her left hand out over the blanket, palm up, close above her face, and her off hand, the right, went still at her side. She counted it without meaning to, the way she had counted it since she was fourteen: a quarter of a second, near enough, a hair less than at the end of yesterday. Then a point bloomed over her palm, small and dim and steady, a coin of light the size of a thumbnail, and its warmth reached her skin.

It was exactly as it had always been.

She held it to a count of twenty and let it go, and watched its third of a breath of shimmer hang over the blanket and fade. Then she laid another, and joined the two along the bedpost with a thin singing line, and let that go too, and lay back on the pillow and looked at the ceiling.

Nothing was missing. Nothing in her had been taken away.

She had known that, really, since the yard. She had felt nothing leave her in the third exchange; she had felt something answer her, which was not the same. But she had wanted to check it in the morning, cold, with her own hands, the way she checked any result that mattered to her before she let herself believe it. Whatever he had done, it was not theft; her Path was exactly the size it had been on Wednesday, and it had not been divided. It had been, she thought, carefully, trying the word in her head and not yet trusting it, *copied*. No. That was not right either. She did not have the word yet.

She got up and washed and dressed and sat down at the little table by her window with both notebooks, because that was what she did with mornings.

The first notebook was easy.

She wrote the match into it as she would have written any bout: the conditions, the panel, the three exchanges, her point counts in each, the time she held them through the resets, the sustain cost that had come due afterward in her fingertips and gone by morning. She wrote that the candidate had read her near points before they lit, and that she had moved her floor outward in the second exchange to answer it. She wrote that he had crossed a channel, the shortest one, and that she had not believed he would. She wrote that her fourth channel's last point had failed to take, because the place she had laid it was already burning, and that the assembly had lost its sequence. She wrote, because it was true and hers to record, that she had been holding every lit channel on that count, and that when the count broke she had lost her hold on all of them at once, and they had gone out.

Then she stopped, with her pen over the page, at the next line.

She did not write *by what*. Clause five of the first terms forbade it, and clause two of the second, in Brom's square capitals, forbade it twice over; she had agreed to both and written one of them herself, and she would not have written it anyway. There were things she did not put on paper because a rule said she must not, and there were things she did not put on paper because she would not, and since yesterday this was both.

She closed the first notebook.

The ledger was harder. It was the ledger of the nulls, twenty-two sessions in her small upright hand with the ruled line under them and the sentence about the missing condition. It recorded conditions and results and nothing else, and a match was not a session; it had not been run in the quiet room, or under her consent spoken aloud, or by any rule she had written. But it had been the twenty-third time she had stood in front of him while he watched, and she could not leave the ledger without it.

So she wrote it under the barley clause, under everything.

*Supplementary. Not a session. Formal yard, fifth sitting, full intensity, under the presiding assessor's conditions. Source: K., Ember Path, Iron Rank Three. Consent: written, filed. Distance: from eight paces to one. Duration: three exchanges. Result, as reported by the subject: a fragment notice. Ember-adjacent. The field reads: acquisition, directed.*

She looked at it for a long time, the first entry in the ledger that did not end in *null*. Her hand did not want to write anything under it, and she let her hand have its way, and closed the ledger.

Then she opened the second notebook, the one that held everything the standard model got wrong. She turned to a clean page, and wrote the date at the top, and did not write anything else for an hour.

---

Quenna stopped her in the yard at the second bell, on her way back from the pump with a cup of water she did not want and had gone to fetch only so that her legs would have something to do.

She did not stop her so much as arrive beside her, in her grey coat with her folder under one arm. She walked with her for a few steps across the frost as if they had always meant to be going the same way. Karis did not slow down, and Quenna did not ask her to.

"I'm not going to ask you anything," said Quenna.

"I know you're not."

"I wanted you to know that I'm not. In case you'd been bracing for it." Quenna looked ahead at the main hall door. "Your consent is in the file, with my initials beside it. It says exactly what you meant it to say. Nobody is going to read it any other way while I'm the one who holds the file."

Karis walked three more steps.

"You read the word *source*," she said. "In your room. I saw you stop on it."

"I did."

"You didn't ask what it meant."

"No." Quenna's voice did not change at all. "I've spent twenty years learning which questions to ask. I've spent nearly as long learning which ones not to." They had reached the door. Quenna stopped, and turned, and looked at her for the first time. It was a level, tired look, and there was nothing in it that Karis could have written down. "You look as though you haven't slept. Eat something today. I don't care what."

She went in. Karis stood on the step with the cup of water in her hand, and after a moment she understood that she was going to do as she was told.

She did not go down to the midday meal, all the same.

She had meant to, because Quenna had told her to eat. But when the fourth bell rang she was still at the table by the window with the second notebook open to a page that held one date and nothing else. She could not imagine sitting at the scarred table with the three of them and saying anything at all about the weather.

At the fifth bell she went to the quiet room, because it was the hour her slate always claimed, and because it was the one place in the academy where nobody would ask her anything. The slate on the nail by the narrow door still said *K. — declaration practice*, and it was still true.

She stood at the end of the whitewashed room where she had stood twenty-two times, and laid points.

Single points, pairs, a channel along the end wall, a sustain to a count of sixty: she did not do it to learn anything, but as some people walk, to keep their bodies busy while their minds got on with something else. And somewhere in the second quarter-hour, with nobody at the far end of the room, she laid a point a fraction early.

She knew why. She had always known why.

For three years at Ternhall there had been a second set of points across the paired floor from hers, always a breath behind or a breath ahead, so that every ignition she laid was half of a conversation and the other half was always coming. She had learned to lay early into that. She had come to Greyvane and laid her points on a lean old man's count into nothing at all, and she was still doing it. She corrected it every time and never wrote the correction down. She had decided long ago there was a small amount of data she would rather carry than file.

She stood in the quiet room with her off hand very still and let herself think about the third exchange.

She had been trying. That was the thing he had read aloud from the back of his binder by a shaded lamp, and it was true, and she had known it was true before he had finished the first sentence. She had been trying with everything she had, for the first time since the paired floor. She had come out of her mark mid-argument and laid sixteen and held them through two resets and never let one go. She had spent more of herself in three exchanges than in any bout of her life, because she had wanted very badly to win, and she had wanted to win against him.

And in the third exchange, at the very end, when she had been certain, when the fourth point was going down and the whole drawing was about to light, something had answered her.

It had answered in her own language: not a channel of somebody else's fire, not a counter, not anything she had a column for, but a point. One small, rough, crooked point, laid exactly where hers was going, a breath before hers, so that hers could not take. It was the oldest rule there was, the first rule they taught an Ember child, *nothing can set fire where a fire already is*. She had shown him that rule herself, on the sixth block, for symmetry. He had said it back to her in the middle of her own sentence, in her own words, with a hand on the frozen ground.

She had not been hurt. She had not been diminished. She had been *answered*.

She stood very still in the whitewashed room. Through the whole of the long cold night she had expected to feel something she would have to put a name to; she had expected to feel taken from. Robbed, or violated, or at the least strange, with some part of the thing she had drilled since she was eleven now lying inside somebody else, for good, because she had signed a sheet and the sheet had worked.

She did not feel any of that. She had gone looking for it, the way she went looking for a fault in a timing table, carefully and on purpose, and it was not there.

What she felt instead was the thing she had not felt since she had walked out of Ternhall's gate nine days before its term. Across a floor, for the length of one breath, she had not been alone in it.

She let the last point go and sat down on the scrubbed floor with her back against the whitewash and her hands in her lap, as she had sat after the long session in the tenth week, and cried. It did not go on for long, and she had not done it in front of anybody since she was eleven, and she was not in front of anybody now. When it was over she wiped her face on her cuff, and noted, because she noted everything about herself, that she felt considerably better, and that she did not know whether that was a finding.

There was half a dark loaf outside her door when she came back up the stairs at the seventh bell. It had been set on the floor squarely in the middle of the doorway, so that nobody could open the door without seeing it. Beside it was a small stone pot with a lid, still faintly warm to the touch, with a spoon laid across the top. She lifted the lid. Barley.

She stood in the corridor and looked at them.

She knew whose they were. Lira stole bread from everyone in the building and gave most of it away again, and she had never once in Karis's sight given anything to anybody in a way that could be thanked for. Brom did not give barley to people. Brom defended barley to people, at length, with real feeling, and ate it in front of them. A pot of it outside somebody's door was not a gift so much as a statement of position.

Neither of them had knocked or left a note.

Karis carried both inside and sat down at the table by the window and ate all of the bread and most of the barley. Then, because she was who she was, she opened the first notebook at the back and wrote, very small, under the last page of timings: *Friday. Bread at the door (L.). Barley at the door (B.). No knock. No note.* She looked at it, and could not think of anything to write under it that would be true and would also be data, and closed the notebook.

Her window looked down across the corner of the training yard to the low wall at the end of the residence wing. Late in the evening, when she got up to draw the curtain, there were two shapes sitting on it in the dark, close together, with their knees drawn up. One of them had a coat pulled round her in a way Karis would have known at any distance. The other sat with one arm held against his chest.

She stood at the window with the curtain in her hand and watched them.

They were not doing anything or, as far as she could see, talking, and neither of them moved. She had watched the three of them on that wall from this window on other nights, and she had always noted it, as a fact about the academy. Tonight she did not want to note it. She wanted, quite simply and without any method at all, to be down there on the cold stone with them, not saying anything.

That was new, and she examined it, standing at the window, as carefully as she had examined her own hands that morning.

At Ternhall she had never once wanted to be anywhere she had not already earned. She had been first, and first had a place, and she had sat in it. She had not known until this moment, at a window in a minor academy in the cold, that a person could want a place she had not earned and could not rank for. Nor had she known that the wanting could feel so plain, like being hungry.

One of the shapes on the wall stood up, shook out her coat and went in, and the other stayed. Karis watched him sit there alone with his arm against his chest until the cold must have come right through him. Then she drew the curtain. Watching him any longer without his knowing was a thing she had promised herself on a coach in the autumn that she would never do, and she meant to keep every one of those promises, including the ones nobody else knew she had made.

She did not sleep much. She did not mind.

---

On the Saturday she wrote the paragraph.

She wrote it the first time at dawn, at the table by the window. It came out as a report, which was the only shape she knew how to make a thing come out. *Session subject reports no distress. The finding is anomalous and the source is unable to account for it.* It went on like that for half a page. When she read it back she found it true in every word and saying nothing at all, like one of Quenna's records, which was useful in a record and not what she meant. She tore the page out, carefully, along the fold, and set it aside.

She wrote it the second time at the third bell and it was the other way, all feeling and no shape, so that she could not bear to read it back past the second sentence, and she tore that out too.

The third time she tried to say only what was true and nothing more, and she went slowly. Twice she stopped and went to the window and looked at the yard and came back. Once she wrote a sentence and then scraped it off the paper with her penknife, very carefully, because she did not want a crossing-out on this page, and the ghost of it stayed in the grain of the paper anyway. She decided to let it stay, as a true record of having been wrong once on the way.

When she had finished she read it four times, because she did not trust herself to have read it right the first three.

*The source is not distressed by the result. I have no framework that explains that, and I am writing it down as unexplained rather than dressing it in language that would make it look solved. This much I can say plainly. At no point did I feel examined. Since the third exchange I have not once felt like the thing being tested. At the very end of it something answered me, in my own language, and for that moment I was not alone in it. I have not been alone in it since. I do not know yet what that makes me. I would rather find out standing with the three of you than standing outside, if you would ever have me.*

She folded it in half, and then in half again, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat, against her ribs.

Then she took it out, and unfolded it, and read it again, and folded it the other way, and put it back.

She did that six times between the fourth bell and the sixth, until the corners had gone soft and the creases had begun to cross each other, and then she made herself stop. She thought of her mother at the kitchen table on the last evening at home. *And if he turns out to be only a boy, and the whole mystery is in the paperwork?* He was not only a boy, and the mystery was not in the paperwork. Part of it, she knew now, was in her, and part of it was in him, and part of it, the part she had never had a column for, was in the space between the two of them across a chalk ring at the moment she had tried hardest. She would write to her mother about the match, but not about this. Not yet. Some things you carried before you filed them, if you ever filed them at all.

And Master Marlowe, she thought, standing in front of his second-years in a warm hall a whole country away, would never know how right he had been, or about what. She was smiling, alone, at the table by the window, and she let herself.

She went down to the archive at the eighth bell, an hour after Prynn usually locked it, because she knew where he would be, and she did not take a notebook. At the bottom of the stairs she put her hand to her coat to make sure one was not there, and it found only the folded page.

---

The archive door opened at the eighth bell on the Saturday, and he knew the step before he looked up.

He was at the far end of the long table with the observation notebook shut on the table before him and his bad arm resting in its loose linen across his lap. He had not seen her for two days, and it had taken him until Friday afternoon to notice, which embarrassed him once he had. Since the autumn Karis had kept time like the water clock: every session, every meal, every evening in the stable, and the archive so regularly that the bells might have been set by her. Then, from Brom's room on the night of the match, nothing. The only sighting was Brom's: Saturday at first light, a figure crossing the yard at speed with the collar up, gone before he could call out. When Cael asked Lira, Lira said only, "She's writing something she doesn't want watched." Lira would know. Lira had once lost two days of her own to four paragraphs from Fenmark, and he did not ask.

Karis came the length of the room and sat down opposite him. She had brought no notebook. She laid her folded hands on the table in the place where one should have been, and looked at that empty place before she looked at him.

"I have to tell you something," she said. "Please don't speak until I'm done. If you stop me in the middle I won't be able to begin again, and it has taken me two days to get as far as the bottom of the stairs."

He nodded.

"I asked to be the source. I put it in writing and I underlined it. *If it works, the documentation matters.* I meant every word. You're the biggest question in the one subject I care about, and you have been for most of a year. Going after you is the thing I'm proudest of. That hasn't changed." She was watching her hands as she spoke, not him, and that was new. Karis looked at everything in the world except her own hands. "The part I never planned for was being the result. Some of what I've drilled since I was eleven is inside you now. It stays there. It stays because I signed a sheet, and the sheet worked."

"Karis—"

"I asked you to let me finish." She took a breath. "That's as much as I can say aloud. The rest is written down. Spoken, it would have come out sounding like something else."

From inside her coat she drew one folded page and held it out to him over the table.

He had half expected two close pages of data, and what he held was a single paragraph in her small upright hand, which he could tell before he read a word had been written more than once, because the paper said so. It had been folded and opened and folded again until the creases crossed each other and the corners had worn soft as cloth. Close to one edge an earlier sentence had been scraped off with great care, and its ghost still showed faintly in the grain.

He read it.

The archive was very still around them, and back in the stacks Prynn's lamp was burning low, left alight once more as if she had forgotten it, and the shelves ran away into the dark with their centuries of other people's lives written down. It seemed to him that a room like this had been built for nothing so much as this: a person setting down the one thing that mattered and passing it across a table to someone else.

When he looked up she was watching him at last. The careful evenness she wore over everything had gone from her face, and what lay under it looked, on Karis, almost painfully like courage.

"You want to be inside," he said. "Not the arrangement. The other thing."

"There isn't any other way that I know of. I've never once had to ask for this." Her hands stayed where they were. "At Ternhall you belonged by where you stood. My name sat high enough on a board, so I was in. This isn't that. Nobody has written down a method for it, and I can hardly stand that. So I'm asking."

He sat holding the page.

Seven clauses, and one word that could stop everything: that had been the first paper between them, drawn up in the stable in the fourth week by four careful people who did not yet trust each other, and it had been armour. This was the opposite: no clauses, no door left open for getting out, only one paragraph asking for the one thing no clause has ever been able to secure for anyone. She had set down the least guarded sentences of her life in the same small hand she used for boundary conditions, and then she had folded them and carried them against her ribs for two days. He thought of Lira on a rooftop in Ardenmere, long ago. Every time his life had changed for the better, he realized, it had been because someone chose to hand someone else the copy with no armour on it.

"I can't answer this by myself," he said. "You knew that when you wrote it. *The three of you.*"

"I know," said Karis. "It's why I wrote it like that."

She stood, and she did not reach for the page; she left it in his hands, and that said something too, because it meant she was not going to carry it off and improve it. It was finished. It was what she had found.

---

Lira read it on the wall.

He took it out to her because she was there, as she was nearly always there, with her knees drawn up and her coat round her and the cold coming off the stone. Without saying anything he held it out, and she looked at it, and at the softness of the creases, and at him, and took it.

She read it, and turned it over, and there was nothing on the back. She read it again in the light from the residence wing's one lit window, slowly, the way she had read the consent across the scarred table. Then she sat with it in both hands on her knees and looked out at the black yard.

"Well," she said, half to herself. "There it is, then."

It was not refusal, and it was not quite welcome yet either. It was what a line sounds like when it gives an inch, as lines do when something real has moved them and not just somebody's say-so.

"She left you bread," said Cael. "Didn't she. On the Friday."

"Somebody left her bread." Lira did not look at him. "I wouldn't know who. People are always leaving bread about in this building." She folded the page once along its softest crease. "Two days. She did it in two days. It took me two years to read four paragraphs."

"It took you a morning."

"It took me two years to get to the morning." She held the page a moment longer. "Where's Brom?"

Brom was in his room with his boots off and his feet up on the sill and Wray's book open on his chest. He read the page standing, when Cael held it out. Halfway down he nodded, and at the bottom he nodded again. He gave it back, and delivered a verdict Cael suspected he had settled on weeks ago.

"Took her less time than I thought," said Brom.

"How long did you think?"

"Till spring." He sat down again and put his feet back on the sill. "I had barley on spring. With nobody, but I had it."

Lira went down the corridor by herself after that, to Karis's door. Cael did not follow her. He heard her knock, two knuckles, quiet, and heard the door open, and heard nothing after that for a long while, because Lira's voice when she meant something went low enough to stay in a doorway. Then the door shut, and Lira came back up the corridor alone, with her hands in her coat pockets and her face doing nothing at all.

"What did you say?"

"I told her she could stop asking properly." Lira went past him to her own door. "She's in. She can be as rude as the rest of us now. I said she'd need practice." At the door she turned. "And I told her about the binder."

He looked at her.

"That it's still yours," said Lira. "That being in doesn't change that, and nobody's going to pretend it does." She opened her door. "She said she knew. She said she promised in the stable never to ask, and she meant it the day she said it, and she means it now." Lira shook her head. "She's going to be very tiring, Cael. I can tell already."

She went in. He stood in the corridor with the soft-cornered page in his hand, and then he went and put it in the back pocket of the binder, with the papers that proved things. It did not prove anything that would ever stand up in front of a panel. He put it there anyway, behind Karis's consent, because the two of them belonged together, and because the back pocket was where he kept the things he meant to have read again when it mattered.

In the morning there were four bowls at the end of the scarred table, and Karis was sitting at the fourth, and nobody said anything about it at all.

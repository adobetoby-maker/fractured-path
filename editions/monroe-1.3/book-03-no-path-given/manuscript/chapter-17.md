# Chapter 17 — Three Audiences

He was the last one into assembly, and everybody in the room knew why.

The assembly room sat at the end of the lecture wing's ground floor, a long plain hall with benches in rows and a lectern at the front that nobody used. By the time Cael came through the door the benches were full and the instructor reading the day's notices had reached the second of them. He did not stop reading, and nobody said anything, but a ripple went along the back rows as Cael found a space at the end of the last bench. It was the small, collective adjustment of two hundred people who had just been reading about him on the board in the main hall and now had to pretend they had not.

He sat down, folded his hands on the binder and looked at the lectern.

The notices were ordinary: a change to the lamp oil allowance in the residence wing, a lecture on Current Path footwork moved from the third bell to the second, a reminder that the kitchen garden was not a shortcut. The instructor read each one in the same patient voice, and Cael listened to every word as if it mattered, because the alternative was to feel the backs of two hundred heads deciding not to turn around.

Lira was three rows ahead, and she did not turn either. But halfway through the notice about the garden she lifted one hand, as if to scratch her neck, held up two fingers where only he could see them, and put the hand down again.

Two fingers was a circuit sign. In Ardenmere it had meant *I see you, stay where you are, I'll come round after*, and he had not known until that moment how badly he had wanted somebody to make it.

Assembly ended on the fourth notice. The room came apart into noise at once, and he stayed where he was on the end of the bench while it emptied, because getting up in the middle of it would have meant walking through it.

Brom found him first. He said nothing about the petition; he sat down on the bench beside Cael with his elbows on his knees and looked at the empty lectern.

"Lira's gone to find out who saw Edran go in to the clerk. She thinks there was a witness to the filing. She's furious she didn't know yesterday."

"There's nothing to find out. He signed it."

"I know. I told her." Brom considered the lectern. "She said finding out was for her, not for you."

Cael almost laughed. It came out as a breath.

"You read it?"

"All of it."

"And?"

"It's right," said Cael. "Nearly all of it. He's got the provision right and the record right. He's got the children right." He turned the binder over on his knees. "He's only got one thing wrong, and it's the thing in the middle that the rest stands on."

Brom nodded slowly, the way he nodded when a fight went the way he had thought it would go and he had not wanted it to.

"Then it's a good petition."

"It's a very good petition."

"That's worse."

"I know."

They sat a while longer in the empty room. Then the second bell rang for lectures, and Cael went to the registry history block, because it was on his schedule and because walking into a lecture hall on time was one of the few things he was sure he could still do correctly that morning.

He did not take a single note. He sat in the back row with the binder open to a clean page and the pen in his hand while the thin woman at the front talked about the third revision of the station fees. Twice he caught himself reciting the conclusion on Edran's second page from memory. *Evidently a practitioner of some classification, which the faculty has elected not to disclose.* It was a good sentence, careful in all the right places, and he would have been proud to write it if it had been true.

At the end of the block a junior clerk was waiting outside the lecture hall door with her hands folded.

"The provost would like a word," she said. "Now, if you can."

She said it in the tone of somebody who had been told to make it sound less important than it was, and she did not quite manage it.

---

Naveth's door was shut.

Cael had climbed the worn stair to that office three times now, and every time before the door had stood open. He stopped on the top step and looked at the plain dark panel with its iron latch, and understood that this conversation had been made private before he was ever sent for.

He knocked.

"Come."

There were four chairs this time. Somebody had carried two more up the narrow stair and set them against the wall, and two of the four were already taken. Quenna sat by the window with her folder on her knee. Wray sat beside her in the brown coat buttoned to the throat, her hands resting flat on her thighs, as still as she had been on the panel at both sittings.

Naveth sat behind his desk. The three pages of the petition lay squared on the blotter in front of him, and Cael saw at once that they were not the originals, which were still downstairs on the board under their brass tacks where the rule said they must stay. These were copies in a clerk's hand. Naveth had had the petition copied before he read it, so that he could read it as often as he liked without once being seen standing in front of the board.

"Sit. This concerns you more than anyone, and I'd rather you heard all of it than the useful parts."

Cael sat in the fourth chair, the one facing the desk.

"You've read it."

"Yes."

"Then I won't read it to you." Naveth laid one long hand flat on the pages. "I'll tell you what I can and can't do with it, and then Quenna will tell you what she thinks I should do instead, and then Wray will tell us both whether it's possible. That's the order we agreed on the stair."

He raised one finger, as he had on the first morning with the alumni ledger.

"I can't dismiss it. It's clean. Every clause is in the right place and every date is correct, and the young man who wrote it has three years of standing behind him and the right to file. If I set it aside because it's inconvenient, I tell every student in this building that a petition is a thing the provost answers when he likes the question." He paused. "A good many of the students in this building came here because some other place answered their questions that way. A petition that gets posted and then answered is the only protection some of them have ever had. I won't spend it on you. You wouldn't thank me if I did."

"No," said Cael.

A second finger.

"I can't grant it either. Suspending a student's floor access pending review of his enrollment isn't a thing a provost can do alone; it needs a formal review of the enrollment itself." He looked at Cael over the copied pages. "And a formal review of your enrollment, a week after a sealed bundle of your assessment records went down the hill to the Compact, is the one thing in the world I least want to sign. Whatever that review wrote would be the first thing anyone read."

"And doing nothing," said Quenna, from the window.

"Doing nothing is a decision." Naveth did not look round at her; it had the sound of a line they had already said to each other once that morning. "It's the worst one I have. Silence from this office reads as weakness to one half of the building and favour to the other, and both halves would be partly right. I need an answer that's public, lawful and true, and I need it today."

He took his hand off the pages and leaned back, and the chair gave its worn creak.

"That's all I've got. Quenna has something better."

Quenna did not stand up. She turned a little on her chair so that she faced Cael directly and put her folder down on the floor beside her, which he had never once seen her do.

"The petition asks two things. Suspend him, or assess him in public under the same framework as students of full standing. Naveth can't do the first. So read the second one again."

Cael did not need to read it again; he had it by heart. *That the candidate be assessed under the same framework as students of full standing, in public, so that the academy's standings might continue to mean what every enrolled student had been told they meant.*

"He's asked for a public assessment," he said slowly.

"He has. In writing, signed and posted." Quenna's voice had taken on the flat, careful cadence of the sittings, each word set down on its own. "Your sittings have been closed: formal, recorded and closed. That was right for the first two. It isn't right now, because now the closed door is the thing being argued about. So we open it."

She held up one hand, palm out, and laid the plan on it one piece at a time.

"An exhibition bout, held in the formal yard, under standings rules, with the full faculty seats present and the school free to watch. Consistent, observable practice: the provision's own three words, and the bout goes into the record under them as this month's sitting. We don't grant his petition and we don't refuse it." She closed the hand. "The petition asked a question. We let the whole school watch it answered." She opened the hand again, as if there were one more piece on it. "It isn't a standings bout. It carries no rank and no tier, and it can't move him or you a single line on the board. A demonstration assessment says one of two things, satisfied or not, and that is all it will say. But it will say it in front of the whole school."

"Against Edran," said Naveth. It was not really a question; he had heard this on the stair.

"Against Edran. He filed it. If anyone is going to find out, it should be the boy who asked."

Cael sat with it.

He did what he always did with a plan that was not his own, which was to walk around it looking for the place where it would break, the way he walked around an opponent in the first exchange. He found the place at once, because nobody had hidden it.

"If I lose, his petition is answered the way he wanted. I'll have shown the whole school that he was right."

"Yes."

"Not partly. In front of everybody. In the record."

"Yes." Quenna did not soften it, and she did not hurry past it. "If you lose, the record will say that the candidate under the demonstration provision was assessed in public against a student of full standing, an Iron Rank Two, and was beaten, and the petition will stand on that page for as long as the page exists. I would not be proposing this if I thought you would lose. But I want you to walk into it with the stakes named, not with the stakes I'd like them to be."

She held his eyes.

"I've watched Edran train since he came here at fifteen. He has a seam, as everyone does. The question isn't whether it's there. What I don't know is whether you'll find it before he's said everything he came to say."

Cael looked down at his hands. He thought of the board in the main hall with its three pages pinned in a row, and of Oona's face working through *Then he's asking the wrong question*. He remembered his own answer to her, that it was a fair question with one fact wrong in the middle of it. A fair question deserved to be answered where it had been asked. Edran had asked in the yard, and then in ink, and answering him now in a closed hall with three people and a slate would only be one more door shut in his face.

"Schedule it."

Wray had not spoken. She spoke now, without moving her hands from her thighs, in the voice she used to read a measurement off a gauge.

"He's the best I have on the Edge floor, better than his rank. He will press early, because there will be a crowd, and he always presses with a crowd. And he learns inside a bout. Most people learn between bouts; he changes his timing mid-exchange when something fails." She looked at Cael. "Whatever you find on him, find it twice. He won't leave it lying where it was."

"I'll find it twice."

"Then I'll assess it." She turned her head toward Quenna. "Standings rules. Exchanges called on my count. A touch is a touch, a yield is a yield, the ring is the ring. No allowance either way for the category."

"No allowance," Quenna agreed.

Naveth reached for a blank posting form from the drawer of his desk, then stopped with it half out and held up his other hand.

"Before that, one thing. I want it said here, with the door shut."

He looked at Cael as he had on the first morning, with the joiner's look that decided whether a beam would carry a floor.

"You understand what Quenna has turned this into. Eight days from now most of this school will be round that ring watching you, and whatever you show them goes into the assessment record with two hundred witnesses to it. Lose to Edran and we're in difficulty." He paused. "If you beat him by too much, I'm not sure the problem we'd have instead is any smaller."

Cael said nothing. He had been thinking the same thing since the word *public*, and he had been waiting to see whether anyone else would say it aloud.

"I'm not telling you how to fight. I wouldn't know how. I'm telling you that this fight has three audiences. The students on the benches, who want to know whether the standings still mean anything. The panel, who will write down what they see. And a file, which will be read some day by people who were never in the yard and don't care how the morning felt." He folded the posting form flat on the blotter. "Only one of the three will be standing across the chalk from you. Don't fight for that one alone."

"I know."

Then, because the man had shut his door to say it and had earned more than two words, Cael said the rest.

"It's what I've been doing since Ardenmere. Every bout on the circuit, I had to win in front of people without showing them more than they'd already seen. Winning at a chosen size." He looked at the copied petition on the blotter. "I've forgotten how to win any other way."

Naveth looked at him for a long moment, and something moved in the old weathered face that Cael could not read. Then he uncapped his pen.

"Winning at a chosen size," he repeated, writing nothing, as if testing the words. "That tells me more about you than your intake file did." He turned to Quenna. "The first bell, eight days from today, in the formal yard. Write me the wording and I'll sign it before the fifth bell."

Quenna was already writing.

---

The answer went up beside the petition before the fifth bell, as Naveth had promised, on a single sheet in Quenna's hand with his signature beneath, pinned with the same brass tacks.

*Answer to the Petition of Standing filed by Edran, Glass Path, Iron Rank Two. The petition's alternative request is granted in the following form. The candidate enrolled under the demonstration provision will be assessed in public, in the formal yard, at the first bell on the eighth day from this posting, in an exhibition bout under standings rules against the petitioner. The bout will be logged as the candidate's demonstration assessment for the month, under the standard of the provision. The faculty seats will sit. The academy may attend.*

That was all of it. It was shorter than any one page of the petition.

Cael saw the crowd in front of the board from the far end of the main hall at the change of bells, thicker and noisier than the morning's, and he did not go to it. He stood by the archive passage with the binder against his chest and watched the reading change as it moved through the crowd from back to front. In the morning the hall had been full of the low, careful noise of a verdict being handed round in pieces. At the front of the crowd now, where people had finished, it had already become something louder and quicker, with laughter in it here and there. At the back two second-years were arguing and one of them was holding up fingers.

Gerda came out of the crowd on its near side, tall and narrow and straight, with her patched coat buttoned and her slip for the floor already in her hand. She saw him by the passage, changed course, and stopped two paces off.

"Answered."

"Yes."

"The same day it was posted." She looked back at the board, at the single sheet beside the three. "That's the good kind. They didn't put it in a drawer and let it go cold. They didn't refuse it in a sentence. They gave him what he asked for, in a shape they could live with." She looked at him again. "Now it's yours to answer properly."

"You've seen one answered badly."

Gerda considered him for a moment, as if deciding how much of a true answer could be spared.

"I've seen one answered correctly. That isn't always the same thing."

She went on toward the floor without another word, checking the clock above the door as she passed it.

Edran was not at the board, and Cael had not expected him to be. He had already been once, early, before anybody else, to read his own petition pinned up in his own hand, and he would not go and stand in front of it now with a crowd watching him read the answer. But at the change of the sixth bell, crossing the formal yard, Cael saw him on the far side by the standings board, where his name now sat second, in the same clerk's hand at the same size, one line below Karis's.

He was not looking at the standings. He was looking across the yard at the main hall door, where a few students were still coming out from the board in twos and threes, talking with their hands, and then his eyes moved and found Cael. He did not come over. He lifted his chin very slightly, a single small movement that meant, as plainly as if he had said it across the chalk, *I've read it. Accepted.*

Cael lifted his own in return.

Edran turned away and went through the north gate toward the open floor, and Cael stood in the yard and understood that the conversation he had told Oona he would have to have was now a great deal harder. He had meant to go to Edran and tell him the one wrong thing on the second page. Now the yard stood between them, and the answer on the board, and eight days, and anything he said before the first bell on the eighth would look like what it was not. He thought about that all the way to supper.

---

Lira had read the answer by the time he reached the stable. He could tell from the far end of the long room by the way she sat, very upright at the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, with her bread untouched in front of her.

"They've put you in a ring," she said, before he had sat down. "For the whole school to stand round and look at."

"Yes."

"Two hundred people. On a calendar. With a date." She pushed the bread away. "Ever since Ardenmere I've watched people look at you as if you were something on a table to be weighed, and now they've sold tickets."

"Nobody's selling tickets."

"Somebody will." She looked at him with her jaw set, the small muscle working at the hinge of it. "Wasn't there anything else? Anything at all, that didn't mean standing you up in front of everybody?"

Brom set down his spoon.

"They've put him in a ring with rules. Wray's count. A touch is a touch. That isn't a market square, and it isn't a back room with a man asking careful questions. It's the floor." He looked at Lira steadily. "You walked a Force Path off the edge of that same ring in the second week because you wanted twenty people to see it. You said so on the wall."

Lira opened her mouth, and shut it.

"That was different."

"It was the same. Except he's got two hundred, and you only got twenty."

For a moment Cael thought she was going to throw the bread at him. Then something in her shoulders gave, very slightly, and she pulled the bread back toward her and tore it in half.

"I hate it when you do that."

"I know."

"I hate it more when you're right." She looked at Cael across the table, and the anger had gone out of her face, and what was left under it was the thing the anger had been standing in front of. "All right. It's a ring. I know rings." She lowered her voice, though the two second-years playing tiles by the fire were nowhere near. "What are you going to show them?"

"The framework."

"And?"

"Nothing else."

"Not the read?"

"Not even the read." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn on the table. "The Iron-adjacent read is on the record twice already, so it's safe. But a fight won with one fragment is smaller on paper than a fight won with two."

Lira considered him in silence, and then gave him the single nod she gave an opponent who had done something she would have done herself.

"Good." She pushed the larger half of the bread across to him. "Eat. You've got eight days of being looked at, and you can't do it hungry."

---

He went anyway, the next morning, because he had told Oona he would, and because a thing being harder had never once in his life been the same as a thing being wrong.

He found Edran where Brom had said he would be, on the open floor before the first bell, alone, drilling in the far corner by the high windows. Nobody else was in the hall. The light came in grey and level through the glass and lay along the boards while Edran moved through it in short, exact sequences, shell after shell forming along his forearms and bursting outward in a glitter that hung for a heartbeat in the grey air before it was gone.

Cael stopped at the rail and waited.

Edran finished the sequence he was in, all the way to the end, without hurrying it. Then he let his arms drop and turned.

"You're early for the rail."

"I didn't come for the rail."

Edran looked at him, and at the empty hall, and back. He did not come any closer; he stood in the middle of the floor with chalk on his hands and his breathing settling, and waited.

"There's one thing in your petition that's wrong," said Cael. "On the second page. I wanted you to hear it from me, before the eighth day, so that you'd know I wasn't hiding behind the bout."

Edran did not move.

"Don't," he said.

It was quiet and quite firm, and it stopped Cael where he stood.

"I mean it. Don't tell me." Edran wiped his palms down the front of his training shirt, slowly, one and then the other. "If you tell me now, I'll have your word for it. And I'll probably believe you. You don't seem to lie much, and I've watched you a while." He looked at the floor between them, at the chalk and the grey light. "Then I'll go into that yard knowing something nobody earned in front of anybody. One more thing on the board that didn't come from the board."

"It isn't about the board."

"Everything I've asked has been about the board." Edran's voice did not rise. "I didn't file it to find out what you are. I filed it to find out whether the framework can still tell. If it can't, I'd rather know that from the floor than from you."

Cael stood at the rail with the thing he had come to say still in his mouth, and found that he could not argue with one word of it.

"All right."

Edran nodded once.

"For what it's worth," said Cael, "it was a fair question. Both times."

Something passed over Edran's face, not quite surprise and not quite pleasure, and it was gone before Cael could name it.

"I know it was. I'm still not sorry I asked it in ink." He turned back toward the high windows and set his feet. "Eight days. Go and watch somebody else. I'll be here every morning, and you're allowed the rail, and I won't change anything for you." He paused with his hands half raised. "I'd think less of you if you didn't come."

He began the next sequence. Cael watched the first shell form and burst. Then he did what he had been told and went to watch somebody else, because the first bell was ringing and because he understood that he had just been given permission to study the man for six days, by the man himself, in daylight, in the open.

Oona was at the board when he came back through the main hall, in front of the answer, with her slate under her arm. She had been waiting for him, and she did not pretend otherwise.

"Did you tell him?"

"I tried."

She turned round. "What happened?"

"He told me not to." Cael looked at the four sheets on the board, the three in Edran's hand and the one in Quenna's. "He said if I told him, he'd have my word for it, and he didn't want my word. He wanted the floor to tell him. In front of everybody. The way he asked."

Oona thought about that for a long time, with the whole of her face, while students went past behind them toward the second bell.

"Then he's not asking the wrong question anymore."

"No?"

"No. Before, he was asking what you are. That's the one he had wrong." She looked at the answer on the board, Quenna's short square paragraph. "Now he's asking if the board can tell. He's allowed to ask that. That's his." She frowned, working it through. "And you can't answer that one with words anyway. It's not a words one."

He looked down at her. She was thirteen and had been at Greyvane for six weeks, and she had just said in four sentences the thing it had taken him a whole night to understand.

"No. It's not a words one."

"So you have to win," said Oona, as if that settled it. "Or lose. Either one answers it." She tucked her slate under her arm and considered him. "I'd rather you won."

"So would I."

"Then you should," she said, quite seriously, and went off toward the lecture wing.

That night he wrote the whole of it down under the four headings, because the four headings were where a fight began.

*Exhibition. Eighth day from posting. Formal yard. Standings rules, Wray's count. Edran, Glass, Iron Rank Two, second on the standings, first for most of three years. Function: answer the petition in public, with the Wind-adjacent framework and nothing else. Benefit: if it goes well, the question gets answered where it was asked. Cost, if I lose: the petition stands, in the record, for as long as the record does. Cost, if I win too well: whatever the record says I did, somebody will read it who wasn't there.*

He looked at that last line for some time.

*Three audiences. Only one across the chalk.*

Then, beneath it, on its own line, because he wanted it where he would see it every night for a week:

*He told me not to tell him. He wants the floor to say it. I owe him the floor.*

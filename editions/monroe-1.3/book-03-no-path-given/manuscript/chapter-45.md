# Chapter 45 — The Wrong Road

The third day went entirely on a wrong turn, and he took it before he took the right one.

The obvious road was the one anybody would have taken, and he took it. He went straight at the demonstration provision: how it had been drafted, how it had been amended, and who had been enrolled under it. If the challenge was that he had been fitted into a category not built for him, then surely the place to fight was the category, and the place to start was the moment it was made. He asked Prynn for the provision's history at the first bell, and she looked at him over her spectacles a moment longer than she needed to and then went and got it without a word, which he understood only later was the most she would ever do to warn a reader off a shelf.

It was all there, in the order it had grown. The provision had been revised three times, and each revision sat in its fascicle with its date and its reasons attached in the flat language of the drafting clerks. He read the newest first, out of habit, and then made himself go back and read them in order, as Prynn had told him to. When he reached the bottom of the oldest box he found the first draft, written in a hand so old that the letters leaned against each other like tired men.

It had been written as a kindness.

It held him still, there in the cold. In those days the guilds had sent their families travelling, carters and drovers and pedlars on a guild licence, and their children came to fourteen on the road, weeks from any station. They arrived at the gates of the academies untested and unplaced and turned away, because a chartered academy could not take a child nobody had tested. Somebody had minded. Somebody had sat at a desk with a pen and written a few lines so that such a child could come in under watching, and be taught, and be kept warm, until the family could get the child to a station and find out what the child was. That was the whole of what it was for. Every revision after it had widened who it covered by a little, a late Kindler here, a disputed assessment there. What it was for had come through all of them untouched.

He thought of Oona on the bench in the grey building, with her mother's hand on one shoulder and his on the other, trembling very finely under the clean grey cloth. He thought of her coming out through the far door with the card held flat against her slate, and her face coming loose over her mother's shoulder. She had come up the hill to Greyvane untested from a guild family, and been taught, and been kept warm, and gone down to the station when the time came and been found to be who she had decided to be. Whoever had written the first draft in that leaning hand had written it for her. They had never imagined him at all.

He walked the road for eleven hours. He traced every revision and every enrollment the digests recorded under it, every late Kindler and disputed child and clerical error the provision had ever sheltered. He was looking for one single case, anywhere, of somebody already Kindled and classified who had been carried by it and allowed to stay. There was none, and the road went nowhere. Read it as kindly as you liked, it was written for the untested. He had been tested. Nothing in its history could change what its words said, or what its first drafter had meant them to say. Coss had walked this same road weeks ago, he was certain of it. Coss had found the box in the basement, or wherever the regional office kept its copy, and had read the leaning hand, and had walked all the way to the end. Coss would be standing there waiting for him, in the assembly room, with the first draft in his document case.

At the eighth bell he drew one long diagonal line through eleven hours of notes and wrote under it, in the square hand:

*Stop studying my own position. Study his. What is his authority standing on?*

He sat back and looked at the line. It was the same correction Lira had driven into him in a cold yard in Ardenmere, a lifetime ago, in other words: *quit watching your own feet.* He had spent a whole day watching his own feet.

Prynn came down off her stool and took the provision's boxes away without being asked, and put them back on their shelf, and came back with a cup of tea, and set it down at his elbow.

"That road," she said, "goes nowhere."

"You knew."

"I've known for forty years. I've certified forty copies of it, and read every one of them aloud." She looked at the crossed-out pages. "I'd have told you if you'd asked. You didn't ask. You wanted to walk it." She went back to her stool. "Most people have to. It's a good road. It's only the wrong one."

---

On the fourth morning he took out the tree he had drawn for Prynn and asked it a different question.

The first time he had asked what each part said; now he went down it asking what each part stood on.

Enforcement stood on the obligations. Every voidance and sanction and removal in it, every power that had walked a fourteen-year-old out of Hesk's workshop in Denvash in one courier's morning, fell on people who owed the obligations. The obligations stood on classification, falling on you once the system had given you a name, and classification stood on the schedule, the long working list of every Path and tier and provisional category the Compact had ever put its formal name to. And the schedule stood on the charter, which was wide and high-minded and full of *the stewardship of practice*, and which bound nobody to anything you could measure.

So it all came down to the top. Push hard enough on the place where the top was held up, and if the top would not bear it, nothing under it would stand either, however long it had stood with nobody leaning on it.

He went back into the charter to find where it said what classification was.

It was in the first article after the preamble, the definitions clause, and he read past it twice. He had been reading for something clever and hidden, and this was neither; it was so plain that his eyes slid off it.

*Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.*

He read it a third time, aloud and very quietly, because Prynn had taught him that ears were more honest than eyes.

*Shall mean.* Not *shall include*. Not *shall be taken broadly as*, or any of the soft wide words the middle centuries had loved. The charter was not describing classification but defining it, shutting it, and leaving the schedule as the one way in. No category could reach the Compact's authority except through the schedule; the Compact's authority could reach nobody the schedule did not name. *For all purposes.* Every *over classified practitioners* he had read in two nights by lamplight ran back through this one sentence to the list. There was nowhere else for it to run.

Nobody had ever gone at the schedule, and so it had no scars. It was no wall; it was the ground under the wall. Nobody hits the ground.

---

He gave the schedule the next two days and most of two nights, and twice in the evenings Prynn came down and moved the lamp to keep the light on his page.

He had spent two years reading things that did not want to be read, and he read this harder than any of them. Every Path family. Every tier. All the provisional categories, too, that the Compact had tried on before it settled: Dual-Registered, Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, and half a dozen more that came and went as the Compact's idea of a Path kept changing under it, slowly and clumsily. He took the amendments in their order, as Prynn had taught him, and watched categories arrive in one generation's confidence and get struck out in another's embarrassment. The struck ones stayed on the page, ruled through but readable, because the code kept its mistakes as carefully as its rules. Everything it had ever recognized had a name, and the name was written in the schedule, in the flat clerk's language that turns a real thing into a lawful one.

He looked for the word they had written next to his name in Denvash.

It was not there.

Then he went through it again, more slowly, in case some subsection or appendix had got past him, or some cross-reference hidden in the margin of an amendment. After that he made a list of every place in the code where a category could lawfully be kept. There was the main enumeration, and the provisional annex, and the appendix of disputed standings. There were the amendment schedules, one for every revision, and the definitions cross-index at the back of the charter. He went through them one at a time, as he would go through every answer an opponent might have, and crossed each off, until nothing was left to guess at, only what remained. It had not got past him.

On the sixth night he read the whole schedule a third time, by lamplight, from the first page to the last, with Prynn at the high desk silently refilling his cup at an hour when no duty on earth required her to still be in the building. The third reading said what the second had. Nowhere in the schedule, in all its generations of piled-up text, did the word appear. Not once. No footnote, no entry struck out and kept in the record of its own repeal, and not as something once proposed and voted down. The schedule kept its refusals as well. He had come across three: categories somebody had argued for and lost, each refusal written up with the same clerical care as everything else.

His word was not even among the rejected ones.

The code had never held it. Only the Arbiter's instrument did: it wrote the word out on the morning of a Kindling, a flag of its own making. Generation after generation of enforcement had simply treated that flag as if it were a classification, because it looked like one and sat where one sat on the form, and because nobody in all that time, so far as he could tell, had ever thought to go and look.

For a long while he sat still with it and did not write anything.

He did not trust it, because a thing that size never deserved trust the first time you saw it, and he had lost his worst bouts by throwing himself at a seam before he had made sure it was really there. There had to be a page he had missed, in this building or in a records hall three districts away: an amendment, an annex, an order somewhere that made the flag lawful. Nothing ran for centuries on nothing.

But things did, and he had seen them do it. The Ardenmere circuit had run for generations on rules nobody had ever written into any charter. A market interview had been held over him on an authority that nobody in the square could have named. And at fourteen his own life had been turned off its road by one morning's procedure that everybody in the room called law, because everybody in every room always had. Custom in law's coat was nothing strange where he came from; so far as he could see, most of the world wore it.

He wrote it down.

---

His tea had gone cold, and he did not notice until Prynn was standing at his elbow with the pot, looking at the full cup, and then at him, and then, because she could not help it, at the page.

She did not pour, but put the pot down on the far corner of the table, well away from the bindings, and read the page upside down while he watched her read it.

It held the four parts of the tree, and the definitions clause copied out in full beside them, and under that was his list of every place a category could lawfully be kept, each one with a tick. And at the very bottom, alone, circled, with no line joining it to anything above, was the word from Denvash.

"Turn it round," said Prynn.

He turned it round, and she came down the side of the table and stood over it, and her lips moved on the definitions clause, once and then again, the way they moved when she was matching a citation to the shelf where she remembered it living.

"Don't tell me," said Prynn. "Let me read what you wrote. If it needs you standing next to it to make sense, it won't stand up in that room."

So he said nothing, and she read it to him off the page, slowly, in the voice she used to certify a copy, with one finger moving down his lines.

"*Classification means designation under the schedule. For all purposes. Shall mean, not includes.*" The finger moved. "*The schedule names everything the Compact has ever reached for. Being named there is the only way the code lets it reach.*" Down again, across his ticks. "*Not in the list. Not in the annex. Not in the appendix, not in any amendment. Not struck. Not refused.*" She stopped at the circle. "*What the instrument writes. Then generations of treating it as though it were listed.*" And last, in the corner, where he had written it smallest: "*Custom isn't code.*"

She straightened and stood a long time with her hand flat on the table beside his page.

"Sixty years I've kept these shelves," said Prynn. "You're the first person to read the code the way it's written instead of the way it's enforced."

He did not know what to do with that, so he did nothing and only looked at her.

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation." She took the cold cup to her desk, poured it into the slop bowl, filled it, and brought it back to the clear place at the corner of the table. Then, with the empty tea tin from beside it in her hand, she turned toward her desk, so that she was talking to him half over her shoulder. "You'll decide that yourself, by what you do next. People who turn up a seam like that usually go straight out and hire somebody to argue it. Why haven't you?"

He answered her back, because it was easier than answering her face.

"Because nobody's ever asked me," he said. "Not about anything. A station in Denvash decided what I was. A courier came with a notice. A man in a borrowed office in Ardenmere, and somebody above him I've never seen, signing things. Every word anybody's ever said about what I am was said over my head." He looked at the circled word. "If I hire somebody to stand up and say this, then even the argument that nobody may speak for me is made by somebody else. I've read it all now. So I get to say it myself."

Prynn stopped, with the tin in her hand, and did not turn round.

"That's the true one," she said. "Now give me the clever one. You'll want it for anybody who asks you in a corridor."

"I couldn't find anyone to take it if I tried," said Cael. "Not in three districts. And whoever did, the magistrate would hear a hired man being clever. It would be his trick. It has to be mine, or it isn't a reading."

"Keep both," said Prynn. "Give the clever one to people who want a reason. Keep the true one for the room." She went on to the high desk with the tin. "Fourth shelf, second case," she said, without looking back. "The enforcement digests. Every case they've ever brought under those chapters." She set the tin down. "If it's going to be your own voice, you'd better know every case they'll throw back at it."

He did not go to the fourth shelf at once. He sat with the circled word in front of him and turned back two pages in the binder, to the small box he had drawn on the night of the council.

*He doesn't need authority over you. He has it over me.*

The definitions clause did nothing about that. It said what classification was and nothing about what the Compact could do to an academy that held its charter from the Compact. If the magistrate agreed with every word he meant to say about the schedule, Coss could still stand up afterward and say: very well, the boy is undefined; the academy that enrolled him is not. He could hear Coss saying it, in that patient voice, without any heat at all.

He did not know the answer, or yet whether there was one.

He wrote under the box, small: *The clause covers me. Does anything cover Greyvane? Find out before he asks.* He left the box as it was and went to the fourth shelf.

---

Karis came to the scarred table late at supper on the sixth day, before he went back down to the archive for the night, with dust to the elbows of her coat and a smear of something grey along one cheekbone that she had plainly not noticed. She sat down at the fourth bowl and ate her barley in silence, and Lira slid half a heel of bread down the table to her without a word.

"I've been on the founding shelves for five days," Karis said at last, to her bowl. "I'll tell you what I've found, because it's fair you should know, and then I'll tell you what I haven't."

"Go on," said Lira.

"I've found how they wrote." She set her spoon down. "Before the code was put in the order it's in now, the registers weren't boxes. They were sentences. An assessor at a waystation would write a whole paragraph about a person: what they'd seen, what they thought, what they couldn't decide. Some of them argued with themselves on the page. Some of them crossed things out and wrote *I was wrong about this* in the margin a year later." She looked at Cael. "Nobody does that now. Now there's a box, and you're either in it or you're a flag."

"And what haven't you found?"

"Anybody like you." Karis picked up the bread. "Not yet. I've read four hundred entries and every one of them is somebody the old words could hold. Ash and Stone and Current. People nobody had any trouble naming." She tore the bread in half. "That isn't a finding. It's the shape of a shelf I haven't finished. There are a great many shelves."

"The hearing can't use any of it," said Cael, and heard himself say it, and stopped.

Karis looked at him across the table with something very like amusement.

"You've said that four times this week," she said. "I've counted. I think you say it to remind yourself that your search is the one with a date on it." She ate a piece of the bread. "Mine hasn't got a date. That's why I can afford to be thorough. Eat your barley. Brom's watching it."

"I'm always watching it," said Brom. "It's never once let me down."

---

On the Tuesday of the twentieth week, the seventh day, Brom came to the archive door at the end of the fifth bell and stood there without coming in, because Prynn did not like people who came in only to fetch other people out.

"Wray's building something," he said, when Cael came to the door. "This afternoon. The sixth bell, on the defensive floor." He looked at the floor of the passage. "I'd like you to watch it. You read my wall before anybody but her did. I'd like somebody at the rail who can see where it is."

Cael went.

The defensive floor was the long hall beyond the training hall where the hardening students worked, with its own pairings board outside the door and its own rail down one side. Wray stood at the edge of the chalk in her brown coat, buttoned to the throat, with her slate on her hip. Four people stood round the ring at four marks, and Cael looked at them and understood before anybody said a word that this was not the three-striker drill. Hobb was at the north mark, broad and planted, with his hands loose. Edran was at the west with his ready pair already built on his forearms, the shell pale on the left and the edge on the right. Karis was at the south with her off hand still at her side. Lira was at the east, up on the balls of her feet, the only one of the four who was smiling.

Brom stood in the middle.

"A month ago," said Wray, to Brom and to nobody else, "I sent three people at you one at a time, each faster than the last, and you saw your wait every time, and you said the second half needed somebody faster than anybody here. You were right. There's nobody on this hill who can come at you at Silver speed. I can't fetch you a Silver." She tapped the slate. "So I'm going to build one. Out of four people who aren't."

Brom said nothing. He settled his feet.

"It isn't a queue," said Wray. "That's the difference. Last time each of them came at you, and you turned it, and then the next one came. Today the next one starts before you've finished with the last. Hobb comes first, with his weight, from the front. You take it and you redirect it, and your redirect has to go somewhere. It goes into Edran's line, because Edran's already coming from the west by the time you're turning Hobb, and he's bursting a shell on your guard as he arrives. Then Karis's channel is under your feet where you have to step, because you'll have to step to turn Edran, and it'll shove up at your shins as you do. And Lira finishes it, from whatever side you've left open." She looked at the four of them and then at him. "No one of them is faster than you can read. All four of them together arrive faster than you can turn. That's what Silver is, from the inside. Too much arriving, too close together, from too many places to finish one before the next."

"How fast?" said Brom.

"I'll start slow. I'll call the intervals with my hand." She raised one finger. "Each round, I take a little out of them. When I've taken out enough, we'll see where you stop."

She lowered the finger.

Hobb came. It was his honest walking weight, both forearms up, the push of a Stone fourth-year who could not be hurried and did not mean to be, and Brom met it with the Iron Skin coming up along his forearms and took it and gave and turned. Hobb's weight went past him, slow and sure, along the line Brom chose for it, and out toward the west, where Edran was already arriving. Edran's shell burst on Brom's guard in a spray of pale glitter. Brom took that too, turning, his feet moving to follow the turn. As his right foot came down a thin line of heat lit across the boards in exactly that place, from one of Karis's points to another. It shoved up at his shin like a stove door thrown open, and he rode it, smoking faintly at the boot, and set his feet. And Lira came in from the open east, light and quick, and her palm was a hand's width from his ribs when he turned her away.

"One," said Wray, and wrote, and raised her finger again.

They went round four times.

On the first round the intervals were long enough that Brom finished every turn before the next force touched him, and he was beautiful to watch, and Cael at the rail could see that he knew it. On the second round Wray took out perhaps a quarter of the time, and Brom finished Hobb and finished Edran, and was still finishing the channel's shove when Lira's palm landed flat on his right side below the ribs. It landed gently, at Wray's half force, but it landed.

"Touch," said Wray.

The third round was where Cael began to see it, and he saw it because he had been watching Brom's wait since the night of the null sessions. It was the small held instant between the read and the turn, the place Wray had shown with her own hand on the seventh strike. At the long intervals it did not matter, because the wait finished before anything else arrived. Now there was no room for it. Hobb's weight came, and Brom read it, and waited his instant, and turned it. In the instant while he waited, Edran was already a pace nearer than he had been the time before, and so the turn into Edran's line came a shade too late, and the burst came a shade too close, and the step onto Karis's channel came a shade too soon. Every lateness piled on the last, until by the time Lira came in, the whole of Brom's body was still busy finishing something else.

She landed. Brom took her palm on the ribs again, and stood, breathing hard, and did not move for a moment.

"There," he said. He did not say it to Wray. He said it to the floor. "I'm waiting there. Right there. After I've got it and before I send it. Every time."

"Yes," said Wray.

"I can see it." Brom looked at his own forearms, at the frost-sheen of the Iron Skin going dull. "I can see it coming at me before it comes. I know exactly where I'll be late." He looked up. "And I'm late anyway."

"Yes," said Wray. "Again."

The others had seen it too, and they were learning. Cael watched them learn, because watching people learn was the oldest thing he knew how to do. On the fourth round Karis laid her channel half a step further along the line Brom would have to take, so that its shove came up at the very moment Hobb's weight was still on his guard, two forces from two directions at once. Edran built his shell and burst it a beat early, before Brom's redirect had finished arriving, so that the burst met the turn halfway. And Lira did not wait for Brom to finish anything at all. She had found his instant on the third round, and on the fourth she came in at the start of his wait instead of at its end, so that her palm arrived in the exact space he left open while he held what he had read and before he sent it anywhere.

She landed before Brom had turned Edran.

"Four," said Wray, and wrote it, and lowered her slate. "That's enough."

Brom stood in the middle of the ring with his hands on his knees. There was a smear of pale Glass glitter along one cheekbone where Edran's burst had caught him, stinging but not cut. His boots were scorched along both toes. His right side would be one long bruise by morning where Lira had landed three times in the same place, at half force each time, which was still Lira. He breathed for a while, and nobody spoke, and the floor was very quiet except for the hiss of the last of Karis's channel going out on the boards.

Then he straightened up.

"Seen," he said. "Every time. At the speed that matters." He looked at Wray. "Not closed."

"No," said Wray. "Not closed. I can make you see it at the speed it matters. You can see it now better than anybody I've taught in twenty years. I can't make you fast enough to close it." She did not soften the next part, because she never softened anything, and Cael understood that this was its own kind of respect. "Nobody on this hill can. I can build you an imitation of Silver for four rounds out of four students who'd rather be eating their supper. To close it you'd need somebody who's Silver all day long, every day, for a year. That isn't here." She tucked the slate under her arm. "I'll keep looking. I said I would. I don't say that twice."

She went back to the chalk at the far end of the floor. The four who had made the imitation came off their marks one at a time, Hobb first, nodding once at Brom as he passed, as one workman nods to another, and then Edran, who stopped.

"You turned me cleaner than anybody's ever turned me," said Edran. "Round one. I wanted you to know."

"Round one was slow," said Brom.

"Round one was slow," Edran agreed, "and it was still the cleanest." He went out.

Karis came over to the rail where Cael was standing, and Brom came with her, slowly, wiping the glitter off his face with the back of his wrist.

"I laid the fourth one where I'd want to be stopped," said Karis. "If I'd been you. I'm sorry about your boots."

"They're Velmere boots," said Brom. "They've had worse."

Lira came last. She stood in front of Brom with her hands on her hips and looked at the side of him where she had landed, and her face was not pleased with itself.

"I found it on the third," she said. "The wait. I could hit it every time after that, the instant it opened." She was quiet a moment. "I didn't like hitting it. It's like hitting somebody who's holding a door for you."

"Then you'd better keep hitting it," said Brom, "or I'll never learn where the door is." He took his number sheet out of his coat and unfolded it against the rail, under the old column of fetch numbers and the second column he had begun on Wray's floor a month before, and wrote in his square capitals while they watched.

*GAUNTLET. FOUR STRIKERS, NO QUEUE. SEEN EVERY TIME AT THE SPEED THAT MATTERS. NOT CLOSED. LIRA IN AT THE LAST, THREE OF FOUR. — B.*

He looked at it, folded the sheet, and put it back in his coat.

Cael had been thinking, all through the fourth round, about Lira's chalk ring in the frozen yard and the night his number had stopped at four. "On the lattice," he said slowly, "Lira told me to pick faster. I was deciding in the space where I should have been moving. Is it that?"

Brom thought about it honestly.

"No," he said. "Yours was a choosing. You had two ways and you couldn't pick, and picking faster fixed it, as near as anything does." He flexed his right hand, slowly, and watched the dull sheen go out of the skin. "Mine isn't a choosing. I know which way. I know where it's going before it's arrived. Mine's a waiting. Something in me has to finish holding it before it'll let me send it on." He looked at the floor. "You can't hurry a wait by deciding to. That's the difference. That's why it's a wall and not a habit."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"It's an honest wall," said Brom at last. "I'll say that for it. I can put my hand on it now. That's more than most people get." He picked up his coat off the rail. "Come on. You're due on a floor at dusk, and I'm due to throw peas at you, and I'd like my supper before either."

# Chapter 30 — Real, Unexplained, Keep

Lira came to Brom's room for the fast catch on the Wednesday night of the eleventh week. She had read the page about the whole push. She had told him at breakfast, cutting his porridge into squares, that the next time he did something new to his chest she was going to be in the room for it with a cold cloth and a very bad temper.

She sat on Brom's bed with her back against the wall and her boots on Brom's blanket, which Brom permitted from nobody else, and a folded cloth from the kitchen pump in her lap, dripping slightly. She did not speak. She had said she would not, and Lira kept her word about speaking less often than she kept it about anything else, so that when she did keep it, it meant something.

"It's different," Cael said, kneeling on the boards with his left forearm braced across his chest. "Everything so far has been a push. A push arrives slow. It comes into the bone and sits there and I've got time to choose the road while it's sitting. A strike doesn't sit. It arrives and it's gone."

"So you can't wait for it," said Brom.

"So I can't wait for it. I have to choose the road before it lands. Before I know the size, or the line, or anything." He flexed the left hand. The elbow the whole push had found was nearly quiet. "Not much. A quarter. If I can catch a quarter of a strike and send it home, that's a thing I've never done."

"How fast?"

"Fast. Not your fastest. Fast enough that it's a strike and not a lean."

Brom considered him with his chalked hands on his knees. Then he looked across at Lira on the bed, and Lira looked back at him and said nothing, with great force, and Brom looked back at Cael.

"Quarter," he said. "Fast. I'll call it."

He brought up the Iron Skin along his right palm, the faint dull sheen coming over the skin, and drew the hand back a little, and struck.

It was not his fastest. It was a short flat palm strike at the braced forearm, with weight behind it and speed in it, the kind of strike Brom used on the floor when he meant a thing to land. Cael had chosen the road before Brom's hand moved, a quarter's width out and back along the line it would come in, and he held it, and the strike arrived. It was in the bone and gone again before the fragment had finished waking, and the fragment reached for the place where it had been and found the bruise and nothing else.

"Nothing," said Cael.

"Nothing. Again."

Seven times it was nothing. The fragment was too slow for a strike; it woke for force that was already his, and a strike was never his for long enough. On the eighth he did something he had not known he could do. He did not wait for the fragment to wake when the force arrived. He woke it first, before Brom moved, the gathering low in his chest begun and held half-ready like a breath drawn and not let out, with the road laid open in front of it, so that when the strike landed the thing in him was already reaching. It closed on the strike in the instant it was in the bone, a quarter of it, and sent it home.

The quarter went back into Brom's palm with a crack like a dropped plate.

And the bill did not wait for anything.

With a push it had always come a moment afterward, rising through the shoulder and across the breastbone in its band of heat. This came all at once, at the same instant as the catch, as if it had been waiting behind the strike. A white flash went across the inside of his eyes. His knees were not under him; he did not remember them going. He was on his side on Brom's boards with his cheek against the wood, and he could not breathe, and he could not feel his left hand at all, and somewhere a long way off Lira was saying his name in a voice he had not heard her use since a night on the Ardenmere road.

He came back slowly. The cloth was on his breastbone, cold and wet through his shirt. Lira was kneeling over him with one hand flat on top of it, pressing, not hard, and her face was very close and very white. Brom was on his other side holding his left wrist in both hands, chafing it.

"Breathe," said Lira. "Breathe, you idiot. I don't care if it hurts. Breathe."

He breathed. It hurt a great deal. He breathed again.

"How long?" he managed.

"Not long. Long enough." She did not take her hand off the cloth. "Long enough that I've got something to say to you, and I'm going to say it tomorrow."

"Say it now."

"Not while my hands are doing this." She held up the one that was not on the cloth, and it was shaking.

Brom was still chafing the wrist. "Can you feel that?"

Cael tried. The hand was a long way off, numb and heavy, like a hand that had been slept on. Then, slowly, the pins came into it, hot and prickling, from the wrist toward the fingertips.

"Coming back."

"Good." Brom let go of the wrist and sat back on his heels and looked at his own right palm. There was a red mark across it where the quarter had come home, and he looked at it with a kind of grim respect. "That was a quarter of a strike. That was only a quarter."

"Only a quarter," said Cael, from the floor.

Lira took her hand off the cloth at last and sat back against the bed, and put both hands over her face, and stayed like that for a moment. Then she took them away.

"Write it down," she said. "Now, before you sleep. All of it. The part where you were on the floor too, not just the catch. I want it in your hand, so you can't make it smaller tomorrow."

He wrote it lying on Brom's bed with the binder held above his face, because sitting up was not yet a thing he could do.

*Compression-adjacent. Brom's room, L. present. First fast catch: a quarter of a quick palm strike, caught and returned, on the eighth try. Done by waking the fragment before the strike, held half-ready with the road laid open. Waiting for the force, the way I do with a push, doesn't work on a strike; it's gone before the fragment wakes. Cost: all of it at once, no delay. A white flash, legs gone, on the floor. Couldn't breathe for long enough that Lira used my name the way she did on the road. Left hand numb, then pins, for most of an hour. Integration: partial. A quarter of a strike, once.*

He read it back, and then he added the line Lira had asked for.

*It is not cheaper because it's smaller. A quarter fast cost more than the whole slow. Speed is its own price.*

Lira read it over his shoulder, upside down, from where she sat on the floor against the bed. She did not say anything. She took the binder out of his hands, closed it, and put it on Brom's shelf in the neat row beside Brom's crest-up tag. Then she put her hand on his forehead for a moment, and took it away.

"Tomorrow," she said. "I'll say it tomorrow."

She said it the next morning on the stair, low and fast and sharp, with one hand flat on the rail.

"I counted. On Brom's floor, with my hand on a wet cloth. I counted six breaths, and you weren't taking any of them." She did not look at him. "I sat there and thought, so this is how I find out. Not in a ring, with a crowd and a reason. On a bedroom floor, because you wanted to know if a thing could be done faster." Her hand tightened on the rail. "I walked over that hill with you and Brom. I'm not doing the rest of it with one of you short because you were curious on a Wednesday. You write *choose the size first* in that binder every week. Choose the size of what you're willing to lose, too. And tell me before, not after."

"Before," he said. "Not after."

At the end of it she put her forehead against his shoulder for the length of one breath. Then she went down to breakfast ahead of him and stole his bread.

The Thursday session in section four was a bad one, and Quenna saw it.

He had known she would. His recovery between passes at the working speed had gone from three counts to five. The framework, which had not minded the ribs, minded this. The quarter of a breath of lead that Karis had measured since the first week held, but only just, and twice in the hour he felt it thin. Karis timed it and wrote it down without comment and read it back: *Recovery slower by two counts. Lead held, narrowly. Cause not stated by subject.* He said nothing to that. The cause was a strike in Brom's room, and it belonged to neither of the two arrangements and to nobody's ledger.

Quenna did not write anything at all for most of the hour. She stood at the chalk with her slate against her hip and watched him. Twice, when he came back to his mark breathing harder than the work had any right to make him, he felt her eyes on him with the weight he had felt on his first afternoon in the intake room, when she had been looking for words for him and not finding them.

At the end she signed the strip and tore it off and held it out, and did not let go of it at once when he took it.

"The fourth sitting is next week," she said. "The calendar has it at the first bell, the eighth day."

"I've seen it."

"Whatever it is you're doing that isn't my session," said Quenna, "is making you tired." She said it flatly. "I'm not asking what it is. I said I'd rather not know, and I meant it. I'm telling you what it looks like from the chalk. Come to the fourth sitting rested."

"I will."

She let go of the strip.

"And Cael." She had turned to go and turned back. "I'll want to see you after it. Both of you." She glanced at Karis, folding her stool, and back. "Not about anything you're doing. About something I'd like to do."

She went out. Karis stood with the stool under her arm and looked at the door after Quenna had gone.

"Something with structure," said Karis quietly.

"You think so?"

"I asked her a question once," said Karis, "and asked her to keep it. I think she's kept it."

The nineteenth session, on the Friday evening, ended early. Karis had fought a standings bout that morning, an Iron Rank Four from the Edge floor who had heard what she had done to Edran and wanted to find out whether it could be done to him, and it could. It had cost her three exchanges and most of her reserves. She ran the session at seven paces anyway, because she had said she would and she did not move a session for her own tiredness. A third of the way through, her off hand went still for longer than a quarter of a second, and her next point came up dim and late and went out on its own, without her count. She let the rest go and sat down on the scrubbed floor.

"I'm calling it," she said. "Not because anything happened to me. Because I can't lay a clean point, and a session with dirty points is a session with a second variable in it." She was already writing. "Null, as far as it went."

They did not leave. They sat on the floor of the quiet room with their backs against opposite walls and the two notebooks open on their knees, her ledger of the nulls and his own pages of session notes, all of her Path and none of his. She would read a line from the ledger and he would find the same session in his pages, to see whether what she had seen from outside matched what he had felt from in. It had become a ritual since the third week without either of them deciding it would: two notebooks open and two pens moving, in a whitewashed room with the high window going grey.

She got through the fourteenth session and the fifteenth, shut the ledger, capped her pen, and raised her eyes to him across the room.

"May I ask you something about the shape of the whole?"

He knew by how she asked it that she had been holding it for some days.

"You can ask."

"I won't ask what you have. That's the first clause, and it stays." She set the closed ledger flat on her knees and laid both hands on it. "You told me before the first session that there are more than the two I've seen. I've never asked how many, and I won't. I want to ask one thing about all of them together, from outside." She paused. "Is there anything that has happened to you only once? Something you've never been able to make happen again, that doesn't fit with the rest? I'd like to know whether the shape I've been drawing from outside is the whole shape, or whether there's a piece of it I've never been near."

He could have answered narrowly. It was a question that stood with its toes on the very line of the terms. She was not asking for the binder or for a source or a mechanism, only whether the outline she had been allowed to see was the whole outline. He could have said *no* and been truthful about every fragment in the front of the binder. He would have said nothing at all about the page behind them, the one set apart, the one he turned past every time without adding anything.

He did not. He had decided in an archive on the first day that she was a person who could hold an honest *I don't know*, and nineteen sessions of her writing *null* in the same careful hand had done nothing but prove it.

"Yes," he said. "Once."

Karis went very still, the way she went still before an ignition, all of her listening at once.

"Months ago. Before Greyvane, before the road. A session with Brom, in his sparring circle in Ardenmere. The read was open; we were working on it the way we always worked on it. Nothing about that afternoon was different from a hundred others." He could still see the circle, the scuffed chalk, Brom's forearm under his hand. He did not reach for anything else. "And for less than a second, through the read, there was something like water. Water moving, somewhere there wasn't any water. Then it was gone, before I could even turn toward it. Nothing came after it. No notice. I've never had it again."

"Tide," said Karis.

"If it was anything. That's the only word I've got that's anywhere near it."

"Did you try to make it happen again?"

"A dozen times. More. The same circle, the same time of day, the same work, as near as my notes could make it. Nothing."

"Give me the conditions. Every one you can remember."

He told her. It did not take long, which was the worst of it. There had never been much to tell, and he had told it to himself so often that the telling had worn smooth. She listened without moving. When he had finished she took him through it again, from the other end, starting from the moment itself and working backward to the morning, and then a third time from the morning forward. She asked about the weather, and what he had eaten, and whether Brom had been tired, and what Brom had been drilling that week, and whether anyone else had been in the circle or near it or had just left it, and whether anything at all, anything, had been different. He answered all of it. Every answer built the same picture his notes had always built, a working afternoon like a hundred others with one second in the middle of it that did not belong.

Karis was quiet a long time.

"There's no Tide practitioner in that," she said at last. "Nobody near you with a Tide Path. Nobody you'd watched. Nothing for you to have watched." She frowned at her own hands on the ledger, and it was not a frown at him. It was the frown of somebody whose model has just been handed a stone it has no pocket for. "Everything I've drawn from outside says you watch first, and then something follows. This has no watching in it at all. It breaks the one thing I was surest of." She looked up. "It shouldn't exist."

"It exists."

"I know it exists. That's the trouble with it." She said it without heat. "Either the shape I've drawn isn't the whole shape, or there's a second thing in you working beside the first that I've never been near, or it happened once and a thing that's happened once can't carry a theory on its back. I can't tell which from here. I don't think anybody could."

He said nothing. He thought, privately, of the page in the binder with his own hypothesis on it, *it takes what's used on me*, and of the afternoon in Brom's circle where nobody had been using anything on anybody. It broke his rule too, and he did not say so. It was not for saying yet, and it changed nothing about the second in Brom's circle, which was what it had always been.

"So what do we do with it?" he said.

"We keep it."

She opened the ledger again, but she did not turn to the nulls. She turned past them, past the plan she had drawn for the last sessions, to a page near the very back that he had never seen. There was a single short line on it in her small upright hand. She must have written it in the few minutes he had been talking. He could read it from across the room only because she turned the ledger toward him. *Session nine, Ardenmere, with B. One reading, Tide-like, under a second. Never repeated.* Beside it she made a small mark with her pen. It was not a letter or a number, nor any symbol he knew, only a short precise shape, made slowly and deliberately, as a person signs a thing they mean to stand behind.

"That mark goes on exactly one thing in this whole notebook," she said. "Real, unexplained, keep. It isn't a failure, and it isn't a hypothesis waiting to be proved. It's something true that I don't understand, and I've written it down as exactly that. I haven't bent it to fit an explanation, the kind that comforts you and teaches you nothing." She looked at the mark. "Most people can't bear to leave a thing like that alone. They explain it, because an explanation is more comfortable to carry than a question. Then they've got an explanation, and it's wrong, and they build on it."

Across the scrubbed floor his eyes stayed on the mark.

Session nine had ridden with him since the road, small and shapeless, the kind of load you cannot show anyone because there is nothing to hold up. He had never once been able to set it down in front of anyone who would neither explain it away nor shrug at it. Lira had believed him and been frightened; Brom had believed him and been puzzled. Neither of them had known where to put it. Karis had just set it down in front of him in her ledger, under the one mark that ledger kept for nothing else, and left it there as it was. It was not answered. Nothing about it had been answered. But it had been witnessed, properly, by somebody who knew what witnessing was, and he had not known until this moment how much of the weight of it had been the being alone with it.

"How many other entries have that mark?" he asked.

"None," said Karis. "Until tonight." She closed the ledger. "I can't tell yet whether that's a compliment to you or a warning. I'll let you know when I've worked it out."

---

He wrote it that night in the binder, on the page set apart from the rest, behind the four sections. For the first time since the road he added something to that page, and he was careful that it was only one thing, and true.

*Told Karis, in the third week of the nulls, because she asked properly whether the shape she could see was the whole shape. She took me through it three ways. No Tide anywhere near. Nobody watched. Nothing on it. It shouldn't exist. It exists. Her mark on it is real, unexplained, keep. Nothing else in her notebook carries that.*

He looked at the line above it, the one he had written on the road and never once touched since, and left it as it was.

*Her mark is worth more to me than any answer would be. Any answer this soon would only be a guess in a good coat. The mark says plainly that nobody knows.*

He told Brom at breakfast, because Brom had been in the circle that afternoon and had a right to know the stone had been shown to somebody.

Brom listened, chewing slowly, and when Cael had finished he set his spoon down.

"I remember the afternoon," he said. "I've thought about it more than you'd guess. You went white for a moment and then you said *did you feel that*, and I hadn't felt anything." He looked at his porridge. "I could tell her what day it was and what we'd eaten and which of my hands was sore that week. I've gone over it that often, every time I saw you turn past that page." He picked up his spoon again. "I'm glad she's got a mark for it. I never had anywhere to put it either."

On the evening before Gerda's supplementary bout, Cael found Lira and Gerda on the open floor after the last bell. Everybody else had long gone. One lamp was lit at the far end, and the high windows were black.

They were not sparring, and Hobb had gone. They were sitting side by side on the edge of the chalk with their backs against the rail, a water flask between them, not talking. Gerda had her slip for the morning in her lap, already filled out, the hour already written on it in her careful square figures, and every so often she looked down at it.

Cael stopped at the rail above them. Lira tipped her head back against the wood and looked up at him upside down.

"She won't sleep," said Lira. "So we're not sleeping here, instead of not sleeping in our rooms. It's better. There's a floor."

"I'll sleep," said Gerda. "Later."

"She says that."

Gerda looked down at the slip again. Then she did a thing Cael had never once seen her do, in two months of watching her at desks and rails and the floor. She folded the slip in half, so that the hour was on the inside where she could not see it, and put it in her coat.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'm going to come out of the haze. I'm going to take whatever they put on me, on purpose, where the chairs can see it. And then I'm going to put my whole weight into somebody." She said it in her procedure voice, every word set down separately, as if she were reading a clause aloud to Prynn. "Whatever the panel writes after that, it'll be about what happened. Not about what I was afraid of."

Lira did not say anything. She put her hand on the flask between them and pushed it a little toward Gerda.

"Drink," she said. "And then we're going to bed. Both of us. I'm not getting you up in the morning if you've sat on this floor all night."

Gerda drank.

Cael went up to the residence wing and left them there. Halfway up the stair it came to him that the part of him he had written down a week ago had been listening too. *Part of me is waiting for it.* None of the nulls since had moved it. It had heard Gerda say she would come out of the haze and take what was put on her where the chairs could see, and it had leaned toward the words, because somewhere ahead there was still a floor that was his. He did not like it any better than he had at the desk. He went on up. At the turn of the stair he looked back through the doorway once and saw them still sitting side by side in the one circle of lamplight at the far end of the empty hall, two girls with dots on their tags and their backs to a rail. Neither of them was looking at the clock.

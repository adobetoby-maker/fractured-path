# Chapter 15 — Public Draft

Karis climbed the standings the way water climbs a stair, without any visible effort and without stopping.

She had full standing as a transfer, and a scheduled bout every few days like anyone else, and in her first fortnight on the board she fought three of them. She won all three, none in more than three exchanges, and after each one the clerk who kept the board climbed his little ladder with his chalk and his rag and moved her name up a section. By the end of the fifth week it sat second, directly beneath a name that had not moved in longer than anyone could remember. The schedule paired the top two of each cohort at the turn of the half-term. Everybody at Greyvane could count, and everybody knew what the next pairing would be some days before it went up on the board.

Cael did not spend those days watching her. He spent them, mostly, on the floor of Brom's room after the eighth bell, with his left forearm braced across his chest.

"Again," said Brom.

The road was a long way behind them now, and the work had moved indoors with them. Brom knelt with his left palm on Cael's forearm, Iron Skin coming up under the skin like frost forming on a pane, and leaned, slow and steady, the weight arriving the way a tide arrives. Cael waited for the force to settle into the bone, chose its road before it could choose its own, out, back along the line it came in, and reached.

He had been catching a quarter of the push for a month. It had become almost reliable; he caught it four times in ten, sometimes five, and the price had stayed exactly the same through all of it, a hot bar laid across the collarbones and two breaths he could not take. He had begun to think of the quarter as a floor he stood on. Tonight he was trying to find out whether it was also a ceiling.

The first six tries went as they usually went. Two caught at a quarter. Four did nothing. On the seventh he reached for more than he had chosen, greedily, a fraction too late, and the gathered force slid sideways off the road he had laid for it and went down into his left wrist. It did not hurt at once. Then it hurt a great deal, a deep, hot throb in the joint, and he sat back on his heels and held the wrist against his chest while Brom waited.

"Joint," said Brom.

"Wrist." Cael flexed the fingers, carefully. They moved. "I tried to take more than I'd decided to take. It went somewhere I hadn't decided."

"So decide more."

"That's the whole problem."

"No," said Brom, who had said this before, on a hillside in the pine needles, and seemed to enjoy saying it again. "That's the whole answer. Decide first, and decide bigger. Again."

The eighth try did nothing at all.

On the ninth, Cael did something he had not tried before. He did not reach harder. He chose a wider road. Before the force arrived he laid the line back out along Brom's arm and imagined it twice as broad, a channel cut for twice the water, and only then, when the push had settled fully into the bone, did he reach. The fragment gathered. It gathered further than it had ever gathered for him, a long, tightening pull that ran from his forearm up into his chest, and for a moment he held half of Brom's push in him like a breath he had taken too deep. Then he sent it home.

It went back into Brom's wrist with a sound like a fist on a table. Brom's arm jerked back a full hand's width. Under the Iron Skin his eyebrows went up and stayed up.

And then the bill came, and it was not the same bill.

It ran up through his right shoulder and across his breastbone as it always had, but it was twice as broad and twice as heavy, a bar laid on him that had become a beam. He could not breathe for two breaths, and then three, and then four. On the fifth he got air into himself and it came back out as a cough so hard it brought water to his eyes, and he coughed again, bent over his knees on Brom's floor, until the bar slowly lifted. His right shoulder had locked. He could not raise the arm past his chest.

"Half," said Brom quietly. He was turning his own wrist over, looking at it. "That was half. I felt it come back."

"Half," Cael managed.

"What does it cost?"

"Double." He tried the shoulder again, and it refused him. "It doesn't give discounts. Twice the catch, twice the bill. Possibly more than twice. I'll know in the morning."

He knew in the morning. The shoulder had loosened by the first bell, grudgingly, but his breastbone was sore to the touch, a deep ache under the bone as if he had been struck there from the inside, and the left wrist was swollen enough that he wrapped it before the lecture so nobody would ask. He wrote it up that night under the four headings, slowly, because it was the first time in a month the Compression section had needed new lines.

*Compression-adjacent. Brom's room. Function: half a slow push caught and returned, once, on the ninth try. Done by choosing a wider road before the force arrived, not by reaching harder. Benefit: none in a real fight yet. Twice what I had. Cost: twice what it was. Four breaths gone and a cough, the right shoulder locked for an hour, the breastbone still sore. Seventh try misrouted into the left wrist when I reached for more than I'd chosen. Integration: partial. Half, once.*

Underneath, in smaller letters:

*The price grows with the catch. Choose first, always. Choose the size first, too.*

---

Lira's second recorded bout of the month fell two days later, against a Current Path third-year with quick feet and a habit of circling left, and Cael watched it from the rail of the formal yard with his wrist wrapped under his sleeve.

It was not the bout she had fought in the second week. That one had been an argument, made in public, for the benefit of everyone watching. This one was work. She measured the third-year in the first exchange, found the leftward circle in the second, and in the third stepped into the circle's path a half-beat early and let him run into her. She took one strike she could have slipped, a short glancing blow to the hip in the third exchange, and Cael saw her take it and saw why, and saw that this time she did not seem to care whether anybody else saw it. The assessor called the bout for her at the end of the fourth exchange, and she collected her initials before the sixth bell without having to be reminded, and came back to the rail toweling her neck.

Karis was at the rail too. Cael had not seen her come. She was standing a few places along, with her notebook shut under her arm, and when Lira came back she turned and waited until Lira had finished drinking.

"May I ask you something?"

Lira lowered the flask. Cael watched her shoulders come up an inch, the way they had on the wall when she talked about people who wrote things down.

"Is this research?"

"No." Karis said it at once. "The seven clauses are about him. This is about you, and you can tell me to go away."

"Then go away."

Karis nodded, without any sign of offence, and turned to go.

"Wait." Lira was frowning at the ring, not at Karis. She seemed annoyed with herself. "Ask it. I'll tell you if I'm answering."

Karis turned back. "In the third exchange you took a strike on the hip that you could have slipped. I've seen you slip harder ones. I'd like to know why you took it."

Lira was quiet for a while. She turned the flask over in her hands.

"There's a file about me at Fenmark," she said finally. "I've never read it. But I know one word in it, because they read it aloud to me on the day they threw me out. *Insubordination.* And the man who read it said, so that I'd be sure to understand, that it meant I had no control of myself." She set the flask on the rail. "So when I take a hit now, I take it where everyone can see I chose to. The ones I slip, nobody remembers. The ones I take on purpose, they remember I didn't have to." She looked at Karis directly for the first time. "That isn't for your notebook."

"It isn't going in my notebook," said Karis. "Thank you."

She stood a moment longer, as if deciding whether to say something more, and then said it.

"I'd have taken it too. For the same reason."

She left. Lira watched her go along the rail and out through the yard gate, and then turned to Cael with an expression of deep suspicion.

"I didn't mean to answer that."

"I know."

"She asked properly, though." Lira picked up the flask again and frowned at it. "That's the trouble with her. She keeps asking properly, and then you've answered before you've decided to."

---

The pairing went up on the first morning of the sixth week, and by the fifth bell the formal yard was fuller than Cael had ever seen it.

The benches along one side held forty, and they were full by the time he arrived, with students sitting two to a space and others standing behind on the low wall. He found a place at the rail along the open side, where an observer could stand and watch as long as he did not step over the chalk, and Lira came and stood at his left with her arms folded and a stolen apple in one hand, and Brom came and leaned at his right with the wrist of his own left arm faintly purple from the night before, which he showed to nobody and seemed rather proud of.

A broad shape came along the rail and stopped beside Brom, and Cael looked up and saw that it was Hobb.

He was alone. He had his hands in the pockets of his training coat and his feet planted a shoulder-width apart even standing at a rail, as if the habit went all the way down. He nodded to Brom, and Brom nodded back, and neither of them said anything, which seemed to satisfy both of them completely.

Cael understood that the moment he had been waiting for had arrived of its own accord, as he had suspected it would, and that if he let it go by now he would be letting a habit form.

"Hobb."

Hobb turned his head and looked at him with the same patient, unhurried attention he had given the panel at two sittings.

"I owe you thanks," Cael said. "For the first sitting, and the second. You hit me as hard as you were asked, every time, and not once harder, and you never tried to make it about you. It was exactly what I needed on both mornings." He held the look. "I wanted to say it to you and not to Wray."

Hobb was quiet for long enough that Cael began to wonder whether he had made a mistake. Then he looked away, out at the ring, where the chalk had been freshly laid that morning.

"Most people thank Wray."

"Wray didn't stand there."

Something moved in Hobb's broad face, slowly, like a stone settling into a bank. He did not smile; Cael was not sure he did. But he took one hand out of his pocket and set it on the rail, and seemed to stand a little more easily.

"You named my feet before I'd picked where to put them," he said. "Nobody's done that."

"Sorry."

"Don't be." Hobb looked back at the ring. "He'll press," he added, after a moment, and nodded at the north side, where Edran was taking his mark. "He always presses when there's a crowd. Watch how long he lets her alone in the first one."

It was, Cael realized, the longest speech he had ever heard Hobb make, and it had ended in a tactical note, freely given. He wrote *Hobb: he'll press with a crowd* in the margin of the binder and felt as though he had been handed something more than the note.

---

Edran took the north mark with his back to the benches, as the higher-ranked fighter was allowed to, and settled into the easy, upright stance of a man who had stood at the top of a board so long that he no longer checked whether he was still there. Karis took the south mark with nothing in her hands and nothing in her face. The assessor was Wray, standing at the ring's edge with her slate against her hip, and she raised one finger and lowered it, and the bout began.

Hobb was right. Edran pressed.

He came off his mark almost at once, faster than the textbook distance, and Cael saw in the first three steps why he had held first place for so long. His Glass came up along his forearms as he moved, bright hard shells like ice forming in a stream, and he led with them, forward and forward, inviting her to hit them. Glass was not afraid of being struck. It wanted to be struck, because a struck shell burst outward in a glittering spray that carried the force away with it, and before the glitter had faded a new shell was already forming in the next place he expected to be hit. He fought like a man walking through rain he had dressed for.

Karis gave ground.

She went back across the ring in short, neat steps, her weight even, her eyes on his chest. Twice, when he came close enough to touch, she laid a single point directly against his leading shell, a small hot bloom the size of a coin, and both times the shell took it and burst in a spray of light and the point died with it, and a new shell was already coming. Cael watched her off hand. It went still a quarter of a breath before each of the two points, just as it had on the training floor, the free hand stopping wherever it was as if it were listening. Then the point came.

That was all she did in the whole first exchange. Two points. A great deal of footwork. When Wray called the end of it, Karis was a pace from the south chalk and Edran was in the middle of the ring with the crowd murmuring, and anyone who had come to see the Ternhall transfer do something remarkable looked faintly disappointed.

Cael was not disappointed. He had seen her let both points go. When she released a point, the warmth did not vanish at once. It lingered for perhaps a third of a breath as a faint shimmer in the air, like heat over a road in summer, before it was gone. He had seen that on the training floor too and not known what to make of it. Now he wrote *lag on release, a third of a breath, both times* and underlined nothing.

"She's measuring him," said Lira, very low, round her apple.

"She's measuring his shells. How long a new one takes to form."

"How long does it?"

"Less than a breath." Cael did not take his eyes off the ring. "She knows that now. So does Edran. He knows she knows."

The second exchange looked, to the benches, like the first. Edran pressed and Karis gave ground. But Cael had stopped watching her hands and started watching the floor behind her, and on her second step back he saw it. A dim bloom, the size of a coin, sat on the chalked earth where her heel had just been. It did not flare. It simply glowed, faintly, the way a coal glows through paper, and stayed. On her next step back there was another. On the step after that, another, slightly to the left.

He began to count.

She laid six in the second exchange, one for each step she gave, and to the benches every one of those steps looked like retreat. Edran pressed into the space she left. He was good, and he was strong, and twice in the exchange he got close enough to strike, and twice she took the strike on her forearm and turned with it and went back another step and left another coin behind her. By the end of the exchange the south half of the ring held six small warm points in an uneven crescent, and Edran was standing in the middle of them.

He looked down.

Cael saw the exact moment. Edran's eyes went to the floor at his own feet, and to the dim coin a pace to his left, and to the next one beyond it, and his face did not change at all. But his weight did. It shifted back half an inch, off his front foot, and his shoulders came up very slightly, as a man's shoulders come up when he has heard a sound behind him in an empty house.

"He's seen them," Cael said.

"He's good," said Brom, with real respect.

---

Edran's answer in the third exchange was the right answer, and Cael admired him for finding it so quickly.

He stopped pressing her and went straight at the points instead. He came off his mark at an angle, not at Karis but at the crescent behind her, and drove through it on a line that would cross three points before she could do anything with them. It was a brave and sensible thing to do. A point was only a small heat. A man with Glass on his shins could take a small heat and burst it and keep going. If he broke up her crescent before she could use it, she would have spent two exchanges laying nothing.

The first point bloomed under his shin, and his shell burst in a spray of light, and he went on. The second bloomed, and burst a second shell, and Cael heard Edran grunt, because the shell had not quite finished forming and some of the heat had reached him. He went on. Karis let the third point go before he reached it. Cael saw the shimmer of its release, that third of a breath of lingering warmth, and saw Edran see it too and decide, in the space of one step, to plant his foot right through the shimmer while it was still dying, betting the lag would be shorter than it looked.

It was not shorter. It was exactly as long as it looked. His foot came down in the last of the warmth, and the heat caught the edge of his boot, and he hissed through his teeth and stumbled a half-step. Wray's pen moved.

He learned. Cael watched him learn it. For the rest of the exchange Edran waited out every shimmer before he stepped through it, a third of a breath each time, and the waiting cost him speed, and the speed cost him the crescent. Karis had used the time he spent breaking it. While he crossed the south half of the ring she had moved round the north half, giving ground in a long curve, and every step had left another coin behind her. Two of the old points were gone. Five new ones glowed on the north side. Near the end of the exchange Edran caught up with her at last and drove a hard shell into her left shoulder, a solid, honest strike that turned her half round, and the benches made a sound, the first real sound of the bout.

Then Wray called the end of the third exchange, and Karis straightened her shoulder and walked back toward the centre of the ring, and Cael counted nine points on the floor around her.

"Even," said Lira, under her breath. "That one was even."

"It isn't even," said Cael. "Look at the floor."

The fourth exchange lasted perhaps three heartbeats.

Wray lowered her finger. Edran set himself to come forward. And Karis, standing quite still in the middle of the ring with her off hand gone motionless at her side, joined the points.

The channels lit all at once. Thin lines of heat ran along the chalked earth from point to point to point, faintly glowing, with a sound like a kettle a moment before it sings. They did not run everywhere. They ran exactly where she had wanted them. In the space of a single breath, a ring that had looked open a moment before became a drawing of a ring, lines across it in every direction a body might want to go, and between the lines, Cael saw, there were only two places left where a person could stand without crossing one. She was already moving toward one of them. The other was three paces to Edran's right, and to reach it he would have to step across a channel.

Edran did not move.

He stood where he was, on the one small island of unlined earth at his feet, and looked at the floor. Cael watched him read it, line by line, as he would have read an opponent's guard: the channels, the two open places, the line he would have to cross to reach the second place, and the line beyond that he would have to cross to reach her. Cael saw him weigh the cost. A channel was hotter than a point; it held while both its points held, and every one of her points was still holding. He could cross. He could burst a shell on the first line and another on the second, and he might reach her with whatever shell he had left, or he might not.

He looked up at Karis. She was standing in her open place with her hands at her sides and her breathing no different from the first bell, and she did not move toward him, and she did not invite him.

Edran lowered his hands.

"Yield," he said.

Wray called it.

---

For a moment the yard was silent. Then the benches broke into noise all at once, the voices of forty people trying to work out how to tell it to someone who had missed it, and the channels faded and the points went out one by one, each leaving its third of a breath of shimmer on the air, until the ring was only chalk and packed earth again with a few faint scorch marks on it that the broom would take by morning.

Karis walked to the edge of the ring and drank from her flask. She did it as a person drinks after a walk, not after a fight, and Cael wrote that down.

He had written a great deal. He looked at the page now, while the yard emptied round him, and read it back.

*Exchange 1: two points, both at contact, both against shells. Footwork only otherwise. Measuring shell timing.*

*Exchange 2: six points laid behind her, one per step back. Looks like retreat. Edran sees them at the end.*

*Exchange 3: he goes for the points. Right answer. She lets three go. Lag on every release, a third of a breath, every time. He tries to step through the lag and gets burned once. Learns. She lays five more on the far side while he's breaking the first lot. Nine on the floor at the end.*

*Exchange 4: channels. Five that I could count. Two places to stand. He yields rather than cross.*

*Off hand still before every point. Lag after every release. Never more than nine points at once. Breathing never changed. Hands never flushed. She finished the way she started.*

He looked at that last line for a long time, and then wrote the one under it that mattered.

*She didn't spend anything she couldn't spare. Whatever she has in reserve, nobody in this yard saw any of it today.*

Beside him, Lira had finished her apple and was looking at the ring with an expression he could not quite read.

"She's going to be trouble for somebody," she said at last. "I'd like it not to be us."

"She signed a page with us."

"I know. That's why I'd like it not to be us." Lira tossed the core over the wall into the kitchen garden. "Did you see her let him take that shoulder? In the third. She could have slipped it."

"I saw."

"So did I." Lira was quiet for a moment. "I do that. When I want people to see something not matter."

Brom said nothing. He was looking at the north side of the yard, where Edran had gone to stand by the standings board.

The clerk was already up his little ladder with his chalk and his rag. Cael watched him rub out the name that had sat at the top of the cohort for longer than anyone could remember, and write *Karis*, and then write the old name one line beneath it, in the same hand, at the same size.

Edran stood and watched him do it. He did not look angry. Cael had expected anger, or the tight polite smile of a man pretending not to mind, and there was neither. There was something stranger in Edran's face, a kind of stillness, like a man standing on a deck who has just felt it move under him and is waiting to find out whether it will move again.

Then Edran turned his head, and his eyes went across the yard to the rail, and found Cael there with the binder open on his arm and the pen still in his hand.

He held the look. His face showed nothing at all. Cael thought he understood it anyway. In the space of a month the best fighter in the building had watched a boy with no Path walk into two closed sittings and come out *satisfied* both times, with nothing anyone could see to show for it but a hollow circle on his tag, and now he had watched a transfer who had been at Greyvane for five weeks take the place he had spent three years earning, without spending, so far as anyone could see, a single thing she could not spare. The ground had moved twice. He had felt it both times.

Edran looked away. He went out through the north gate alone, without hurrying, and the students who would once have fallen in round him like water round a post let him go.


Cael closed the binder slowly. He found that he was not glad, though he had every reason to think he would be. He had spent a month watching Edran and had filled three pages on him, and the line at the bottom of the Glass page was about breaking first and on purpose, with a question under it that he still could not answer. He had not, until this afternoon, seen Edran choose not to break at all.

---

By supper the rumor market had already rebuilt itself around the afternoon, and Lira delivered the new theories in the stable with the brisk competence of a woman reporting on a district she had always understood.

"First," she said, holding up a finger. "She's a Compact inspector, sent to see whether Greyvane is fit to keep its charter. That one used to be yours. It's moved."

"I'm hurt," said Cael.

"You should be. It was your best one." A second finger. "Second, Ternhall sent her to beat everybody here as a lesson, and she'll go home at midwinter with the board in her pocket. Mostly the third-years. They don't like that one, but they keep telling it." A third. "Third, Edran yielded on purpose, because his family owes hers money. That one is very stupid, and very popular on the stairs."

"And the fourth?" asked Brom, who never asked and always listened.

"The fourth is mine." Lira tore her bread in half and gave Cael the larger piece without being asked, which told him how she felt about the afternoon better than anything she might have said. "Nobody's started it yet. It's that she came here because of somebody, and that the somebody is sitting at this table eating barley." She looked at Cael. "I'm not going to start it. But it's the only true one, and somebody's going to work it out before the month's over."

"Then what do we do?" said Cael.

"Nothing." Lira shrugged. "Same as always. Four stories pulling different ways and everybody arguing. Worry when they start to agree."

He laughed, because she meant him to, and ate the larger half of the bread, and did not tell her that of the four, the true one was the only one that worried him.

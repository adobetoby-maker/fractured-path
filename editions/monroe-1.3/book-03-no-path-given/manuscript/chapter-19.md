# Chapter 19 — The Observer's Floor

The formal yard had never held so many people, and it had not been built to.

Its benches were meant for forty. By the time Cael came through the gate from the main hall, a quarter-hour before the first bell, the benches had stopped meaning anything. Students sat on them two to a space and stood on them behind the sitters. They lined the low wall along the open side three deep and sat along the top of it with their heels hanging down. A row of first-years had climbed onto the roof of the little tool shed in the corner and were being ignored by every instructor in the yard, which was a thing Cael had never seen at Greyvane before and suspected he never would again.

It was cold. The first frost had come in the night at last, and the packed earth of the yard was grey with it except inside the ring, where somebody had swept it bare before dawn and laid fresh chalk. Two hundred people breathed out white into the morning, and the yard smelled of frost and chalk and wool coats, and under all of it, faintly, of the kitchen's bread.

He stopped at the edge of the ring and let himself take the crowd in one piece at a time, because the alternative was to take it all at once.

The three chairs stood at the north end with their chalk ticks and the little table and its inkwell. Quenna sat in the middle with the assessment ledger open. Wray sat on her left in the brown coat, upright and still. The rotating seat this month was the lean old instructor who took the heat Paths, the one with scorched cuffs, who had watched Karis lay six practice points on her second afternoon at Greyvane and said only *Ternhall*. He had a slate on his knee and a pen held loosely, and he was looking at the crowd with the mild, appraising interest of a man who had seen a great many crowds and expected to be mildly disappointed in this one.

Lira and Brom were at the front of the rail on the open side, near the south mark, where they had plainly been since before the frost lifted. Lira had her arms folded and her chin up. Brom stood beside her with his feet planted and his hands clasped behind his back, as he had stood at the rail of section four for his witness session, like a man attending something he had been told was important and meant to take seriously.

Karis was three places along from them with her notebook already open and her pen already moving. She was not charting him yet. She was charting the crowd.

Hobb was at the far corner of the rail, alone, with his hands in his pockets. Gerda was near the gate with her arms folded and her patched coat buttoned to the chin. And on the front bench, squeezed between two first-years who were both taller than her, sat Oona, with her slate on her knees and both hands pressed flat on top of it.

He made himself stop counting faces somewhere past fifty, and breathed.

Then he did the thing he had come to do, before anything moved, because that was the rule.

He chose.

The framework, and only the framework. Wind-adjacent, the evasion he had shown at both sittings and on the circuit a hundred times, at whatever speed the fight asked of it, and nothing under it. He would not open the surface read. It was safe to show, it was on the record twice already, and he would not use it, because he did not need it, and because a fight won with one thing was a smaller fight on paper than a fight won with two. The Pressure redirect stayed banked where it had been banked for a year. The Compression fragment stayed in his chest where it belonged, and if anything Edran did woke it, he would give it no road.

The size was chosen. He set it the way Lira had taught him to set panel speed, the walls built before the water came.

"For the record," said Quenna.

Her voice dropped into its slow, flat cadence, each word set down separately, and the yard went quiet from the front backward like a field going still in front of a wind.

"Demonstration assessment, unclassified observer track. Third sitting, held in public, in answer to the Petition of Standing posted on this term's board. Present: the presiding assessor, the instructor's seat, the rotating faculty seat. The bout is an exhibition under standings rules, three exchanges on the instructor's count, against the petitioner." She looked up from the ledger. "The candidate takes the south mark. The petitioner takes the north."

Edran was already there.

He stood on the north mark with his back to the chairs and his hands loose at his sides. Up close he looked exactly as he did every morning in the corner by the high windows, rested and exact, with nothing in his face that Cael could use. He was not hostile. He had the look of someone about to learn the answer to a thing, who needed very badly to know which answer it would be.

Cael took the south mark. Eight paces of swept earth lay between them, and the chalk.

"I said I wouldn't change anything," Edran said, very quietly, so that only Cael could hear it under the noise of two hundred people settling. "I haven't."

"I know. I watched."

Edran's mouth moved, very slightly. It might have been the beginning of a smile, if he had allowed it to go on.

Wray rose from her chair, came to the edge of the ring, and raised one finger.

She lowered it.

---

Edran came at once.

Wray had said behind Naveth's shut door that he always pressed with a crowd, and Hobb had said it at the rail before her, and he pressed now, off his mark before Wray's hand had finished falling, faster than any morning in the corner. Cael had known he would be faster. Everyone was faster at a fight than at a drill. He had still not been ready for how much.

Both of the ready pair were up before he had taken two steps, built at his mark before Wray's hand ever fell. The shell on his left forearm caught the low winter sun and threw it, a bright hard sheath along the bone, and the edge ran along the outside of his right. He came in behind the two of them like a man walking into weather he had dressed for.

The first strike came high, at the line of Cael's jaw. Cael read it off the shoulder before it had any speed, and the framework moved him half a pace to the left and the edge went past his ear. He heard it go. A Glass edge in motion made a small thin sound, like a wet finger round the rim of a cup, and a chill came off it as it passed, sharp as the frost on the ground.

The second came low, a kick at his knee with nothing on the shin but cloth. He stepped back off it. The third came across, the left forearm swinging round flat at his ribs, and he slipped inside it and out the far side, and found himself four paces from the south chalk with Edran already turning.

He gave ground where ground was cheap. He held where it cost. He watched.

Edran was not trying to finish anything yet. Cael saw that by the fourth strike. He was asking questions, quick ones, one after another. How far did the observer give? How did he move when he was pressed toward the chalk? Did he go left or right when he had the choice? Would he panic, with two hundred people watching and a ledger open? Each strike was a question, thrown whole and finished and impossible to take back, and between them the ready pair stayed whole and unspent, because Edran was not yet spending anything.

At the sixth strike Edran drove the left shell straight at his chest, a short hard push meant to move him, and Cael had nowhere clean to go and took it on his raised forearms.

The shell burst against them.

It went outward in a spray of glitter, bright as a handful of ice thrown into sunlight, and the force of the push went with it in a hundred directions, but not all of it. Enough arrived to drive his forearms back against his own chest and put the weight into the bone.

Something low and tight in his chest woke and reached for it.

He had felt it every morning on the road and every night on Brom's floor and once, in this same cold month, in a closed hall with three pens moving. The force was already his. The fragment closed on it, the way a hand closes on something dropped into it, and a road opened back along the line the shell had come in.

He gave it nothing.

He let the gathered force go as he had let it go at the first sitting, and it spilled out of him harmlessly, and his forearms took the push the ordinary way, with a dull flat ache. Nobody in the yard saw anything. A boy had taken a shell on his guard and stepped back from it. That was all.

The framework moved him off the line before the next strike. He let the breath out slowly.

Edran pressed him for the rest of the exchange with the edge alone, and did not spend it, and Cael gave ground and took it back and did not once let his heels near the chalk. He did not try to draw a sequence out of him to see the seam better. He had six days of mornings behind him. He did not need to see it today, not yet.

Wray called the exchange.

There was no touch. The crowd made a sound that was half a breath let out and half disappointment, because nothing had happened that anyone could tell anyone else about. On the north side, the Edge floor students who stood together on the benches looked satisfied. Their man had owned the ring, start to finish. The observer had spent the whole exchange stepping out of the way.

Cael walked back to the south mark and breathed, and looked at Edran across eight paces, and Edran looked back at him with exactly the same face as before.

Edran was faster than the mornings by about a quarter. Cael wrote it in his head, because he could not write it anywhere else. *A quarter faster. Same shape.*

---

Lira had decided before the first bell that she was not going to shout. She lasted the whole first exchange, which she considered a personal triumph.

"He's slow," said somebody on the bench behind her. A third-year from the Edge floor, by the sound, all collar and opinion. "He's just running away."

"He's measuring," said Lira, without turning round.

"He's running away and calling it measuring."

She did turn round then, and looked at the third-year until he discovered something interesting about his own boots. Then she turned back to the ring. Beside her, Brom had not moved at all. He was standing the way he stood when he meant to stand all day, with his hands behind his back and his weight in his heels, and only his eyes were going.

"The shell," Brom said, very low. "On his arms. Sixth strike."

"I saw."

"Did he—"

"No." Lira kept her eyes on the south mark, where Cael was standing with his hands loose and his breathing slow, looking at nothing. "He felt it go for it. I saw his shoulders. He let it go."

Brom let out a breath that came out white and hung in front of him in the cold.

She had been watching for that since the frost lifted, if she was honest. Not for the framework, because she had taught him the framework without knowing it and could read it in her sleep. Not for Edran, because she had watched Edran fight three times this term and knew roughly what he would do. She had been watching Cael's chest. A shell bursting against his guard was a push arriving in the bone, and the thing he had taken from Reydan on the last night in Ardenmere lived in exactly that place, and woke for exactly that. She had seen it wake on Brom's floor, and she had read in the binder what it cost him to catch half of a slow push and seen him the morning after, holding his shoulder. If it woke in front of two hundred people and three slates and he let it go home, the whole yard would hear the knock.

It had woken. He had let it go. She could breathe now, for one exchange.

Wray raised her finger for the second.

Lira read it before Wray's hand came down, which was a thing she could do with Cael and nobody else in the world, and had been able to do since the second month in Ardenmere. She read it in the way he set his feet on the south mark: a fraction narrower than in the first exchange, his weight a little forward. He was not going to give ground this time. He was going to stand closer than was sensible, inside the reach of a sequence, and stay there.

"He's going to take one," she said.

Brom's eyes came round to her. "Where?"

"Left arm." She was already sure. "He'll want it where he can see it."

Wray's hand fell.

Edran came in harder than the first time, because the first time had told him the observer would give ground, and a fighter who gives ground can be pressed to the chalk. He came in with a fresh pair built at his mark, shell up on the left and edge on the right, and a sequence already running, and Lira saw Cael not give.

He stepped in.

Not far. Half a pace, when everything about the first exchange said half a pace back. It put him inside the arc of Edran's right arm, close enough that the first strike, the left shell driven at his chest, had to come short and cramped, and the framework took him round it by a finger's width as it burst on empty air. The second came down from above, the edge on the right forearm, the other half of the ready pair. Cael slipped it too. He slipped it by less than he could have, Lira saw. He could have gone out wide, out of the sequence altogether, and the third would have cut nothing but air.

He stayed.

Lira's hands closed on the rail. She did not mean them to.

He stayed for the third. She could see him wait for it, see the stillness come over his face that she had seen on him across a hundred circuit rings, when he had stopped trying to do anything and was only looking. The edge burst on Edran's right arm in its spray of light. For a sliver of a moment, so short she would not have seen it if she had not been watching Cael's eyes instead of Edran's arms, there was nothing on either of Edran's forearms at all.

Cael's eyes were on the bare right forearm, where the third would have to come. Not on Edran's face, or his feet, or the space the next strike would cut. On the empty arm.

Then the third edge came up on that arm, made cold, and arrived, and Cael moved a fraction late off it, on purpose, because he had been watching it form instead of watching it come, on purpose, and the edge went across the outside of his left forearm.

It did not go deep. Lira saw that at once, with the part of her that had stitched cuts on the Ardenmere circuit. A graze. A thin line opened across the outside of the arm a hand's width below the elbow, and a few beads of red came up along it, bright in the cold. But it had connected, cleanly, in front of three slates.

"Touch," said Wray. "Petitioner."

The north benches came up off their seats with a noise like a flock of birds lifting. The Edge floor students were shouting, and the second-year tout on the end of the near bench put his face in his hands, and somebody on the wall whistled.

Lira did not hear most of it. She was watching Cael.

He walked back to the south mark holding his forearm away from his body, the way you hold something you do not want to drip on your coat. He did not look at the cut. Not once. He walked with his eyes on Edran's forearms, where Edran was building a new pair at his mark, and he had the faint, distant look that she knew better than her own face in a glass.

He had found it. Whatever he had spent six mornings at the rail looking for, he had just paid a strip of skin to see it once from the inside, and he had seen it.

"He paid for something he already owned," Lira said.

Brom turned his head. "What?"

"He knew it was there. He's known for four days." She heard the edge in her own voice and did not try to take it out. "He just wanted to see it from where Edran's fist was. Because he doesn't trust the fourth day anymore."

"That's not stupid," said Brom slowly.

"I didn't say it was stupid." She let go of the rail and found her palms had gone white where it pressed them. "I said it cost him skin to learn something for the second time. That's him all over. Everybody else pays to learn a thing once."

Brom considered that, and then nodded, which from Brom was a long speech of agreement.

Along the rail, Lira saw that Karis's pen had stopped. It had been moving the whole time, through the first exchange and into the second, in long even passages. Now it had stopped, with the nib resting on the page, and Karis was not looking at the cut either. She was looking at Edran's right forearm, with her lips pressed together, exactly where Cael had looked.

Lira watched her for a moment and felt something she did not have time to name, part annoyance and part the thing that came after annoyance when it turned out the other person was right. Karis had seen it too. Of course she had. She had laid nine points on that boy not a fortnight ago and walked him into a drawing of a ring, and she would have laid them exactly so that he never reached his third.

Further along, at the gate, Gerda had unfolded her arms.

"He let it land," Gerda said, to nobody, in her flat procedural voice. "The edge. He could have gone wide."

"Yes," said Lira.

Gerda looked at her down the rail. "You knew he would."

"Before Wray dropped her hand."

Gerda considered that for a moment, and then folded her arms again and looked back at the ring. "Then he's not losing," she said. "He's buying."

Lira decided, in that moment, that she liked Gerda a great deal and would tell her so some other day when nobody's skin was involved.

On the front bench, Oona had both fists pressed into her slate so hard that her knuckles had gone white, and the first-year on her left was trying to tell her something, and she was not listening to him either. Her eyes were on Cael, on his arm and then his face, with the fierce, whole-faced attention she gave a sum she had not worked out yet.

A second-year leaned down from the bench above her. Lira knew him: one of the crust-wagering crowd from the stable fire. He said something to Oona, quick and wheedling, and Oona turned round on the bench and looked up at him.

"Ask him," said Oona clearly, in a voice that carried three rows. "Not me."

The second-year straightened up and went pink, and the girls on either side of him laughed, and Lira grinned for the first time since the frost.

In the middle of the ring, Wray had come forward to look at the cut. She looked at it the way she looked at everything, as a measurement, and then asked Cael something too quietly to carry. Cael shook his head. Wray looked at him a moment longer, then stepped back to the edge of the ring and wrote on her slate, and Lira understood that she had asked whether he wanted it bound before the third and he had said no.

Of course he had. He wanted the arm where he could feel it.

Lira looked at her own hands on the rail, at the white marks fading from her palms. Somewhere on the calendar in the main hall there was a line with her name on it and a date not very far off. A formal reassessment, two bouts, a panel, a file. She had not thought about it once all morning. She noticed that now, standing at the rail with the frost going off the ground round her boots, and decided that she would think about it tomorrow.

Today she had a different fight to read one exchange ahead.

She looked at the way Cael was standing on the south mark, the cut forearm held a little away from his side and his weight perfectly even, and she knew what the third exchange was going to look like before Wray had even risen from her chair.

"He's going to stop running away now," she said to the third-year on the bench behind her, without turning round.

---

Edran pressed to finish.

Cael had known he would. Four losses in three years, every one after the third exchange, every one because he had pressed to finish a bout that was going his way. He had a touch. He had two hundred people who had just seen him take it. He had the Edge floor on its feet. A fighter like Edran did not wait with all that in his hands.

Wray's finger fell, and Edran came across the ring with a full sequence called.

Four structures. Cael could see it in the set of his shoulders before the first was even up: the long sequence, the one meant to end things rather than ask questions. The ready pair first, rebuilt at his mark between exchanges: the shell on the left and the edge on the right, the held breath. Then the third, made cold. Then the fourth.

Cael let the first one land where it had to land.

It came at his chest, the same hard short push that had woken the fragment in the first exchange, and he did not try to slip it clean. He took it on the outside of his right forearm, angled, so that the shell burst along his arm instead of into it. The glitter went past his face like sleet, and most of the force went with it, and what was left he let go before anything in his chest had time to reach for it.

The second came from above, the edge on the right, down at his collarbone. The framework read it off Edran's shoulder and moved him inside it, half a pace to the right, and the edge went down past his left shoulder close enough to feel the chill.

Then it burst. Edran had to let it go to call the third, and it went in its spray of light, and for the length of a sliver, between the second breaking and the third forming, both of Edran's forearms were bare and his weight sat on a commitment that had just ended.

Cael was already moving.

He had not decided to move when the sliver opened. He had decided six mornings ago. The framework read the shoulder that was meant to carry the third structure, read that it had nothing yet to carry, and moved him in along the line it would have taken, into the space where Edran's arm was empty. It was the same thing the framework had done with every strike since Lira taught it to him without knowing. It read where something was about to be and put him beside it. Only this time the something was not a strike. It was the absence of one.

He put his right palm flat on Edran's breastbone.

He did not strike. He laid the hand there, firm, the way Lira had laid two fingers on the Force Path's chest at the edge of a ring in the second week, a touch that was not a blow and could not be mistaken for anything but a touch. Under his palm he felt Edran's heart going hard, and the third structure forming on Edran's forearm a sliver too late, bright and cold and finished, with nothing left in the world for it to do.

He stepped back out.

"Touch," said Wray. "Candidate."

For a moment the yard did not make any sound at all.

It was not spectacular. Cael had made sure of that, as he had made sure of everything that morning. No fragment had been shown beyond the framework the panel had logged twice. Nothing had flared or knocked or burst except the Glass. A boy had stepped into a gap and put his hand on another boy's chest and stepped out again. That was all anybody on the benches had seen, and most of them could not have said how he had got there.

Then the noise came, all at once, from every direction, and it was not like the noise after the second exchange. That had been a cheer for one side. This was the sound of two hundred people turning to the person beside them at the same moment with the same question.

Cael walked back to the south mark. His left forearm stung where the edge had crossed it, a thin cold line of heat. His heart was going nearly as hard as Edran's had been under his palm. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at Edran.

Edran stood where Cael had left him in the middle of the ring, with the third structure still on his right forearm, bright and useless. He was looking at it. He looked at the shell he had called a sliver too late as if it were a page in his own handwriting with an error on it he had never seen before.

Then he let it go, and it burst into glitter and was gone.

At the table, Quenna had lifted her pen from the ledger. "One touch each," she said. "Three exchanges are concluded. The bout stands—"

"A fourth."

Edran had not raised his voice. He did not need to. The whole yard was listening to him.

He had turned from the middle of the ring to face the three chairs, and he stood very straight, the way he had stood at the rail beside Cael on the open floor when he asked what the board was for. "The petitioner requests a fourth exchange, if the presiding assessor allows it."

Quenna looked at him across the frost-grey earth for a long moment. Then she looked at Wray, and Wray looked back at her and gave the very smallest nod, and Quenna looked at Cael.

Cael nodded too.

"A fourth exchange is allowed," said Quenna, "at the request of the petitioner, on the instructor's count." She wrote it down. "Take your marks."

Cael went back to the south mark and understood, as he went, that he had just learned more about Edran from those four words than from six mornings at the rail.

Most fighters, watching their best sequence fail against a gap they had not known they carried, would have needed a moment. They would have taken the draw Quenna was offering and walked away to think about it, and come back next term with the gap narrowed. Edran had not taken the moment. He had asked for another exchange at once, in front of everybody, with the gap still open in him and the whole school knowing it was there.

He wanted to fix it now. On the floor. While it was still costing him.

Cael set his feet.

---

Wray's finger fell, and Edran came across the chalk, and Cael saw at once that something had changed.

It was the timing of the shoulders. Six mornings of watching had taught him the rhythm of a sequence of four the way he knew the rhythm of the bells, and this one was wrong. The ready pair was up, rebuilt at the north mark, the shell on the left and the edge on the right. The shell went first, at his guard, and burst. The edge came next. And before it had burst, while it was still whole on Edran's right arm, Cael saw the left shoulder begin to load.

Edran was calling the third early. On the other arm. While the second still stood.

It was so simple that Cael almost laughed, and so expensive that he saw at once why three years of mornings had never tried it. The sliver lived where it lived because a full structure needed the whole of a Glass fighter's mind, and nobody had a whole mind to spare while another structure still stood. So Edran had stopped waiting for a whole one. He was building the third on his free left arm while the edge still held on the right, so that by the time the edge burst, the third was already half made. In the space of four words and one walk back to the north mark, he had found a way to fold the gap in on itself, and had decided to pay for it.

It narrowed the sliver to almost nothing.

Almost.

The framework had already read the shoulder and moved him out of the line of the second edge, and he saw, as he went, the cost of what Edran had done. A third structure called while the second still stood was a structure called with half a mind. It was coming up on Edran's left forearm thinner than the others, paler, not quite finished. It would be there sooner. It would not be as strong.

The second burst.

The gap opened, and it was half the width it had been in the third exchange, and there was a thin pale edge already rising in it on the left arm, where no third had ever come before.

A seam did not have to stay wide once you knew exactly where it lived.

Cael went in earlier than he had in the third exchange and lower, under the line of the half-made edge instead of beside it. He did not wait to see whether it would finish forming. He knew it would, a hair too late to stop him and a hair too soon to miss him entirely.

It finished forming as he passed it. The thin new edge came round with Edran's arm and caught him low on the right side, across the ribs, as he went by. It was a glancing thing, half formed and fast, and it did not cut so much as rake. It still hurt, a sudden hot line under the coat that drove the breath out of him.

His right palm was already on Edran's breastbone.

He held it there for a heartbeat, flat, firm, and felt Edran's heart under it, going as hard as ever. Then he stepped back.

Wray did not answer at once. She came to the edge of the ring and looked at Edran's half-formed edge, and at the line it had left in Cael's coat, and at Cael's hand, and her face did not change.

"Touch," she said. "Candidate. The petitioner's edge was unformed at contact. Glancing."

Quenna's hand came up from the ledger before Edran could open his mouth.

"That's the bout," she said. "Logged."

For a breath the yard was silent, with the silence of two hundred people who had come to see one particular thing and had not truly expected to be shown it.

Then the quiet broke, and it did not break into cheering, because Greyvane was not a place that cheered. It broke into the enormous, churning noise of two hundred people who all needed to say something to the person beside them at once. The Edge floor was on its feet and silent. The second-year tout was on his feet and not silent at all. The first-years on the tool shed roof were pounding it with their heels. Somebody on the wall was explaining the third exchange with both arms.

Cael stood at the centre of all that noise, ribs on fire, hands hanging loose, breath coming hard and white, and let it roll past him without trying to sort any of it.

He found, when he looked, that in the whole loud yard there were exactly three things that were still.

Wray, at the edge of the ring, writing on her slate as if the noise were weather.

Karis at the rail, with her pen stopped on the page, looking at him the way she looked at a timing that agreed with something she had written down before.

And Edran.

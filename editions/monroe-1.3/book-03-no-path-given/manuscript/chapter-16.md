# Chapter 16 — Three Pages

It was Brom's turn to be the witness, and he took it as seriously as everything else he did.

He came into section four two minutes after the fifth bell, unannounced, as the second clause allowed, and stood at the rail with his feet set and his hands clasped behind his back like a man attending a funeral he had been told was important. Karis looked up from her borrowed stool, nodded to him, and changed nothing. Quenna, at the chalk with her slate, did not look up at all.

"I'd like to do the read today," Cael said. "Not the framework."

"The read needs a body," said Quenna.

"Brom has one."

Quenna looked at Brom over the top of her slate, and Cael could see her turning the request over against the written refusal in the back of his binder, the one with her own initials on it. *The provision does not presently authorize regular partnered practice.*

"Standing still is not sparring," she said at last. "He may stand at the edge of reach and shift his weight. He may not strike, and you may not touch him. If either of you forgets that, the session ends and I write down why." She made a note on the slate. "Go on."

So Brom came over the rail into the section and stood two paces from Cael and shifted his weight wherever he liked, whenever he liked, doing nothing to hide it, and Cael stood still and let the read open and called it.

"Left. Heel."

"Forward. Both."

"Right. Toes."

Brom was a better body for it than Hobb had been, because Cael had read Brom for most of a year and knew the grain of him, and a worse one, because Brom also knew exactly what Cael was doing and kept trying, with a scowl of deep concentration, to beat it. He leaned one way and went the other. He started forward and stopped and went back. He stood perfectly still for a long count and then shifted with no warning at all. Cael called every one of them, and called most of them before Brom had properly decided, and after the twentieth Brom straightened up and said, with real indignation, "That isn't fair," and Lira was not there to laugh at him, so Cael did it for her.

Karis wrote throughout.

He had grown used to the sound of her pen in the weeks since the first session. It ran in long, even passages while he worked and stopped the moment he stopped, every time, as reliably as a mill wheel stops when the race is shut. He had stopped listening to it, the way he had stopped hearing the pipes in the residence wing.

So he noticed at once when it stopped while he was still working.

It happened on the twenty-sixth call. Brom had gone forward onto his left foot and Cael had named it, and somewhere in the naming the pen at the edge of the section went quiet. Cael called the next shift, and the next, and the pen stayed quiet, and then it started again in mid-phrase as if nothing had happened. He kept working. Eleven calls later it happened a second time, at no moment he could identify as special, and again it held still for the space of two calls and then went on.

When the session was done and Quenna had signed the strip, he went over to the stool.

"Clause six," he said.

Karis turned the notebook toward him without a word.

The page was dense with her small upright hand, columns of calls and times and Brom's weight in a shorthand he could not quite read. In the margin, at two places, she had drawn a short vertical stroke. Beside the first stroke was a page number, and beside the second the same number again.

"Your pen stopped," he said. "Twice. Not when I stopped."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She looked at the two strokes for a moment, as if deciding how much of the true answer was observable from outside.

"It stops when something I'm seeing now agrees with something I wrote down before," she said. "It doesn't happen very often. When it does, I stop writing for a moment so that I don't write down what I expect instead of what I see." She touched the page number. "That's the page I wrote at your first session, when you ran the framework with nobody striking. You make up your own strikes out of the air. So I timed the gap between your eyes settling on where a strike would come from and your feet going. Today I timed how far ahead of Brom's weight you called it." She turned the notebook back toward herself. "It's the same amount. A quarter of a breath, near enough. Two different things you do, with two different results, and they both lead by the same margin."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know yet." She capped the pen, with the small definite click. "That's why the pen stopped instead of writing a theory. Clause five. I can write that the margins match. I can't write why."

He looked at her, and she looked back, entirely calm, and he understood that she was telling him the truth, all of it, as far as it went.

"It fits something," he said.

"It fits something," she agreed. "When I know what, you'll read it first."

They walked out together, with Brom a little ahead of them, still muttering about fairness.

The afternoon had turned cold. The leaves on the hill below the wall had gone yellow at the edges in the last week, and the wind coming up over the training yard smelled of smoke from the town and of the first frost that had not arrived yet. Karis walked with her notebook held against her chest and her free hand in her pocket, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"You let him take your shoulder," Cael said at last. "Edran. In the third exchange."

"Yes."

"You could have slipped it."

"I could have." She did not seem to mind being asked. "In a standings bout, a clean hit taken costs one touch. If I'd slipped it, I'd have had to step north, and I'd been laying the north side for a whole exchange. A touch was cheaper than the side." She considered. "And I wanted to know how hard Glass hits when the shell's already formed. Now I do."

"And when he yielded?"

Karis was quiet for several steps.

"He read the floor correctly," she said. "Every line of it. He's very good." She looked out at the yellowing hill. "I'd rather he'd crossed."

"You wanted him burned?"

"No." She said it quickly and firmly, and turned her head to make sure he had heard it. "I wanted to know what Glass does to a channel. Whether a shell bursting outward breaks the line or only breaks itself. I'll never find out from him now, because he was sensible." She frowned faintly. "I wanted the result. I didn't want him hurt. Both of those are true at once. I'm not sure what to do with that, except write it down."

"Is that in the second notebook?"

"It will be tonight."

They reached the corner of the residence wing, where their ways divided. She stopped, and then, as if she had been deciding something for the whole length of the yard, she asked.

"You charted the bout. I saw you."

"Yes."

"May I see it?" She held up one hand before he could answer. "You can say no. None of the clauses cover what you write about me. I'm only asking."

He thought about it. The binder was the one thing the first clause kept from her, and she had promised never to ask for it, and he did not mean to make her break the promise by accident. So he opened it at the page, kept it turned toward himself, and read the chart aloud to her, line by line.

She listened standing in the cold, with her lips pressed together, all the way down: the two points at contact, the six behind her, the lag on every release, the nine on the floor, the channels, the yield, and at the bottom, *She didn't spend anything she couldn't spare.* She asked him to read the last line again, and he did. Then he closed the binder.

"That's accurate," she said. Something in her voice had changed, very slightly. "All of it. Even the lag."

"Is the lag a flaw?"

"Ternhall thought so." She looked at him with an expression he could not read at all. "You count the way I count," she said. "I didn't know anyone else did."

She went into the wing without another word, and he stood at the corner in the cold with the binder under his arm and the odd sense that he had been handed a compliment by someone who had never once in her life wasted one.

He wrote it up that night in a single line, because it was a single fact.

*Her pen has started stopping. Not when I stop. When something fits.*

---

Edran came to the rail the next afternoon.

Cael was at the edge of the open floor during the fifth bell, watching a pair of Storm Path fourth-years trade pressure across a chalk ring and trying to work out why the air between them kept smelling of rain. He did not see Edran come in. He only became aware that someone had stopped beside him at the rail, closer than a stranger would have stood and not as close as a friend, and when he looked round it was Edran, with his arms folded on the rail and his eyes on the Storm pair.

They watched together for a while without speaking.

"You were charting her," Edran said at last. He did not turn his head. "Yesterday. In the yard. I saw the binder."

"Yes."

"What did she spend?"

Cael considered the question, because it was a good one, and because it deserved an honest answer and not a careful one.

"Nothing I could see."

Edran let out a breath through his nose, slowly. "That's what I saw too." He watched one of the Storm fighters stagger and recover. "I've been trying all night to decide whether I yielded because I read the floor right or because I was afraid of how it would look written down. *Crossed a channel, touch. Crossed a second, touch.* In Wray's hand. With a date." He was quiet a moment. "I still don't know. I think I read the floor right. I'll never find out, because I didn't cross."

Cael said nothing. There was nothing to say to that which would not have been a kindness Edran had not asked for.

"You passed your second sitting," Edran said.

"Yes."

"With what?"

"Consistent, observable practice."

Edran's jaw moved. "That's the provision's language."

"It's what they wrote."

For the first time Edran turned his head and looked at him directly, and Cael saw that he was not angry. He was something harder to answer than angry. He looked like someone who had been carrying a heavy thing a long way and had just been told the road was a different length from the one on the map.

"I came to Greyvane at fifteen," Edran said, "and I've earned every line on that board, in public, bout by bout, against everyone they put across the chalk from me. Every one of those lines has a date and an assessor's initials beside it. I've been first since the end of my first year." He looked back at the ring. "Yesterday a transfer took it. I don't mind that the way you'd think. She beat me on the floor, in front of forty people, and the board wrote it down, and that's what the board is for. I can live with it. I'll take it back or I won't."

"Then what is it?"

Edran was quiet for so long that Cael thought he would not answer.

"What I can't work out," he said finally, "is what the board is for, if somebody can stand outside it." He straightened and took his arms off the rail. "That's all. That's the whole question. It isn't about you. It's about whether the thing I've spent three years climbing means what I thought it meant." He nodded once, the same slow nod he had given in the yard when he set the first question down, and walked off across the floor without looking back.

Cael watched him go, and then opened the binder at the Glass page and wrote one line in the space he had left there, under the question of what Edran believed.

*He's not asking what I am anymore. He's asking what the board is.*

---

Brom came back from the defensive floor that afternoon with chalk to his elbows and Karis's sixth notebook under his arm, and sat down on the end of Cael's bed without being asked, which he had never done before.

"I showed it to Wray."

Cael put down his pen. "The notebook?"

"The page. The struck one. *Can't test this. No body to test it on.*" Brom set the notebook on his knees and laid both hands flat on its cover, the way Karis had laid hers flat on the stable table. "I asked Karis first. She said a page she'd crossed out at fourteen didn't need protecting, and that if Wray wrote in the margin she wanted to see it." He paused. "Wray did write in the margin."

"What did she write?"

Brom opened the notebook at the page and turned it. Beneath the struck paragraph, below the younger hand's *Strike until there is one*, someone had added a line in a hand Cael did not know, square and plain, every letter separate.

*There is one now. Prove it. W.*

"She read the whole page first," Brom said. "Twice. Standing up, in the middle of the floor, with Hobb waiting and the fetch drill stopped. Then she asked me whose it was, and I told her, and she said, *Ternhall*, the way you'd say the name of a good knife." He closed the book again. "Then she said a fourteen-year-old with no body to test it on had got further than half the instructors she'd trained under, and that if I meant to prove it I'd better start keeping numbers like the fourteen-year-old did, because she wasn't redrawing a curve on the strength of my feelings."

"Are you going to?"

"I started this afternoon." Brom took a folded sheet from inside his coat. It was ruled into columns in his large square capitals. *Day. Opponent. Opened myself up: how many times.* There was one line filled in. *Hobb. Fetch. 9.*

"Nine," said Cael. "You said eleven, the first week."

"Eleven the first week. Nine today." Brom folded the sheet away again with great care. "That's not proof of anything. Wray said so. It's one number. But it's a number, and it's going down, and I've never once in my life written down a number about myself that was going in the right direction." He looked at the notebook on his knees. "She gave me a way to keep count. Karis did. She didn't mean to. She was fourteen and crossing out her own page."

He stood, and tucked the notebook back under his arm, and went to the door.

"I'll give it back to her at the end of the month," he said. "With my marks in it. She asked for them."

---

At supper, Lira had news, and for once she did not seem to enjoy it.

"Edran went to the provost's clerk this morning," she said, sliding onto the bench with her tray. "Before the first bell. He asked for a form and took it away with him. Nobody knows which form."

"Somebody always knows which form," said Brom.

"Somebody does." Lira tore her bread. "Nobody's telling me, which is new, and I don't like it."

Gerda was at the next table, alone, as she usually was, eating with the careful economy of someone who had once been hungry for a long time and did not intend to waste anything again. She did not look up. But after a moment she spoke, to her bowl, in the flat calm voice she used for procedure.

"If it's the form I think it is, it'll be posted."

Lira turned round on the bench. "Posted where?"

"On the board in the main hall. Petitions of standing are always posted, within a day of filing, all of them, in full." Gerda set down her spoon. "It's the rule that protects whoever files one. If a petition's posted, everyone can see it was asked, and nobody can quietly lose it in a drawer."

"You know a lot about petitions," said Lira.

"I filed one once." Gerda picked up her spoon again. "At the other place."

"What happened?"

Gerda ate a mouthful of barley, slowly, before she answered.

"It was posted," she said. "And then it was answered."

She did not say anything more, and Lira did not ask, and after a while Gerda finished her bowl and carried it to the hatch and left, tall and narrow and very straight, without looking back. Lira watched her go with an expression Cael had seen on her only when she was looking at someone across a ring whom she had begun to respect.

"Bitten before," she said quietly, to nobody.

Brom looked at Cael across the table, and Cael knew that Brom was remembering the same thing he was: a stable evening some weeks ago, Brom's spoon pointing at the dark window, and Brom saying, *Whatever he does next, he'll do it by the book, in daylight, with his name signed at the bottom, and you'll see it coming a week off.*

"A week off," said Brom.

"A bit less."

"He's quick," Brom conceded, and went back to his barley.

---

It went up the next morning, before the first bell.

Cael saw the crowd before he saw the paper. The main hall was full of students who should have been at assembly, standing three and four deep in front of the board where the public assessment calendar hung, and the noise they made was the low, busy noise of people reading something aloud to each other in pieces. Several of them turned as he came in. Then more did, and a space opened in front of the board, not because anyone stepped back on purpose but because nobody wanted to be standing in the place where he would have to stand.

He stood in it.

The petition was three pages, pinned in a row with brass tacks beside the calendar, written in a clear, careful hand that did not shake. Across the top of the first page, in capitals:

*Petition of Standing. Filed by Edran, Glass Path, Iron Rank Two, third year. Concerning the supervised floor access of the candidate enrolled under the demonstration provision.*

He read it all, from the first line to the last, before he let himself think anything about it.

The first page set out the provision. It did so accurately. It quoted the half page Cael had read more times than he had counted in the archive, and then explained, in plain sentences, what the provision was for: the academic exposure and supervised training of candidates still waiting on their formal Kindling assessment, which was to say, in nearly every case, children. It noted that such candidates were assessed monthly by demonstration, and that the standard was consistent, observable practice. It observed, correctly, that a child who had not yet Kindled would ordinarily have very little to practise.

The second page set out the record. It did that accurately too. It noted the dates of two closed sittings and the finding of each, *assessment satisfied*, which the public calendar had recorded. It noted that the candidate had been seen at the rail of every floor in the building, filling a binder, and had been present at the standings bouts of the cohort's leading students. It said, without any heat at all, that whatever the candidate had demonstrated to the panel in closed session, it could not have been the practice of a child without a Path, because the panel had found it consistent and observable and the provision did not reward nothing. And then it drew its conclusion, in a sentence Cael read three times.

*The petitioner therefore submits that the candidate is evidently a practitioner of some classification, which the faculty has elected not to disclose, and that his enrollment under a provision designed for those without one places him outside the framework by which every other student's standing is measured.*

The third page made the request. It asked that the candidate's supervised floor access under the provision be suspended pending a review of his enrollment; or, in the alternative, that the candidate be assessed under the same framework as students of full standing, in public, so that the academy's standings might continue to mean what every enrolled student had been told they meant. And then, beneath it, in a last paragraph that Cael could hear in Edran's voice as clearly as if he had been standing at the rail, the real grievance, where petitions always put it.

*The petitioner has earned his standing at this academy in public, over three years, under its framework, and does not dispute any result that framework has produced. He asks only whether the framework is still the measure. If a student may stand outside it and be found satisfactory, then every student who has met it honestly is entitled to know by what measure they now stand.*

It was signed at the bottom of the third page, *Edran*, in the same hand that did not shake, with the date.

Somebody small pushed in at his elbow. He looked down, and it was Oona, with her slate under her arm and her tag on its string, reading the first page with her lips moving.

"Is that about you?"

"Yes."

She read on. She was a slow and thorough reader, and he waited for her. Somewhere behind the lecture wing the first bell began to ring, slow and unhurried, and the crowd came apart reluctantly round them, in twos and threes, still talking, until the hall had nearly emptied and the two of them stood alone at the board with the brass tacks. When she reached the bottom of the third page she frowned at it with her whole face.

"Is it true?"

He thought about it. He had told her he would not lie to her, and he found, standing there in the emptying hall, that the promise was easier to keep than he had feared, because the honest answer was the simplest one.

"Nearly all of it."

"Which part isn't?"

He put one finger beside the sentence on the second page, not on it, as Prynn had put her finger beside the routing code.

"That part. Where he thinks the faculty is hiding what my Path is."

Oona read the sentence again. "Aren't they?"

"No," said Cael. "There isn't one to hide."

Oona looked at the sentence, and then up at him, and he watched her work it through, the way she had worked through *shattered* in the taxonomy lecture: where it went, why it wasn't on the chart.

"Oh," she said at last. "Then he's asking the wrong question."

"No. He's asking a fair question." Cael looked at the three pages, at the careful hand and the brass tacks and the signature at the bottom. "He's only got one fact wrong in the middle of it. Everything else is right."

"Will you tell him?"

The bell had stopped. Somewhere above them a door shut, and feet went quickly along a corridor toward assembly, late.

Cael did not answer her straight away. He read the conclusion on the second page one more time, the way he had once read a category line three times over a cup of tea going cold, and found that he did not resent a word of it. Edran had asked in the open, in the yard, in front of a dozen people. Now he had asked again, in ink, in front of everyone, with his own name signed at the bottom, by the book and in daylight. It was the most honest thing anyone at Greyvane had done about him, apart from a girl who had walked into an archive to ask permission. It was right about the provision, and right about the children, and right about the board, and right about him in every particular but one.

The one was the only one that mattered, and nobody had ever written it down.

"I think I'll have to," he said.

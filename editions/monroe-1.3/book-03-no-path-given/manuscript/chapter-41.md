# Chapter 41 — The Bench

Lira walked with him as far as the top of the hill and then stopped.

It was a hard bright morning, the frost still white in the shadow of the gate and the town below them sharp in the cold air, every roof and chimney drawn clean against the slope as if somebody had inked it. The road went down from Greyvane's gate in long turns between stone walls, past the draper's and the bakehouse and the street worth walking, to the corner at the bottom where the grey building stood with its board over the door. From up here it looked very small. He had been looking at it from the wall every evening since the autumn and had never once let himself look at it for long.

"I'm stopping here," said Lira.

"You can come down."

"I know I can. Somebody ought to be at the top of the hill." She sat down on the low wall beside the gate with her coat round her and her knees drawn up, as she sat on the other wall at dusk. "You told her you'd be at the top of the hill. Then she asked you to come down. So now you're both, and I'm the top." She looked at him. "Go on. You'll be late, and she'll have it written on her slate that you were."

He went down.

He took the road at the pace he would have taken it to any errand, and he noticed partway down that his feet had slowed without being asked, and he made them go on at the same pace, and they did. The town was waking round him, shutters coming open, a cart backing into the bakehouse yard, the draper's boy sweeping the step under the post-counter sign. Somebody was frying onions. It was an ordinary Saturday in an ordinary town, and the closer he came to the corner the more ordinary it was, and none of it helped at all.

The grey building was two storeys of plain dressed stone with a slate roof and a single door, painted dark green and recently. The board over it said *Registered Arbiter Station. Certification and Evaluation.* in square black letters that somebody had also recently painted. The door stood open. Inside was a short flagged passage with a door at the far end, shut, and along the left-hand wall of the passage, under a window of thick old glass, a wooden bench.

He smelled it from the doorway. Soap, and the wet stone of a floor scrubbed that morning, and under it a smell of lamp oil and paper.

He stood on the step with his good hand on the door frame, and the bench in Denvash came up out of him all at once and stood in the passage in front of the real one, the way a reflection stands in a dark window with the room behind it.

He had sat on the end nearest the far door. His feet had not quite reached the floor flat, so he had rested them on their edges, and he had looked at the grain of the boards between his shoes for the whole of a morning because looking anywhere else had been worse. Hesk had sat beside him with his big careful hands on his knees and said nothing, and every so often he had shifted his weight so that the bench creaked, which Cael had understood even then was on purpose, a sound to say *I'm still here*. There had been other people on the bench, a girl with a ribbon and a boy with a cough and their families, and one after another they had been called and gone in and come out with their faces changed, and the families had stood up and made the small glad noises families make, and gone. And Cael had sat, and been called last, and Hesk's hand had come down on his shoulder, here, and stayed there until he stood.

He stood on the step of the grey building in a strange town with the soap in his nose and his heart going hard, and he could feel the exact weight of that hand.

Then the far door of the passage opened and a clerk looked out, and the morning was this morning again, and Oona and her mother were sitting on the bench.

---

Oona was in the middle of it, in a clean grey dress he had never seen her wear and her hair pinned so tight it pulled at her temples. Her slate was on her knees, face down, and both hands were pressed flat on top of it. Her mother sat on her left, very straight, in the dark travelling cloak, with her hands folded in her lap. There was nobody else on the bench. It was the only Kindling the station had that morning, the clerk said, and he said it kindly.

"You came down," said Oona.

"I said I would."

"You said the top of the hill first." She did not look at him. She was looking at the far door. "I'm not saying you're late. You're not late. I'm only saying."

"Lira's at the top of the hill," said Cael. "So it's covered."

Oona considered that, and seemed to find it acceptable, and made room on the bench on her right without being asked, by moving a finger's width toward her mother, which was all the room there was. He sat down. The bench was the same height as the one in Denvash and he was not the same height, and his feet were flat on the stone now, and that was a strange thing to notice, and he noticed it.

Oona's mother turned her head and looked at him past her daughter. She had Oona's grave dark eyes in an older face, and the same way of looking at a person as if he were a sum that had been set her.

"You're the one with the strange kind," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"She's written about you every week since the autumn." The woman's voice was low and level, and it did not seem to care whether the clerk heard. "She says you've never once told her anything that wasn't so."

"I try not to."

"That's rarer than people think." She looked at him a moment longer. "Thank you for coming down."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and she seemed to think that was the right answer, and turned back to the far door.

They waited.

It was not long, as waits went, and it was the longest he had sat still since the formal yard. The passage was cold, and the window of thick glass let in a pale wavering light that lay across the flagstones. The soap smell did not fade. Somewhere behind the far door a chair was moved, and somebody coughed, and paper rustled, and once there was a low hum like a struck bell held against a hand, which made Oona's whole body go still, and then it stopped. He had heard that hum before. He had heard it in Denvash through a door like this one, the sound of an Arbiter's instrument being made ready, and his own hands had gone cold on his knees then, as they went cold now.

He made himself breathe. He looked at the grain of the bench between his knees and then made himself look up from it, because he was not fourteen and the morning was not his. He looked at Oona instead. She was sitting perfectly upright with her hands pressed on the slate, staring at the door, and she was so frightened that her lips had gone pale, and she was not letting any of it out.

"Can I ask you something?" she said, very low, without moving her eyes.

"You can ask."

"Was it quiet? Inside you. You said it goes very quiet."

"Very quiet," said Cael. "Like a held breath. And then it isn't, and you'll know. You won't have to be told."

"And it doesn't hurt."

"It doesn't hurt. Your mother's right."

"My uncle says it's like being hit by a door."

"Your uncle's wrong," said Cael, and Oona's mother made a small sound on the far side of her that might, in another room, have been a laugh.

The far door opened. The clerk stood in it with a ledger open on one arm, and he looked at the bench, and then at the ledger, though there was only one name that could be on it that morning.

"Oona," he said.

Her mother's hand came down on her left shoulder. Cael put his right hand on her right shoulder, flat, the way Hesk had done it, and left it there. Under his palm her shoulder was rigid as a post, and very thin, and he could feel the whole of her trembling, very finely, under the clean grey cloth. He did not press. He only let the hand be heavy, so that she could feel it was there.

She sat for a breath, two breaths, with both their hands on her.

Then she stood up, and they took their hands away as she rose, not before. She picked her slate up off the bench and held it against her chest in both arms, and walked down the flagged passage to the far door, and did not look back, and went in, and the door shut behind her.

Her mother let out a long breath that she seemed to have been holding since the coach.

---

He did not know, afterward, how long they sat there. Long enough for the pale light to move a hand's width along the flags. Long enough for the bakehouse cart to come back up the street outside with a clatter of empty trays. Not as long as he had sat in Denvash. Nowhere near.

But Denvash sat with him the whole time. It did not come as a memory of the kind you can choose to look at and then put away; it came into his body, without asking. His knees remembered the bench. His hands, pressed flat on them, remembered the cold. His shoulder remembered the hand. His stomach remembered the long quiet, the quiet that had gone on past where it should have stopped, the clerk's face when he came back to the door to call Hesk in, and the low voices behind the door afterward, and the word they had for him that was not a Path. Then the walk home with Hesk's hand on his shoulder all the way, through streets that had not changed and somehow had. And the courier on the second morning, knocking once, with the notice in his satchel. And the bag on the bed that evening, and the three things Hesk had set beside it one at a time, and the long walk out of the city in the grey of the next morning with Hesk standing at the workshop door and not waving, because waving would have been for Hesk and not for him.

All of it went through him on a bench in a strange town while a girl he had known for one term was behind a green door finding out what she was. He let it. He had learned in the formal yard that some things are better let through than fought. He sat with his hands on his knees and his shoulder remembering a hand, and let Denvash go through him all the way, from the bench to the road, and come out the other side.

When it had, he was still sitting on a bench in the grey building, and it was still this morning, and Oona's mother was looking at him.

"You've done this before," she said. "Sat here."

"Not here. Somewhere like it."

"It was bad." It was not a question, and she did not make it one. "She said it was the strange kind, and that it was rare. She didn't say what came after." The woman looked at the far door. "I could see on your face what came after. You don't have to tell me."

"It's a long time ago."

"A year and some," said Oona's mother. "She told me how old you are." She was quiet a moment. "That isn't a long time ago. That's last winter, near enough, to somebody my age. You came down anyway."

He did not have anything to say to that either, and this time she did not seem to need him to.

The far door opened.

Oona came out with her slate held against her chest in both arms, as she had gone in, and her face was white and very calm, and her eyes were enormous. She walked down the flagged passage at the same careful pace she had walked up it, and stopped in front of the bench, and looked at her mother, and then at Cael, and then at her mother again.

"Anchor," she said. "Anchor Path. Copper."

Her mother stood up and put both arms round her, slate and all, and held on.

Oona let herself be held, with her chin on her mother's shoulder, looking past it at Cael, and he saw her face change over that shoulder. It did not break. It came loose, a little at a time, from the careful calm she had walked out with, until underneath it there was a fourteen-year-old girl who had been afraid for seven weeks and two days. The instrument behind the green door had just told her that she was exactly who she had decided to be.

"It was quiet," she said, into her mother's cloak. "It was very quiet. And then it wasn't. And I knew." She pulled back a little. "It's on the chart. Anchor's on the chart. It's in the hardening family, between Shield and Wall. I've known where it was since the first week." She looked at Cael again. "It's ordinary."

"It's ordinary," said Cael.

"I said it would be."

"You said it would be in the first week," said Cael. "You've been right about everything."

She nodded, once, as she did when a sum came out. Then she turned the slate over in her arms and looked at it and did not write anything on it, which he had never seen her do before. She was holding a small card in the same hand as the slate, pressed against its frame, just as he had once held a card of his own that said nothing anybody could read, and she did not show it to him and he did not look at it. It was hers. It was the first thing she had ever owned that nobody else would ever be allowed to read, and she held it as if she already knew that.

The clerk was writing in his ledger at the far door. Cael watched him write, and knew without having to see the page what the line would be, because he had copied out the gloss of the category on his own form three times over cold tea in the first week of the term. *Has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.* Somewhere in the clerk's ledger, and by the end of the week in a regional book at the bottom of a long road, and on Monday morning at the intake desk at the top of the hill, Oona was being moved out of a category she no longer fitted, by the plainest procedure there was. The observer track at Greyvane had held two names since the autumn. On Monday it would hold one.

None of that troubled him. He was glad, in a large plain way that surprised him with its size. The category had never been built for him. He had borrowed it, and everybody at the intake table had known he was borrowing it. And here was the person it had actually been built for, walking out of it on the far side with a card in her hand and her mother's arms round her. Whatever happened to his own name on that roll, the thing had worked once this winter as somebody long ago had meant it to.

---

They walked back up the hill together, the three of them, Oona between her mother and Cael and her slate in her arms, and nobody said very much.

Halfway up, by the draper's, Oona stopped.

"Are you sorry?" she said.

He looked at her.

"That I'm not on the track any more." She was looking at the road in front of her boots. "It's only you now. I worked it out. They'll change my tag on Monday, and then it's just you with the hollow circle."

He thought about it properly, as he had promised her in the first fortnight he always would.

"A little," he said. "I'll miss having somebody on the bench next to me at the taxonomy lecture. And it was good to have another name on the roll." He looked up the road toward the gate. "But mostly I'm glad. It was built for you. It was never built for me. You used it the way it's meant to be used, and now you've walked out of it the way it's meant to be walked out of. That's what it's for."

Oona thought about that for several steps.

"You said I could study you," she said. "After. In the second month. You said after my Kindling, if I still wanted to."

"I did."

"I still want to. I'll have to sit on a different bench in the taxonomy lecture now. With the hardening first-years. But I'll still study you." She glanced at her mother. "With permission."

"With permission," said Oona's mother gravely, and did not explain what she meant by it.

Lira was where she had said she would be, sitting on the low wall by the gate with her knees drawn up, and she did not get down when they reached the top of the hill. She looked at Oona, and at her face, and at the card in her fingers that Oona had not put away.

"Well?" said Lira.

"Anchor," said Oona. "Copper. It's ordinary."

Lira looked at her from the wall, with an expression Cael had seen on her face once before, on the morning she had read four paragraphs from Fenmark and decided they did not get to say who she was.

"Ordinary's the best kind," said Lira. "Nobody can take it off you. It's on every chart there is." She slid down off the wall. "Congratulations. You'll want breakfast. Everybody wants breakfast afterward. I'm going to steal you some bread and give you most of it back."

Oona looked at her with something close to alarm.

"She does that," said Cael. "It's the rule."

"I know," said Oona. "I've seen it. I didn't know it was going to be me."

---

On the Tuesday of the eighteenth week Karis asked if she might try two things, and asked it at breakfast, formally, before the porridge, so that everybody at the scarred table could refuse it in front of witnesses if they wanted to.

"Both inside the terms," she said. "Contact only. Brom's post. Lira's buckets. I write nothing." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. "I'd only like to know what *single channel* means. It's in the notice. I'd like to know whether it's true."

Nobody refused, and at the sixth bell they were in the quiet room again with the post in its sand.

The first thing she asked was whether he could light two at once. She did not mean two in a row with the count between; she meant two together, his palm and his fingertips, one ring under the heel of his hand and another under the pads of his fingers, at the same instant. He chose both sizes, and held both in his mind, and turned inward, and asked.

One ring came, under the heel of his hand. Under his fingertips the oak was clean.

He tried it the other way round, fingertips first in his mind, and one ring came under the fingertips and nothing under the heel. He tried it a third time, wanting both equally, holding the two sizes side by side like two coins in one palm. This time he felt the thing in him hesitate for the smallest part of an instant. Then one ring came, under the heel, and the cold came after it as it always did, and not one hair more.

"One," said Karis softly. "Whatever you ask for, it's one." She had knelt by the post to look at the clean wood under where his fingertips had been. "It doesn't divide. It isn't weaker when you ask for two. It simply picks."

"Single channel," said Cael.

"Single channel. The notice was right again. I can lay sixteen. You can lay one. And yet." She did not finish the sentence, and he did not ask her to.

The second thing she asked for was paper. She had brought a sheet from the back of one of her old notebooks, blank, torn along the fold, and she laid it flat over the top of the post and held it there by its corners with her fingertips well clear of the middle.

"Through it," she said. "If you can. Your palm on the paper, the paper on the wood. I'd like to know which one it belongs to."

He laid his palm on the paper and chose the size and asked.

The paper caught. Not the wood under it; the paper. A ring of brown went through the sheet under his palm, and in the middle of it a bead of flame came up and began at once to eat outward across the page, fast, as paper burns. Karis let go of the corners, and Brom put it out with a scoop of sand before it had reached the edge. When they brushed the sand away the oak beneath was barely marked, a faint brown shadow where the heat of the burning paper had warmed it and nothing more.

Karis bent close and looked at the shadow on the oak.

"It belongs to whatever you're touching," she said. "Not to whatever's under that. Not to what you meant." She picked up the scrap of charred paper and turned it over. "That's the spark again. You strike it on the first thing your hand is on, and after that it's the paper's business, or the wood's, or whatever's there."

"Is that a second word?"

"No." Karis folded the burnt scrap in half, and then in half again, and did not throw it away. "It's the same word with more evidence under it. I'll have a better one some day. I can feel it sitting somewhere just past where I can reach it, like a name you've forgotten." She looked up at him. "I'm not going to force it. A wrong word's worse than none. You'd carry it about for years thinking it was true."

Lira, at the bucket of water, had said nothing through all of it. Now she looked at the scrap in Karis's fingers.

"You're keeping that," she said.

"I'm not writing anything," said Karis. "That's the term. It doesn't say I can't keep a piece of paper." She tucked it into her coat. "It's the first thing he's ever lit that I gave him. I'd like to have it."

He wrote the session in the binder that evening, reading it aloud to her as he wrote, as he had promised.

*Tuesday, week eighteen. Asked for two at once, three ways. Always one. It doesn't split and it doesn't weaken; it picks. Single channel. Then through a sheet of K.'s paper laid on the post: the paper burned, not the wood. It belongs to whatever my hand is on. Count between, ninety-four. K. kept the paper.*

---

At the regional registry office, on the Wednesday after the girl came back up the hill, a clerk with a cold that had not gone away since the autumn entered one line in a book under the heading for Greyvane Academy. The notation sheet had come down from the town station by the station's own courier on the Monday.

*Kindling assessment entered. Candidate formerly enrolled under demonstration provision. Classification: Anchor Path, Copper. Category of enrollment vacated; standard enrollment to follow.*

That night the line went into the long index with all the others and was run against every standing file, as every line always was, and it matched nothing. But it was copied, the next morning, onto the short daily sheet that went to the officer who had asked to see anything the registry received about that academy, because he had asked, a fortnight before, for exactly that. It reached Coss at his borrowed desk on the second floor at a little after the third bell, between a bound volume of enforcement digests and a sheaf of drafting records tied with faded tape, and he read it.

He had been working on the packet at the regional office since the autumn, longer than he had ever spent on any challenge.

The briefing had taken one afternoon. Everything after it had taken the season, and he had known that it would, and had not minded. A challenge to a chartered academy's enrollment was not a request for papers. It went before a magistrate in public and into the permanent registry, and every line of it would be read aloud by people who wanted it to be wrong. So every line had to stand on something that could be produced in a room and handed across a table. He had spent the season finding those somethings, one at a time, from offices that kept their papers in the order that suited them and not in the order that suited him.

The plain-face argument had been sound from the first night, at the waystation table, with its two true lines. A category whose gloss kept it, word for word, for a candidate who *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment*. A respondent who had undergone it, at the station in Denvash, on a date the registry held. That much he could have filed from his district office. But a tribunal did not like to be told that a provision meant what its plainest sentence said, if another sentence of the same provision said something wider; it liked to be shown. So he had gone looking for what the provision had been written to do.

The drafting records had been in three places, none of them where the index said. He had found them at last in a basement room under the regional chamber, in a box marked for a different provision entirely, and he had read them in an afternoon by a window that let in more cold than light. The demonstration provision had been written long ago, and it had been written as a kindness. The guilds in those days had sent their families travelling, carters and drovers and pedlars with a guild licence, and their children came to fourteen on the road, a month from any station, and arrived at the gates of the academies untested and unplaced. A chartered academy could not take an untested child. The provision let it, under watching, until the child could be brought to a station and found out. That was all it was for. It had been amended three times since, and every amendment had widened who it covered by a little and narrowed what it was for by not at all.

He had sat with that in the cold basement room. He had thought it was a decent law. He thought so still.

Then he had found the precedents, which had been easier, because the enforcement digests were indexed by people who expected to be asked. Three times in other districts, across the long life of the provision, somebody had tried to stretch it over a person it had not been written for. Once it had been a youth past the age of Kindling who had refused to be tested. Once it had been a disputed assessment the family would not accept. Once it had been a clerk's honest mistake, never caught until it was challenged. Three academies, three enrollments, three challenges. Each enrollment had been voided. None had survived the hearing, and one had not survived to a hearing at all. He had read each digest twice and copied the citations onto a clean sheet in his small plain hand.

And now this line, on the short daily sheet, from the academy at the top of the hill.

*Candidate formerly enrolled under demonstration provision.* A child, then, a real one, of the kind the drafters had meant. A child who had come to Greyvane untested and sat under watching and gone down to the station at fourteen and been found to be something ordinary on every chart there was, and moved out of the category by the plainest procedure there was, exactly as the provision had been built to do. Coss read the line a second time and thought that if he had been asked to invent an exhibit to show a tribunal what the provision was *for*, he could not have invented a better one. The academy had sent it to him itself, on the third-day post, because the procedure said it must.

He did not take any satisfaction in it. He noted it. That was different.

The roll of the observer track at Greyvane, after this line, held one name.

---

"You're sure you want to go up yourself?"

The senior officer who had briefed him in the autumn asked it from the doorway of the borrowed room at the end of the afternoon, with his coat already on. He was a heavy, courteous man near the end of a long career, who signed whatever was put in front of him after reading every word of it, and Coss had come over the season to like him a good deal.

"I'm sure," said Coss.

"The standard procedure is service by the regional channel. A sealed packet to the provost by courier, a copy to the magistrate's office, the date set from there." The senior officer looked at the packet on the desk, which was now as thick as a hand and tied in two directions. "It's a good packet. I've read it. It will stand up by itself. You don't need to walk it up a hill."

"No," said Coss. "I don't need to."

He did not try to explain the rest, because the rest did not fit on a form, and the senior officer had been in the service long enough to know that a man who said *I don't need to* in that voice was not finished. A packet sent by courier was read first by a provost alone at his desk, and then explained to its subject secondhand, by people who loved him and were frightened for him and would explain it in their own words. Secondhand bred errors. Errors bred appeals. And under that, where he did not need to look at it for long, there was a borrowed office in Ardenmere and a boy with his hands pressed white on his knees saying *Thank you*. Coss had stamped a summons closed-pending that afternoon with more force than a stamp required. He did not intend to do this one at the end of a courier's arm.

"The subject attends a proceeding on his own status by right," he said instead, which was true and fitted on a form. "I'd prefer he hear it from the officer who drafted it, in the room, with the provost present. It saves a step later."

The senior officer looked at him a while.

"I'll sign it," he said. "Put the request in tonight."

He put it in that night, in the flat language the form demanded, and it came back approved by the middle of the next morning. That was faster than the senior officer could have signed it and sent it up and had it back, and so it had not gone through the senior officer at all. It had gone to the same desk as before, somewhere above, where somebody had been waiting. The registry returned the challenge packet to him stamped for service. On its cover sheet the last field before the seal was the one he had stopped expecting to read: the short ruled bar, as neat as a line drawn under a sum, and the small type beneath it. *Field restricted. Recipient clearance insufficient.* He tied the packet up again in both directions and left it tied.

The credential came the same day, from a different window. It was a small card, stamped, plainly printed, his name and his grade and the seal of the Compact's field service, the sort of card that did not need to be large because the people it was shown to had been taught to recognize it. He had carried one for many years and almost never shown it. A Warden was a word a district would accept at its gate without thinking twice. A field agent of the Compact was a word that made a gate think. He had found, over a long career, that the first word got more honest work done than the second, and he had let it.

He would show the card at Greyvane, at the gate and again in the provost's room, because the procedure required that the respondent know exactly what kind of officer was standing in front of him. That, too, he did not mind. He had been honest with the boy every time they had sat across a desk, as honest as the job allowed, and the job now allowed a little more.

An aide was assigned him the same afternoon, a young man two years out of the regional school who could carry cases and copy fair and did not yet know when to stop talking. Coss liked him well enough. He told him they would leave before dawn on the Saturday and reach the academy on the Monday, two days by the coach to the hill town and half a morning on the road up from there. The aide was to bring the bound digests and the drafting records and a great deal of clean paper. He would not need anything to read on the coach, Coss told him, because there would be the file.

That evening he wrote to his daughter, as he did every week, a short letter about nothing: the regional town's bad bread, a chamber bell that rang the hours wrong, the senior officer's coat. He did not mention the case, and never had. At the bottom, after he had signed it, he sat for a moment with the pen over the page.

*I'm going up into the hills for a few weeks,* he added. *It's a long road. I'll write from the other end.*

He sealed it and put it in the evening bag. Then he went back to the borrowed desk and untied the challenge packet after all, and read it again from the first page, slowly, as if he had never seen the subject's name before.

# Chapter 55 — Not Allowed to Hit Anybody

Lira had been told three times that she was not allowed to hit anybody, and by the second hour she had begun to keep a list of the people she was not allowed to hit.

Quenna had told her first, on the stair the week before, in the voice she used for the record. Gerda had told her second, at supper, with the exact words of the procedure: a member of the gallery who disturbed the sitting might be removed by the clerk, and a cough counted, if the clerk decided it counted. Cael had told her third, at dusk in the swept yard, by asking her to write it down, which was worse than being told. So she sat on the second bench from the front at the left, by the aisle, directly behind Oona's straight back and polished tag, with her arms folded so that her hands would have somewhere to be, and she kept the list in her head instead.

The tout was on it. He was four rows back and to the right, with his crust book open on his knee, and an hour into the sitting he had started taking wagers again in a voice he thought was low. She had heard the odds. Two second-years by the windows were on it, because every time the Warden, Coss, laid another paper down, one of them breathed *that's it, then* to the other, as if they had been asked. The man from the guild's legal office was on it for the way he smiled at his own pen. And the Warden was at the top of it. That was not because he had done anything she could have named. It was because he stood at that table in his road-coloured coat and laid papers down one after another in his patient voice, and every paper was true. She could feel the room coming to believe each one as it landed, and there was nothing whatever to hit.

She had watched a great many fights from a great many benches. She knew the sound a crowd made when the fighter it had come to cheer started losing slowly, and the room was making it now. It was not a noise so much as a change in the quiet. At the first bell the benches had been bright and tight with people come to see their own boy win something. She had heard it in the passage, in the second-years' good coats, in the tout's first odds. Now, two papers in, the brightness had gone flat and uneasy, and people had begun to shift on the benches and look at their hands.

Cael had not moved at all. He sat at the bare table with his hands in his lap and his eyes on the Warden, and from where she sat she could see the back of his head and one ear and the set of his shoulders, which were where she had put them the evening before. She watched them the way she would have watched a fighter's hips. They did not come up once.

*Good,* she thought. *Stay there.*

---

At the second hour the magistrate said, "The panel will hear the witnesses of fact entered for the respondent, at the hour named," and the academy's counsel stood up.

He was a narrow grey man with a satchel, engaged from the regional town in the autumn, and Lira had never heard him say anything before. He said very little now. He named Edran, and Edran came out of the front of the gallery through the gap in the rope. He walked to the place on the open floor where witnesses stood, a square of boards marked at its corners with chalk, and stood in it with his arms at his sides.

She had not liked Edran for most of a term. She had changed her mind about that slowly and against her will, starting on the morning he stood in the formal yard and withdrew his petition in his own hand. She had not told anybody she had changed it, least of all him. She watched him now with the old dislike and the new respect sitting side by side in her, neither one giving way.

Counsel asked him to say what he had seen.

Edran said it. He said it as he had said he would, plainly, in the flat honest voice that had always made him sound a little older than he was. He had watched every one of the candidate's sittings that anybody was let in to see. He had fought him once, in the exhibition, in the formal yard, under standings rules, and lost. He described the loss without softening it: the gap in his own sequence where his third structure came up cold, and the way the candidate had found it on the second exchange and gone into it, and found it again. He had watched the match against Karis from the bench by the wall. He had watched the sixth sitting, and had seen three marks put on a post at a size named in advance. Where he was sure, he said so; where he was not, he said that too, and the not-being-sure made the rest weigh more.

"Thank you," said counsel, and sat down.

Lira let her breath go.

"The officer of record may put questions," said the magistrate.

The Warden stood. He did not come round his table. He stood behind it with his hands at his sides and looked at Edran across the open boards, and Lira thought he looked at him as if he had already read everything Edran was going to say and was only checking the copy.

"You were first on the standings of this academy," said the Warden. "Until this term."

"Until this term," said Edran. Lira saw what it cost him, and saw him pay it without wincing, and so did everybody in the gallery who knew him.

"This term you put your name to a petition against the respondent's place on the training floor. And withdrew it."

"Yes."

"One question, then." The Warden's voice did not change at all. "This respondent was enrolled under a provision written for children who haven't yet been to a station. In your own judgment, as a practitioner who has fought him, at what level does he fight?"

Edran did not look at the respondent's table. He looked at the Warden.

"Iron-tier, at minimum," he said. "Perhaps higher. I couldn't say how much."

It went through the gallery like cold water.

Lira felt it go. She felt the two second-years by the window lean together, and heard the tout's pencil, and she watched the back of Cael's head and saw it not move. The Warden did not underline the answer. He did not turn to the panel or say *thank you* like a man handed what he came for. He only said, "Thank you. Nothing further," and sat down. He left the answer standing in the middle of the floor by itself, where it looked much larger than if he had pointed at it.

Edran did not leave the chalk square at once.

"I don't know what it was," he said, to the bench, before the clerk could lift the rope for him. "What he did. I'd like that on the record as well. I'm not saying it to help him. It's only true."

"The panel notes it," said the magistrate, and Edran went back to his bench with his flat honest face giving nothing away, and sat down, and folded his arms.

To her own annoyance, Lira wanted to stand up and shake his hand. She sat on her own hands instead.

Hobb took less time.

Counsel named him. He came through the rope with his feet a shoulder-width apart, as he walked everywhere, and stood in the chalk square as if he had been asked to hold up one end of the room. Counsel asked him how many of the candidate's sittings he had stood in as the partner. Hobb thought about it for so long that somebody in the back of the gallery coughed, and the clerk looked round.

"Four," said Hobb.

Counsel asked him whether anything had been different in any of them. Hobb thought about that too.

"Same as last time," he said. "Four times."

There was a sound from the gallery that was very nearly a laugh, and the clerk turned round properly this time, and it stopped.

"The officer of record may put questions," said the magistrate.

The Warden looked at Hobb for a moment across the boards, and Lira, watching, could have sworn that something at the corner of his mouth moved and then thought better of it.

"In your judgment," said the Warden, "what Path does the respondent practise?"

"Didn't say," said Hobb. "Said what I saw."

"Nothing further," said the Warden.

Hobb went back through the rope and sat down beside Edran, and Edran moved along the bench to give him room without looking at him, and that was all. In front of Lira, Oona bent over her slate and wrote something and underlined it twice, and Lira did not need to see it. She would have bet her bread it said *two b's*, or *eight words*, or both.

It was the best testimony anybody would give all day, Lira thought, and nobody in the room but Brom would know it.

---

Before the midday bell the Warden came back to the form.

He did it so quietly that half the gallery did not notice the case had changed direction. He took the certified copy of the enrollment form from the first case, where it had lain since the morning, and set it on the green cloth's edge in front of the bench. Beside it he laid a single registry sheet, which Lira could not read from where she sat but could guess at. Then he read the gloss under the category line once more, slowly. *Has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.* Then he read from the registry sheet the name of a station in Denvash, and a date, and the word *assessed*.

"The form says one thing," said the Warden, "and the registry says the other, and the registry was there first. The panel need not take the Compact's word for which is so. It has both in front of it." He squared the two sheets together. "The enrollment is invalid on its own face. The Compact will show the panel that the respondent knew it."

Lira saw Oona's pencil stop.

Then he read the deposition into the record.

He had kept it for the end, after the provision and the drafting records, the witnesses and the form. Lira, sitting on her hands, understood that he had saved it on purpose, as she saved the best of the bread for last. He did not stand any differently or speak any louder. He took a bound fair copy from the third case, in the young recorder's best hand, and opened it at a marked page, and began.

He read the respondent's name and number as the registry held them. He read the question about the enrollment form and the signature, and the answers, which were *yes*, and the date. He read the question about how many times the deponent had read the category line before he signed it, and the answer. "*Three.*" Somewhere behind Lira a girl drew in her breath. He read the questions about the circuit at Ardenmere, and the answer that the circuit's ledger said so. He read the question about what the deponent's architecture was, in his own words, and the answer, "*That's a classification question.*" He read the question after it, and the answer, "*I understand that everything may be noted. That's what a record is.*" At that one a ripple went along the front bench that was not quite anything. Lira could not tell whether it was approval or the start of a laugh, and neither, she thought, could the people making it.

He read the question about the sixth sitting. *Before that sitting, was that capability entered in any record of this academy?* "*No.*" *Why not?* "*Because I hadn't elected to show it. The provision lets the candidate elect.*" *Had you possessed it for some time?* "*Four weeks.*"

He read that without any weight on it, and went on to the next page, and Lira saw the panel's three heads all go down at once to their papers and come up again.

Then he read the question.

"*When you signed the enrollment form, did you understand the category to describe you accurately?*" The Warden paused. "*No.*"

He looked down at the page, and then up, not at the gallery and not at the respondent but at the bench.

"The record marks a silence of nine counts after the answer," he said.

At the recording desk below the bench the young recorder's pen stopped moving. Lira saw it stop. She saw him look down at his own roll, and then not look at anything.

"*Then why did you sign it?*" read the Warden. "*Because it was the door the provision had, and I wanted to go to school.*"

He closed the fair copy.

Lira had heard all of it before. She had heard it at the scarred table on the Wednesday, in Cael's own voice, with his bread in front of him and his hands turning it over. She had torn that bread in half and given him both halves, because she had not known what else to do with her hands. She had heard it then as a true thing told among friends. Now she was hearing it in a stranger's patient voice, read off a stranger's page to two hundred people and three officers of the Compact and seven who had come up a hill to listen, and it was the same words and not the same thing at all.

The gallery made no sound for a moment. Then it made a low, confused one, and the confusion was the part Lira had not expected. She had expected contempt. She had braced for the noise a crowd makes when it hears the fighter it cheered for admit he took a dive. That was not this. Somebody near the windows had said *oh*, softly, the way you say it when you see a small child fall. Somebody else had said *he wanted to go to school* in a voice that did not know whether it was sorry for him or for itself. The tout had stopped writing. In front of her, Oona's back had gone very straight and very still, and her pencil was not moving on the slate at all, which Lira had never once seen.

And the Warden stood beside his cases and let it lie, as he had let everything lie, and Lira understood for the first time why Cael had stopped being angry at him. The man had not twisted it. He had not needed to. He had read the boy's own words exactly as they were said, the nine counts included. The words were true, and they were the best thing in his whole case, and he had set them down without once pretending they were anything else.

She was still not allowed to hit him. She no longer particularly wanted to, and she found that worse.

"The panel will rise for the midday interval," said the magistrate. "The sitting resumes at the fifth bell."

---

Brom was the one who found the corner.

The yard had filled the moment the doors opened, all of it in knots, all of it talking low, and the four of them came out into it and every knot turned to look. Brom did not say anything. He walked them across the yard, round the end of the lecture wing, to the narrow angle where its end wall met the old wall of the yard, out of the wind and out of the way, with nothing in it but a broken barrow. He stood in the mouth of the angle with his back to the yard and folded his arms, and the yard, after a moment, found something else to look at.

Lira had the heel of a loaf in her coat, because she always did. She broke it and gave Cael half. He held it and did not eat it.

Karis did not wait for anybody to begin. She had her notebook shut under her arm, which in Karis meant that everything she needed was already written down and she was only thinking now.

"There's one thing about his case I want you to have," she said, "before you go back in." She looked at Cael and at nobody else. "It's whole. I don't mean strong; strong is a different word. I mean there's nothing missing from it and nothing held back. He told the panel three things at the first bell. He's proved two and the third is lying in his last case waiting for after the interval, and not one of them needs anything from you to stand up. He hasn't kept a single paper for tomorrow." She tapped the notebook against her palm, once. "Nothing's being kept back, because in his head tomorrow is only the day the panel reads out its ruling. So the moment you stand up, he's got nothing ready. Anything he says after that, he'll have to invent standing up. He hasn't had to do that once in three weeks."

"Is that good?" said Lira.

"It's the only thing I've seen since the first bell that I'd call good," said Karis. "So I'm going to hold on to it."

Brom had not turned round. He was looking out of the mouth of the angle at the far door of the assembly room, where the knots of people stood and talked and did not go in.

"He fights like you," said Brom.

Lira looked at the back of his head. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning he waits." Brom still did not turn. "He doesn't give you anything early. He sets everything down where it's safe, one thing at a time, and he doesn't put his weight on any of it until he knows what you've got. Then he puts it all in at once, at the end." He turned his head at last and looked at Cael. "That's how you fight. You always have. I've stood across from it. I've never had to sit and watch it from the outside before, done at somebody I'm on the side of. It's very hard to sit still for."

Cael was looking at the same door. He had not eaten any of the bread.

"Someone who commits later," he said.

Brom gave one nod and turned back to the yard.

Lira waited until Karis had gone to the pump for a cup of water and Brom had gone with her, because there was a question she did not want to ask in front of either of them.

"Is it bad?" she said. "In there. I can't tell. I've watched a hundred fights and I can't tell. Everybody on my bench thinks it's finished."

"It's what we said it would be." He turned the bread over. "That isn't bad. That's the plan, being done to us, the way we said it would be."

"I know it's the plan. I sat at the table every night while you made it." She heard her voice climbing and brought it down. "I didn't know that sitting on a bench while the other man carries out his half would feel like this. Nobody told me. I'd like to have been told."

"I didn't know either," said Cael. He looked at the bread in his hand and finally ate some of it, slowly. "He hasn't put one thing on me. All of it's on the enrollment, and I'm going to give him the enrollment before I say anything else. All day he's been making me somewhere to stand." He swallowed. "Even the last thing. The *no*. That was always going to be his. All I did was hand it over on the Wednesday instead of the Monday."

She watched him finish the bread, all of it. She watched him wipe his fingers on his coat and lay his hand flat over the inside pocket, where she knew her slip lay against Brom's page. When the fifth bell went across the yard he straightened off the wall, and she checked his shoulders out of habit, and they were where they belonged. He walked back across the yard at an even pace with his hands empty, and the knots of people at the door moved apart to let him through.

She stood in the angle of the walls and watched him go in. Her hands had made fists in her coat pockets without asking her, and she let them stay that way all the way back to her bench.

---

The afternoon began with the dead.

Cael thought of it that way afterward, though the Warden would never have put it like that and nobody in the room would have known what he meant. Coss took a fourth case from beside his table, and from it three folders tied with tape. He laid them side by side on the green cloth's edge where the bench could reach them, and entered them as the precedents of the Compact.

He gave each its district and its year and its name. The first was Sarnholt. A chartered academy there had enrolled under the provision a youth who was past the age of Kindling and had refused to be tested, on the reasoning that a person who would not be assessed had not yet been assessed. The enrollment had been voided inside a single sitting. The second was Wexley, where a registry clerk had made an honest error that nobody caught for three years, so that a girl who had Kindled and been classified was entered on an academy's rolls as untested. When it came to light the academy had defended the enrollment on the ground of the girl's good standing, and lost, and had spent two years under review of its own charter for having tried. The third had no name read out, only its district and its year, because it had never come to a hearing at all. A disputed assessment, an enrollment under the provision, a challenge filed; and the academy had withdrawn the enrollment before the magistrate arrived.

"In each instance the enrollment was voided," said Coss. "In none was the provision found to reach a person it was not written for. The Compact is not aware of any instance in which it has been so found." He put his hand flat on the third folder. "In every one of them, the institution either withdrew, or argued, and lost. There has never been a sound argument for this enrollment to make. The provision says what it says. It always has."

Under the table Cael folded down a third finger. *Precedent. Theirs. Unbroken.*

He was right, Cael thought, about every case but the one that had never been brought. And there was nothing on any page in the room to tell anybody that, yet.

Then Coss came to the records of the sittings, and entered them as capability.

He read Wray's line from the first, the seven words she had chosen and counted and would stand by. "*Unorthodox architecture. Consistent execution. No safety concerns.*" At the side wall Cael saw her not move. He read the second's and the third's. He read Quenna's from the fifth, the match that had been halted, word for word, in the voice he used for everything. "*Atypical adaptive response, consistent with previously documented irregular architecture. No safety violation noted. Assessment satisfied under demonstration provision.*" And then he took the last sheet in the folder, which was the sixth, and held it a moment before he read it, as if to be sure of the date.

"*The candidate demonstrated, at his own election, an ignition-point capability, contact-adjacent, single channel. Three times, at a size stated in advance, with a rest between. Consistent, observable practice. No safety violation noted. Assessment satisfied.*" He laid it down. "Entered as of its own date. The Compact offers the record of the sittings as the academy's own account of what the respondent can do. The Compact does not dispute a word of it."

Cael sat with his three fingers folded against his knee and heard the three small black rings on the grey oak go past him in the room, in the dullest language Quenna owned. Nobody laughed. Nobody gasped. A man in a grey coat near the back wrote something down. In the front of the gallery he knew without looking that a girl with a polished tag was holding her pencil very still over a slate on which she had once written that she had seen the first one.

It held. That was what he noticed most, sitting there. The dullest record in the room had been read aloud by the man who most wanted to make something of it, and there had been nothing in it to make anything of. It was a thing with a size. It did what it was said to do, three times, with a rest between. It held.

He folded down a fourth finger. *Records. Theirs. All true.*

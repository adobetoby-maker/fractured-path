# Chapter 1 — The Last Climb

Brom knelt in the pine needles with his right hand open, like a man about to push a stuck door.

"Ready."

Cael was not ready, but he set his left forearm across his chest anyway, braced it, and nodded.

It was the third morning out of Ardenmere, and the fire was down to a red eye in the ashes. Lira was still rolled in her blanket at the edge of the clearing, awake and pretending not to be, because watching the two of them fail before breakfast had become one of her small pleasures on the road. Cold air came down off the hills above the camp and smelled of wet stone.

Brom's palm met his forearm.

The push always started slow. Brom let a little Iron Skin harden through his hand first, so that the contact arrived like a flat stone rather than a palm, and then he leaned. The force came in steady and even, a fraction of a real strike, the kind of weight a man could count.

Cael waited for it to arrive in full. That was the first thing the road had taught him: the fragment did nothing with force that was still on its way. It woke only when the force was already his, already inside the bone, and by now the waking had a feel he could recognize, a tightening low in the chest, a sense of something gathering.

He reached for it.

The force gathered and began to turn, and for a quarter of a heartbeat he held a piece of Brom's push the way a person holds water in cupped hands. Then he had to decide where it went.

It wanted to go down.

He felt it slide toward his left knee along the same lazy path it had taken two days ago, toward the joint that still remembered being used as a drain. The memory was so exact that his whole body flinched from it before his mind had finished recognizing what it was.

He let go.

The gathered piece spilled out of him harmlessly, and his forearm bore what was left of the push like any arm. Brom eased off.

"You dropped it."

"It was going to my knee again."

"You're sure."

"Same road as last time." Cael flexed the knee and found only the faint ache that had been there since the first day, fading, with nothing new on top of it. "It wants the nearest joint. If I don't give it somewhere better before it moves, it picks for itself."

Brom considered that the way a smith considers a bar of iron, turning it slowly before deciding what it was good for.

"Then give it somewhere better first."

"That's the whole problem."

"No. That's the whole answer. The problem is you're deciding late." He set his hand again. "Again."

They went on until they had made twelve tries between them, and most of the twelve were failures of one kind or another. On the next two nothing caught at all; the fragment sat in him like a word on the tip of the tongue, present and unreachable, and Brom's push simply arrived and left. On the fourth he caught something and sent it the wrong way, not into the knee this time but into his own right shoulder, the one the Reydan bout had already used hard. It flared so sharply that he hissed through his teeth and Brom stopped at once.

"Shoulder."

"Shoulder." He rolled it. Under the new hot line he could feel the old complaint, the one that announced itself on certain angles. "Better than the knee."

"Better how?"

"The shoulder's already paying. It's an account that's open."

Lira's voice came from the blanket. "That is the worst thing I have ever heard a person say about his own body."

"You're awake."

"I'm always awake. Keep going. I want to see you do it right once before I have to walk all day next to someone sulking."

He did it right on the seventh try.

The force arrived, and he waited until it was in the bone, and before it could pick a road of its own he chose one for it — out, back along the line it had come in, into Brom's own braced arm — and held that choice steady while the fragment gathered.

A quarter of the push went home.

It went back into Brom's wrist with a small, solid knock, like a door shutting in another room. Brom's eyebrows lifted. Under the Iron Skin his forearm had felt it, and Cael knew he had felt it, because nobody read where weight had gone better than Brom did.

Then the bill came.

It always came the same way, running up through the shoulder and across the breastbone in a band of heat and pressure, as if someone had laid a hot iron bar along his collarbones and leaned on it. For two breaths he could not fill his lungs, and he waited them out on his knees in the pine needles.

"There," Brom said quietly. "Quarter."

"Quarter," Cael managed.

He caught a quarter twice more before they broke camp, on the tenth try and the twelfth, and each of those cost what the seventh had cost, no more and no less. That was oddly comforting. A price that stayed the same was a price he could plan around. The tries in between went nowhere, because he had reached a breath too early while the push was still coming and the fragment had simply not answered. He was almost grateful for those; a failure that did nothing was the cheapest lesson the fragment had to offer.

Lira was sitting up by then, blanket around her shoulders, eating the last heel of yesterday's bread.

"Three in twelve."

"Three in twelve."

"On a push Brom could do while asleep."

"He'd have to be asleep for me to catch more than a quarter of it."

She grinned. "That's honest, at least."

---

He wrote it up while Brom kicked dirt over the fire.

The Power Log lay open on his knees, a binder now, its rings stiff with cold. Once it had been a single slim notebook, and he had added pages so many times since then that the original cover was mostly a memory; he had long ago given up pretending the binder was tidy. It was not tidy. It was honest, and those had turned out to be different things.

Every fragment had its own section, and every section used the same four headings, because the same four questions had turned out to matter every time: Function, which was what it did; Benefit, which was what it was good for; Cost, which was what it took from him; and Integration, which was how much of it answered when he called. He had not so much invented the format as surrendered to it, because every other way of writing about the fragments had eventually collapsed into bragging or panic, and the four headings allowed neither.

He turned to the newest section and wrote.

*Compression-adjacent. Road session, third morning. Function: catches force already inside the body and sends it back out. Must wait for the force to arrive. Reaching early gets nothing. Benefit: none in a real fight yet. Cost: shoulder and breastbone, two breaths of not being able to breathe. Same price on every success. Integration: partial. Three of twelve. A quarter of a slow push each time.*

Then he added the line that mattered most.

*If I don't choose the road before the force moves, it chooses the nearest joint. Knee on day one. Shoulder today. Choose first. Always choose first.*

When he had read it back, he turned to the front of the binder, where the oldest notices were copied out, and went through the four confirmed fragments as a carpenter runs a thumb along his chisels before starting work, not to learn anything, only to know they were all still there.

Wind-adjacent was first, and Lira's. It was an evasion framework he had once needed a full second to assemble and now did without thinking, reading a strike off an opponent's body a half-beat before it landed and moving him into the space beside it. Its range was the length of a fight. Its price was fatigue, and a choice of direction he had to make before he moved, and against faster opponents it read less. He had shown it on the circuit a hundred times.

Pressure-adjacent was second, a redirect borrowed from a Bronze-tier fighter named Feryn who had beaten him fairly. It had three clean uses mapped, each paid for in the right shoulder, and it worked at contact and nowhere else. Nobody outside the three of them had ever been told it existed.

Iron-adjacent was third, and Brom's: a pressure-read along the skin that told him where a person's weight was about to go. It worked at contact and out to a few paces, thinner with distance and thinner still with speed, and it was quiet enough that people who watched him fight saw only that he was rarely surprised.

Compression-adjacent was fourth. Days old. Bad at everything.

Its notice was copied out on the page in front of him exactly as it had arrived, the night after his last circuit bout.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Compression-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: force absorption component. Damage redirect, contact range.
```

Nothing in it had changed overnight. Contact range. Partial. Unknown.

Behind the four sections sat a fifth page, set apart from the rest, with a date, a short account of an afternoon in Brom's sparring circle, and a single line underneath.

*Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.*

He did not add anything. He never did. That page was for reading, not for writing; he shut the binder and got to his feet. His knee did not hurt and his shoulder did, which by the log's arithmetic was an improvement, and he decided to count it as one.

---

The road climbed all morning, through scrub at first and then low oak, and then out into open hill country where the grass had been cropped short by sheep and drystone walls stitched the slopes into uneven squares. The sky was high and pale. Early autumn had put a cool edge on the wind without yet taking the green out of anything.

They walked three abreast where the road allowed it and single file where it didn't, and they had been doing it for so long that none of them noticed the switching anymore.

"How many of them will be Copper?" Lira asked.

She asked Brom, not Cael, because Brom was the one who had grown up close to an academy's way of thinking; Velmere had been a family that kept tutors. He thought about it for fifty paces.

"Most. Same as everywhere. Six in ten of anyone who fights for a living is Copper, and most people who Kindle into Copper stay there."

"Most adults."

"Most adults. They get to Rank Four or Five, the gains slow down, they stop pushing. They get a trade. They have a family. Copper's enough to work a dock or run a stall. Nobody needs Iron to bake bread."

"I'd like to see Iron bread."

"You'd break a tooth."

Cael knew the numbers, as everybody in Valdris knew them. But he had never heard them said by someone who had been raised to think of them as a ladder his family expected him to climb. Ten ranks in each tier, with Rank Ten as the threshold. And to cross from one tier into the next you needed two things, the rank you earned through use and a formal evaluation at a registered Arbiter station that confirmed it. Copper, Iron, Bronze, Silver, Gold, and after Gold the tiers became history and then rumor. Most people stopped on the first rung. A quarter made Iron, the journeyman's tier of professional fighters and serious tradespeople, the ones who kept pushing. One in ten made Bronze. Silver was the kind of tier that got your name said in other cities.

"Greyvane's a minor-tier academy," Brom went on. "They take Copper and Iron and turn out Bronze, if they're lucky. A Silver graduate would be a good year for them."

"Quenna is Silver."

"Quenna is faculty. They buy faculty." Brom shrugged. "They can't buy students. Students go where the prestige is."

Lira kicked a stone off the road, and it bounced down the slope through the sheep, who ignored it.

"Then what are we?"

She meant herself and Brom, who were both Copper on paper and both carried an honest rating from Vell's circuit ledger that said something else.

"Iron-equivalent," said Brom.

"Which means what, at an academy?"

It was a fair question, and Cael had been chewing on it since Ardenmere. On the circuit, Iron-equivalent had meant what it said: Vell rated you by what you did on her floor against whoever stood across from you, and if you beat enough Iron-tier fighters, consistently, her ledger called you Iron-equivalent, whatever your registry document said. It was a rating of results, and the circuit trusted results.

"It means you can beat Iron fighters," he said slowly. "It doesn't mean an Arbiter has said you're Iron. It doesn't give you an Iron rank. It doesn't move you up the ladder."

"So it means nothing."

"It means something. It just means it to the people who were watching."

Lira walked on without answering.

"I'm Copper," she said. "On paper. I'm going to walk in that gate as Copper, and people who never saw me fight are going to read the word and think they know what it means."

"Yes."

"I hate that."

"I know."

"I'm going to hate it less than I would have a year ago, though. That's the strange part. I can feel myself hating it less."

She was looking straight up the road with her chin a little high. Cael thought of himself beside her: Iron-equivalent by the same honest ledger, because he had beaten an Iron Rank Eight in front of six hundred people, and on paper no tier at all, no rank, not even Copper, only a word on his registry file that was not a tier but a classification the registry had never known what to do with. He had stopped needing to say that out loud months ago. He noticed now that he had also stopped needing to think it every hour, and that was new, and he filed it away to write down later.

At the midday rest Lira tried, for perhaps the fifth time since Ardenmere, to get Brom to show her a declaration.

"Just one. The first one. The one you got at Kindling."

"No."

"I showed you mine."

"You described yours. You never showed me."

"Describing is showing."

"It isn't," said Brom, unbothered, eating cold barley from a tin with every sign of contentment. "You know it isn't."

Cael knew it too. Declarations were the language an Arbiter used, and every practitioner received them from Kindling on: a small glowing sigil only you could see, and then words in a form only you could read, telling you what your Path had just given you at your new rank. They were the private record of a person's growth, written in a hand nobody else ever saw. People did share them, brothers sometimes, or married couples, or a teacher and a student who had trusted each other for years. But you did not ask, any more than you would ask to read someone's letters, and you did not show unless showing meant something. Lira knew all of this perfectly well. She asked because she liked watching Brom refuse.

"Tell me the shape, then. Not the words."

Brom set the tin down.

"There's a header. It names the Path and the tier and the rank. Then a line with the ability's name in brackets. Then a sentence about what it does, sometimes two. How far it reaches. When it works. That's all." He picked the tin up again. "It's very plain. People think it'll be poetry. It's more like a receipt."

"A receipt," Lira repeated, delighted.

"A good receipt. It tells you exactly what you paid for."

Cael found himself listening harder than the conversation deserved. His own notices were nothing like that. A declaration named a Path, a tier, a rank and an ability; his notices began with two words in capitals, *Fragment Acquired*, and gave every ability the same name, which was no name at all. Where a declaration would name a Path, his gave an adjacency, near something and never the thing itself, and where a declaration gave a tier and rank, his said only *Tier equivalent: unknown.* A declaration was a receipt. His notices were more like a note left on a table by someone who had been in the room and gone.

"What's yours look like?" Brom asked him suddenly.

"You've read the log."

"I've read your copies. I've never asked what it's like when one arrives."

It was a very Brom question, Cael thought. Brom had been studying him for most of a year and had never once asked something he could find out by watching.

"Quiet," he said. "No sigil. Nobody's there. It's just suddenly in front of me, like a line someone already wrote. The words don't change if I look away."

"Like a ledger."

"Like a ledger."

Brom nodded slowly. "Then you got Vell's version and I got the receipt."

Lira laughed out loud, and a pair of sheep looked up at her in mild disapproval. Cael laughed too, more surprised by it than anything, because for as long as he had carried the notices he had thought of them as a lack, a declaration that had come out wrong. Brom had just set the two side by side as if they were simply two different kinds of record. He would think about that later. Probably in writing.

---

The last stretch of the climb came in the early afternoon, and the road grew better as it rose. Somebody had cut the verges back within the month, there was new gravel in the ruts, and the wall beside them had been rebuilt in places with stones paler than the old. Cael noticed all of it, because maintenance meant somebody whose job was maintenance, and a place that kept its road kept other things too.

A hill town showed itself in pieces: slate roofs, smoke from cooking fires, and somewhere below them a mill wheel turning slowly with a deep wooden groan they heard long before they saw it. The wind carried cut grass and wet stone and, under those, bread.

Lira lifted her head like a hound.

"Later," said Brom.

"I didn't say anything."

"You smelled it. I watched you smell it."

Above the town the road forked, one branch bending down toward the roofs and the other climbing on.

"Town or academy?"

"Academy," said Cael and Brom together, and Lira laughed and was ten paces up the hill before either of them moved.

Greyvane came over the brow of the hill a quarter-mile later, and it was small.

That was Cael's first clear thought. In the weeks since Quenna had first said the name he had built the place much larger in his head, and here instead was a wall of old grey stone, plain and weathered, laid in the coursing of something far older than any school. A gate of iron-strapped oak, newer, set into it. And beyond the wall a few roofs, a training yard with a wall of its own, a long building with uneven windows, and further back a clean-cut building three floors high, the only thing in sight that looked as if it had been built on purpose for what it was now.

That was all.

A bell rang somewhere inside, once, slow and unhurried, marking some hour of the day that had nothing to do with them.

Cael stopped walking. He did not mean to; his feet simply stopped, and Lira and Brom went on two paces before they stopped as well and looked back at him.

"What."

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing."

"I'm not cataloguing." He was, and he knew he was. The wall was old waystation stone; the gate was two generations newer at most. There was a single clerk's booth set into the gatehouse instead of a guardroom, which meant the place logged people far more often than it stopped them. "I'm looking."

"Those are the same thing for you."

"Not always."

She waited, and Brom waited, and below them the mill wheel groaned. He tried to name what he was feeling and could not, and decided that was all right. The afternoon light was coming in low across the wall and turning the old stone almost gold, and there had been a time, not so long ago, when he would not have stopped for that. Ardenmere had taught him to look at a place before deciding what it meant.

"It's small," he said finally.

"It's small," Lira agreed.

"I like that it's small."

Brom kept his eyes on the wall while they climbed another twenty paces. "It's the size it is. It isn't pretending. That's rarer than big."

They walked the last quarter-mile together, and at the gate the clerk had a list.

She was a woman of about forty with ink stains on two fingers of her writing hand and the patient face of someone who copied things all day. She did not get up. She ran a finger down the page in front of her, stopped, and read the names back without looking at them.

"Brom, standard. Lira, re-certification. And Cael, on the observer track." Then she did look up, at each of them in turn, fitting faces to the ink. "The three of you are on my list. Go past the yard. The first door is intake, and they know you're coming."

That was all she said, and it was enough.

Cael lingered at the booth for a breath after there was any reason to. It was a small thing, and he knew it. Every institution in the world kept lists; Denvash had kept one, the certification office had written his name on a schedule before he'd ever asked to be written down. The Compact had him in a registry that ran the length of the continent. But those had all been the same kind of list, a door opening because a rule said it had to, and this was a different kind. Someone in this building had looked at his name weeks ago and decided it belonged on the far side of the gate, and had written it down in ink before he arrived, trusting that he would come.

Most of his life he had been handled as paperwork, walking into rooms where he had to prove he existed before anyone would hand him a chair.

His name was already here.

"Cael," Lira said quietly, from inside the gate.

He realized he had stopped again. "Coming."

He walked through behind them, and the gate's shadow passed over him and was gone. On the far side the yard opened up, swept and empty, with chalk lines faint on the packed earth, and a bell rang again at a different pitch from the first. He did not know what that bell meant. He would learn, and he noticed, with something close to surprise, that he wanted to.

For once he did not reach for the notebook. He only noticed the feeling and let it sit there unwritten.

Lira would have called it a miracle.

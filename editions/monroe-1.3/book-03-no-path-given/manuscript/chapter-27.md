# Chapter 27 — Correctly

Nobody fetched the rumor market out of bed for Gerda.

The yard at first bell on Friday was colder than it had been on Thursday, and emptier, and the frost lay on the benches unbroken because nobody had sat on them. Five people stood at the rail on the open side. Cael was one, and Lira beside him with her bruised shoulder strapped under her coat, and Brom, and Karis a little along with her hands in her pockets and no notebook again. Hobb stood at the far corner, alone, as he had stood at every bout Cael had seen him watch. That was all. Somebody's re-certification review at the first bell on the last day of the week was not a thing anybody at Greyvane had a reason to come and see, and so nobody had come.

The panel sat at the north end, Quenna in the middle and Wray on her left. The rotating seat this morning was the lean old instructor with scorched cuffs who took the heat Paths, Ember and Ash together. He looked like someone the file in front of him had already told most of what he expected to see.

Gerda came through the gate as the bell finished, with her ash-pale hair cut close and her patched coat folded over her arm and her hands wrapped. On her way to the ring she stopped, and looked up at the clock above the main hall door, and checked it against something in her head. Then she went on.

"For the record," said Quenna. "Re-certification review, formal. The candidate: Gerda, Ash Path, re-certification track. Two recorded bouts before the panel, under standings rules. The originating academy's record of finding has been received and read. The candidate's recorded tier is Copper, Rank Nine at the date of the originating record. The finding is termination of enrollment for failure to meet the standing requirement." She turned a page. "The panel will assess advancement against the recorded tier. First bout. The candidate takes the south mark."

Lira's hand had closed on the rail.

The first opponent was the Blade fourth-year with the very fast hands, the one Wray had put across from Edran in the week before the exhibition, a slight dark boy who drilled in silence and fought in silence and seemed to Cael to be listening the whole time to something nobody else could hear. He took the north mark and Gerda took the south, and Wray lowered her finger.

Gerda laid her haze.

Cael had seen it twice from the rail and once in the near corner of the open floor with a first-year walking slowly through it, and it was still strange to watch at close range. It came off her low and grey and spread along the swept earth like smoke that had decided to lie down, ankle-deep and then knee-deep, two paces out from her in every direction. Anything that moved inside it moved as if through cold honey. A strike thrown into it arrived late, and a foot set down in it came down slow. She stood in the middle of it with her hands loose and waited.

The Blade fourth-year did not come in.

He had watched her. Cael saw it in the way he stopped a pace outside the edge of the grey and stood there, light on his feet, his very fast hands loose at his sides, looking at the haze the way a swimmer looks at cold water. He had no reason to go into it. A standings bout scored touches, and a touch had to be made by somebody, and if neither of them made one the exchange was empty and so was the next. He circled the edge of the haze, and she turned inside it to keep him in front of her. He circled the other way, and she turned again. The first exchange went by like that, two people walking round a patch of fog.

"She has to go out to him," Lira said under her breath. "She has to. She's got the haze, and he's got the hands, and her haze is no use to her if all she does is sit in it."

The second exchange was the same. Wray called it with no touch. The heat-Paths instructor wrote something brief and then sat with his pen lifted, not writing anything at all.

In the third exchange the Blade fourth-year began to come in and out. He would step into the edge of the haze and out again before it had properly closed on him, a feint and a withdrawal, a feint and a withdrawal, his fast hands never quite committing. Gerda turned and turned. Cael watched her shoulders and her feet, and saw the moment she decided to go out after him, the weight shifting onto her front foot. Then he saw the moment she decided not to, the weight going back. Then he saw her decide again, too late. She stepped out of her own haze at last, after him. The instant she was out of it she was only a tall careful girl on swept earth with nothing slowing anyone down, and the Blade boy's hand flicked out and touched her shoulder before she had finished setting her foot.

"Touch," said Wray. "That's the bout."

Gerda went back to the south mark. She did not look at the rail. She looked up, once, at the clock.

The second bout went to her, and it was worse to watch than the first. The opponent was the second-year Current Path girl whose footwork never stopped, the one Cael had watched in his first fortnight. She came into the haze without a moment's hesitation, because she did not know any better, and the haze took her feet. Inside it her endless footwork became slow and pale and easy to read, and Gerda could have touched her four times in the first exchange. Cael saw her see it. He saw her not take it. She waited until the girl had slowed almost to a stop in the grey, and then she reached out and touched her breastbone with two careful fingers, as if she were laying a cup down on a table she did not trust.

"Touch. Candidate."

She did it once more in the second exchange, the same way, from the same distance, without taking a single step that might have cost her anything. Wray called the bout for her at the end of the second. The Current girl walked off the ring looking pleased to have lasted so long.

Nobody on the rail said anything.

The panel went to the far wall and did not talk for long. When they came back Quenna wrote, and then lifted her pen.

"Re-certification review, formal. Two recorded bouts. The first lost by the candidate, the second won. The panel's notation." Her voice did not change. "Performance consistent with recorded tier. No advancement demonstrated against the originating record. Recommend reconsideration of candidacy at the term's end."

"Initials," said Quenna. "And the candidate's signature that she has heard the notation."

Gerda walked to the table and signed. She did it carefully, every letter separate. Then she straightened and looked up at the clock above the main hall door, and Cael saw her lips move.

He knew what she had said. He had heard her say it twice before, at the board in the main hall, about other people's petitions.

*Correctly.*

It took him most of the walk back across the yard to understand what he had just watched, and when he understood it he did not like it at all.

Nothing had gone wrong. That was the whole of it. Nobody had cheated her, or lied, or made a sentence up out loud at the end of a table. The procedure had worked precisely as its writers meant it to. Three people had read her originating record and watched her fight twice on a cold morning and written down what they saw. What they saw was a careful Copper fighter who did not move out of her own haze unless she had to and never once took a risk she could avoid, which was the tier and rank the record said she was, and so the record and the floor agreed. Yesterday a floor had disagreed with a file, and the disagreement had been written down and would stand. This morning the floor had agreed with one, and the agreement had been written down too, and it would stand just as long.

No floor had come to rescue her, because she had not let it.

Lira had stopped walking. She was standing in the middle of the yard with her strapped shoulder held away from her side, looking back at the gate, where Gerda had gone out toward the training hall with her coat over her arm, not hurrying, checking the clock above the door as she passed it.

"I'm going to help her," Lira said.

Cael looked at her.

"Did she ask you?"

"No." Lira did not take her eyes off the gate. "Nobody asked Quenna to write to Fenmark three weeks early, either. Nobody asked her to put the envelope on the table and tell me it was a version of a choice. She just did it, because it was there to be done." She turned her head and looked at him. "On Monday I found out that nobody ever argued for me. Not one person, not once, in a room I spent two years filling with people. I'd like to find out what it's like to be the one who does."

"She may not want it."

"Then she'll tell me so, and I'll go away." Lira was already walking. "She's very good at telling people things. It's the asking she's bad at."

He watched her cross the yard after Gerda, quick and short-stepped and favouring the shoulder, and catch her up at the training hall door. Gerda stopped. They stood at the door, the tall careful girl and the short fierce one, and Cael could not hear anything they said. He saw Gerda look at the clock. He saw Lira not look at it at all. Then the two of them went in together, and the door shut.

Brom had come up beside him at some point, as he did.

"That's Lira," Brom said, "deciding."

"That's Lira deciding," Cael agreed.

---

Gerda came to the scarred table at the midday meal on Saturday, because Lira brought her. She sat at the end of the bench with her bowl in front of her and ate with the careful economy of somebody who had once been very hungry for a long time and had decided never to waste anything again.

She did not speak at first, and Lira did not push. Karis was at the other end of the table with her notebook shut, and Brom was opposite, and the long room was full of the noise of a Saturday, second-years arguing about tiles and somebody laughing by the hatch. Cael had begun to think that Gerda had come only to sit near people for an hour and would go away again without a word.

Then she put her spoon down.

"You'll want to know why it's Rank Nine and a termination," she said. "Anybody would."

"We don't need to know anything," said Lira.

"No. But you'll wonder." Gerda laid both hands flat on the scarred wood on either side of her bowl, as Cael had seen Karis lay hers. "I'd rather you knew than wondered. Wondering gets things wrong."

She told it in her procedure voice, flat and level and very exact.

She had been in her second year at a school in the south, Copper Rank Nine, one rank short of the threshold, and her instructor had told her in the autumn that she would cross before midwinter. In the winter her mother had fallen ill at home, three days' coach away. Gerda had gone to her instructor and asked for leave, and he had given it to her in the corridor outside the floor, nine days, *go*, and she had gone. In the nine days three of her recorded bouts had fallen due, and she had not been there to fight them. The leave had never been written down. Her instructor had meant to; she believed that still. Before he did, he had been offered a post at a larger academy in the north and taken it, and gone, within the week.

"The three bouts were entered as forfeits," Gerda said. "Correctly. I wasn't there. A forfeit is what you call a bout where one fighter isn't there. Three forfeits in a term means the standing requirement isn't met, and that means a review, and the review looked at the record and found three forfeits, which were there." Her hands did not move on the table. "I filed a petition. I said I'd had leave. It was posted. It was answered. There was no record of leave. The forfeits stood."

"Correctly," said Lira, very quietly.

"Correctly." Gerda looked at her bowl. "That's the thing nobody understands, when I say it. Everyone wants somebody to have been cruel. Nobody was. Everybody did their job. The instructor meant to write it and didn't, and the clerk entered what happened, and the review read what the clerk entered, and the petition was answered by people reading the same page I was reading. If any one of them had done anything wrong I could have stood up and said so. They didn't. That was the worst of it."

Nobody said anything. At the far end of the long room somebody won at tiles and the second-years groaned.

Then Gerda's voice changed. It was a small change, and Cael almost missed it, but he had been listening to her procedure voice for two months and he heard it go.

"She got better," Gerda said. "My mother. She's well now. She writes to me every month, on the day the coach goes, and she never once mentions it. She thinks I don't know she feels it's her fault." Her right hand lifted off the table and came down again. "I check the clock because a clock is a thing that's written down. Every slip I sign, I write the hour on it, and I look up to make sure the hour's the hour, and I get my initials before the sixth bell, every day, because nobody is ever again going to find a thing about me that should have been written and wasn't."

She stopped. Her hands lay on the table in front of her, and she looked at them.

"So yesterday," she said, "I fought the way you fight when the only thing you're afraid of is giving them something to write. I didn't go out of the haze. I didn't take a hit. I didn't do one thing that could go wrong." She looked up at Lira. "And they wrote down that nothing happened. Which was correct."

Lira had not moved during the whole of it. Now she leaned forward on her good elbow.

"What does the provision say about reconsideration?"

Gerda answered at once, without having to think, as Cael would have answered with the three words of his own provision.

"A candidate whose review recommends reconsideration may elect, before the term's end, to fight one supplementary recorded bout before the same panel, by written request delivered to the presiding assessor with the hour noted. The panel shall consider the supplementary bout with the review."

"Then do that."

"I don't file things." Gerda said it simply, the way a person says they don't eat fish. "I filed one thing in my life. You've heard what happened."

"It isn't a petition. It's a form." Lira did not raise her voice. "Nobody posts it. Nobody answers it on a board. You write down that you want one more bout, and the hour, and you hand it to Quenna, and Quenna writes a date on the calendar. That's all it is." She held Gerda's eyes. "I'll sit with you while you write it. I'll stand at the desk while you hand it in. And between now and the bout, I'll get you out of that haze if I have to drag you."

Gerda looked at her with her pale level eyes, and Cael could not tell what she saw.

"Why?" said Gerda.

Lira sat back.

"Because on Thursday a floor got me out of a file," she said. "And yesterday there wasn't one for you. I'd like there to be one."

---

The second null was that afternoon, in the quiet room, with the slate on the nail outside saying *K. — declaration practice*.

Brom came as witness. He sat on the floor against the wall by the door as Lira had, with his knees up and a heel of bread in his coat. For the first quarter of an hour he watched with the grave attention he gave a bout. Then, a little after Karis had begun her paired points, he took out the bread and began, very quietly, to eat it.

Karis did not stop working. She laid a point and held it and let it go on her count, and laid the next, and between the third and fourth she reached down to the ledger on the boards beside her without looking and wrote something short, and went on.

At the end, when Cael had said nothing for the third time in his life and she had written the result, she turned the ledger round so he could read it under the fourth clause, and he read it.

*Session two. Source: K., Iron Rank Three, demonstration intensity, consent given aloud. Distance nine paces. Duration one quarter-hour. Declarations: single points only, eight. Subject's reach: attention alone, held throughout. Witness: B. Witness eating. Result: null.*

"You wrote that I was eating," said Brom, from the floor.

"You were eating."

"It isn't relevant."

"I don't know yet what's relevant," said Karis. "That's why I write everything down. If it turns out bread is the missing condition, you'll be very glad I noted it."

Brom considered this with great seriousness, and then took another bite.

The third was that evening, after supper, at five paces. Karis worked in pairs, two points at a time a pace apart, held to a count of twenty and let go together. Cael tried invitation, with his eyes shut between each pair and open for the bloom, the space held open in him beside the four like a hand held out across a room. Nothing came of it. *Session three,* the ledger said. *Source: Iron Rank Three, Ember, demonstration intensity. Distance five paces. Declarations: paired ignition points, held to count twenty. Subject's reach: invitation, eyes closed between declarations. Notice: none. Result: null.*

The fourth was on Sunday morning, before the first bell, with nobody else in the quiet room at all. Karis had asked whether he wanted to try it close, and he had said yes, and they had argued a little about how close. She settled it by walking up to a pace from him and saying, "Here. If it's too near, I'll know before you do." From one pace he could feel the heat of her points on his face. Each one bloomed a hand's width from his cheek, steady as a coin of lamplight, and the warmth came across his skin like standing near a hearth with his eyes shut. He wanted, plainly and with his whole chest, as hard as he had ever wanted anything. Nothing happened.

"Null," he said, when she had let the last point go.

"Null." She wrote it. She looked up at him from a pace away, close enough that he could see the tiredness at the edges of her eyes that he had not known was there. "Was it too near?"

"No."

"Good." She wrote that too. "Then we know distance alone, at the nearest I'm willing to go, isn't sufficient either."

He told Lira at the midday meal, in the general shape the third clause allowed. When he got to the part about one pace and the heat on his face, Lira stopped with her bread halfway to her mouth. She put it down. "One pace," she said. "With the thing in you reaching as hard as it can, and her hand's width from your face." She picked the bread up again. "If anything had gone wrong, at one pace, how fast would the clause have stopped it?" He did not have an answer, and she saw that he did not, and she let it go because the second-years were at the next table, but she did not eat the bread, and she did not look at him again until the meal was over.

---

At the sixth bell on Sunday Lira took Gerda onto the open floor, and she brought Hobb.

Cael never learned how. He suspected Brom had been asked to ask, and that Brom had gone to the defensive hall and stood beside Hobb until Hobb looked at him, and said one sentence, and that Hobb had said nothing back and simply come. However it had happened, Hobb was there, at the near section of the open floor, standing on the chalk with his feet a shoulder-width apart and his hands loose, as patient as a gatepost.

"He's going to stand there," Lira told Gerda. "That's all. He's Stone. He doesn't move unless he's made to. You're going to make him."

Gerda looked at Hobb, who looked back at her with his broad calm face and said nothing at all.

"With what?"

"With your whole weight. Out of the haze. You can lay it if you like, but you can't stand in it. You have to come out and put yourself into him, and he'll hit you back exactly as hard as Wray would tell him to, and you'll take it." Lira folded her arms on the good side. "Then you do it again."

Gerda laid her haze. It went out from her across the boards, grey and slow, and reached Hobb's feet and climbed his shins. Hobb did not move. A Stone fighter standing still in slowness was only a Stone fighter standing still, and the haze had nothing to do to him.

Gerda stood at the edge of her own grey and looked at him.

Cael watched her shoulders from the rail. He had watched them yesterday in the formal yard, the weight going forward and coming back, and he watched it happen again now, forward, back, forward, back, three times, four. On the fifth she came out of the haze, two quick steps, and put her shoulder into Hobb's chest. It was a good honest shove with real weight in it. Hobb took it the way a wall takes rain. He put one broad hand flat on her collarbone and pushed, not hard, exactly hard enough, and she went back two paces into her own haze and stood there with her breath gone and her face white.

"Again," said Lira.

Gerda did it again. Hobb did it again. She went back two paces.

She did it eleven times in the hour, and eleven times Hobb sent her back, and not once did she move him a finger's width. She did not take a single hit she had not been made to take. Every time she came out of the haze Cael could see her decide it, as if she had to make the choice from the beginning each time. And every time she went back into it, he could see her relief.

At the end of the hour she stood at the edge of the chalk with her hands on her knees, breathing.

"That's nothing," she said, to the boards. "Eleven times and nothing."

Hobb, who had not spoken once in the hour, took his hands out of his pockets.

"Eleven," he said. "Yesterday, none."

He nodded to Lira, and to Gerda, and went off across the floor toward the defensive hall without another word. Gerda straightened and looked after him until the hall door had shut behind him.

---

On Monday morning of the tenth week, in the archive, Gerda read the reconsideration clause aloud to Prynn.

Cael was there because he was always there at that hour now, at the far end of the long table with the statute book and a cup of tea he had not yet found the courage to drink. Lira had brought Gerda in at the second bell. Prynn looked up from her high stool, took off her spectacles, and looked at the two of them for exactly as long as it took Gerda to wonder whether she ought to leave. Monday morning was not one of Prynn's afternoons for certifying copies, and everybody at the table knew it.

"The re-certification provision," Lira said. "The clause on reconsideration. She needs a copy of it to cite on a form, certified."

Prynn came down off her stool, took a thin bound volume from the end of the nearest aisle, laid it on the long table, and opened it without looking to the right page. Then she set a sheet of heavy paper square to the table's edge and uncapped her pen.

"Sit," she said to Gerda. "You'll read it to me."

Gerda sat. She read it, one phrase at a time, waiting at each comma while Prynn's pen caught up, in her flat exact procedure voice. *A candidate whose review recommends reconsideration.* The pen scratched. *May elect, before the term's end.* Prynn's pen stopped.

"Again."

"May elect, before the term's end."

Prynn considered her over the top of the spectacles.

"You read *may*."

"It says *may*."

"It does." Prynn dipped her pen. "Most people who have been bitten by a procedure read *may* as *may not*. They hear it as a door somebody might shut. It isn't. It's a door you hold." She wrote. "Go on."

Gerda went on. When she reached the end Prynn read her copy back against the book, silently, with one finger moving down each, and signed the certification at the bottom and blotted it, and handed it to Gerda without a word. Gerda took it in both hands.

At the assessors' room at the third bell, Lira stood beside the desk while Gerda wrote the request beneath the certified clause in her careful square hand, every letter separate. The candidate elected to fight one supplementary recorded bout before the term's end, as the provision allowed. At the bottom she wrote the hour.

Then she looked up at the clock above the door.

Quenna took the sheet, read it, initialed it in the margin, and wrote something in the calendar book on her desk.

"The last day of the eleventh week," she said. "The first bell. The same panel." She looked at Gerda over the desk, and something passed across her face that Cael, standing in the passage outside the door, could not read. "Filed."

"Filed," said Gerda, very quietly, as if testing the word to see whether it would hold her weight.

Lira, beside her, did not say anything at all. She only put her good hand on Gerda's elbow, for a moment, and took it away again.

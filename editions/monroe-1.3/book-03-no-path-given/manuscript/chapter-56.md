# Chapter 56 — The Answer After

"The challenge rests on documents," said Yorlan. "The panel will hear the officers of the respondent institution who made them, as to the documents and no further. The presiding assessor of the candidate's sittings."

Quenna stood up from beside him.

She did not touch the table as she rose, or look at him, or do anything at all that a room could have read as a word passed between them. She walked to the chalk square on the open boards where Edran and Hobb had stood that morning, and stood in it with her hands folded in front of her and her slate left behind on the respondent's table, face down, beside his empty hands.

Coss stood behind his cases.

"Assessor. You presided at each of the respondent's six sittings under the provision."

"I did."

"And wrote, in each of them, the lines the panel has just heard."

"I drafted my own lines. The instructor's seat and the rotating seat drafted theirs."

"Your own lines, then." Coss did not raise his voice, and he did not lower it, and he did not look at anything but her. "You hold the Silver tier, and you have sat as an assessor in this district for twenty years. You chose these words." He laid two fingers on the sixth sitting's sheet. "You chose every one of them."

"I chose every one of them."

"Out of how many you might have used?"

For a moment Quenna said nothing. Cael, at the bare table with his hands in his lap, watched her decide to answer the question that had been asked and not the one beneath it.

"A great many," she said.

"Then I'll ask the one under it plainly, Assessor, so that neither of us has to pretend I haven't." Coss's hands were still. "When you wrote these lines, were you choosing words to say what you saw? Or were you choosing words that would say as little of what you saw as could be said and still be true?"

The room went so quiet that Cael heard the sand in the brass glass on the recording desk.

He had known this question was coming. He had known it since the morning in Naveth's office, when she had said that the next three weeks could have whatever they wanted of her; he had known it every time he had read one of her records, which he had done until he could have recited them. And knowing did not help at all, because the person standing in the chalk square was the person who had put her name to him in front of a panel when nobody else on the hill would, and she was about to be asked, in front of everybody, to choose between that and the truth.

She did not take long.

"Every record is a choice," said Quenna. "No assessor ever wrote down everything she saw. She couldn't. She'd be writing still." Her voice was the voice of the record, flat and dry, and it did not tremble anywhere. "I chose narrowly. I chose inside the standards of my seat. I chose in a case where one loose word could do harm out of all proportion to the truth in it, and I knew that, and I wrote with it in mind." She looked at Coss, and only at Coss. "Nothing in those records is false."

Coss let it rest a moment. Then he turned his head, not to her but to the bench.

"Nothing in those records is false," he said to the panel, gently, as a man repeats a figure so that the clerk can be sure of it. He did not add anything to it. He did not have to. The sentence had been true when she said it, and it was true when he said it, and in his mouth it meant something it had not meant in hers. "Thank you, Assessor. Nothing further."

She came back to the table and sat down beside him, and took up her slate, and turned it face up, and did not look at him.

Cael did not look at her either. He found that he could not have, for a while, without something happening to his face that the room was not entitled to, and he had promised her nothing on his face. He sat and watched the bench, and felt her beside him, very straight, and thought that he had owed her more than he had ever stopped to add up, and that he had only just this moment found out how much.

---

Naveth came next, and it was harder.

It was harder because Naveth had never stood on a floor in his life. He had sat behind a desk for longer than Cael had been alive, and made careful paper for people the registry had priced wrong, and he knew a great deal about how a sentence could be built so that it held, and nothing at all about how a sentence sounded when it was read back to you across open boards by a man who had built a better one. He walked to the chalk square slowly and stood in it like somebody who has been asked to stand on ice, with his long hands hanging, and Cael saw counsel at the academy's table shut his eyes for a moment and open them.

Coss was not unkind to him once. He did not need to be.

He walked the provost through the enrollment from the beginning: who had first raised the provision, and when; who had read the category line and the gloss; what the academy's council had been told, and by whom, at the meeting in the autumn when it approved the observer track for a candidate the scout of record had brought in. He asked each question plainly and accepted each answer as it came. And every careful thing Naveth had said in that council room in the autumn, every hedge he had meant as caution, came back to him across the boards in the order it had happened. Set out in that order, it drew a man who had seen the truth on the first afternoon and had signed his name under it anyway.

"The council's minute of that meeting," said Coss, and read from it. "*The provost advises that the enrollment is defensible on the face of the form, and expects it to be tested.*" He looked up. "Defensible on its face. You chose that phrase."

"I chose it because it was accurate," said Naveth. "This academy has always—"

"The academy isn't before the panel, Provost. One form is." Coss's voice was mild. "On its face, then. And underneath?"

Naveth opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it.

Cael saw it happen and could not look away. He saw the provost's right hand come up a little way from his side, as if to rest on a rail that was not there, and stop in the air. He saw his long face go very still, and then not still. He saw the muscles along the jaw work, once, twice, as a man's jaw works when he is trying to hold something down in his throat by force, and fail to hold it. Naveth took off his spectacles. He held them in one hand by the folded arms and looked at them as if he did not know whose they were. The silence went on past where a silence ought to stop, and two hundred people sat in it, and nobody coughed.

When he spoke his voice had gone somewhere Cael had never heard it go.

"I knew the category didn't fit him," said Naveth.

He stopped. He put the spectacles back on, carefully, with both hands, the way an old man does a small thing he has done ten thousand times and must suddenly think about. His eyes behind them were wet, and he did not hide it, and he did not wipe them.

"I signed it anyway."

There was a sound from the gallery, very small, from somewhere near the front. Cael did not turn to see who had made it.

Naveth looked across the open boards at the respondent's table, at Cael, for the first time since he had stood up. He looked at him for a long moment, and whatever was in his face then was not anything the provost of Greyvane had ever let anybody see at his desk, with the door open or shut. Then he looked back at the man who had asked him the question.

"I'd sign it again."

Coss did not move for a moment.

"I don't doubt it," he said at last, and there was nothing in it but the plain respect of one careful man for another who has just paid, in public, the whole price of telling the truth. "Thank you, Provost. Nothing further."

Naveth walked back to the academy's table. He walked as he always walked, upright and slow, and he sat down beside counsel and folded his long hands on the wood, and he did not take them apart again for the rest of the day. Behind Cael the gallery made a noise that was not words and settled. When Cael finally let himself glance aside, Quenna was writing on her slate, and the chalk was moving very slowly, and he saw that she was not writing anything at all, only drawing the same short line over and over in the same place.

---

The light had gone low and yellow in the high windows by the time Coss laid down his last paper.

It had been a long afternoon, and every hour of it had belonged to him. Cael sat with all four fingers of his left hand folded down against his knee and the thumb with them, and ran the count in his head the way he would have run a scorecard in the corner after a bout he had not been allowed to enter. *Text. Theirs. Drafting records. Theirs, signed. The form's face, and the deposition. Theirs, in my own voice. Precedent. Theirs, unbroken. The records of the sittings. Theirs, every word true. The witnesses. Theirs. Both of mine.*

Every exchange to the challenge, and nothing on his side of the page but a bare table.

That was the plan. He told himself so, quite calmly, and his heart went on doing something behind his ribs that did not agree. There had never been any point answering a man who had not yet put down all he had. All day he had watched Coss lay everything down and keep nothing back, and now it was all down, and the floor was full of it.

Behind him the gallery had gone very quiet in the way he had heard rooms go quiet at the Ironyard in the last exchange of a bout that was already decided, when the crowd stops shouting because there is nothing left to shout for and begins, in a low murmur, to talk about something else. He heard the murmur begin. He heard somebody in the second row say *well, that's that*, not unkindly. He heard the benches shift.

Yorlan closed the thin book on his finger.

"The challenge is heard," he said. "The answer is for the second sitting. The panel will—"

Cael stood up.

The chair scraped on the boards behind him, loud in the high quiet room, and he heard the murmur in the gallery stop dead, as a crowd stops when a fighter who is supposed to be finished gets up off the chalk. Beside him Quenna's hand moved a finger's width on the table. It was not to stop him. He knew the difference. It was the movement of somebody setting her feet.

"I won't be defending the enrollment, Magistrate."

Yorlan did not move. He looked straight at the respondent's table, which he had not done since the morning, with the book still shut on his finger, and on his left the grey-bearded assessor's pen stopped in the middle of a word.

"I'll be challenging the Compact's jurisdiction to void it."

For the space of a breath nobody did anything at all.

Cael stood in it with his hands at his sides and felt it, the whole length of it, the way he had once felt the half-second at the Ironyard before the board went up with Reydan's name on it beside his own. He saw Ilsev, on the right of the bench, look up sharply from her papers, the first time all day he had seen her eyes leave the page. She did not look at him. She looked at the stack of dark-spined volumes at her elbow, closed, where somebody had set them that morning in case anybody needed them. She looked at them for a long moment, the way a woman looks at a tool she had brought along without expecting to have to pick it up.

And across the open boards, at the delegation's table, Cael saw something cross Coss's face and go. It was small, and complicated, and gone almost before it arrived. It was not surprise. It was the look of a man who has spent three weeks watching a lamp burn late in an archive window across a dark town, and has guessed, and has made himself not ask, and has just heard the one thing he guessed walk out onto the record in a boy's voice.

"The panel notes it," said Yorlan.

He opened the thin book again, and turned a page, and read from it, as if the answer to what had just happened had been printed there all along, which perhaps it had.

"The panel will not hear argument for which it has not been shown the ground. The respondent will show the panel its ground at the second sitting, or the panel will not hear it." He closed the book. "When it sits, the code will be on the bench in front of it. The second sitting will open at the first bell. This sitting is closed."

He struck the small wooden gavel on the bench, once.

The room went up.

It broke over the benches in one wave, like the Ironyard when an outsider takes the third exchange, so loud and so sudden that Cael felt it in the boards under his feet before he heard it. People were on their feet behind the rope. Second-years were shouting at one another, asking things none of them knew, and the tout was standing on his bench with his crust book held over his head as if it might be taken from him. Somebody near the windows had begun to laugh and could not stop. The people who did not belong to the hill were already moving: the two grey coats first, fast, toward the open doors, with the walk of men who had letters to write before the evening coach; the woman in the dark blue coat writing as she went; the man with the guild's satchel pushing along a bench and being pushed back. And through all of it, dry and level and perfectly audible, came Prynn's voice from the back wall, informing somebody with sixty years of authority behind it that he would not be climbing over her bench to get to the door any quicker, and that the door would be there when he arrived.

Cael stayed where he was, with his palm on the wood. On the circuit he had learned to hear a crowd the way he heard rain on a roof, as something happening to the room that did not need him to do anything about it, and he heard this one that way.

Across the floor Coss was packing his cases.

He did it as he had done everything that day, in order, each paper back in its folder and each folder back in its case, the drafting records and the precedents and the bound fair copy of the deposition, without hurry, the way a man puts away his tools at the end of a long day's work that has gone exactly as he planned it, which until a few breaths ago it had. His aide was talking to him in a low urgent voice, and Coss did not appear to be listening. When the last case was closed and its clasp shut he stood with his hand on it a moment.

Then he looked up, across the scrubbed boards and the noise, straight at Cael, which he had not done once since the first bell.

He gave him one nod. It was small and plain and nothing was withheld from it. It was the nod Cael had given and been given across a hundred chalk lines at the end of a first exchange, when both men have taken the other's measure and know now what kind of fight it is going to be, and when.

Cael gave it back.

Quenna's hand settled on his shoulder. The roar reached him then, all of it together, and the first sitting was done.

---

The stable was worse than the assembly room.

He could not get to the scarred table for a long time. The whole of the stable seemed to want to stand near him without speaking to him, and the second-years kept coming past the end of the table and stopping and opening their mouths and going away again. The tout sent a heel of bread down the table with his compliments, and somebody from the movement floor whom Cael had never spoken to clapped him on the back so hard that his bowl jumped. In the end Lira stood up from the bench, turned round, and looked at the room. It did not quite go quiet, but it remembered all at once that it had business at the other end.

"Eat," said Lira, sitting down again. "And don't talk. Everybody in this building wants to ask you what you meant, and if you start telling one of them you'll be telling all of them till the eighth bell. Tomorrow you'll be telling the panel. Save it."

He ate. Brom ate beside him, steadily, and every so often looked up at the room over his spoon with the expression he wore at a rail when somebody was leaning on it who ought not to be, and the somebody always moved. Karis did not eat at all. She had her notebook open beside her bowl and was writing in it with her face very close to the page, and twice she stopped and looked at Cael across the table, and opened her mouth, and shut it again, and went back to writing.

"Ask," said Cael at last.

"I'm not going to ask. I know what you're going to say. I sat behind the bench of books for a week." Karis put the pen down. "I was going to tell you something, and then I decided it would only be me talking because I can't bear to sit still. So I've written it down instead, and you can read it in a month." She closed the notebook. "His face. When you stood. Did you see it?"

"I saw it."

"I'd like to know what that was," said Karis, "more than I'd like to know almost anything. And I never will. That's what I've written down."

Brom put his spoon down in his empty bowl.

"Tomorrow," he said, "everybody in that room is going to come in thinking it's a different fight from the one it was this morning. They'll have spent all night guessing at it." He looked at Cael. "You don't guess. You know what it is. That's the advantage. Don't waste it by lying awake."

"I'll try."

"Don't try," said Brom. "Sleep. It's not a thing you try."

Lira took the card out of her coat pocket, the one she had confiscated at breakfast, and laid it on the table in front of Karis, face down, and then the pencil and the chalk in front of Cael, side by side, in a row.

"Supper," she said. "I said you'd have them back at supper."

---

Quenna's note was under his door when he went up, folded once, in her square hand. *My room. Now. Not long.*

He went up the narrow stair at the back of the main hall and knocked, and she said *come*, and he went in past the cold grate to the desk under the window. She had a lamp lit and the shutters closed on the dark yard, and a single sheet of paper on the desk in front of her, and she did not tell him to sit, so he stood.

"Procedure," she said. "Then you can go to bed. Tomorrow you are the respondent answering a challenge under the charter, and the charter has a rule about that, and I want you to have heard it from me before you hear it from the magistrate." She put one finger on the sheet. "The respondent's primary presentation is made standing, and it is made uninterrupted. Nobody may break into it. Not the officer of record, not counsel, not a member of the panel. The magistrate may stop you for order and for nothing else. You may speak until you have finished, and you will be the one who decides when you have finished." She looked up. "That's very rare. Most people who stand on a floor like that never get it, because they don't speak for themselves. You do. So it's yours, all of it, from the first word to the last. I'd like you to know it's there before you need it."

"Uninterrupted."

"Uninterrupted. If the Warden stands up in the middle, the magistrate will sit him down. He knows that. He'll stand up anyway, if he thinks it's worth being sat down." She turned the sheet over, and there was nothing on the back of it. "That's the procedure. Now the other thing."

She was quiet for a while, and he waited, because he had learned in twenty weeks that a Quenna who was quiet was a Quenna choosing which of several true things to say.

"Today I sat beside you," she said. "I'll sit beside you tomorrow, if you want me there. I'm your assessor of record. Nobody will think anything of it. It's where I'm supposed to be."

He had known she would offer. He had thought about it, on and off, since the stair after the council in the nineteenth week, and he had decided, and the deciding had not been easy, and it was not easy now that she was in front of him.

"I'd like you not to," he said.

Quenna did not move.

"It isn't that I don't want you there." He made himself go on, because she had earned the whole of it and not a piece. "It's that tomorrow has to be mine. Every word of it. If you're sitting beside me, then whatever I say, half the room will think it's the academy's argument with a boy standing up to say it. Your drafting, with my name on the front. And the other half will think you're there to catch me if I fall." He looked at the blank side of the sheet on her desk. "Nobody's ever let me say what I am. Not at the station, not in Ardenmere, not on the form. It was always somebody else's words, written over my head. I've spent three weeks earning the right to stand up and say this one thing myself. I'd like the room to see me do it by myself."

For a long time Quenna said nothing, and the lamp hissed between them.

He watched her take it, and watched what it cost her, and did not look away from any of it. She had staked her name on him in front of a panel when nobody else would. She had stood in a chalk square that afternoon and chosen the truth over him, and then come back and sat down beside him as if nothing had happened. Now he was asking her to stand up from that chair in front of the whole room and move it, so that everybody could see her not protect him.

"A row back," she said at last. "Behind the table. Close enough that you'll know where I am. Far enough that the record will show a respondent who answered for himself." She pulled the sheet toward her and folded it in half, and then in half again. "That's the last thing I can give you on that floor, you understand. Taking the chair away."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do. Not yet." Quenna stood up, which meant he should go. "You will in about twenty years, when somebody asks it of you." She went to the door and held it for him, and on the threshold she stopped him with two fingers on his sleeve, very lightly, which she had never done before. "Go to sleep. You never do the night before. Tonight you will, because I'm telling you to, and because there's nothing left to do."

He did sleep.

It did not come at once. He lay in the dark of the narrow room with his coat on the chair and the two papers in its pocket, and the fear came and lay down beside him as it always did on the night before a floor, the same size it had always been. He let it. Under it there was the feeling he had found on the eve and had not lost: something in the middle of him that did not move. He thought of Naveth taking his spectacles off in the chalk square, and of Quenna drawing the same short line on her slate over and over. He thought of a girl on the front bench who had stopped writing, and of Hobb, and of a man packing his cases in order, who had looked up once.

Before he put out the lamp he had written one line in the binder, in the square hand, under the eve's.

*The first sitting is his. Every word true. Tomorrow I stand up alone, and nobody may interrupt me.*

He read it once and did not change it, and the last thing he heard before he slept was the town's lamps going out below the wall, one by one, though he could not have heard that, and knew he could not, and heard it anyway.

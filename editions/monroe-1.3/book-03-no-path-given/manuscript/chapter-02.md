# Chapter 2 — Three Lines

The intake room had three desks, and that told Cael most of what he needed to know before anyone spoke.

It was a low old room built from the same grey stone as the wall, with ceiling beams close enough that Brom ducked under the first one out of caution and then looked faintly embarrassed when he saw he hadn't needed to. Four other arrivals sat on a bench along the back wall with their bags at their feet, and a window let the afternoon in so that the light lay across the floorboards in a long pale square. Each of the three desks had its own clerk and its own line, and above each line hung a small painted board.

*Standard Enrollment.*

*Re-certification.*

The third board was blank.

"That's you," Lira murmured.

"It might be for transfers."

"Transfers go in the standard line. I asked the gate woman while you were having your moment." She nudged him. "Blank board. That's you."

He looked at it, a board somebody had hung up and then not known what to paint on, and thought it was the most accurate sign anyone had ever made about him.

Brom went first, because his line was empty. His clerk was a brisk young woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She took his papers, scanned them, and turned them face up on the desk in one smooth motion: a Copper-tier certification from a registered Arbiter station, Iron Skin Path, and a clean record of two seasons' documented circuit work with Vell's stamp at the bottom of every page.

"Standard. Good."

She wrote and stamped. She asked him two questions, his age and whether he had any injuries the floor instructors should know about, and entered the answers in a neat column, checked something on a list, and stamped again. Cael, waiting on the bench, counted the time without meaning to, because he counted most things without meaning to.

Eleven minutes.

At the end of them the clerk reached into a drawer and set a small metal disc on the desk in front of Brom, coin-sized and stamped with the academy's crest, a tower above a road, very simply cut.

"Your enrollment tag. It opens the training hall, the lecture wing, the residence wing and the library. Keep it on you. Supper's at the seventh bell, and orientation for new students is tomorrow after the first. Upper corridor of the residence wing, third door on the left. Your name's on it."

Brom looked at the tag. "That's it?"

"That's it."

He picked it up and turned it once in his fingers, and his face did something Cael had not seen it do before. On anyone else it would have been a smile. On Brom it was a kind of quiet astonishment, as if he had put his weight on a step expecting it to creak and it had simply held.

He came over to the bench and held the tag up between two fingers.

"Eleven minutes," said Cael.

"You counted."

"I count everything."

Brom sat down beside him and looked at the tag again.

"Two years I've been the complicated one. Every room. Every hall. My family, the circuit, all of it. You can't just be a Copper Iron Skin from a good estate who walked away from the estate; there's always a question after." He shook his head slowly. "She didn't ask the question."

"Maybe she didn't know there was one."

"Maybe there isn't one here."

He put the tag in his pocket, took it out again a moment later as if it might have changed its mind, found it hadn't, and put it away properly.

"I'm going to find my room. I've never had a door with my name on it."

He left, and Cael watched him go and found that he was smiling.

---

Lira's line took most of an hour.

Her clerk was an older man with half-moon spectacles and the slow, careful manner of someone who had handled difficult paper before, and he did not hurry. From a box at his elbow he took a thin file, and it took Cael a moment to understand that it was her Fenmark file. It had arrived here before she had, because academies talked to one another about the students they had thrown out. The clerk laid it open beside a printed sheet of rules and compared the two line by line, moving a finger down each in turn, making a small mark now and then, sometimes stopping to read a line twice.

Lira stood in front of the desk with her arms folded. There was a chair, and she did not sit in it, and she did not look at the file. Cael knew that stance. He had seen her take it across a sparring circle a hundred times while she waited out an opponent she hadn't finished measuring, weight even and shoulders loose and nothing given away.

The bench emptied and filled again. A boy with a Blade Path crest on his collar went through the standard line in nine minutes, and a tall girl was sent away to fetch a missing letter and came back flushed.

At last the clerk set a single sheet on the desk and turned it to face her.

"I'm required to read this aloud, each clause, and you'll confirm you understand it."

"I can read."

"I know you can. It's the procedure."

She nodded once.

From where he stood, Cael could read the heading whether he wanted to or not. *Acknowledgment of Prior Standing.* The clerk read it in a flat, even voice: that Fenmark Academy had ended her enrollment; that the record of that ending was to stand as written; that her candidacy for re-certification at Greyvane was granted on the understanding that she accepted that record as accurate and did not dispute it as a condition of entry.

"Do you understand the clause?"

"Yes."

"There is a separate process for a formal dispute, should you wish to pursue one later. It is not connected to your enrollment here and would go through channels outside this academy." He tapped a small unmarked box at the bottom of the sheet. "You may note an intention to dispute. It doesn't affect your candidacy."

"I understand."

He turned the sheet toward her a little further and laid a pen beside it, and she read it again herself, slowly.

Her jaw did most of the talking. A small muscle worked at the hinge of it, tightening and easing and tightening again, while the rest of her face stayed perfectly still. Cael understood that he was watching two years of a thing being held down by force to a size that would fit an intake desk. She had spent those two years refusing to say *expelled* without saying *wrongly* right after it; he had heard her do it in Ardenmere to strangers who had not even asked.

She picked up the pen and signed, and she did not mark the box.

The clerk blotted the signature, put the sheet in the file and the file in the box, and gave her a disc like Brom's, crested like his, with one difference: under the crest, small and neat, someone had stamped a single raised dot. She looked at the dot for a moment, then put the tag in her pocket without a word, and when she came over to the bench she sat down beside Cael and stared at the opposite wall.

"Well."

"You didn't mark the box."

"No."

"You could have."

"I know." She breathed out. "I could have marked it and fought about it later. I could have stood there and told that nice man exactly why Fenmark was wrong; I've had the speech ready for two years." She turned her head and looked at him. "I'd rather have a bed in this place than be proved right on paper about what Fenmark did to me at fourteen. That's new, for me. Wanting the one thing more than the other."

"How does it feel?"

She was quiet a long time.

"I was ready for it to be Fenmark all over again. That day, standing in a room while somebody reads you what you are." She looked down at her hands. "It doesn't. It feels like setting a pack down at the end of a road. I didn't know how much it weighed until it was on the ground."

Then she stood up abruptly, the way she did when a feeling threatened to become a conversation.

"Go and see what they make of you. I'll be on the wall outside."

---

Nobody called him forward, because there was no line for him to stand in. Instead, as the old clerk finished stacking Lira's box, a door at the side of the room opened and Quenna came through it.

She was exactly as he remembered her from the Ironyard: about forty, dark hair cut short and plain, a grey coat with no ornament on it at all, a leather folder under one arm, and the faint air of a woman who had been arguing with someone in another room and had won, but only just.

"Cael. Come and sit."

She did not hand him to a clerk. She led him to the third desk, under the blank board, and sat on the far side of it herself. He registered that it could be read two ways — as a courtesy, because she had brought him here and meant to see him through it, or as a warning, because he was the kind of case that could not be trusted to a clerk. After a moment he decided it was both, and that both were reasonable.

"This will take some time, and I'm going to be honest with you about why. There isn't a form for you."

"I noticed the board."

The corner of her mouth moved. "The carpenter asked what to paint on it. Nobody could tell him."

The afternoon went in pieces. Papers came to the desk in twos and threes: a letter of acceptance; a copy of his circuit record with Vell's stamp; a form that asked for his Path and tier and rank, which Quenna read, frowned at and set aside; another that asked the same questions in different words; and a third, older, with a line reading *registry document attached*, which led to a long, quiet search through her folder. His registry document was in there. She had obtained a copy from somewhere, and he saw the corner of it as she leafed past, and the word in brackets, before she covered it again.

Twice she excused herself and went through the side door, and through it, faintly, he heard her voice and another voice, low and patient, going back and forth. The words did not carry, but the tone did, and it was the tone of two people trying very hard to fit a large thing through a small opening without breaking either.

A boy from the kitchen brought tea on a tray. Cael thanked him, held the cup in both hands, and forgot to drink it. The patch of sun from the window crept down the back wall, crossed the floorboards toward the desk legs, and went thin. The other clerks finished their days; the man with the half-moon spectacles nodded to Quenna on his way out, and the young woman who had enrolled Brom gave Cael a look he read easily as *better you than me*. The bench emptied. Somebody, somewhere, lit a lamp.

He did not mind, and that surprised him. He had imagined this afternoon a dozen times on the road, and in every version he had been angry by now, or frightened, or both, and instead, watching Quenna come back from her second argument with a fresh sheet in her hand and her mouth set, he understood that what he was looking at was not a wall and not someone trying to keep him out but a building straining, in good faith, to find words for him. He had been described in bad faith before, in Denvash and in a market square and in a hundred small moments on the circuit before Vell's ledger gave people something better to go on. Good faith was slower. It was worth a whole afternoon.

"Here," Quenna said at last.

She turned a long printed form toward him. It had his name at the top in a clerk's careful hand, a seal pressed into the bottom corner, and a blank signature line with a pen laid neatly beside it. Above the signature line was a box labeled *Category of Enrollment*.

The box said *unclassified observer.*

He read it.

Beneath the category, in smaller type, a printed note explained what the category was. It was reserved for any prospective practitioner who *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment*. Such students were usually children whose families had asked for early schooling before their certification appointments. A Path, a tier, a rank, any record from an Arbiter: the category conferred none of those. What it did confer was a seat in lectures, training under supervision, and academic standing.

He read the category line again.

*Unclassified observer.*

He reached for the tea, found it had gone lukewarm, and set it down without drinking.

He read the category line a third time, and the gloss under it. *Has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.*

The tea was colder when he looked at it again. He did not reach for it this time.

"I underwent it." His voice came out quieter than he meant it to. "I was fourteen. Denvash. There's a record."

"There is."

"Then this is wrong."

"There's a record, and the classification on it is one the Compact's own schedule never defines." She said it evenly, without softening it; she had never softened anything she'd said to him. "This category exists for anyone without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter. A child who hasn't Kindled has no standard classification. Neither do you. Yours isn't standard. The schedule has no line for what yours is."

"So you found a gap."

"I didn't make it. It was already there. I'm only using it."

He looked at the line again. "It says something that isn't true. In ink."

"Every word on that page is used correctly, and it will bear any reading anyone cares to give it. That matters; that's what protects you." She leaned forward slightly. "What holds up less well is the purpose. The people who drafted the provision were thinking of twelve-year-olds whose parents wanted them to sit in on lectures. They were not thinking of you. Someday someone will read this form more carefully than a clerk does, and they will ask about the purpose."

"So someone will come and read it properly."

"Yes."

She did not hesitate over it, and he was grateful.

"And then?"

"Then we need to be established fact before they get here." She laid one finger on the seal. "A student with a monthly assessment record. Public, dated, signed by the people who watched. Each month you spend here, passing, in ink, makes the case against you a little harder to make."

"And if they argue anyway?"

"Then they'll have to argue their way past a stack of dated pages first. I've backed worse odds than these, Cael. I don't back anything I expect to lose."

His eyes stayed on the form. He thought of the four other people the registry had ever written his word against, four cases, all of them gone within weeks, whose names he did not know and whose number he had carried since he was fourteen the way he carried his own heartbeat. This form would not make him a fifth. It would make him something else, not a known problem with a file but an error the registry had never been asked to hold. Progress, of an odd sort. He was not sure it was better. As far as he could see, no other door stood open.

He picked up the pen and signed in the careful hand he used for the Power Log, the slow, square hand he had learned to keep for anything he meant to stand behind.

The ink dried. Neither of them mentioned how far the words sat from the truth. Quenna knew it, he knew it, the empty room seemed to know it, and saying it aloud would not have made the page more honest.

Then Quenna did something he had not expected. She lifted the signed form, and he saw that it was three sheets, not one, pressed together with a thin layer of copying paper between them. She separated them with practiced fingers. The top sheet went into her folder. The second she slid across to him. The third had a line printed across its head that the others did not — *Registry Copy, for Notation* — and she initialed it in the margin, folded it twice, and put it into a long brown envelope already addressed in a clerk's hand to the regional registry office.

"What's that?"

"Routine." She sealed the envelope. "Any enrollment that attaches a registry document goes up to the regional office to be noted against the file. It leaves with the town post on the third day."

"Does anyone read them?"

"Usually a clerk enters a line and files the sheet." She set the envelope on the corner of the desk, square to the edge. "Usually."

He looked at the brown envelope, with his name inside it and the hollow word on his form, sitting on a desk in a room that was getting dark, and found that he did not like it there at all.

Quenna took a disc from her folder and slid it across to him. It was the same size as Brom's, but it had no crest; where the crest should have been, someone had stamped a hollow circle, a ring with nothing in the middle.

"Provisional access. It opens what Brom's opens. Anyone who checks it will learn that the reason isn't theirs to ask."

He turned it over. The back was blank.

"Welcome to Greyvane." There was something under the flatness of it that was almost gentle. "Supper's at the seventh bell. You've missed the sixth. I'd eat."

---

Lira and Brom had claimed the low wall beside the intake door, and they had left a space between them that was exactly his width, with the evening's last light lying along the stones. He saw it from twenty paces off and it did something to his chest. They had sat on walls like this in more towns than he could count, and he had never once had to ask where to sit.

He sat.

"Well?"

"I'm an unclassified observer. In writing. With a signature."

Brom considered that the way he considered a new kind of hold. "That isn't you."

"No."

"Do you mind?"

Cael gave it an honest answer, because an honest answer was the only kind he trusted himself to give about something that mattered.

"Less than I expected. The words hold up; Quenna's right about that. I've spent so long being something the paperwork had no name for that one more wrong form hardly shows." He paused. "I'm still going to write about it."

"Two pages," said Lira. "Possibly three."

"The form's not what interests me," said Brom, "for whatever that's worth."

"What is?"

"That they built anything at all." Brom looked across the yard at the old wall going grey in the dusk. "Most places don't build you a door. They let you walk around the outside until you work out there isn't one. This one's the wrong shape. Fine. You've been getting through wrong-shaped things since I met you."

Lira stared at him. "Did you just say something wise before supper?"

"I'm hungry. It loosens me up."

"Show me the tag," she said to Cael, and when he held it out she tilted it toward the last of the sky so that the hollow circle caught the light. "A ring with nothing in it."

"Quenna says it means the reason isn't theirs to ask."

"It means *please don't ask*." She handed it back, sounding very slightly pleased. "Mine has a dot. Brom's has a tower. You have a hole. We're a set."

Brom took his own tag out, as if to check the tower was still there. It was.

A bell rang somewhere beyond the training yard, not the seventh, Cael could tell by the pitch, but some earlier signal in a rhythm he had not learned yet. He got the binder out anyway and wrote it plainly, without decoration.

*Enrolled. The word is doing a lot of work.*

He looked at the line, and then added a second one beneath it, smaller.

*It isn't doing it alone.*

Lira read it over his shoulder without asking, the way she always had, and said nothing, only bumped his arm with hers once. And then the seventh bell rang, and the three of them got up and went to find out what an academy fed people.

It was barley: barley and onions and a little mutton, ladled out in a long low room that had plainly been a stable once, its old hay door bricked up but still outlined in the wall. Students sat in loose groups at trestle tables, most of them already talking over empty bowls, and a few looked up when the three of them came in, and most didn't.

Brom ate two bowls with an expression of deep contentment.

"It's honest food," he said, when Lira looked at him. "It doesn't pretend to be anything."

"It's porridge with ambitions."

"It's barley. Barley has never once lied to me."

"Neither has a rock, and I don't eat those either."

Cael ate. It was hot and plain and there was enough of it, and he was more tired than he had known. Three days of walking and a whole afternoon of sitting still had left him hollow in a way the barley filled quite well. Afterward the three of them climbed to the residence wing, where the corridors bent wherever the old building had needed them to bend and the doors were of six or seven different ages and woods, each with a small painted board beside it. Brom's was across the corridor from his, and Lira's was two doors down. Passing the stair, Cael noticed a roster pinned to the wall that showed empty rooms in two other halls, which meant that someone had put the three of them together on purpose, someone who had decided before they arrived that they were a unit. Quenna, almost certainly. He did not mention it. He suspected the others had noticed too, and that none of them wanted to say it aloud and make it smaller.

"Good night," said Lira at her door.

Brom stood in front of his own door a moment before opening it, read his name on the board, and went in and shut the door softly behind him.

---

Bed, desk, shelf: the room held those and very little else, between whitewashed walls, and its one window showed him the blank grey side of the training yard's wall, which asked nothing of anybody. Cael set his pack down, sat on the bed, and listened: feet on a stair, a latch somewhere, voices through the wall too faint for words, a door closing, water moving in a pipe. In a week he would know which latch was which and whose feet those were. He had done it in Ardenmere, and at Hesk's long before that, without ever thinking of it as work. Tonight he knew none of them. Tonight they were only the sounds of a place that had decided to have him.

He lit the lamp and took out a sheet of paper. He had made a promise in a boarding house in Ardenmere with Hesk's letter open beside him — *I'll write when I arrive* — and he had arrived, and that was all there was to it.

He wrote slowly.

*Hesk —*

*I'm here. Greyvane is small, smaller than I imagined. There's an old wall, older than the school, and a hill town below it with a mill and a baker Lira had already smelled from a quarter-mile away.*

*The gate clerk had our names on a list, all three of them. I don't know how to explain why that mattered so much. I think you'll understand without my explaining it.*

He paused over the next part and thought about leaving out the form. He did not leave it out. Hesk had never once asked him to make anything smaller than it was.

*They've enrolled me under a category meant for children who haven't Kindled yet. It's called unclassified observer. It isn't true, and everybody at the table knew it wasn't. The woman who brought me here says the words will hold up, and I think she's right. I signed it. I want you to know I signed it with my eyes open.*

*Brom got through intake in eleven minutes and didn't know what to do with himself. Lira signed a paper that said Fenmark was right about her, and she says it felt like putting something down. I'm not sure yet what it felt like for me. I'll tell you when I know.*

*You told me to find more people who have seen me clearly and haven't left. I haven't found new ones yet. But I came through the gate with two, and there's a woman here who argued with someone in a back room all afternoon to find a word for me. That might be the start of a third.*

*I'm well. The shoulder still complains on certain angles.*

*I'll write again after the first assessment.*

*Cael*

It was not long, but Hesk's letters were never long either. He folded it, wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the desk to go down to the town. It would leave, he realized, on the same third-day post as Quenna's brown envelope. He sat looking at the folded letter for a while with that thought, two pieces of paper with his name on them going down the same hill in the same bag to very different readers.

Then he opened the binder to the back pages, where he kept the things that were not fragments, and thought about the board above the third desk, the blank one, a carpenter asking what to paint and nobody knowing. He thought, for the first time with no fear in it at all, that he would rather be a blank board somebody had bothered to hang than a painted one in a building that wanted him gone.

He wrote that down too. Then he blew out the lamp and lay in the dark, listening to the building he didn't know yet, and fell asleep before he had learned a single sound.

# Chapter 6 — Panel Speed

The morning after the notice, Quenna gave him the rules in the corridor outside the archive, standing, with her folder under her arm and the air of a woman already late for something else, as if the rules were short enough that sitting down for them would have been a waste of a chair.

"There'll be three chairs. Mine in the middle. Wray has the instructor's chair this month. The third goes round the senior faculty in turn, and nobody tells the candidate whose turn it is; we do that on purpose. Spend what's left of your four days on yourself, not on guessing a stranger."

"All right."

"The format is yours to fill. You'll have a training partner, assigned by the instructor's seat, who will strike when he's told to strike and stop when he's told to stop. Nowhere does the provision ask you to name a Path."

"Good. I haven't got one."

"The drafters seem to have foreseen you." Her voice gave the irony nothing at all. "The standard is three words. Consistent, observable practice."

She let them sit in the cold corridor air for a moment, as if she wanted him to hear each one land separately.

"Attend to those three words, because nothing else in the room will matter that morning. *Consistent*: they must see it more than once, built the same way, ending the same way. Once can be luck. Twice is a pattern. *Observable* means it happens in the room, at a scale a person holding a slate can see and write down, nothing internal, nothing they have to take your word for. And *practice*—" She paused a fraction longer than she had on the others. "*Practice* means it is something you do on purpose. Not something that happens to you."

Cael heard, inside that pause, everything she was not saying. He had told her nothing about how the fragments arrived, and she had never asked, but she had watched him fight for months before she ever spoke to him and she was not a fool, and she had just drawn a line on the floor between *a thing he did* and *a thing that happened to him* with the care of a woman marking a boundary she did not intend to step over.

"Read the words for yourself before you decide anything, and notice what isn't in them. Nothing about your best. Nothing about all of it. Whoever wrote that provision knew how far a loose phrase will stretch, and left it loose."

"And whatever I show goes on the record."

"Whatever you show goes on the record, in three hands. Think about that half harder than the first."

She went off down the corridor without saying goodbye, and Cael stood a while outside the archive door, looking at the iron bands on the dark wood without seeing them. He was free to show anything at all, as long as it came out the same twice and a stranger with a pen could follow it and it was his own doing. That was a gift. It was also, he saw a moment later, a trap of exactly the same size, because anything he was free to show he was also free to show too much of, and whatever he showed would be written down and kept.

So he knocked on the frame.

Prynn looked up from a ledger at a high desk just inside, took off her spectacles, and looked at him for exactly as long as it took him to wonder whether he ought to leave.

"The observer."

"I'd like to read the demonstration provision. The academy's copy."

"Would you." She did not move. "Most people ask their assessor to tell them what it says."

"My assessor told me to read it."

Something changed very slightly at the corner of Prynn's mouth. She put her spectacles back on, came down off her stool, and opened the door perhaps two fingers wider than its usual angle, which was not an invitation but the absence of a refusal, and from her that seemed to be the same thing.

The archive was colder than the corridor and much quieter. The shelves ran back in narrow aisles one person wide, and the light came from three high windows and a single lamp on the reading table. Prynn walked him to the end of the nearest aisle, which held the academy's own records, newer than anything else in the room, took down a thick bound book with *Statutes and Provisions* stamped on its spine in faded gilt, laid it on the table, and opened it without looking to the right page.

"You've done that before."

"Every year or two, for sixty years." She set a weight on the facing page to hold it flat. "Mostly for Quenna. Once for a man who wanted to know if he could use it for his nephew. He couldn't." She looked at him over the spectacles. "Tea?"

He had not expected that. "Yes. Thank you."

She went away between the shelves, and he sat and read. The provision was short; after an afternoon of arguments in a back room he had expected something longer, and it was barely half a page. It said that a candidate without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter might be enrolled for academic exposure and supervised training, that such a candidate would be assessed monthly by demonstration before a panel of three, that the candidate must demonstrate *consistent, observable practice*, and that the panel's finding should be entered in the candidate's record. That was very nearly all of it. He read it twice, slowly, paying attention to what was not there. There was no word about best, no word about all, no word about naming anything, and no word, he noticed, about how a candidate had come by whatever he practiced. The provision asked what he could do. It did not ask where it came from.

He sat back and let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

Prynn came back with a cup on a saucer and set it at his elbow, well away from the open book. The tea was very strong and very hot and had plainly been stewing in its pot since the morning.

"Don't spill it on the statutes."

"I won't."

"Everyone says that." She returned to her high stool and her ledger and did not look at him again.

He drank the tea, which was terrible, and drank all of it anyway, because she had brought it, and because the room was cold, and because he had a feeling that refusing it would be noticed and remembered for a very long time. When he left he closed the book and set it square on the table exactly where she had laid it, with the weight beside it, and carried the cup and saucer to the end of her desk. Prynn looked at the cup, and then at the book, square and closed, and then at him.

"Hm," she said, and went back to her ledger.

Two days later he found that she had left the book out for him. It was still on the table, closed, with the weight beside it, when he next came in. Nobody had reshelved it, and he was fairly sure that nobody in that room did anything Prynn had not decided they should.

---

That night he sat on his bed with the binder and began, as he began most decisions that mattered, by writing down everything he could show and then striking through most of it, until what was left would fit in the palm of his hand. Then he made himself do something harder, which was to imagine the record being read.

Not by Quenna and not by Wray. They would be in the room, and whatever they wrote would be honest. He imagined it being read a year from now, in some office he had never seen, by somebody who had never been in the room at all. He built that reader carefully, because a careless version would be useless. Not a villain; a villain would make mistakes out of spite, and spite could be planned for. He built a clerk instead, patient and thorough and correct in every procedure, with a long file of notes on him and no particular feeling about him either way, who would sit at a plain desk under a plain lamp and read the panel's entry line by line, looking not for what it said but for what it implied. How fast had the observer grown? Where was his ceiling? What could he do that a child who had not yet Kindled could not possibly do? What, in short, made it harder to call him an *unclassified observer* and keep a straight face?

He had met people like that, a man in a market square with careful questions and a careful voice, officials who had looked at him across desks and written things he never got to read. He let them blur into one patient figure under a lamp, at a desk somewhere at the end of the road the brown envelope had gone down. He sat across the desk from that figure in his imagination and went through what he could show one capability at a time, and watched what the reader did with each. He hated every minute of it. He was fairly sure it was the best use of his time all week.

When he was done, he wrote the rule across the top of a clean page in the slow, square hand he kept for things he meant to obey.

*Show enough to pass. Bank everything that matters.*

Then he went through the binder section by section, and decided.

Wind-adjacent first: Lira's framework, drilled through every bout he had fought since she had taught it to him, neither of them knowing at the time what was being taught. Its range was the length of a fight. It read a strike off an opponent's body a half-beat before the strike arrived and let him step, slip or turn into the space the strike would not occupy. Its price was fatigue over long exchanges and one structural weakness, which was that he had to choose his direction before he moved, so that a sharp opponent who saw the choice could close the door behind it. Against faster opponents it read less. He had shown it on the Ardenmere circuit a hundred times in front of crowds and judges and at least one man from the Compact, and it was not a secret anymore. It was simply skill.

*Show.*

Iron-adjacent, Brom's gift: a pressure-sense along the skin that told him where a body's weight was about to go, at contact and out to a few paces. Its range was short and speed thinned it, so that against a fast opponent at distance it gave him almost nothing. Its price was close attention, a listening with the whole body that tired the mind more than the muscles. It had been on the circuit too, more quietly, visible to anyone watching only as a boy who was very rarely surprised.

*Show.*

Pressure-adjacent, Feryn's redirect: three clean uses mapped across a long, careful year, each paid for in the right shoulder at a price he knew to the ounce. Contact only. It took force aimed at him and turned it, the way a door on a good hinge turns a shove into a swing. It had never been shown to any judge or panel or official. Lira and Brom knew, and no one else.

*Bank.*

Compression-adjacent: days old, a quarter of a slow push caught on a good try, paid for through the shoulder and breastbone, with a habit of draining into the nearest joint whenever he forgot to choose a road for it first. It was the youngest thing he owned and the least understood. If he showed it badly, the record would say *erratic*; if he showed it well, the record would say something far worse.

*Bank.*

And under all four, the thing that was not a fragment at all: how any of them had come to him. Each had a name beside it in the binder, the person it had come from, Lira and Feryn and Brom and Reydan, and beyond that he did not know anything. They had arrived the way weather arrives, without asking and without explaining, and he had spent a long time learning the shape of each one after it came and none at all understanding why it had. The binder had a section for each, and a page for each notice, and between its lines a quiet suspicion that the list was not finished. That was not something a person demonstrated. It was the ground every demonstration stood on, and the provision asked for practice; it said nothing about digging.

*Never.*

Two shown, two banked, and one thing never. Written down that way it looked almost tidy, and because the log was the one place he never lied, he added a line at the bottom: *Iron-equivalent by Vell's ledger. That means I can beat Iron-tier fighters. It does not mean I have a tier. It does not mean the panel will believe either.*

---

On the sixth morning Oona found him in front of the assessment board.

He had not meant to stop there. He kept doing it anyway, the way a hand goes back to a bruise. His line was still there in the same ink as everybody else's — *Eighth day. First bell.* — and Oona stood beside him with her slate under her arm and read it too, moving her lips.

"That's yours. The demonstration."

"Yes."

"I don't have one of those."

"No."

"Why do you?"

He thought about how to answer. He had decided at the classroom door that he would not lie to her. He was finding that a promise like that was much harder to keep with a thirteen-year-old than with anyone else, because she asked the questions everyone else was too polite to.

"Because I'm older, and they want to see what I can do."

"Can you do things?"

"Some."

She studied him with great seriousness. "Are you scared?"

He opened his mouth to say *no*, the way he would have said it to Naveth or the gate clerk or anyone who asked across a desk. Then he looked at her, small and solemn with her slate, and found he did not want to give her a grown-up's answer.

"A little."

Oona nodded, as if he had confirmed something she suspected.

"I'm scared about my Kindling. Not very. A little. My mother says it doesn't hurt. My uncle says it's like being hit by a door you didn't know was there." She frowned. "I don't know which one is lying."

"Neither of them, probably. It's different for everyone."

"What was yours like?"

He was quiet for a moment. He thought of the certification office in Denvash, the scrubbed floor and the clerk's gentle voice and the silence that had gone on far too long, and the word in brackets afterward that had followed him across half the continent.

"Short," he said at last. "And then very long."

Oona turned the answer over the way she turned over the instructor's answers in class, and Cael braced himself for the next question. It did not come. She looked up at him with something that, on an adult, he would have called sympathy.

"That sounds like a bad one."

"It wasn't a good one."

"Mine will be ordinary," she announced. "I've decided."

"I hope it is."

"It will be." She tucked her slate more firmly under her arm. "Good luck on the eighth day. I hope you're not boring."

She went off down the hall before he could tell her that boring was the entire plan. He stood in front of the board a while longer and found that he was smiling, and that the small cold knot he had been carrying under his breastbone since the notice slid under his door had loosened by about a thread.

The refusal came that same afternoon. It was waiting in the narrow wooden box with his name on it at the assessors' desk, folded once, written in a clerk's hand under a printed heading.

*Request for assignment of training partner, supervised sessions, demonstration provision. Request declined at this time. The provision does not presently authorize regular partnered practice for candidates in the observer category. The request has been entered in the candidate's file.*

At the bottom were two sets of initials, and one of them was Quenna's.

He read it twice in the corridor, and was surprised to find that what he felt was not disappointment but something much closer to satisfaction. It said *no*, and *not yet*, and *we have written down that you asked*, and he had spent most of his life in front of doors that said nothing at all; a no with a date and two sets of initials was better company than any silence. He took it up to his room and slid it into the pocket inside the binder's back cover, behind the stranger's note from Ardenmere and Vell's folded sheaf, where he kept the papers that proved things.

---

That night after the eighth bell he knocked on Brom's door. Brom opened it already in his shirtsleeves, looked at Cael's face, and stepped back without a word to let him in.

The room was the mirror of Cael's own, narrow and whitewashed, with the one difference that Brom had already arranged everything he owned in neat rows on the shelf, as if he expected an inspection, and had set his enrollment tag in the very centre of the row, crest up.

"Compression," said Cael.

"Before an assessment?"

"Not for the assessment. It isn't going anywhere near the assessment." He sat on the floor with his back against the bed. "But it's the only one that still gets worse when I leave it alone. Two days without practice and the timing slides."

Brom lowered himself onto the floor opposite, slowly, keeping his right side stiff. The ribs the broad Stone student had found four days ago had gone a deep, spreading purple. He had shown them to Lira, who had said something sharp about Stone Path students and then fetched him a cold cloth from the kitchens.

"Left hand. The right side still doesn't like leaning."

"Left's fine."

They worked quietly, the way they had on the road. Brom set his left palm against Cael's braced forearm and hardened it, a little Iron Skin coming up under the skin like frost forming on a window, and leaned. Cael waited until the force was in the bone, and chose the road before it could choose for itself — out, back along the line it came in — and reached.

The first try failed; he had been early, and the fragment simply did not answer, and the push came and went like any push. The second caught, and he sent it back, and a quarter of Brom's weight knocked against Brom's own wrist with that small solid sound. Then the bill arrived, the hot bar laid across his collarbones and the two breaths he could not take, and he sat with his eyes shut and waited them out while Brom waited with him.

They did ten. Four caught. None went to a joint, and each one that caught cost exactly the same, down to the breath.

"Four in ten," Brom said at the end. "On the road it was three in twelve."

"Same size, though. A quarter. Every time."

"The size is a different problem. You've fixed the timing." Brom turned his left hand over and looked at it, as if it might report something. "When you started, you were reaching early and hoping. Now you wait, and you wait well. I could feel you waiting."

"What does waiting feel like, from your side?"

Brom thought about it seriously. "Like pushing on a door and knowing someone's standing on the other side of it, deciding whether to open it."

Cael wrote that down, not knowing yet what it was evidence of, only that it felt like evidence. He did not reach for the Pressure redirect, though he thought about it. His right shoulder had spent its credit on the road, and it would be asked for nothing in two days' time, and he wanted it quiet and unbothered and dull. Some things were banked in the binder, he decided. Some were banked in the body.

When they were done, Brom sat back against the desk leg and looked at the ceiling.

"She had me on the floor again today. Same partner. He still hasn't told me his name, and I haven't asked." His mouth twitched. "It's started to feel like a rule, as if asking would spoil something."

"What did she have you do?"

"Fetch." He said the word with a kind of rueful respect. "He held back, and I had to make him give me something without opening myself up to get it. I couldn't. I opened myself up eleven times, and every time she stopped it and showed me where." He touched his ribs very lightly. "She didn't let him hit me again, though. She's careful about that. She said the bruise had already taught me everything a bruise could teach."

"Did it help?"

"Not yet." He did not sound discouraged. He sounded, if anything, pleased, the way he had sounded at the end of their first fight in Ardenmere, when he had found the whole result interesting. "But I know what I'm bad at now. Two years I didn't know. That's not nothing."

Cael thought of the page in the binder with its four headings, and of Brom spending four days filling in the cost column for himself and being happy about it.

"No. It isn't nothing."

---

On the seventh evening Lira took the whole question away from him and turned it into a training session, because whatever she could not settle by arguing she settled with her hands.

She had found a way around the refusal. Cael was not entirely sure how; he suspected she had gone straight to Quenna and asked a question shaped very precisely to fit the rules, and he suspected Quenna had enjoyed it. The training-hall roster for the last supervised block that evening now carried a new line in a clerk's hand — *Assessment preparation. Observer track. Demonstrator: Lira, re-certification track.* — on a different form from the one he had filled out, a form that said nothing at all about a partner.

"I'm not your partner," Lira said, signing in at her end of the desk while he signed in at his. "I'm a *demonstrator*. I'm here to demonstrate strikes so you can demonstrate getting out of their way. It's completely different."

"It's exactly the same."

"It's on a different form," she said serenely, "which makes it different."

An assessor Cael did not know sat on a stool by the wall with a slate on his knee, pretending not to find them interesting, and Lira took her mark in the chalk ring.

"Framework. Half speed."

He ran it, and she struck, clean and simple, from a dozen angles, and he read each strike off her body a half-beat early and stepped where it would not be.

"Again. Demonstration speed."

He ran it faster.

"Stop," she said after the fourth pass. "Second strike. You went out too far."

"I corrected."

"You corrected halfway through the step. You were making up your mind on the move." She reset her feet. "Listen. In a fight, deciding while you move is the whole point; that's what the framework is for. But in that room tomorrow a choice made mid-step reads as reflex, and reflex reads as something that happens to you, and that's the last thing you want on their slates."

He stopped, thinking of Quenna in the corridor. *Practice means it is something you do on purpose.*

"Let them see a skill somebody chose. A choice made beforehand looks finished. So finish every choice out here, tonight, where the only slate belongs to him, and he doesn't count."

The assessor by the wall did not look up.

"How fast?"

Lira considered, and then invented a speed on the spot, the way she invented most useful things.

"Panel speed. Quick enough that nobody thinks you're pretending. Slow enough that a pen can keep up."

They ran it eleven more times. The first three were bad, because his body knew the framework at full speed and kept trying to use it that way in the middle of the pattern. Lira stopped him each time with a single raised hand and a flat look. On the fourth he found the pace, and it felt wrong in his legs, slower than anything he had ever done for real; on the fifth it felt less wrong; by the seventh he had stopped thinking about it. Somewhere around the eighth, something changed in the work. It stopped being a framework he was holding back and became one that had been built to a certain height beforehand, so that he was no longer catching himself mid-step and reining in, because the limits were already there, set before he moved, like the depth of a channel cut before the water came, and there was nothing left to flex and nothing for a watching eye to catch adjusting. He felt the change before he understood it, and on the ninth pass he understood it and nearly laughed out loud, because it was the same lesson the Compression fragment had been teaching him all week — *choose first, always choose first* — and Lira was teaching it to him in a completely different language, and he had not noticed until now that they were the same sentence.

On the eleventh pass Lira lowered her hands and stepped back. "There."

She tossed him his water, and he drank while she watched him with her head on one side.

"That's a fence. You've built it to the provision's height and not a finger over." She took the water back and drank herself. "Whatever's on the other side of it, keep your eyes off it tomorrow."

"That's your professional advice?"

"I'm a demonstrator. It's on the form."

Brom was at the rail. Cael had not seen him come in; he had simply become part of the room at some point in the second half of the drill, leaning on his good side with the patient interest he gave to most things worth watching. He fell in beside them as they left the hall.

"The surface read. Do the last part with your eyes closed."

"Why?"

"It's true, and it reads as restraint." Brom shrugged, carefully, minding his ribs. "You don't need your eyes for it; you never did. But they don't know that. If you shut them at the end, they'll see you aren't watching the partner's hands or his face or his feet. They'll know you're reading something they can't see you read."

"That sounds like the opposite of what I want."

"No. Listen." Brom stopped on the path and turned to face him, which was how he signalled that a thing mattered. "Somebody with a slate is always hunting for one word, and it's the word that tells them you won't hurt anyone. *Controlled.* Give them a reason to write it, and it goes with you everywhere that file goes." He started walking again. "Shut your eyes for the last one."

Lira looked at Brom with open admiration. "When did you get good at evaluators?"

"Velmere," said Brom shortly. "I spent my whole childhood learning what evaluators liked so I could stop giving it to them." He considered. "Turns out it's still useful the other way round."

He lay awake that night in the narrow room and ran the morning through the way he used to run fight preparation the night before a circuit bout: the framework first, four times, at panel speed, the same structure each time and only the angle varied. Then the surface read, four calls, eyes open for three and closed for the last; and nothing else, no Pressure, no Compression, nothing about where any of it had come from. Small. The same every time. Dull, with any luck. Dull would get him a second month, and a third. On the circuit he had learned to make crowds lean forward, and tomorrow the whole skill would be to make three people with slates sit back in their chairs.

Across the corridor Brom shifted in his sleep and grunted at his ribs. Two doors down there was no sound at all, which meant Lira was awake and lying very still.

He was not afraid, he found when he looked. He was nervous, which was different. Afraid would have meant he did not know what was going to happen; nervous only meant he did. He thought of the sentence across the top of the clean page, and of his line on the board in the main hall in the same ink as everyone else's. He fell asleep somewhere between the two, and did not dream.

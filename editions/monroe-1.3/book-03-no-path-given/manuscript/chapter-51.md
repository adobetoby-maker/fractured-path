# Chapter 51 — The Older Word

The list went up on the main board on the Thursday afternoon, in the magistrate's office's hand, and by the fifth bell there was a small crowd in front of it.

Cael saw the crowd before he saw what it was standing in front of. He had come from the yard with his hands cold and the observation notebook under his arm, meaning to go straight down the archive passage, and he slowed at the edge of the people, who were most of them first- and second-years. They parted for him without being asked, the way people part for somebody whose name is on a thing they have been reading, and he found himself at the front, in front of the board, with Oona at his elbow.

She had been there already, with her slate under her arm and her new tag on its string, reading the list with her lips pressed tight, the way she read a chart she meant to be quizzed on.

*In the matter of the enrollment of the candidate on the unclassified observer track. Witnesses of fact entered for the respondent, through the academy's counsel: Edran, Glass Path, Iron Rank Two, third year. First sitting, second hour. Hobb, Stone Path, fourth year. First sitting, second hour. Witnesses of fact entered for the challenge: none. The challenge rests on documents.*

"It's spelled right," said Oona. "Both of them. I checked."

"You checked."

"Gerda told me to. She said they strike a witness for a wrong letter, so somebody ought to look who isn't the person who wrote it." Oona did not take her eyes off the board. "I looked. Edran's right. Hobb's right. Two b's." She frowned at the board. "I asked him. He said *two*."

Behind them somebody laughed, and stopped laughing when Oona turned round and looked at them.

Gerda was at the back of the crowd, tall over the first-years, in her patched coat with her hands folded. She caught Cael's eye over their heads and did not smile, but gave him one small nod, her procedure nod, the one that meant a thing had been done the way it was written to be done.

"*The challenge rests on documents*," Oona read aloud, slowly. "What does that mean?"

"It means the Warden isn't calling anybody to stand up and say what they saw," said Cael. "He doesn't need anybody. Everything he's going to say is already written down."

Oona considered that for some time, from every side.

"Then everybody who stands up is for you," she said.

"Everybody who stands up says what they saw. That's different."

"I know it's different. Brom told me. That's why I'm not on it." She said it without any bitterness at all, as she would have said what Path somebody was. Then she shifted her slate into the crook of her other arm. "I'm on the front bench of the gallery. On the left, by the aisle. Gerda says the gallery may not speak, and if anybody so much as gasps the clerk can put them out."

"Can you not gasp?"

"I don't gasp," said Oona, with great dignity. "I write things down."

Hobb came down the passage from the training hall with his hands in his pockets and his feet a shoulder-width apart even when he walked, and the first-years flattened themselves against the wall to let him by. He stopped behind the crowd and read the board over all their heads, without hurry, and then he looked at Cael.

"Second hour," said Hobb.

"Second hour."

Hobb considered the board a moment longer. "Say what I saw."

"That's all anybody wants."

Hobb nodded, once, as one workman nods to another, and went on down the passage toward the stable, and Oona watched him go with her whole face.

"He said four words," she said. "In a row. I counted."

"That's a great many for Hobb."

"I know. I'm going to write it down." She wrote it on her slate and underlined it twice, and did not show him.

Edran came last, from the stair of the lecture wing, with his arms folded and his flat honest face giving nothing away. He read the board once, and did not stop to read it twice, but came along the edge of the crowd until he was beside Cael and stood there with his eyes on his own name.

"They've spelled it right," he said.

"Oona checked."

Edran looked down at Oona, who looked back up at him without blinking. Something in his face moved, very slightly, the way Cael had once seen it move at the rail when a gap he had not expected closed in front of him.

"Thank you," said Edran to her, gravely. Then to Cael, more quietly, still looking at the board: "I'll say what I saw. Every sitting I watched, and the exhibition, and the match from the bench. I won't say what I think it was." He paused, and looked at the floor. "I've thought about whether I ought to say I don't know what it was. In case they ask."

"If they ask, say it."

"I will." Edran unfolded his arms. "It'll be true. It's been true since the frost." He turned to go, and turned back. "My third's held four in four this week. I said I'd tell you."

"You did."

"I'm telling you anyway," said Edran, and went.

Cael stood a moment longer in front of the board, with Oona at his elbow and the first-years pretending not to look at either of them. *Witnesses of fact entered for the challenge: none.* Coss had come up the hill with a case he did not need a single living person to stand up for. Everything he had would be read out of a document case, in a patient voice, and every word of it would be so. And everybody who would stand up on Monday and open their mouths would be somebody who had stood across a chalk line from him, or sat on a bench and watched, and had decided by themselves to say so.

"You've got the face," said Oona.

"Which face?"

"The one Lira says you do when a board goes up." She tucked her slate under her arm. "I'm going to supper. You should go to the archive. Karis is down there with the old books and the clerks are getting closer. Brom says they did a whole case today." She looked up at him, very serious. "Bring her something to eat. She never does."

He went down the archive passage at the eighth bell with a lamp in one hand, the notebook under his arm, and a heel of bread in his pocket that Lira had given back to him at supper without being asked.

---

The boxes were stacked inside the archive door when he came down at the eighth bell, eleven of them, each tied crosswise with grey string and sealed with a blot of the delegation's wax where the strings met.

He stopped and looked at them with the lamp in his hand. They were plain flat boxes of the kind a draper packs cloth in, stacked very square, the way Prynn stacked anything she had been made to let go of, with a slip of paper tucked under the string on the top of each one. He bent and read the nearest without touching it. *Founding stock, case one, shelf two. Yearbooks, eleven volumes.* Then the marks, eleven of them, in a clerk's careful hand that had plainly been asked to go slower than it liked. Then a signature, and under it, in Prynn's small dry hand, *Counted at the door.*

Prynn's stool was empty and her lamp was out, because she had gone up, and the iron-banded door had been left the width of two fingers open, as it always was now. There was one lamp still burning, far down the long room at the back, against the old wall.

He went down to it between the cases. The front of the founding stock was bare already, two cases of empty shelves with the dust standing in pale oblongs where the books had been, and he did not look at them for long. Karis was at the far end, at the reading shelf of the second-to-last case, with her satchel and her long flat box of cards at her elbow and a volume open flat in front of her under the lamp. She had a stool, and there was a second stool beside it that had not been there yesterday.

"I brought it down from the lecture wing," she said, without looking up. "Sit. Don't talk to me for a while."

He sat, and set his lamp on the shelf next to hers, and put the observation notebook down beside it, shut. He had carried the notebook everywhere for so long that he no longer noticed it was under his arm until he put it down somewhere. Then he laid the heel of bread beside her box of cards. She did not look at it, but a little later her hand went out and took it, and she ate it without lifting her eyes from the page.

He did not open the notebook, because he had nothing to open in it. He sat with his hands on his knees and watched her read, the way he had watched her for three nights at the long table, and after a while he stopped watching her and only sat, and listened to the archive.

It was a different place at night with the front half emptied. There was a cold draught from somewhere he had not felt before, as if the shelves had been keeping it out, while the old wall at his back gave off the stored chill of stone. Down at the front, very faintly, he could hear the wax on the newest box creaking as it cooled.

He thought about Monday, a little, and then made himself stop, because there was nothing left in Monday to think about. He thought about Coss on the far side of the chalk saying *five days* as if it were the hour. He thought about Lira saying *then that's worse*, and found he understood exactly what she meant and could not have said it any better.

Karis turned a page.

She turned it with a paper knife, flat, and smoothed it with the side of her hand, and went on reading. The hand in the volume was small and steady, and every seven in it wore a little crossed bar and then a second bar across that. He had heard her describe that hand at supper on the sixth day of the three weeks and had not thought about it since.

She turned another page.

---

He noticed before she said anything.

It was not that she stopped moving, for she stopped often, when a line wanted reading twice; it was what kind of stopped it was. Karis at work was never quite still. There was always the pen, or the paper knife, or the small movement of her lips over a number, or her eyes going back a line and forward again. This was not that: her hand had gone flat on the open page, the fingers spread, as though she were holding the book down against a wind. All of her had gone quiet at once, the eyes and the lips and the breath.

He had seen her go still like that once before, in the formal yard, in the third exchange, with her fourth channel breaking under her feet, in the half a breath before she had started to laugh.

"Cael," she said.

He looked at her, but she had not turned her head.

"Come and read this."

He got up and came round behind her and stood at her shoulder, and she moved her hand a little to one side, not off the page, so that he could see.

It was a register entry, near the foot of a left-hand page, in the crosshatched hand, small and level. The traveller's name at the head of it had faded into a brown smudge that might once have been two words or three, and whence and whither were there, but he did not read them. The line under them was longer than the lines around it, because the assessor had said something and the clerk had written all of it down.

*Capabilities observed across multiple signature families. No single designation applicable per standard practice. Assessor's judgment: designate UNBOUND per provisional custom, pending further study. Study not recorded as completed.*

He read it once.

He read it again, and on the second reading his body understood it before he did. *Multiple signature families.* He knew what a signature family was, because he had spent a term sitting under a chart of them in a lecture room, with the families laid out in boxes and joined by lines, and his own word on none of them. *No single designation applicable.* Somebody had stood in front of an assessor, in this house, and shown things that crossed the lines between the boxes. Perhaps it had been at the gate in the wall he could feel at his back, or in the hall that was now an archive. And the assessor had not reached for a flag, or written *removal* or *voidance* or anything of the kind. He had written that he could not decide, and had given it a word for the while, *pending further study*.

And then nobody had studied it. *Study not recorded as completed.* Whoever it was had gone on up the road, east or west, and the clerk with the crossed sevens had closed the line and written the next traveller under it.

"There's more," said Karis, very quietly. "Underneath. A different hand."

There was. Below the entry, squeezed into the space between it and the next, there was a second line, in an ink that was blacker and had not faded as much, and a hand that was sharper and more upright and did not cross its sevens. It was a later hand, though he did not know how much later, and nothing on the page said.

*Per standardization directive, prior designation UNBOUND to be re-entered under flag SHATTERED; original terminology discontinued.*

He read it a third time before he could take in what it said, and then a fourth.

*SHATTERED*, in the same capitals, on the same page, set down by a pen that had come along after the crosshatched clerk, and read what the clerk wrote, and written over it what it was to be called from now on.

"They put the one word in the place of the other," he said. His voice sounded strange to him in the cold.

"That's what it says." Karis had not taken her hand off the page. "That's all it says. It doesn't say why, or when, or who. *Per standardization directive.* It doesn't even say which one."

He stood behind her with his hand on the back of the stool, and the old stone at his back, and he did not know what he felt. It was not fear, nor anything he had a name for. It was more like the moment on the bench in the grey building when the memory of Denvash had come up through his body without asking, except that this time nothing came up, and there was only the cold, and a line in a stranger's sharp hand.

Then he turned and went back round the shelf to his own stool, and picked up the observation notebook, and came back.

---

He did not have to look for the page. He had not opened it in months, and his fingers went to it anyway, past the circuit pages and the first pages from the road, past a year and more of everything he had written down about everybody he had ever had to understand. Near the front there was a page with almost nothing on it, a single line copied out in a hand that was his and did not look like his any more. It was a year and some younger and it sloped more, and it had been written very carefully by somebody who did not understand what he was writing and knew that he did not.

There was a small box drawn round it in pencil. He had drawn that box in the week of the match, on a night when he was drawing boxes round everything he did not have the key to yet.

*Assessed per pre-registry terminology as UNBOUND.*

He laid the notebook open on the reading shelf beside the register, so that the one lamp fell on both pages at once. There was his own younger hand, out of Vell's archive in Ardenmere, on an evening when Vell had let him into the oldest volumes of the circuit's ledger. And there was the crosshatched clerk's, out of a road-house on a hill. The two of them had never been in the same room until this one.

Karis looked from one to the other.

She did not say anything at first. She bent close, and read his line, and read the register's, and read his again. Then she did what she did with everything: she checked it. She checked the spelling, letter by letter, with the tip of the paper knife held a hair's breadth off the paper. She checked the capitals, his and the clerk's. She checked the form of the word, whether it stood alone or had anything joined to it. She looked at the word *terminology* in his line and the word *custom* in the clerk's, and he could see her deciding that they were not the same word and did not have to be. He watched her run out of things to check, one after another, and come to the end of them, and keep looking anyway.

"Where did you copy yours?" she said.

"Vell's archive. Ardenmere. The oldest ledger the circuit has, the first one that survived." He kept his voice low. "A bout, a fighter whose name had gone, and a note in the margin beside it. I didn't know what it meant. I still don't. I copied it because it was about somebody like me, and I'd never found anything that was."

"And you never told anybody."

"There was nothing to tell. One line in a margin, about somebody whose name had gone." He looked at the register. "It was one line in a margin. Now it's two."

Karis put the paper knife down on the shelf, very carefully, square.

"Two sources," she said. "That never touched. Yours came out of a fighting ledger in a city I've only seen from a coach. Mine's been sitting on this shelf since before the school existed, and nobody's read it in Prynn's lifetime, she told me so. Neither one can have been copied from the other. Neither one can have been written by somebody who'd read the other." She stopped. "And they say the same word. The same word, in the same capitals, and nothing on this hill or in that city that could have told either of them to say it."

He waited, and did not help her.

"I'm not going to tell you what it means," she said. "I don't know what it means. I'm not going to make something up and hand it to you because you're standing there and it's late and I'd like to have a finding." She looked at the later hand's line, the black one, and her mouth went thin. "I'll say one thing, and it's only a reading of the page. Nobody on this page found anything out. Nobody tested anybody. A clerk wrote down an assessor's word, and later somebody else wrote down an instruction to change it." She touched the edge of the register, not the writing. "*Shattered* is an editorial choice. The question is whose."

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

He found that he did not want to know. Not yet. It was not that it didn't matter. It mattered so much that he did not trust himself anywhere near it, the way he had not trusted himself near a seam he had not tested. He had learned at a cost what happened when he reached for something large before he had the measure of it.

"I don't understand it," he said at last. "I don't think I'm meant to. Not now."

"No," said Karis. "Neither am I."

---

She copied it twice.

She did it at once, there at the reading shelf, while the lamp was still good. She took her first notebook from the satchel, the one with the bout logs and the timing tables, and turned to a clean page at the back. Then she took the second notebook, the newest volume of the long one, and turned to a clean page in that too. She wrote the entry out in full in the first, every word, and the annotation under it in its place. She wrote the shelf mark at the top, and the volume's place in the run, and the page, and which side of the page. She noted the faded smudge where the name had been, and the colour of each ink, and that the second hand was later and did not cross its sevens. Then she did all of it again in the second notebook, from the register and not from her first copy, so that the second would not carry any mistake she had made in the first.

He copied it once, into the observation notebook, directly under his own old line, inside the same pencilled box. He made the box bigger to take it. He wrote in the square hand he kept for the things he meant to stand behind.

"Now read it," said Karis. "Out loud. From the register."

"All of it?"

"All of it. Every word, every capital, where every line breaks." She had her first notebook open on her knee and her finger on the first word. "I'll follow in mine. Then you read it again and I'll follow in the second. Then I'll read the register and you follow in yours." She did not look up. "You're a second source now. Act like one."

So he read it aloud, low, in the cold, from the register, with the lamp between them. He read the crosshatched clerk's entry and the later hand's line, word by word, and stopped at every line's end and said *break*, and she followed in her own copy with her finger and said *yes* at every break. When he had done it once he did it again, and she followed in the second notebook, and at one place she said *stop*. She found she had written *signature family* where the register said *signature families*, and struck it through with a single line and wrote it again above, and said *go on*. Then she read it from the register while he followed in the observation notebook, and he found nothing wrong in his. Then she checked his anyway, letter by letter, with the paper knife.

At the end of it she sat back.

"Three copies," she said. "Two in mine, one in yours. Each read against the register out loud by somebody who wasn't the one who wrote it." She closed the second notebook and laid her palm on its cover. "If anybody ever asks whether this page existed, there's that much."

She wrote a card for the volume, and put a dot in its corner, the mark for read through. Then she closed the register itself, gently, with the paper knife's flat under the cover so that the old leaves would not crease. She carried it back to the shelf it had come from and set it in its place in the run, square with its neighbours, its spine exactly in line with theirs, as if putting it back right might matter to whatever came next.

He watched her do it and did not say anything, because they both knew what came next: string and wax and a clerk counting spines at Prynn's door on Saturday morning.

---

The stable was empty by the time they came up. The fire had burned down to a red bed at the far end and the tiles had been put away. Somebody had left a lamp on the scarred table, low, the way Lira left it when she wanted him to know she had gone up and would be cross if he sat in the dark.

They sat at the end of the table under the bricked-up hay door. Karis put her two notebooks on the wood in front of her and squared them to the edge, and he put the observation notebook beside them, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"You should use it," said Karis.

She said it quietly, but not as if she had only just thought of it; he could tell by how ready it was that she had been thinking it all the way up the archive passage.

"Not as evidence. I know it isn't evidence. A road-house register isn't a document of the Compact, and on Monday it'll be under the Compact's own seal anyway, and you can't produce what they're holding." She put her hand on the top notebook. "But you could say it. You could stand in that room and tell them the word they've built everything on was put there by somebody, once, over another word, on an instruction nobody even bothered to name. You could show them it was never something anybody found. It was something somebody wrote." Her voice was very level. "And it's so, Cael. Nothing in the schedule is as plainly so as that page. You know it isn't."

He made himself not answer at once, because she had earned more than a quick answer.

"Think what the room does with it," he said at last.

She waited, with her hand still on the notebook.

"First, it asks where it is. And I say: under your seal, Magistrate, in a box on a cart, and here's a copy in my notebook in my own handwriting. So now the room is looking at my handwriting, and asking whether a boy who wants something very badly copies carefully." He touched the cover of the observation notebook with two fingers. "We know he does. They don't. And it stops being a question about their text and becomes a question about my sources, which is the one question I can't win, because I'm the only witness to both of them."

"I'm a witness to this one."

"You're the person who's been with me for a term. Brom won't call Oona because she loves me. You're not much better." He said it gently, and she did not argue. "That's the small reason. Here's the big one."

He laid both his hands on the table, palms down, as if to hold the next part still.

"Everything I'm going to say on Monday stands on one thing. The text governs. Not what people have always done. Not what everybody's always taken a word to mean. What's written, and only what's written. Custom isn't code. That's the whole argument." He looked at her two notebooks squared on the table. "If I stand up and say, look, here's an older custom, here's what they used to call it, here's a road-house clerk who'd have written me down kindly, then I'm asking the panel to weigh a custom. Mine. And the moment I ask them to weigh one custom, I've told them customs can be weighed. I've given them the scales." He shook his head slowly. "And their custom is generations of enforcement in every district on the map, with a seal on every page. Mine is one clerk who crossed his sevens. If customs count, theirs wins. I'd be handing Coss the one thing he hasn't got."

Karis sat very still, and he watched her take the argument she had brought up the passage and turn it over in her hands, the way she turned over a timing that did not agree with her notebooks. He watched her look for the place where it was wrong, and not find it. Then he watched her do the thing she always did, which he had seen her do over a hundred small defeats: stop being sorry she had lost, and start using what she had lost to.

"It has to be closed," she said slowly. "On both sides. If you bring in anything from outside their text, however true, you've opened the door, and everything outside their text can come in after it." She breathed out, long and slow. "And there's more outside their text on their side than on yours. There always will be."

"Yes."

"That's correct." She looked at the notebooks. "I hate it. I want you to know that I hate it, so it's on the record somewhere. It's correct, and I hate it."

"It's on the record," said Cael. "Mine."

She laughed, once, very short, and looked away at the low lamp.

"So you're going to sit on it," she said, still looking at the lamp. "The one thing on that hill nobody knew was there. You're going to walk into that room with it in your notebook and not say a word."

"I'm going to keep it." He turned the observation notebook toward himself, and opened it, and looked at the box with the two lines in it. "It was written before anything in their text. It isn't theirs to weigh, and I'm not going to carry it into their room and ask them to. When I know what it means, I'll know where it goes. I don't." He was quiet a moment. "I don't think I'm going to understand it on anybody's timetable. Not mine, and not the Compact's."

Karis looked back at him across the low lamp for a long time. Then she nodded, slowly, with the nod of somebody who has argued for something and lost and finds, a little to her own surprise, that she minds losing less than she expected, and respects the reason entirely.

"Into the notebook, then," she said.

"Into the notebook."

---

He wrote it at the scarred table before they went up, with Karis watching and not reading, because she had asked once in the second week never to read his pages over his shoulder and had kept that as she kept everything.

Under the box, under both lines, in the square hand:

*Two hands that never met. One in the margin of Vell's oldest ledger in Ardenmere. One in a road-house register on this hill, the third clerk, with a later hand over it. The same word. K. made two copies, I made one. Read aloud, all three, against the page.*

He stopped there, and then went on, smaller.

*I don't know what it means. Not for Monday. Kept.*

He closed the notebook, and Karis gathered up her two and put them in the satchel and buckled it. Then, after a moment, she unbuckled it again and took out the second notebook, and put it inside her coat, against her chest, and buttoned the coat over it.

"In case," she said, when she saw him looking. "I'll sleep better."

"In case of what?"

"I don't know. That's why it's in my coat." She stood up with the satchel on her shoulder. "Saturday morning. The third clerk's case. Prynn will want somebody at her elbow while they count it, and it ought to be the two of us." She looked at the low lamp at the end of the table. "Lira left that for you."

"I know."

"She'll be cross that you sat in the dark anyway." Karis picked up the lamp and held it out to him. "Take it up. You can tell her you didn't."

He took it, and they went up the stair together, and he saw her to her door. She went in without saying good night, and he stood a moment in the bending corridor of the residence wing with the lamp in his hand and the notebook under his arm. The word was in it twice now, in two hands, and there was a third copy buttoned inside a coat on the other side of a door. Down at the back of the archive there was a register on a shelf, in its place in the run, waiting for the string.

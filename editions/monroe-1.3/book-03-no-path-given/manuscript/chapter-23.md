# Chapter 23 — Better Your Hand

She was already on the wall.

He saw her from the residence wing door, a dark shape against the darker yard, sitting where they always sat on the low stone wall with her heels against it and her coat pulled round her. She was not reading, or doing anything at all; she was looking out at the training yard with her chin on her knees, and she did not turn her head when the door opened, though she must have heard it. So Brom had said something, or she had counted the days since Wednesday herself and worked out that tonight was the night. Nobody alive read him better, and she had watched him carry this for two days without once asking him to put it down.

The night was clear and very cold. The frost had come back after sunset and the top of the wall was rough with it under his hand when he sat. Below the academy the hill town's lamps were scattered down the slope like coals shaken out of a grate, with the valley black beyond them all the way to the far hills and the stars out over the training yard, more of them than Ardenmere had ever let him see.

He sat down beside her. He did not soften it first; with her, softening had never been honest.

"I think I might have taken from you," he said. "On purpose. Only I didn't know it was on purpose."

Lira did not move.

"Karis thinks it can be aimed. If she's right, then what happened with your framework, in Ardenmere, in the mornings, wasn't something that happened near me. It was something I did." He made himself keep going, all the way to the end. "To you. And I never asked, because I didn't know I was a thing that could have."

Lira did not answer at once. She looked out at the dark yard, the way she did when she was really thinking and not performing having thought. He made himself wait for it with his hands flat on the cold stone, because hurrying her through this would have been its own small taking, one more thing had from her unasked, and he meant to be finished with those, or wanted to mean it. The frost crept into his palms. Down the hill a dog barked twice and stopped.

"You told me what you knew," Lira said at last, "when you knew it."

Her voice was steady, with no give in it anywhere.

"In Ardenmere, you sat with it two weeks on your own, and then you told me. You didn't wait for me to catch you. You didn't wait until it was useful. You told me the day you were sure there was something to tell." She did not look at him. "You've told me every one since, the day it came. In your own handwriting. In a binder you put in my hands because you wanted me to read it. I never once made you."

"That's not—"

"I'm not finished." She turned her head then and looked at him properly, and in the starlight her face was very clear. "Say the thing in you was pointed at me, somehow, where you couldn't see it and I couldn't feel it. That doesn't touch a word of what I just said. Go on as you have. When you know something, tell me." She looked back at the yard. "I can't forgive you for something you didn't know you were doing, Cael. There's nothing there to forgive. There's only what you do with it now that you know."

"That's generous."

"It isn't generous. It's arithmetic." She bumped her shoulder against his, the old worn knock from more walls than either of them could count. "You want to know what would cost you with me? Really cost? Knowing, and keeping it from me. That's the day I'd stop believing the binder." She let that sit in the cold. "You've never done it. You aren't doing it tonight. You're sitting on a frozen wall telling me before you even know it's true."

He looked down at his hands.

"So stop apologizing for something you haven't done yet, and go and find out whether it's real."

They sat for a while without speaking. Somewhere in the town below a door opened and shut, a tiny sound from far off with somebody's whole life on the other side of it that he would never know, and a lamp went out on the street worth walking, and then another on the street worth avoiding.

"You're not afraid of it." It was not quite a question.

"I didn't say that."

She was still looking at the yard.

"I'm afraid of most of what's coming for you, if you want the whole ledger. I'm afraid of the Compact the day it stops being patient. I'm afraid of that request, and whoever sent it, and whatever they make of three people's handwriting saying you're satisfactory." She drew her knees up tighter. "I'm afraid of the bill, every time the thing in you comes out bigger than anybody measured it last. I've read what half a push costs, and I saw you the morning after. I can count."

She breathed out, white, into the dark.

"But I'm not afraid of *you*. So a girl with lovely handwriting thinks there may be a handle on it. That isn't going to make me start being afraid of you." She turned her head and looked at him again. "If there's a handle, better your hand than nobody's. That's all I think about it."

"Better my hand than nobody's," he said after her, slowly, testing how much it would hold.

"Somebody's steering," said Lira.

She said it plainly, the way she said the score at the end of a bout.

"That's what her hypothesis means, if it's true. Somebody's been steering this whole time. In Ardenmere, in the mornings, with me. With Brom. With all of them." She did not soften it. "What's left to decide is whether the steering stays with the part of you that never asks."

He sat with that. The cold had gone right through his coat now and into his ribs, into the line across his right side, and he let it.

"I don't know how to steer it. I've never once tried. I don't even know what trying would be."

"No. You don't." Lira unfolded herself off the wall and stood on the frosted gravel in front of him with her hands in her coat pockets, looking down. "So learn it, Cael. You were going to anyway. Not understanding something has never once made you leave it alone. It's the most reliable fact about you." The corner of her mouth moved. "And I've been keeping records on you longer than she has."

He looked up at her.

"In Ardenmere, the first time, when you didn't know what you were doing with me, I told you to tell me when you were sure. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"That's still the rule." She took one hand out of her pocket and put it on his shoulder for a moment, light and brief. "Tell me when you're sure. About any of it. And when you're not sure, tell me that." She took the hand back. "Same rule. Heavier load. It'll hold."

She went in. He heard the residence wing door open and shut behind her and did not turn to watch her go; he sat on the wall in the cold and understood that the thing she had just done had been done once before, a long time ago and much lighter. It was the covenant from Ardenmere, made over again under a load it had never been meant to carry, and it had held anyway, because things that had been built properly the first time tended to hold when the weight went up.

He stayed on the wall long after she had gone in, while the cold came through his coat and the lamps of the town went out below him one and then another, each one a household closing its accounts for the day. Behind him in the residence wing Karis was probably still up, adding a line to a page called *Not asked*. In a city three days to the west a woman kept a ledger of every bout ever fought on her floor, so that the truth about people would be stored somewhere against the winter. Further off, in Denvash, a man kept a workshop, and had once stood beside a boy with a hand on his shoulder on the worst morning of his life. The world was full of people keeping accounts. He had been one himself his whole life, and he had never once audited the account that mattered most, the one written into his own architecture, in handwriting he was only now beginning to think might be his own.

He took the binder out of his coat. He could barely see the page, but he wrote anyway, slowly, in the square hand he saved for lines he meant to keep believing after he had started to doubt them.

*If it can be directed, I have to learn to direct it. Not to have more. To stop not-knowing what I'm doing. Not-knowing is the version that eventually gets someone killed.*

He read it back once by the light from the residence wing's one lit window, and understood as he closed the binder that the fear underneath the sentence had never really been about power. It was about whoever stood near enough to him to be used up by it, whether he had ever meant to use them or not. He thought of Lira on the frozen wall telling him the arithmetic came out clean, Brom with his column of numbers in his coat, defending barley, Karis with her chalk line and her page of half-questions. He thought of Edran, even, with his thin third structure, and of Hobb, who had stood in front of him twice and hit exactly as hard as he was asked. They were close, and he had been watching all of them. It was their account too, as much as his, and always had been, and the only honest payment he could make on it was to learn to read it.

---

Brom was at the stable table at the first bell with porridge and his number sheet. He looked up once when Cael sat down, looked at his face, and looked back at his porridge.

"You talked to her."

"Last night."

"Good." Brom ate. "What did she say?"

"Better my hand than nobody's."

Brom chewed slowly, considering it as if testing it with his teeth.

"That's right. That's the right one." He turned the number sheet over so the clean side was up. "Seven again yesterday. Didn't go down. Wray says that's the more important number. The one where it stays. The first number's luck, and the second one's the floor." He folded the sheet back into his coat. "You'll want to remember that, for whatever you're about to start."

He did not ask what Cael was about to start. He went back to his porridge with every sign of contentment, and Cael ate his own and found, for the first time in two days, that he could taste it.

Karis came through the stable on her way out with her notebook under her arm. She slowed at the table, enough to glance down at the slip for Tuesday's session that Cael had just signed and left by his bowl, and then she looked at him.

"Your handwriting's back."

"Is it?"

"To about the size it was before the request. I'll enter it. Cause unknown."

"Probable," said Cael.

He saw the flicker at the corner of her mouth that she usually corrected, and she did not correct it.

"Tuesday."

"Tuesday."

Quenna stopped him on the stair at the second bell, with her folder under her arm and the air of a woman already late for something else, and held out a folded sheet between two fingers.

"The third sitting. I copied it into the ledger the night of the bout; the fair copy took me longer than it should have. Yours. You've read every other page of your own record before anybody else has, since Karis wrote that clause. I didn't see why the academy's should be the exception." She was already moving on up the stair. "Keep it with the others. The day somebody reads the file who was never in the yard, I'd like you to have already read every line they'll read."

He read it standing on the stair while students went past him on either side, Wray's twelve words and the rotating seat's long paragraph and the withdrawal entered with the hour, and found nothing in it about a fragment, nothing but a framework and a gap and a hand on a breastbone. Then he folded it into the back pocket of the binder, behind the refusal and the stranger's note and Vell's sheaf and Karis's first four pages, among the papers that proved things.

---

Lira took him down the hill at the fourth bell, because the post had come up from the valley the evening before and the man at the draper's counter had sent a boy to the gate to say that there was something for Greyvane.

She did not say much on the way down. She walked a half-pace ahead of him on the steep part of the road with her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the town below, and when he said something about the frost on the mill race she answered it, and when he said nothing she said nothing either. It was not an unhappy quiet, but it was not a quiet he had heard from her before.

The man at the draper's counter knew Lira by name and had the letter ready before she asked: a single folded sheet sealed with a plain blob of wax, with Cael's name and *Greyvane* on the outside in a square, careful hand that Cael would have known in the dark. He did not open it at the counter, or in the street, or at the baker's, where the baker looked at his tag with fresh contempt and wrapped Lira's loaf as if it had personally offended him. He carried it unopened all the way back up the steep road, and at the top, at the corner where the road met the gate and the whole valley lay below, he stopped and broke the seal.

*Cael —*

*Both of yours came. The second a week behind the first, which is the post's fault and not yours.*

*You signed it with your eyes open. Good. A form is a form. It says what it says and you are what you are, and the two of them can sit in the same drawer without either one minding.*

*The shop is the same. The stove still smokes when the wind is east. I mended the bench you used to sit on, so it is ready for whenever.*

*You wrote that there is a woman who argued all afternoon to find a word for you. Mind her. People who will argue for a word are rarer than people who will argue for a wage.*

*I am glad of the two you came through the gate with. I am glad of the third.*

*Write when something happens. Write when nothing does. I read both kinds the same.*

*H.*

He read it three times, standing on the corner in the cold with the valley spread out below. It was not long; Hesk's letters were never long. But he could hear every line of it in Hesk's voice, slow and dry, said across a workbench with his hands still busy. When he came to *I mended the bench you used to sit on* he read that line a fourth time and had to look at the far hills for a while.

Lira waited beside him with the loaf under her arm and did not ask what the letter said. When he folded it at last and put it inside his coat, against the binder, she only nodded, as if something had been set down in the right place.

"He mended the bench."

"Good."

"He says mind Quenna."

"He's right." She looked out at the valley. "He's always right, your Hesk. It's very annoying in a man you've never met."

They went in through the gate together.

---

He saw her stop at the board in the main hall on the way through.

It was not the petition board. The petition and its answer and Edran's withdrawal had come down from their brass tacks two days ago, after their week on the board, and the space beside the calendar was bare again. She stopped in front of the calendar itself, the public assessment calendar in its weekly columns, and stood looking at one line in it. He knew which line before he reached her, because he had seen it go up three weeks ago in the clerk's small even hand, in the same ink as everything else.

*Re-certification Review, formal. Lira. Two recorded bouts before a panel of three. Formal yard, first bell.*

The date beside it was five days off. Two lines below it, on the next morning, in the same hand, sat Gerda's name with the same words after it.

Lira did not say anything. She did not touch the line or point at it, or make a joke about it, or tell him what she thought the panel would make of her. She looked at it for about as long as it would have taken to read it four times. Then she shifted the loaf under her arm and went on through the hall toward the residence wing with her chin level and her stride unbroken, as she had walked past the marker at the floor desk every morning since the second week. He watched her shoulders as she went. A half-second after she passed the board, they came down by about an inch.

He did not follow her. He had been told something, on the wall, about telling and being told, and it went both ways or it did not hold.

Gerda came in from the yard a minute later, with her patched coat buttoned to the chin and her slip for the floor in her hand, and stopped at the calendar where Lira had stopped. She did not look at Lira's line. She looked at her own, two below it, and read it once with her lips pressed together, and then looked up at the clock above the door, as she always did, and checked it against something in her head. She saw him by the archive passage and did not come over, and then, after a moment, did.

"Five days for her. Six for me."

"Are you ready?"

Gerda considered the question as if it were a clause she had been handed to read aloud.

"I've read the procedure. All of it. Twice this week. Two bouts, a panel of three, the results entered with the assessors' initials, and a recommendation in the file." She folded her slip once along a crease that was already there. "The procedure is very clear. It doesn't care whether I'm ready. That's what's good about it."

"Is it?"

"It's what's good about it," said Gerda again, more quietly, "and it's the other thing as well." She looked at the calendar. "At the other place, I was ready. It didn't matter. The procedure was very clear there too."

He waited, and she did not go on. She looked at the clock again instead, and he understood that whatever had happened at the other place was folded along a crease that was already there, and that she was not going to open it in a hall with people passing.

"Lira will be all right. She fights like somebody who's already lost once and decided it didn't count." She tucked the slip into her coat. "That's worth a good deal on a floor. I used to have it."

"You could have it again."

Gerda looked at him then, properly, with her pale level eyes, and he could not tell what she saw.

"Perhaps. Six days. We'll find out what the floor thinks." She nodded once and went on toward the training hall, and at the door she checked the clock one more time before she went through it.

---

At the sixth bell, on his way back from the archive, he looked in at the open floor.

It was nearly empty. The light was going, grey and cold through the high windows, and the last of the full-standing students were packing their bags at the far end, and in the near corner, alone, Lira was working. There was nobody to spar with, so she was running the old Ardenmere drills, the ones she had taught him in the mornings before either of them knew what he was learning. They were short sharp sequences of footwork against an imagined opponent, slip and turn and step and slip again, at full speed, over and over, until the boards rang faintly under her feet. She did not see him at the rail, or she saw him and did not look up, which with Lira was the same as a closed door.

He knew every one of those sequences. He knew them because he had watched her run them a hundred mornings in a cold yard in Ardenmere with the whole of his attention, and because some part of him had taken them and kept them, without asking and without her knowing. They lived in him now with her timing in them, so deep that Karis could count it from the far side of a chalk line. He watched her now, and he knew he was watching, and he made himself know it.

She was very quiet, working. She did not talk to herself as she sometimes did, or mutter at a slip that came late, or laugh when one came right. She moved and turned and moved again in the grey light. There was something in it he had seen on her only a few times in all the time he had known her, the stillness of a drawn bow, the quiet of a thing pulled back as far as it would go and held, waiting to see where it would be sent.

He did not interrupt her. He went up to the residence wing and sat on his bed with the binder on his knees and Hesk's letter folded inside the front cover, and after a long time he took out a sheet of paper, because Hesk had said to write when something happened, and something had.

*Hesk —*

*Yours came today. Lira fetched it from the draper's and the baker was rude to us both, so it was a good day before I even opened it.*

*Thank you for the bench.*

*There was a petition about me. A student filed it, the best fighter in the school, and it was fair, and it was right about everything but one thing. They answered it with a bout in public. I fought him in front of nearly everyone here and won, not by much, and he stood up afterward and withdrew it, out loud, in his own words. He didn't have to. I think he's one of the people you'd call honest. I don't think he's a friend. I think he's something else, and I don't have the word for it yet.*

*I took a cut on the arm and one on the ribs. Both fine. The shoulder still complains.*

*There's a girl here from a bigger school who studies how people fight, and she asks before she looks. Nobody has ever done that. I've let her. I think you'd approve of the asking.*

*I'm minding the woman who argued for a word. You'd like her. She doesn't say anything she can't stand behind.*

*Something happened, so I've written. I'll write when nothing does.*

*Cael*

It was not long, and he had left out more than he had put in: the fragment that woke for a shell and was given no road, the binder read backward, appetite, a girl he could not look at across a stable table. Some things were for the binder. But he had written nothing in it that was untrue, and he had told Hesk about the asking, and that was nearer to the whole of it than any letter he had ever sent down a hill. He folded it, wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the desk for the next third-day post.

Then he turned to a clean page, wrote the date at the top of it, and under the date, one line.

*Week eight. Something in me has always been looking, and taking, and I have never once told it where to look. That has to change. Not for more. So that the people next to me stop being the ones it lands on.*

He looked at it for a while. Then he wrote one more line underneath, smaller, because it was the honest one, and because Lira had told him to say so when he was not sure.

*I don't know how. I've never aimed it once. I'm going to find out.*

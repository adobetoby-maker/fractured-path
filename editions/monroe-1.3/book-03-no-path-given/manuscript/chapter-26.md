# Chapter 26 — Priority Review

The formal yard at first bell on Thursday was grey with frost everywhere except inside the ring. Somebody had swept the earth bare there before dawn and laid the chalk fresh, as they had for the exhibition, and as they did, Cael supposed, for anybody whose name was on the calendar in the main hall in the same ink as everyone else's.

It was not the exhibition. Nobody had climbed onto the roof of the tool shed. The benches held perhaps thirty, which for a re-certification review at the first bell was, Brom said, a crowd, and the low wall along the open side held a dozen more with their hands in their sleeves. The rumor market had not been told anything, because Lira had told it nothing, but the rumor market at Greyvane did not need to be told things to know them. It knew that the girl with the dot who had walked a Force Path off the chalk in the second week was being formally reviewed this morning. It knew that something had come up the hill on Saturday under a green seal. That was enough to fetch people out of bed early in the cold.

Cael stood at the rail on the open side with Brom on his left. Karis was a few places along, and he noticed, because he could not help noticing, that her hands were in her coat pockets and that she had not brought her notebook at all.

Gerda was at the far end of the rail, alone, with her arms folded and her patched coat buttoned to the chin. Tomorrow at this hour it would be her. She had the look of a person standing at the edge of cold water, watching someone else go in first to find out how cold it was.

Further along, by the standings board, Edran stood with his arms folded, looking not at the board for once but at the ring. Hobb was beside him, the two of them saying nothing, as companionable as two posts.

The three chairs stood at the north end with their chalk ticks and the little table. Quenna sat in the middle with the review ledger open and a folder beside it that Cael knew held one sheet of weak-tea-coloured paper. Wray sat on her left in the brown coat. The rotating seat was the broad-faced woman from the movement floor who had sat Cael's second sitting. Cael thought that was a piece of luck or of somebody's quiet arranging. She taught the movement Paths, and Wind was hers to read.

Lira came through the gate from the main hall exactly as the first bell finished ringing, with her hands wrapped and her hair tied back hard and nothing in her face at all.

"For the record," said Quenna.

The yard went quiet from the front backward.

"Re-certification review, formal. The candidate: Lira, Wind Path, re-certification track. Two recorded bouts before the panel, under standings rules. The originating academy's record of finding has been received and read by the panel. The candidate's recorded tier is Copper." Quenna did not look up from the ledger as she said it, and did not slow down, and did not hurry. "The panel will assess advancement against the recorded tier. First bout. The candidate takes the south mark."

Lira took the south mark.

Cael watched her take it and knew from the way she set her feet that she had read the sheet in the folder three times on Monday, and every night since in her head. She had come out here to make three people who had read it too sit and watch a floor disagree with it.

---

The first opponent was a third-year from the movement floor, Current Path, Copper Rank Seven by the board, a solid square boy with a reputation for doing everything the way the lecture said to do it. Current moved like water down a slope, a long gliding step that seemed to carry a body further than its legs had any right to. The boy had a textbook advance built on it. Two glides in, the second one curving, and a strike off the end of the curve while his opponent was still working out which way the water was going.

Wray lowered her finger.

Lira gave him the first exchange. She always gave the first exchange; Cael had watched her give it to fighters in Ardenmere who had not deserved the courtesy. She gave ground in short neat steps and let him show her everything. He glided in, and in again with the curve, and struck off the end of it, and she was not there, and he glided back out to the textbook distance and set himself and came again in exactly the same shape. She let him. Cael watched her watching. Her eyes were on his hips, where the glide began, and on his back foot, where it began a moment before that.

Wray called the exchange with no touch.

The second exchange lasted perhaps four breaths.

The third-year came off his mark into his first glide, and Lira came off hers at the same moment, straight at him, not to the side as the textbook said an evading fighter must but along his own line, inside it. She was there before the second glide had finished loading. It needed the first to finish and the weight to come round onto the forward foot before it could carry him anywhere, and the weight never arrived, because she was standing on the spot where it would have landed. He had committed to a curve, and the curve had nowhere to go. She put two fingers flat on his breastbone while he was still deciding what had happened.

"Touch," said Wray. "Candidate."

The third exchange he tried to change it, and Cael liked him for trying. He came in short, one glide only, and struck off the end of it early. It was a sensible answer and she had already read it. She read it off his back foot before he had moved, slipped it so close that his sleeve brushed her ear, and was behind his shoulder when he turned. Then the fingers were on his back, between the shoulder blades, light as a moth.

"Touch. Candidate. That's the bout."

The third-year stood for a moment with his hands at his sides, breathing, looking at the chalk at his feet as if it might have something to tell him. Then he nodded to her, once, a decent nod, and walked off the ring with the face of a boy who intends to go back to his lecture notes that evening and read them again with some suspicion.

At the table, the broad-faced woman had stopped writing. She was not writing anything at all. She was sitting with her pen lifted and her slate on her knee, simply looking at Lira, the way Cael had seen people at the Ironyard look at a fighter they had come to watch somebody else beat.

"She didn't take anything," said Brom, very low. "Not one hit."

"She didn't need to," said Cael. "Not with him. Wait."

The second opponent was the Storm Path fourth-year who had stopped Cael on the stair before the exhibition to ask whether he meant to fight Edran close or at range. He had come over the threshold into Iron at the end of last term, and the board gave him Iron Rank One. That put him at the lower edge of the tier, and above Lira on every list in the building except the one this yard was writing this morning. He was quick and narrow and long in the arm, and when he took his mark on the north side the air around him already smelled faintly of rain.

He had watched the first bout. Cael could see that in the way he set himself, further back than the textbook distance, his weight light and even. He was not going to give her a line to stand on.

He did not. When Wray lowered her finger he stayed out at the edge of the ring and worked from there. A Storm release was short and sharp, a slap of hard wet air off the palm that could knock a fighter a step sideways or throw grit into her eyes. He threw them small and fast and never stayed where he had thrown one from. Lira went after him, and he left. She went after him again, and he left again, out along the chalk and round, keeping the length of a long arm between them and never once committing to a strike that would have let her inside it. For the whole of the first exchange she chased him round the ring, and for the whole of it he made her pay a little for every pace. Once, she nearly had him against the north chalk, and he went out along it sideways like a cat on a fence. When Wray called it neither of them had a touch, and the yard had begun to make a low uncertain sound.

The second exchange was the same, and it was even, and it went on, and the yard began to tighten round it the way a yard did when a bout stopped being about who people had come to see and started being about which of two people would make a mistake first. Students who had drifted in late from the lecture wing stopped at the gate and did not come further. On the wall a first-year had his fist in his mouth.

Lira took the first hit crossing the middle of the ring. Cael saw her see it coming, a release thrown at her shoulder as she came in, and he saw the space where she could have slipped it, a clear half-pace to her right. She did not use the space. She took it across the top of the left shoulder, a grazing slap of storm-air that turned her a quarter round and would be a bruise for a week. She kept coming. The turn the hit had given her put her a pace closer to him than he had meant to let her be.

He was good. He left again, at once, before she could use the pace, and she chased.

Three moves later she took the second. This one she blocked, or seemed to, her forearm coming up a fraction late, so late that the release went half through her guard and rocked her back on her heels. Cael heard Brom draw in his breath beside him. But the block had been late on purpose, and he saw that too, because he had watched her do it on a circuit floor in Ardenmere with the rent due in the morning. She had let the hit move her because being moved put her exactly where she wanted to be, inside his reach on the side he had just thrown from, with his arm still out.

She had spent two hits in that exchange, and she had spent them without a flicker, the way a person counts out coins she came intending to spend and does not stop to look at them on the way across the counter. That was the whole of her argument, Cael understood, standing at the rail with his hands tight on the cold wood. She was not trying to win cleanly. She was showing three people with the Fenmark sheet at their elbows how far past a Copper ceiling she could go, and what she would pay to go there. She would pay it in front of them without looking down at the bill.

Wray called the second exchange with still no touch, and the Storm fourth-year was breathing hard.

The third exchange she finished with the barrow turn.

He had not seen her use it since Ardenmere. It was a thing she had found in the market yard behind the grain exchange, where the circuit fighters who could not afford a hall trained in the evenings between the stacked barrows. The lanes between the barrow lines were so narrow that you could not slip anything wide. You had to go under. When somebody came down the lane at you with his arm out, you ducked under the arm and turned in against him, so close that your shoulder went into his armpit, and you kept turning, the way a carter turns a laden barrow on a lane too narrow for it. His own weight, still going forward, went over his own front foot, and there was nothing he could do about it. She had used it on half the market yard in her first season. She had stopped using it when the fighters she faced got good enough to see it coming.

The Storm fourth-year did not see it coming, because nobody at Greyvane had ever seen it at all. He threw a release at her as she came in, and she went under it and turned, close, her shoulder into him. He went over his own front foot and caught himself on one hand on the chalk. Her two fingers were on the back of his neck before he had got the other hand down.

"Touch," said Wray. "Candidate. That's the bout."

For a moment the yard said nothing at all.

Then it did not cheer, because Greyvane did not cheer, but it made the low churning sound Cael had come to know, forty people turning to the person beside them with the same question in their faces. On the wall the first-year had taken his fist out of his mouth. Karis, along the rail, had her hands out of her pockets and was holding the rail with both of them, and she was smiling, openly, without correcting it at all.

Lira helped the fourth-year up. He took her hand and stood a moment with his other hand on his knee, getting his breath, and then he laughed, a short surprised breath of a laugh, and said something to her too low to carry, and she laughed back, once, and he went off the ring shaking his head.

---

The panel went to the far wall and talked. They talked for a shorter time than Cael had expected and a longer time than he could stand, and he stood it anyway with his hands on the rail and his eyes on the chalk. Lira stood on the south mark with her left shoulder held a little away from her side and her breathing slowing and her face as empty as a swept floor. She did not look at the panel. She did not look at the rail. She looked at the north mark, where nobody stood now, as if there might be another bout coming and she meant to be ready for it.

They came back to their chairs. Quenna opened the folder beside the ledger and took out the sheet of weak-tea-coloured paper and laid it flat, beside the ledger, where she could see both. Then she wrote. She wrote for some time, and then she lifted her pen and her voice fell into the flat slow cadence of the record, every word set down by itself.

"Re-certification review, formal. Two recorded bouts. Both won by the candidate. The panel's notation."

She read it off the ledger without hurrying.

"Advancement inconsistent with recorded tier. Recommend continued track with priority review."

Cael heard it once, and then once more inside his own head to make certain of the words, and then a third time, because he could not stop himself.

He knew what it was. He had spent his whole life inside records and he knew exactly what it was, and he felt it go through him in the cold yard like a draught under a door. It did not change her tier. She was Copper on Fenmark's sheet and Copper on Greyvane's ledger and she would be Copper on the board this evening with the dot still beside her name. It did not reverse anything, or retract anything, or call anyone wrong. It only stated, in an academy's own language, initialed by three people and dated, that the recorded tier and the floor did not agree. For two years the only paper in the world about Lira had been four paragraphs written by somebody who had never watched her fight. Now there were two pieces of paper, and the second one, written by three people who had, said in plain ink that the first did not add up.

It would stay. Few people alive understood that better than he did: a line in a ledger like that one outlasts the morning it was written, and outlasts the memory of whoever wrote it. Everything he had at Greyvane was built on exactly that.

"Initials," said Quenna. "And the candidate's signature that she has heard the notation."

Lira walked to the little table. She took the pen and signed, quickly, in her slanting hand. Then she stood a moment and looked down at the ledger, at the line Quenna had written, and Cael saw her read it, once, the way she had read nothing else that morning. Her face did not change. Her shoulders did.

She came off the ring toward the rail with her water flask, and stopped in front of him and Brom, and did not quite meet either of their eyes.

"I'm going to put cold on this shoulder," she said, "and then I'm going to sit somewhere by myself for an hour. Don't come and find me."

"All right."

"I mean it." She drank. "The wall, at dusk. Both of you. Not before."

She went out through the gate toward the residence wing, holding the shoulder. Gerda watched her go from the end of the rail with an expression Cael could not read at all. Then she looked up at the clock above the main hall door, and went in.

---

He spent the fifth bell in section four with the post, as Thursday required, and Karis did not say a word about the morning. She timed him and wrote and read back. Once, near the end, when he was getting his breath by the felt, she looked up and said, "She took both of those on purpose." He said, "Yes." She nodded and wrote nothing at all, and that was the whole of it.

At the sixth bell he went to the defensive floor. Brom had said at the midday meal, with a careful face, that Wray had asked him to stay late, and Brom never said anything with a careful face unless he did not yet know what it meant.

The far section was nearly empty. Hobb stood at the edge of the chalk with his hands in his pockets, plainly told to wait and not told why. Brom stood in the middle in his low loose stance, his weight sunk into his heels, and across from him stood Wray, with her brown coat off for the first time Cael had ever seen, folded on the boards at the edge of the section.

She was going to strike him herself.

Cael understood it as he reached the rail, and understood too that he had never seen her do it. In two months he had watched her measure Brom against Hobb, against the fetch drill, against two partners at once and against a Stone fourth-year told to commit everything. He had never once seen her stand across the chalk from him. Shield Path, Bronze. He had written on her page that Shield believed nothing should be spent that didn't have to be, and he had written it from the way she stood, because he had never seen anything else.

"Redirect," said Wray. "Whatever I send, take it and turn it. No more than that."

She struck.

It was not fast, the first one. It was a flat hard shove off her forearm, and as it came a faint pale plane went along the outside of her arm from wrist to elbow, like a pane of glass seen edge-on, so that the shove arrived as a wall arrives, all at once along its whole width. Brom took it on the forearm and gave, the inch and then the second inch, and turned it. It went back round on her along a line she plainly had not braced for, and she took it on her planted feet without moving at all, as if she had expected it to the last ounce.

"Again."

She struck again, a little faster.

Cael watched her count, though she did not count aloud. He could see it in the stillness of her face between strikes, the way he had seen Karis count. Every strike was a measurement, each a fraction quicker than the last, one condition changed and everything else held still. She went up the speeds the way a person goes up a stair in the dark, one step at a time, feeling for the next before putting weight on it.

On the seventh strike she stopped.

It had been fast, faster than anything Cael had seen Hobb throw or Edran either, a Bronze-tier strike from a woman who had not spent a single unnecessary motion since he had known her. Brom had taken it and given and turned, and the turn had been clean. But Cael had seen something in it that he had seen before without ever once seeing it as a thing, and when Wray lowered her hands and stood still he knew she had seen it too.

"Again," she said, quietly. "Same speed. Brom, I want you to do nothing different."

She struck. Brom took it, and gave, and waited, and turned.

The wait.

It was the thing Karis had asked him about in the first week, the second half that he held longer than anybody taught. Cael had written it on Brom's page as the best thing about him, the patience that let a strike commit itself fully before Brom took it away, because if he turned too soon the other fellow felt it and kept his weight back. It was the whole of what made his redirect work against people who meant it. And at this speed, for the first time, Cael could see the shape of it as a shape. Between the instant Brom's body had read the strike and the instant his body began to turn it, there was a gap. It was very small, small enough to live inside a breath with room to spare. At Iron speed it was invisible, and at Copper speed it was a gift.

Wray stepped back and put her hands down at her sides. She was not breathing hard; Cael had never seen her breathe hard.

"There's a gap," she said, "between the moment you've read a strike and the moment you begin to turn it. You hold it on purpose. It's the best thing you do." She looked at Brom steadily. "And it's a gap. Against anybody you've ever fought, it's worth holding, because they can't use it. I can see it. I'm Bronze, and I'm not quick enough to use it, but I've stood in front of Silver, and I can tell you what a fast Silver would do with it. He'd be through it before you'd started your turn."

Brom stood very still in his stance.

"Can you fix it?"

"I can show it to you. I've just done that." Wray bent and picked up her coat and put it on and buttoned it to the throat, one button at a time. "Closing it is another matter. You'd close it by meeting strikes fast enough to punish it, every day, for months, until your body learned to start the turn before the wait was done without losing what the wait gives you. That needs somebody faster than the gap." She buttoned the last button. "I haven't got anybody faster than the gap. Not me. Not Edran, not the Ternhall girl, not anybody on this academy's roster. Greyvane doesn't have one."

Hobb, at the edge of the chalk, took his hands out of his pockets.

"So it's a ceiling," said Brom slowly. "After all."

"No." Wray said it at once, and firmly, and Cael saw that she had been waiting for him to say it so she could say that. "You've made me careful with that word, and I'm not going to waste the care now. A ceiling is a place people stop because somebody drew a line on a board and told them to. Your column says I drew it in the wrong place, and I'm watching your column." She looked him in the face. "This is a wall. It's a real thing in the road, in your body, not in anybody's belief. You can see it now. Seeing a wall isn't the same as getting over it, and you'll not get over this one here. I'm sorry for that. I'd rather tell you today than let you find it on a floor somewhere with somebody fast on the other side."

For a long moment Brom only watched her. Then came the nod, single and slow, the nod he gave a fight that had gone the way he thought it would and he had not wanted it to.

"A wall."

"A wall," said Wray. She turned to Hobb. "Thank you. You were here in case I needed something slow and heavy to show him the difference, and I didn't. Go and eat." Hobb nodded and went, and passing Brom he stopped, and put one broad hand on Brom's shoulder for a moment, and took it away, and went on without a word.

Wray stopped at the rail where Cael stood.

"You saw it," she said. It was not a question.

"On the seventh."

"So did I." She looked at him with her level measuring eyes, and he could not tell what she was measuring. "Write it down properly. He'll want somebody to have seen it besides me."

---

Lira was on the wall at dusk, as she had said she would be, with a cold cloth folded under her coat over the shoulder and her knees drawn up. When they came out she moved over so that the gap beside her was exactly the width of two people.

She did not wait for them to ask.

She told them what was on the sheet. She told it flatly and in order, the four paragraphs, the one signature that belonged to nobody she had ever met, the nineteen words. She said the nineteen words aloud, all of them, and Cael counted them as she said them and hated himself a little for counting and could not stop. She told them about the threshold rule, *formal hearing not required*, and the room with the green cloth that had not been a hearing at all, only a reading. Then she told them about the sentence the man at the end of the table had added out loud, *it means you have no control of yourself*, and how she had looked for it on the sheet and it was not there.

"He made it up," she said. "Two years I've had that sentence in me like a splinter, and it isn't even written down anywhere. They didn't think it was worth writing."

Brom said nothing. He put one hand flat on the stone between them.

Cael thought for a long time before he said anything, because it mattered, and because the things that mattered most to say to Lira were the ones she would know at once if he had not thought them through.

"When Vell gave me the sheaf," he said slowly, "the last night in Ardenmere. Her copy of my whole record off the Ironyard ledger, every bout. She said, the records know you existed here. I didn't understand what she meant. I thought she meant it as comfort, because the registry had me down as a word and nothing else, and her ledger had me down as everything I'd done." He looked out at the yard going dark. "I think she meant it as a fact. Whatever anyone writes somewhere more important, the people who were there and wrote it down honestly, their page stays. It knows what happened. Nobody gets to argue it away by writing something different in a better building."

Lira was very still beside him.

She said it slowly, as if she were testing the weight of each word before she put her own on it. "The records know what happened here. Nobody gets to say otherwise."

"That's the one I'd keep."

She sat with it a while. Then she did a thing he had not expected, which was to laugh, really laugh, a sound that came up out of her as if it had been kept waiting on a stair for two years and had just been told it could come down. She took the four paragraphs out of her coat. Quenna had given the original back to her after the panel, he learned later; the file kept a certified copy. She folded them once more along the old crease and put them back inside her coat, against her ribs, on the side away from the bruise.

"Evidence," she said. "For when it's obvious."

"When what's obvious?"

"That they were wrong." She said it with no heat in it at all, the way she said the score at the end of a bout. "I'm not filing a dispute. Not now. Maybe not ever. This morning's line does more for me here than any argument with Fenmark would, and I'd have to stand up in front of more people who were never in the room." She patted the coat. "But I'm keeping these. For the day the proving runs the other way, and some office owes me an admission instead of me owing it proof."

She turned to Brom. "You've been sitting there with a face on since you came out. I noticed. I've had my turn. Say it."

Brom looked at the yard.

"Wray found my wall," he said.

He told it as plainly as he told everything, the gap between the read and the turn, the wait that was the best thing he did, a fast Silver going through it before he'd started. Nobody at Greyvane quick enough to teach him to close it. Not Wray, not Edran, not Karis.

"She called it a wall," he said. "Not a ceiling. She was very particular. A ceiling is a thing somebody draws. A wall's a thing in you." He was quiet a moment. "It's an honest wall. Nobody's fault. Just the sort of thing you hit if you get good enough to hit it."

Lira leaned across and bumped his shoulder with hers, and winced because it was the bruised one, and did it again with the other on principle, and Brom made the low sound in his chest that did him for a laugh.

The three of them sat a long while after that without saying anything, while the yard went from grey to dark and the hill town's lamps came on below them. Cael thought about the two things that had been found that day, one in a ledger and one in a friend, and how differently they sat. Lira's had been paper all along, and paper could be answered with a floor. Brom's was bone and habit and two years of being right, and there was no ledger anywhere that could be argued with about it.

That night it went into the binder, written in the plain hand he saved for the evenings that moved something.

*Lira's review. Two bouts, two won. She spent two hits in the second like coins and finished with the barrow turn from the Ardenmere market yard. The notation, read aloud, in their words: Advancement inconsistent with recorded tier. Recommend continued track with priority review. First paper anywhere that says her file might be wrong. She's keeping Fenmark's four paragraphs. No dispute.*

*Brom's wall. Wray struck him herself and found it on the seventh strike, the gap between the read and the turn. She called it a wall and not a ceiling and meant the difference. Nobody here is fast enough to close it.*

He looked at the two paragraphs side by side, and then wrote the line underneath them that he had been carrying all the way up the stairs.

*Some walls come down when you finally push. Some stand because nobody ever built a door in them. I think learning to tell which is which might be most of what this place is for.*

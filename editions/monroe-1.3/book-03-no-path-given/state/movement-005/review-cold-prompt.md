# Chapter 32 — Both Versions

The fourth sitting was exactly as dull as he had meant it to be, and he was proud of every minute of it.

He came into the training hall at the first bell rested, as Quenna had told him to, after two nights of going to bed when Brom did and one morning of doing nothing at all with his chest. The six sections had been swept back to their pale ghosts again and a single ring laid fresh at the centre. The three chairs stood at the far end with their chalk ticks. Quenna sat in the middle with her slate, and Wray sat on her left in the brown coat. The rotating seat this month was the white-haired, spare man who had sat the first sitting, the one who waited like a heron in a shallow, and he nodded to Cael as if they had parted yesterday.

Hobb was in the ring. He stood at his mark with his feet a shoulder-width apart and his hands loose, as patient as a gatepost, and when Cael came to the south mark Hobb looked at him once, briefly, with something in his broad face that had not been there at the first sitting. It was not friendliness, exactly. It was the look of one workman to another on a job they had both done before.

"For the record," said Quenna, and the room went flat and slow.

He showed the framework, four passes, at panel speed. Wray broke the count on the third pass, as she had broken it at the first sitting, and he read Hobb's hips through the broken rhythm as easily as through the steady one. He showed the surface read, four calls at two paces, the last with his eyes shut. Hobb's weight went where Cael said it would go every time. On the fourth call Hobb tried to change his mind halfway through the lean, and Cael named the change before it was finished, and Hobb huffed through his nose exactly as he had huffed at the first sitting, like a man hearing someone finish his sentence.

Nothing woke in his chest. Nothing came near enough to wake it. He had built the morning so that nothing would.

The panel did not go to the far wall this time. They conferred at the chairs, low and brief, and the white-haired man asked no question at all. Wray spoke without any change in her voice.

"As previously recorded. No safety concerns."

"Assessment satisfied," said Quenna, and set down her pen.

That was all. Four sittings, and the record said the same thing four times in slightly different words. Somewhere at the far end of a road, at a desk he had built in his own head under a lamp, someone would one day read four pages that agreed with each other and found nothing on any of them worth underlining. He had spent a quarter of an hour in a swept ring making sure of it. Show enough to pass. Bank everything that matters. It had become a craft, the way taking a hit on purpose had become a craft for Lira, and he was beginning to be good at it.

Hobb walked past him on the way out of the ring and slowed, as he slowed at rails.

"Same as last time," said Hobb.

"Same as last time."

Hobb considered this, and seemed to approve, and went out after Wray.

Quenna stayed in her chair with her slate face down on the little table. When the door had shut behind the others she looked up at him, and her voice came down out of the record into the lower one she kept for things she meant to say only once.

"The third bell," she said. "Don't be early. I'll still be finishing this."

---

Quenna's room was up a narrow stair at the back of the main hall, over the assessors' room, and he had never been in it. It was smaller than he had imagined and much tidier. A desk stood under a window that looked down onto the formal yard, with three chairs, a shelf of folders tied with tape, a cold grate and a kettle on it that had plainly not been used since the autumn. There was nothing on the walls at all. It looked like the room of somebody who did her real work somewhere else and came here only to write it down.

Karis was already on the landing when he came up. She stood by the door with her hands at her sides and her notebook under her arm, and when she saw him she nodded once, the nod of a colleague arriving at the same lecture.

"You were early," he said.

"I was exactly on time. You're early." She looked at the closed door. "She said not to be."

"She said that to me too."

"Then we'll both have been told." Karis knocked.

Quenna let them in and gave them each a chair and took the one behind the desk. She did not offer tea. She laid her hands flat on a single sheet of paper in front of her, written in her own hand, and looked from one of them to the other.

"Next month's sitting," she said. "The fifth. It falls in four weeks. I'd like it to be something with structure." She let that sit for exactly one breath. "A supervised match. Formal conditions, a full panel, logged under the demonstration provision like every other sitting. Against Karis."

The room was very quiet. Down in the yard, through the window, somebody was sweeping.

Cael found that he had known it since the note under his door. He had not let himself know it, which was a different thing. He sat with his hands on his knees and made himself say the first sentence a form would one day hold, because he had learned that it was worth saying the official version aloud before anybody said the other one.

"The panel would like a look at the observer against an Iron-tier opponent at full intensity."

"The panel does want that," said Quenna. She did not blink. "Four sittings of a framework and a read against a partner told how hard to hit. A careful reader starts to wonder what the candidate does when nobody's told the partner anything. I'd rather the record answered that question while I'm the one holding the pen." She paused. "It's also true. Both are true. Most things worth doing here have two versions, and both of them true. It's the one rule of this building I'd write on a wall."

She did not say what the other version was. She did not look at Karis when she said *both*. Cael watched her not look, and understood that this was how Quenna had always worked with him: she stood at the edge of what she would let herself know, and she built a door there, and she held it open without ever once walking through it. She had never seen the binder. She had never heard the word *acquisition* from anybody. But she had spent a career on irregular development, and she had watched him get tired for three weeks doing something that was not her session, and nothing Quenna had ever arranged in his sight had been an accident.

"Three exchanges," Quenna went on. "Faculty discretion to halt at any moment. I'll preside. Wray will take the instructor's seat. The rotating seat will be whoever the rota gives us, and I won't change the rota." She turned to Karis. "You're entitled to refuse. You're a student of full standing and this isn't a standings bout. Nothing goes on your record if you say no."

"I'm not going to say no," said Karis.

"I know you're not. I'm required to tell you that you may." Quenna slid the sheet across the desk to her. "The standard waiver is on that page. It says that you understand a supervised match is fought at full intensity and that you accept the ordinary risks of one. It satisfies the requirement as it stands. Sign it and we're done."

Karis read it. She read it twice, the way she read everything, and then she set it down square to the edge of the desk.

"May I have a clean sheet?"

Quenna looked at her for a moment. Then she opened a drawer and took one out and laid it on the desk, and laid a pen beside it, and sat back.

Karis did not use Quenna's pen. She took one of her own two from her coat. She wrote for some time, slowly, in her small upright hand with every letter closed at the top, and twice she stopped and looked at the window and then went on. When she reached what Cael guessed was the second-to-last line, she drew a line under it, once, steadily, from one end to the other. Then she wrote one more sentence and signed and dated it, and turned the sheet round so that it faced him rather than Quenna.

He read it.

*I, Karis Dellenmoor, Ember Path, Iron Rank Three, a student of full standing, consent to a supervised match against the candidate enrolled under the demonstration provision, on whatever conditions the presiding assessor sets. I have read the standard waiver and accept everything in it. I ask that nothing in the conditions be softened on my account. I will fight at full intensity because a match fought at less would not be worth recording. I want to be the first documented source. If it works, the documentation matters. If it doesn't, that matters more. I have read this twice before signing.*

The line drawn under the middle of it ran beneath those three short sentences and nothing else. He read them a second time. Then he read the whole page again from the top.

Twenty-two times in the quiet room she had stood at the far end of the whitewash and said *I consent to this session* aloud, and written the hour, and he had stood and reached, and nothing had come of any of it. This was not that. This was ink, on a sheet that would go into an academy's file with an assessor's initials beside it. And it was consent to a thing with something real on it, which the quiet room had never once held. She did not know that the second part was the part that might matter. He did. He sat in Quenna's tidy room with the page in front of him and felt the knowledge sit in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Quenna turned the sheet round toward herself and read it. Cael watched her come to *source*. He watched her eyes stop on it for a fraction longer than on any other word, and then go on, and not come back. She did not ask what it meant. She initialed the margin beside Karis's signature, and dated it, and laid it in a folder.

"It's longer than it needs to be," said Quenna.

"I know," said Karis. "I wanted it to be mine."

---

They went down the narrow stair together and out into the yard, where the sweeper had finished and gone, and walked along the low wall toward the stable without either of them deciding to.

"You didn't say anything," said Karis.

"I didn't know what to say."

"Neither did I, until I had the pen." She walked a few steps in silence. "I asked her once whether there could ever be anything more than watching, inside the rules. She said not as the provision's written. I asked her to keep the question." She looked straight ahead at the stable door. "A sitting is inside the rules. A match is a sitting. She kept it and then she found a way to answer it without breaking anything."

"You think that's what this is."

"I think it's both, as she said. I think she'd like to see you against something that isn't told how hard to hit. I think she'd also like to know what happens." Karis was quiet a moment. "So would I. A match is a room we never built. Twenty-two times we tried everything I could think of in the quiet room, and something was missing. I don't know what. It might be in a formal yard, in front of three chairs, with a girl across the chalk trying to win. It might not be in there either. If it isn't, then I'll have a clean null in front of a crowd, and that would be worth writing down too."

Cael said nothing.

He had the sentence in the binder in his own square hand, under the four names. *It takes what's used on me. The stakes aren't the weather round it. They're part of how it works.* She had written *We are missing a condition* under a ruled line in her ledger, and he had sat three paces away and kept the condition in his chest, and he was keeping it now, on a frosted path in the middle of the morning, with her walking beside him close enough to touch.

He had told Lira why. If Karis knew, she would carry a hypothesis she could never test without breaking the line at the bottom of her own page. But now there was a way to test it that broke nothing, and he had to find out, quickly and honestly, whether that changed anything.

He thought it did not. If she knew, she would walk into the yard in four weeks fighting the hypothesis instead of fighting him. She would be watching for the condition the way he had watched for it in the quiet room, reaching and watching himself reach. Whatever happened then would be something they had arranged together. And Lira had said, on a wet night on the wall, *let it be tested by something you didn't go looking for.*

"You've gone quiet," said Karis.

"I'm thinking about four weeks."

"Good. So am I." At the stable door she stopped and turned to him, and for the first time since the terms she looked faintly uncomfortable. "There's something I want to say about those four weeks. You've watched me at demonstration intensity for three weeks in a room. You've watched me fight Edran once. You haven't watched me build. I've watched you fight Edran, and I've timed your framework at every session since the first, and you've read every line I wrote about it before anybody else. That isn't even."

"It's the sixth clause."

"It's the sixth clause, and I'm glad of it, and it still isn't even." She held his eyes. "I'm going to train in the open for the next four weeks. You can watch any of it. All of it, if you like. I'd rather lose to someone who'd seen everything I had than beat someone who hadn't."

He looked at her for a long moment in the cold.

"That's not how anybody prepares for a match."

"It's how I'd like to." She opened the stable door. "Symmetry. You may have noticed I like it."

---

He told them at supper, because the rule was the day he knew it, and he had known it since the third bell.

They had the end of the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, the corner farthest from the fire, with Brom on the bench opposite and Lira beside him and a pot of barley between them that Brom regarded as his own. Karis had eaten early and gone. She had told them she would, and why, in her proposing-a-variable voice: she thought the three of them should have the evening without her, and she would be in the archive if anybody needed her for anything.

He laid the fair copy on the table between the bowls. Quenna had given it to him on the stair as he left, folded once, without being asked. "It's part of your record now," she had said. "You read every other part of it first. I don't see why this should be different."

Lira picked it up.

She read it twice. The second time she read it slowly, with her thumb moving down the margin, which was how she read anything she had not yet decided to believe. When she got to the underlined line she stopped, and her thumb stopped, and she read that line a third time on its own. Then she set the sheet down very carefully beside her bowl, and kept her hand on it.

"She writes a better waiver than Fenmark ever wrote an expulsion," she said.

Brom looked up.

"Four paragraphs to end me, and not one of them said whether I'd been right." Lira tapped the sheet. "Five sentences to walk into a ring with you, and she said exactly why in every one of them." She was trying very hard to keep her face dry, and it was not entirely working. "I don't know whether to be impressed or frightened."

"Both," said Brom. He had gone back to his barley, and he did not look up from it. "Both is usually correct, with her."

Lira laughed, short and unwilling, and pushed the sheet back across to Cael.

"Four weeks," she said.

"Four weeks. The fifth sitting. Three exchanges, Quenna presiding, Wray in the second chair."

"And Karis is going to train in the open the whole time so you can watch her."

"She called it symmetry."

"Of course she did." Lira tore her bread in half and held both halves without eating either. "Four weeks of you studying the best Ember fighter either of us has ever seen, on purpose, with her blessing, so that you can stand across a chalk ring from her and be beaten slightly less badly."

"That's one way to say it."

"It's the way the stairs will say it." She put one of the halves in front of him. "There's another way, and you know it, and I'm not going to say it at this table."

Brom ate a spoonful of barley, slowly. Then he set his spoon down.

"I'll say the part that can be said at a table," he said. "I've watched her lay her points on a floor twice. You won't beat her by watching what she does. You'll have to watch what she's about to do." He considered his bowl. "That's your one thing, out of everybody I've ever met. Watching what's about to happen. It'd be a shame to spend four weeks watching anything else."

---

Lira was on the wall afterward, as he had known she would be, and he sat down beside her without being asked. The night was clear and very cold. The hill town's lamps were scattered down the slope below them, and the training yard was black and frosted.

She waited until he was settled before she said anything.

"On this wall," she said, "three weeks ago, I told you something. Do you remember it?"

"Don't you ever make her the stakes."

"Don't you ever let anybody arrange for her to come at you for real so you can find out." Lira did not look at him. "Not Quenna, not anybody. I said it in those words. I've been thinking about those words since the stable door shut."

He had been thinking about them since the third bell. He waited.

"I've been trying to decide whether this is that," said Lira. "Whether Quenna's done the thing I told you never to let anybody do. She's put a match on the calendar. She's put Karis across from you, at full intensity, with the whole yard watching. That's somebody coming at you for real. And you know what you wrote in your binder, and so do I, and Karis doesn't."

"Quenna doesn't know it either."

"No. That's the first thing I worked out." Lira drew her knees up and put her chin on them. "Quenna isn't arranging anything for your binder. She's never read it. She wants a match because a match is what a careful reader would want to see, and she'd rather the record had one in it while she's holding the pen. Whatever else she thinks, she's never asked, and she won't." She was quiet a moment. "And Karis isn't being arranged. Karis walked into Quenna's room and asked for a clean sheet. Nobody put her anywhere. She put herself there and wrote down why."

"So it isn't that."

"It isn't that yet." Lira turned her head and looked at him in the dark. "There's only one person who could make it that, Cael. You. If you walk into that yard in four weeks meaning to find out, if you stand at your mark watching yourself and waiting for your binder to come true, then you've arranged it. You'll have made her the stakes on your own, in your own head, and she'll never know you did."

He sat with that. The cold came up through the stone into him and he let it.

"So what do I walk in meaning to do?"

"Fight her." Lira said it at once, as if she had been holding the words ready since the stable. "Go in to fight her. Not to find out. She's the best you've ever stood across from, and she's going to try to beat you in front of three chairs and half the school, and you're going to try to stop her. That's a match. That's the only thing it's allowed to be." She unfolded one arm and pointed at him. "If anything else happens in it, then it happens because of a fight. Not because you went looking. That's the difference between letting and looking. It's the only one I've got."

"And Karis?"

Lira was quiet for a long time.

"You told me you were keeping it off her, not from her," she said at last. "You said when it was tested, if it ever was, you'd tell her the first day. I said I'd hold it with you." She looked out at the yard. "If you tell her now, she goes in fighting your binder instead of you. She'd try to help. You know she would. She'd stand at her mark thinking about conditions, and that's the two of you arranging it together, very carefully, with clauses. I don't know if that's worse. I know it isn't a fight."

"Then I don't tell her."

"Then you don't tell her before." Lira looked at him hard. "And the first day after, whatever happens or doesn't, you tell her everything you wrote. All of it. That's the price. I'm not holding this with you a day longer than the match."

"The first day after," he said.

"Good." She stood up off the wall and shook out her coat. "Now I'm going to go and be frightened in my room, where it's warm. You can be frightened out here if you like. It's a better view."

She went in. He stayed on the wall, and he was frightened, a little, and it was a better view.

By the next morning there was a new sheet pinned beside the assessment calendar, in the clerk's small even hand.

*Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track. Fifth sitting. Supervised match under standings rules, three exchanges, faculty discretion to halt. Opponent: Karis, Ember Path, Iron Rank Three. Conditions to be posted. Week sixteen.*

By the midday meal the rumor market had built three new theories and torn down one of the old ones. Lira reported them at the scarred table with the brisk satisfaction of a woman whose district had just become very busy.

"First," she said, holding up a finger. "The faculty are tired of you and have hired the Ternhall girl to get rid of you in public. Mostly the Edge floor. They're very pleased about it."

"Naturally," said Cael.

"Second. Karis asked for the match herself, because she came all this way to beat you and the board wasn't enough. That one's nearly true, which is why I don't like it." A third finger. "Third, and this one's mine, you're secretly the Ternhall girl's younger brother and the whole thing's a family quarrel. I started it at breakfast. It's already on the second floor."

"Why?"

"Because it's stupid, and stupid ones travel fastest, and the faster that one goes the slower the true one goes." She took his bread. "Also the tout's opened a book. Five to one against you. Brom wouldn't put any barley on you."

"I don't wager food," said Brom, with great dignity. "Food is not a game."

"You'd wager it on her, though."

"I wouldn't wager it on anybody. That's what not wagering means."

Oona was at the board before the first bell the next morning, in front of the new sheet, reading it with her lips moving, and she did not look round when he came up beside her.

"Are you going to win?" she said.

It was the question she had asked before the exhibition, in the same voice, as if it were a sum somebody had set her.

"Probably not," he said.

Oona turned her head and looked at him.

"Then why are you doing it?"

He thought about that properly, because she had asked properly.

"Because she's the best I've ever seen," he said. "And she wants to find out what I am against somebody who's really trying. So do I. That's the honest way to find out. You don't find out what you are by fighting people you know you'll beat."

Oona considered this for some time. Then she looked back at the sheet.

"*By her written consent*," she read. "That means she asked."

"She asked."

"Then it's fair." Oona wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice. "I'll be on the front bench. I'm going to be honest with you. I'm going to want her to win a little bit, as well. She showed me a point over my hand." She looked up at him with her whole serious face. "Is that all right?"

"That's all right," said Cael. "I'd think less of you if you didn't."

She nodded, satisfied, and went off toward the lecture wing, and he stood in front of the sheet a while longer with his name on it and Karis's under it, in the same ink.

Edran found him two days later, crossing the yard between the archive and the lecture wing, and fell into step with him for exactly as long as it took to say one thing. He had been at the rail of the heat-Paths floor every afternoon since the notice went up, with his arms folded, watching Karis train, and Cael had watched him watching her. It had the look of a man revising something he had thought was finished.

"After she beat me," said Edran, without looking at him, "I sent for Ternhall's standings record. Theirs is public, if you write for it and pay the copying." He kept walking. "It goes back three years. Nobody's crossed that grid at Ternhall in two years. I thought you'd want the number."

He turned off toward the lecture wing before Cael could answer. It was not friendship. Cael watched him go and thought that it was exactly what Edran had promised on the exhibition floor, a witness, and that it had begun to run in both directions.

He wrote it all that night, at the desk by the narrow window, in the plain hand he kept for the evenings that turned a corner.

*Fourth sitting. Satisfied, as previously recorded. Nothing woke.*

*Quenna has put a match on the calendar for the fifth sitting, week sixteen, against Karis. Both versions true. Karis wrote her own consent and underlined one line of it. Quenna read the word source and didn't ask. I have the fair copy in the back pocket.*

*Edran says nobody has crossed her grid at Ternhall in two years.*

*Lira's rule: go in to fight her. Not to find out. The first day after, whatever happens, Karis gets the whole page.*

He looked at that for a while, and then wrote one more line under it, because the binder was the one place he never lied.

*I don't know if I can walk into a ring with that sentence in me and not be waiting for it. I'm going to have to learn how in four weeks.*

# Chapter 33 — Sixteen Promises

Karis trained at the fifth bell in section two, on the heat-Paths floor, and on the first afternoon of the study she stopped at the floor desk before her block and said something to the lean old instructor with scorched cuffs that made him look across the hall at the rail.

Cael was at the rail with the binder open on his arm. The instructor looked at him for a long moment, the way a man looks at a stray dog that has wandered onto a farm and sat down as if it lived there. Then he said something short back to Karis, and Karis nodded, and the instructor picked up his slate and went to his chalk.

"What did he say?" Cael asked her afterward.

"I told him the observer would be watching my blocks until the fifth sitting, with my leave. He said, 'Then he'll see how it's done properly.'" She did not smile. "He meant it as a compliment to Ternhall. I've decided to take it as one to me."

He watched four blocks that first week, and by the end of the second he understood why he had learned more about Ember in an afternoon than the circuit had taught him about any single Path in a season.

On the circuit, people learned to fight from whoever was nearest. A Storm fighter in Ardenmere had been taught by a cousin who had been taught by a man in a tavern, and every one of them had added something of his own and left something out, so that by the time the Path reached the ring it came wrapped in a whole family's habits. Cael had spent his whole time on the circuit reading those habits. A dropped elbow here, a breath held too long there, a shoulder that always came up before the hard ones because somebody's uncle had taught it to come up. They were tells, and they were gifts, and every one of them was an accident.

Karis had no accidents.

That was the first thing he wrote down, and it took him most of a page to say it properly. Ternhall did not train its students out of their tells. It put the tells *in*, on purpose, and called them correct form. The off hand went still before an ignition because a still off hand steadied the one doing the work, and Ternhall had decided it should, and had drilled it into a thousand students until it happened by itself. The half-breath before a sustain was there because a sustain held cleaner on a settled chest. Every commitment showed exactly as much as correct form required it to show, no more and no less. He had thought, on the first afternoon, that this would make her easy to read.

It made her the hardest person he had ever read.

*There's nothing extra,* he wrote that night. *Every tell is in the right place and is exactly the right size. On the circuit the tell was the mistake. Here the tell is the form, and the form has no mistakes in it. I know when her points are coming to a quarter of a second. Knowing doesn't help. She's built so that knowing doesn't help.*

He read it back and added a line under it.

*A good school is a kind of honesty. It shows you everything and hides it all by being exact.*

---

Edran was at the same rail on the third afternoon, at the far end, with his arms folded.

He had been there every afternoon since the notice went up. Cael had watched him come in at the fifth bell and take the same place at the end of the rail and stand without moving for the whole of Karis's block, and go out again at the sixth without speaking to anybody. On the third afternoon, when the block was over and the section was emptying, Edran did not go out. He came down the rail instead, and stopped a pace from Cael, and looked at the binder open on Cael's arm.

"You watch her early," said Edran.

"Early?"

"The start of her block. The first points." Edran nodded at the empty section, where a faint warmth still hung over the boards. "I watch her late. The end of the block, when she's tired. I've been trying to see whether it changes." He was quiet a moment. "It doesn't, much. She's the same at the end as at the start. I've never seen anybody like that."

"It changes a little. The release gets longer."

Edran turned his head and looked at him properly.

"How much longer?"

"A few hundredths of a breath, after the first half-hour. More after an hour." Cael closed the binder on his finger. "She can't feel it. She didn't know until I told her."

"You told her."

"She'd have wanted to know."

Edran looked at him for a long moment with his flat honest face, and Cael could not tell whether he thought that was admirable or mad. Perhaps both.

"When she beat me," Edran said at last, "I didn't see the first six points until the end of the second exchange. Six coins on the floor round my feet, and I'd walked over all of them." He unfolded his arms and looked at his own right forearm. "I've thought about that every day since. I was watching her. I thought I was. I was watching her hands."

"Everybody watches her hands."

"You don't." Edran held out the right forearm, palm down, the free arm, the one he had been teaching all term to build a third structure while the second still stood. "Since you watch everyone. Is it thicker?"

Cael had watched him in the corner by the high windows every morning he could, as he had promised to. He did not need to look now. He looked anyway, because Edran had asked him to.

"Thicker," he said. "Not thick. It came up whole three times in four this morning. A week ago it was two."

"Three in four." Edran turned the arm over and back. "Wray said a term."

"It's been five weeks."

"Five weeks. Three in four." Edran let the arm fall. "I'll take it." He looked back at the empty section. "The match won't move the board. I read the notice. It's a sitting, not a standings bout."

"No. It won't move anything."

"Good," said Edran. He said it with real feeling, more than Cael had ever heard from him. "I'd like the board to stay about the board. Whatever you two are doing out there, I'd like it not to be about where anybody's name is written." He nodded once, to Cael or to the section, and went out through the north door, and did not look back.

---

The second thing he learned was how she talked.

Circuit Ember fighters, the few he had seen, talked about heat. How much, how hot, how wide a sheet they could throw across a ring. Karis never once said *heat* in a block. She said *lay* and *hold* and *join* and *let go*. Her instructor called counts, and she answered every count with one of those four words and an action to match, and after a week Cael understood that they were the whole of her vocabulary, and that the vocabulary was the Path.

Hers ran on control, not output. He wrote that down too, and underlined it, which he almost never did. If a small release would do a job, she never spent a large one on it. She set a point, a dim warm coin of light the size of a thumbnail, exactly where she meant it, and it stayed there exactly as long as she meant it to. When she joined two of them, the line of heat that ran between them along the boards was thin and faint and singing, never a wall, never a sheet. Everything she did was small, and nothing she did was weak.

"Why small?" he asked her at the rail at the end of the fourth block, when she came to drink.

She considered the question as if it deserved more than the answer she already had, which was how she considered all his questions.

"Because a big fire tells everyone where it is," she said. "A small one only tells the person it's for." She drank. "And because I can hold sixteen small ones longer than I could hold two big ones. That isn't a philosophy. It's arithmetic."

"Sixteen."

"Sixteen is as many as I've ever held at once." She looked at him over the flask. "You've seen nine. Against Edran. I'll show you sixteen before the sitting. That was the agreement."

The heat-Paths instructor came to the rail on the fifth afternoon, while Karis was drinking at the far end of the section, and stood beside Cael without looking at him.

He was a lean old man with a long, weathered face, and his cuffs were scorched brown in a ring round each wrist, the way a smith's apron scorches, from a lifetime of standing too near other people's fire. He had sat the exhibition. He had sat Gerda's review. He would sit the fifth sitting, by rota, and Cael had wondered more than once whether the rota knew what it was doing.

"You're the observer," said the instructor.

"Yes."

"I've read your record. All four sittings. It's very short." He watched Karis cap her flask. "You've been at my rail every block for a week. I don't usually allow it. She asked."

"I know. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank Ternhall. I wanted to see how long a Ternhall girl would let somebody watch her before she found it uncomfortable." He was quiet a moment. "She hasn't. I find that interesting."

Cael waited. He had learned with Wray that people who taught Paths for a living often said the important thing second.

"You'll have noticed she talks in four words," said the instructor. "Lay, hold, join, let go."

"Yes."

"That's Ternhall. Every Ember school in the country teaches the same declarations; the Arbiter gives what it gives. What a school teaches is the order you use them in." He nodded at the section. "Ternhall teaches orders the way a grammar teaches sentences. Points first, always. Joins last, always. Never join what you can still move. A Ternhall Ember child can lay you a dozen different drawings by the time she's fifteen, all from the same two declarations, the way a clever child can say a dozen different things with the same forty words." He sniffed. "Ternhall calls it a lattice. Most schools haven't got a word for it at all, because most schools can't do it. It isn't a declaration. It's a habit of putting them in the right order."

"Can it be broken?"

The instructor looked at him for the first time.

"Anything can be broken," he said. "I've never seen a Ternhall one broken from inside. Only from outside, by somebody stronger crossing it, and nobody here is strong enough to cross hers twice." He looked back at Karis, who was coming back to her chalk. "If you want my advice, and I don't give it to observers, you'll stop her before she's composed anything. After that, it's grammar, and she's better at grammar than you are."

He went back to his slate. Cael wrote *Never join what you can still move* in the binder, and under it *Stop her before she composes*, and looked at the second line for a long time without knowing yet how a person would do it.

On the sixth block she showed him a thing that went into the binder on a page of its own.

She had laid a point at the centre of her section on her instructor's count, held it, and let it go. Its third of a breath of shimmer hung in the air after it, the release Cael had timed in every session since the quiet room, and on the next count, while the shimmer was still dying, she tried to lay another in exactly the same place.

Nothing happened. Her off hand went still, and her breath went in, and the air in that one small spot did nothing at all.

She waited until the shimmer had gone. Then she laid it again, and it bloomed at once.

"You did that on purpose," Cael said, when she came to the rail.

"I did it for you." She wiped her hands on a cloth. "A point won't take where there's still heat standing. It needs cold air to stand in. Even my own heat. My last point has to finish dying before I can lay another in its place, and you've seen how long mine take to finish dying when I'm tired." She shrugged. "Nothing can set fire where a fire already is. It's the first rule they teach an Ember child, and most of us forget it's a rule because we never think about it after we're eleven."

"Why show me?"

"Because it's true about me, and you didn't know it." She folded the cloth. "Symmetry. If you're going to study me, you should study the real thing, the edges as well."

He wrote it in the binder that evening as plainly as she had said it, under the heading he had begun to keep for her: *A point won't take where heat already stands. She has to wait out her own lag to lay twice in one place.* Then he sat and looked at it for some time, without knowing why, the way he had once looked at the line about Brom's wait before anybody knew it was a wall.

---

On the last afternoon of the second week she built the whole thing.

She had asked Wray for a partner. She had asked, she told Cael, for somebody who would not be told how hard to hit and would not be told to lose, somebody who knew he was outmatched and meant to be expensive about it. Wray had looked at her for a while and then sent Hobb.

The heat-Paths floor had never had so many people at its rail. Word had got round, as word always got round at Greyvane, that the Ternhall girl was going to fight a full bout in the open with the observer watching. Edran was there, at the end of the rail, with his arms folded. Lira was there, and Brom, and Gerda, and the second-year tout with a stub of pencil behind his ear. Oona had come in from the lecture wing with her slate and stood on the bottom rung of the rail to see over a third-year's shoulder.

Hobb stood at the north mark of section two with his hands loose and his feet a shoulder-width apart. He did not look like furniture today. Cael could not have said exactly what was different. Perhaps it was the set of his jaw. Perhaps it was only that nobody had bent to his ear before the bout to tell him what to do.

The heat-Paths instructor called it, four exchanges, no touches counted, the bout to end on a yield or on his word.

Hobb came out hard.

It was a good opening and Cael admired it. A Stone fighter could not be moved and could not be hurried, and Hobb knew exactly what Karis's points preferred, which was room. So he took the room away. He pressed her early, with short honest steps and his heavy forearms up, and refused to give her the distance her point-work liked. He did not chase her. He walked her down, the way a wall walks somebody down by being somewhere they wanted to stand.

Karis gave ground.

To the untrained half of the rail it looked even, and then it looked as if Hobb was winning. He came forward and she went back, and he came forward again and she went back again, a step at a time, and twice he got close enough to put a forearm into her guard and rock her. Cael heard the tout behind him say, "Oh, now, that's interesting," in the voice of a man reconsidering his book.

Cael was not watching the fight. He was watching the floor behind her.

On every step she gave, she left a point. He saw the first one go down on her second step, a dim coin of light on the boards where her heel had just been, and her off hand went still a quarter of a breath before it as it always did, and the coin stayed there glowing faintly, like a coal through paper. Then another. Then another, a little to the left. None of them flared. None of them did anything at all. They sat on the boards behind her as she retreated, patient and small, each one beneath anyone's notice, and Hobb walked her back across them without once looking down.

Four in the first exchange. Five in the second. Cael counted aloud under his breath, and Lira, beside him, stopped watching Hobb.

In the third exchange Hobb looked down.

He saw them. Cael watched him see them. Nine coins of light lay on the floor round him in a loose curve, and the Stone fourth-year stood among them with his broad calm face and understood, all at once, that he had been walking into something for two exchanges. He did not panic. Hobb did not have panic in him. He stopped pressing and set his feet, and Karis, a pace beyond him, joined two of her points.

A single line of heat ran along the boards between them, thin and faintly singing. It lay across Hobb's path to her like a rope laid on a floor.

Hobb looked at it. Then he did the thing nobody had told him to do, and stepped across it.

The channel answered. Cael had not known it would, and he would remember for a long time exactly what it looked like. The moment Hobb's shin broke the line, the heat in it did not simply burn. It came out of the floor at him in a single hard push, like the breath that comes out of a stove when the door is opened, and the push hit Hobb in the shins and knees and threw him back a full step, Stone or no Stone. He came down on his heels, braced, smoking faintly at the boots. The channel had gone out. It had spent itself on him, all at once, the way a trap spends itself on the foot that springs it.

Hobb stood where the push had put him and looked down at his own shins. Then he looked at the dark line on the boards where the channel had been.

"Huh," said Hobb.

The rail made a sound. Karis did not. She laid two more points while Hobb was looking at his boots, and one more as he looked up, and the exchange ended with twelve on the floor.

The fourth exchange lasted perhaps three breaths.

The instructor dropped his hand. Hobb set himself. And Karis, standing quite still with her off hand motionless at her side, joined everything she had laid. The channels lit across section two all at once with a sound like a drawn breath, faint lines of heat running from point to point to point along the boards in every direction a body might want to go. Cael counted them. He counted sixteen points, four more laid in the instant before she joined them, and he could not count the channels fast enough before he saw what they had made. The section had been an open floor. Now it was a drawing of a floor. Between all the lines there were exactly two places a person could stand without crossing one, and she was already walking toward the nearer.

The other was three paces from Hobb, across two channels.

Hobb looked at the floor for a long time. He looked at the two lines he would have to cross, and Cael watched him remember the stove door, and weigh his boots, and his shins, and how far a body travels when it is thrown back twice.

He put his hands down.

"Yield," said Hobb.

That night Cael drew it.

It took him an hour and most of a lamp's oil, sitting on the floor of his room with the binder open on the boards and Brom sitting on his bed reading over his shoulder, because Brom had seen the bout from the rail and had opinions about heat that no notebook could supply. Cael drew section two from above, as a bird would see it. He put in every point where it had gone down and numbered them in the order she had laid them, and then he drew the channels last, in a different ink, so that he could see what they had been for.

When he had finished he sat back and looked at it.

"She never fought him," he said.

"No," said Brom.

"Not once. Four exchanges, and she never tried to hit him. She spent three of them walking backward and dropping coins, and he thought he was winning, and he was standing in her sentence the whole time." Cael touched the page. "The bout was four exchanges. The fight was one. It was the first, when she chose where his feet would be three exchanges later, and he walked there for her."

He wrote it at the bottom of the diagram, because it needed writing.

*She doesn't beat people. She turns the floor into an argument, and then she wins the argument. Most of them never notice it's about the floor until it's over.*

Brom took the binder off his knees and looked at the drawing for a long, silent while, and then put one blunt finger on a channel line.

"I'll tell you what it's like to stand near," he said. "I was on the rail at the north end, two paces from the chalk, with the read open. I couldn't help it." He frowned at the page. "It's like standing near somebody who's already made up their mind. You can feel the air's decided before she has. It leans."

"That's what I felt in the quiet room. At three paces. Before her points."

"I know. I've read your page." Brom tapped the channel. "So listen to me. The joins are where you'll be tempted to get clever. Don't. She's proudest of the joins, so the joins are where she's drilled the most answers. Anybody who's ever beaten a lattice—"

"Is that what it's called?"

"That's what Edran called it at the rail. He says it's Ternhall's word." Brom shrugged. "It isn't a declaration. I asked her, after. There's no declaration called a lattice. It's her points and her channel, the two things on her card, laid in an order Ternhall teaches. Like a sentence is just words in an order." He went back to the drawing. "Anybody who's beaten one has beaten it before it was one. While she's still laying. While it's just points."

"Points," said Cael.

"Sixteen small promises she hasn't kept yet." Brom handed the binder back. "That's all a lattice is while she's building it. Promises. And I've never met anybody who reads a promise before it's kept the way you do." He lay back on Cael's bed with his hands behind his head, as if he had finished a meal. "You won't out-draw her. Nobody's out-drawn her in two years, according to Edran. You'll have to out-early her."

At the head of a clean page, in the square hand, Cael wrote *out-early her*. Under it, smaller, he wrote the entry for the night, and it was shorter than most.

*Sixteen promises she hasn't kept yet. That's Brom's. Once it's lit there's no answering it, so whatever answer there is has to come while she's still laying. All four exchanges are really the first one, drawn out long. The early part costs her nothing and makes no noise and looks like retreat, and it's the only part that matters. Watch her when she's quiet. By the time it's loud she's only collecting.*

---

He found Hobb the next morning on the defensive floor, before the second bell, standing at the edge of the chalk with his hands in his pockets, waiting for Wray.

Hobb's shins were wrapped under his training trousers; Cael could see the lump of the linen above the boot. He did not seem to be favouring them. He turned his head when Cael came along the rail and looked at him with the same patient, unhurried attention he gave everything.

"Can I ask you about yesterday?"

Hobb thought about it.

"You can ask."

"The channel. In the third. Nobody told you to cross it."

"No."

"Why did you?"

Hobb was quiet for long enough that Cael began to think he had asked the wrong thing. Then Hobb looked down at his own boots.

"Wanted to know," he said. "Four years, people tell me where to stand. Stand there, hit this hard, don't win." He considered the boots. "Nobody told me yesterday. So I wanted to know what it did."

"What did it do?"

"Shoved." Hobb took one hand out of his pocket and made a flat short push in the air, low, at shin height. "First thing. Hard. Like a door. The shove's what stops you. Stone or not." He put the hand back. "Then hot. The heat's what you remember after. But the shove's what puts you back where you started."

Cael stood very still at the rail.

"The shove first," he said. "And then the heat."

"Both. Same moment, near enough. But the shove's what stops you." Hobb looked at him with something faintly curious in his broad face. "You're going to cross one."

"I don't know."

"You're going to think about it." Hobb nodded at the far door, where Wray had just come in with her slate. "Don't, if you can help it. My shins say so." He took his hands out of his pockets and went to meet her. Halfway across the chalk he stopped and turned back. "She lays early," he said. "Before you think it's started. Watch for it."

---

He went over it all that evening on the wall, alone, with the binder shut on his knees because it was too dark to read and he did not need to read it.

The trouble was not the lattice. He had expected the lattice, in some shape, since the afternoon she had walked Edran into a drawing of a ring. The trouble was where the lattice sat.

His framework had a seam, and he had known it since Ardenmere. When the Wind-adjacent read put a strike into his head a half-beat before it arrived, it gave him the strike and nothing else. It did not tell him which way to go. He had to choose a direction before he could move in it, and in the tiny space between choosing and moving, he was nobody, neither here nor there, committed to a turn he had not finished. Against most fighters it did not matter, because most fighters gave him one strike at a time and one choice to make. Against Reydan it had mattered. Reydan had found the seam in the third exchange and leaned on it, and it had nearly been the end of everything, and the seam had been a door Reydan had to find.

Karis did not need to find it. Her lattice sat on top of it the whole time. Every point she laid was a small promise of a line that might run from it, and every line was a direction he could not go, and every step he took inside a lattice was a choice about which way to turn with a dozen small threats standing in every direction at once. Reydan had made him choose once, hard. Karis would make him choose all the time, a little, until the choosing itself was what wore him down.

The seam was a door, and against her it would be standing open on every side.

He sat with that for a long time in the cold, and then he went in and found Lira in the corridor outside her room, with her coat off and a towel round her neck from the pump.

"I need you," he said. "Every night until the sitting. I need you to be a lattice."

Lira looked at him for a long moment, with the towel in both hands. Then her face did the slow thing it did when she had been waiting to be asked for something, and was too proud to be glad about it in front of him, and was glad anyway.

"Chalk," she said. "And a sack of something to throw. I'll find the sack. You find the chalk." She went into her room and turned in the doorway. "And Cael. You'll hate it."

"I know."

"No," said Lira. "You don't. But you will."

# Chapter 34 — Pick Faster

The sack turned out to be Brom's.

Lira had gone to the kitchen and come back with a flour sack and a bag of dried peas the cook had given her for reasons nobody ever learned. Brom had taken one look at them on the stable table, and sighed, and gone to his room for his leather needle and the waxed thread he kept for boots. By the sixth bell on the first night there were a dozen small heavy bags on the table, each the size of a fist, each sewn shut along two sides with stitches so small and even that Lira picked one up and turned it over and looked at Brom with real suspicion.

"Where did you learn that?"

"Velmere," said Brom. "Boots are expensive. Feet aren't."

They took them out to the training yard at dusk, the open square of packed earth below the wall, where nobody went after the sixth bell in winter because there was nothing out there but frost and a pump. Lira drew a ring on the earth with a stick of chalk the size of her thumb, eight paces across, the size of a formal ring. Then she stood in the middle of it with the chalk in one hand and a bag in the other and explained the rules as if she were reading them off a board.

"I'm her," she said. "I'm also her floor. When I say *point*, I draw a circle." She drew one, a hand's width across, by her foot. "That's a point. It's lit. You don't stand in it. Every time I say *point*, there's one more, and the ring gets smaller."

"And the bags?"

"The bags are channels." She tossed one to Brom, who caught it without looking. "Brom throws them. Flat, along the ground, from one circle toward another, whenever he likes and without telling anybody. If a bag touches you, you've crossed a channel, and I count it out loud, and I don't feel sorry." She looked at him. "And I'm also me. I'll be pressing you the whole time. Making you turn. Because she won't just stand there laying coins and waiting for you to trip. She'll be making you choose."

"She never touched Hobb."

"She didn't need to touch Hobb. Hobb was walking forward. You won't be." Lira set her feet. "Framework. Panel speed is for panels. Go."

It was the worst hour of practice he had ever had, and it was not close.

She came at him from the first breath. She did not strike, not really, but she moved as if she would, short sharp feints off the front foot that made the framework wake and read and want to slip, and every time it slipped him she said *point* and bent and drew a circle on the earth where he had been about to go. Within a few minutes there were six. The ring that had been eight paces across was full of small chalk mouths, and every step he took had to find the space between them, and every slip the framework offered him had to be checked against the floor before he could take it.

Then the first bag came.

It came low and flat along the frozen earth from Brom's hand at the edge of the ring, skidding from one circle toward another, and Cael never saw it. He was looking at Lira. It caught him on the ankle with a soft heavy thump and fell over.

"One," said Lira.

The second caught him on the shin while he was choosing between two gaps. The third he saw, and jumped, and landed in a circle.

"Three. And that was a point, so four. You stood in her fire."

He did not argue. He set his feet again.

By the end of the hour she had drawn sixteen circles and Brom had thrown more bags than Cael could count, and eleven of them had touched him. "Eleven," said Lira. She was breathing hard too. She bent and put her hands on her knees. "That's your number. Tomorrow we beat it."

"Was that what it'll be like?"

"No," said Lira, straightening. "She's better than me at this, and she won't be throwing peas."

"She looks where she's going to lay. Her off hand goes still."

"Then you can cheat." She drew another. "Point."

Nine bags touched him on the second night.

On the third night Lira was tired. She had been on the open floor with Gerda and Hobb at the sixth bell, as she still was most evenings, and she came out to the training yard in the dark with her shoulder strapped again under her coat and her temper very short. She did not say so. She came at him harder instead, and when she saw his legs begin to go in the second half of the hour she aimed at them. She pressed him toward the edges of the ring where the circles were thickest, and called *point* at the exact moment he was too tired to take a slip at full width. She was crueler than any lattice could ever be, because a lattice could not see him getting tired, and Lira could.

Brom threw from the edge of the ring without a word, flat and unhurried. He threw the way he did everything, without any wasted motion, and every bag went exactly where he had meant it to. Twice Cael heard him say, quietly, under his breath, "Sorry," as a bag hit.

"Don't say sorry," said Lira, without turning. "She won't."

"I'm not her," said Brom. "I'm a man with a sack."

Six touched him on the third night. He stood at the end of it with his hands on his knees in the middle of a ring that had nineteen circles in it. He had not believed six was possible, and he had done it on legs that had stopped being his own somewhere around the fortieth minute.

"Six," said Lira. She was sitting on the frozen earth with her back against the pump, holding her shoulder. "That's half."

"That's nearly half."

"I'm not counting the one where you fell over. That was the ground's fault." She looked at him in the dark. "You're picking better."

"I'm picking. I don't know about better."

"You're picking." Lira got up, slowly, using the pump. "Last week you'd have stood there deciding until the bag made the decision for you. That's better. Better's just less standing there." She went to collect the bags. "Tomorrow we find out if it's real, or if it's only my shoulder making me slow."

---

He found her on the wall an hour later with the strapping off her shoulder and a cold cloth from the pump held against it under her coat, and he sat down beside her and held out the second cloth he had brought, because she had come out without one for her neck and he had seen her rub it all through the last quarter-hour.

She looked at it. Then she took it without a word and put it round the back of her neck and closed her eyes.

"You're supposed to be the one with the cloth," she said. "That's how this goes. I've a whole system."

"I know. I've read it."

"You've *lived* it." She opened one eye. "Don't make a habit of this. I don't know what I'd do with my evenings."

They sat. The yard below them was dark, with the chalk ring still faintly pale in the middle of it and the pump standing up out of the frost beside it like a man waiting for somebody. Down the hill the town's lamps were going out one at a time.

"Why are you doing it?" he said.

"Doing what?"

"This. Four nights. Your shoulder. Being a lattice." He looked at the ring. "You never asked me once whether it was worth it. Whether the fight mattered more than the other thing. You just found a sack."

Lira did not answer for a long time. She moved the cloth on her neck.

"At Fenmark," she said, "I knew the right foot for the third turn, and I said so, and they wrote me down in nineteen words and put me out." She was looking at the ring. "I've spent two years knowing things about floors that nobody let me teach. I taught you, in Ardenmere, but I didn't know I was doing it. You were just a boy in a yard I was trying to keep alive." She shifted on the stone. "This is the first time I've stood on a floor and known exactly what I was doing to somebody, and why, and that it was right. Nobody asked me to. Nobody's going to sign it or write it in a ledger. There's no panel." She almost smiled. "It's the best teaching I've ever done, and it's in a frozen yard with a sack of peas, and the only people who'll ever know are you and Brom."

"And Karis. She'll know on Thursday."

"Karis will know on Thursday." Lira took the cloth off her neck and folded it in her lap. "She'll know somebody taught you to stand on a floor that isn't there. She won't know who. That's all right." She looked at him then, in the dark. "I said in the stable once that we were a family and she wanted data. I was being clever. I meant it, but I was being clever about it." She stood up and shook out her coat. "This is what the sentence costs, when it stops being a sentence. A sack and a shoulder and four nights. I'd pay it twice."

She went in. He sat on the wall a while longer, with the cold coming up through the stone, and found that he had nothing at all to write down about it, and that this was because it had already been written somewhere better.

---

The next evening, at the sixth bell, before the fourth night's chalk, he went to Brom's room. He had told Lira at breakfast that he would, and she had made him promise in the eleventh week to tell her before and not after.

She was already there. She was sitting on Brom's bed with her back against the wall and her boots on Brom's blanket and a wet cloth from the kitchen pump folded in her lap. She looked at him as he came in, the way she had looked at him on the stair the morning after the fast catch.

"Tell me what it is," she said. "Before."

He knelt on the boards and set his left forearm across his chest, braced, as he did for everything in this room.

"Hobb said something," he said. "About crossing her channel. He said it shoves first. Hard, like a stove door. And then it's hot. The shove's what puts you back where you started, and the heat's what you remember."

Lira went very still on the bed.

"I've never been able to do anything with a strike except catch it and send it home," Cael went on. "And sending it home costs. You saw what a quarter fast cost." He did not look at her. "But there's the drop. I gather it and let it go and give it no road. I've done it four times, slowly, on a push. It never cost anything I could find. I've never tried it fast."

"You want to know if you can drop a strike," said Brom slowly.

"I want to know if I can drop a shove." Cael made himself say the rest. "If I had to go through something that pushed. So that it didn't put me back where I started."

Nobody said anything for a while. A cart went along the street worth walking far below the window.

"It won't take the heat," said Lira. Her voice was very level. "Will it? Nothing you have takes heat."

"No. Nothing I have takes heat."

"So if you ever did it, in a ring, you'd be choosing a burn."

"If I ever did it. Yes." He looked up at her then. "That's why I'm telling you before. I'm choosing the size first. A quarter. Fast. Woken before Brom moves, the way the catch worked, and then let go instead of sent. If it costs what the catch cost, I stop, and I never think about it again."

Lira looked at him for a long moment over the wet cloth.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll know I could. That's all. Knowing isn't doing."

"No," said Lira. "It isn't." She held up the cloth. "Go on, then. I'm here."

Brom brought up the Iron Skin along his right palm, the faint dull sheen coming over the skin, and drew his hand back. He looked at Cael. Cael woke the fragment.

He did it as he had learned to on the night of the fast catch, before anything moved. The gathering began low in his chest and he held it there half-ready, like a breath drawn and not let out, with the road laid open in front of it. He held it, and held it, and Brom struck.

The strike came into the bone and the fragment closed on it in the same instant, a quarter of it, and there was the road, wide open, back along the line it had come in. Every part of him wanted to send it home. He did not. He let it go. He opened his hands on it, inside, the way you open your fingers on something hot, and the gathered quarter spilled out of him and went nowhere at all.

He waited for the bill.

It came, but it was small. A tightness across the breastbone, like a strap pulled one notch, for the length of one breath. Then it eased. His knees were under him. His hand was his own. He could feel the floor.

"Well?" said Lira. She had the cloth half-raised.

"One breath," said Cael. "And a strap. That's all."

Brom looked at his own palm. "I didn't feel anything come back."

"Nothing did. I dropped it." Cael looked at his forearm, where the strike had landed. It would be a bruise. Nothing else. "It's the sending. The catch doesn't cost much. Sending it home costs. Letting it go almost doesn't."

He did it twice more, because one was luck and two was the floor, as Wray had told Brom. The second was the same: one breath, the strap. The third he woke too late, and the strike landed before he had the road open, and the fragment found nothing to close on and he took it the ordinary way. Nothing went into a joint.

"Two of three," said Brom.

Lira put the cloth down on the blanket. She had not used it.

"Write it," she said. "All of it. The part about choosing a burn as well."

He wrote it kneeling on Brom's floor, with the binder on the boards.

*Compression-adjacent. Brom's room, L. present, told before. The drop, fast: a quarter of a quick palm strike caught and let go, unsent, with the fragment woken before the strike. Two of three. Cost: one breath, a tightness across the breastbone. The cost lives in the sending, not the catching. It takes the shove. It does not take heat. Nothing I have takes heat. If I ever used it to cross something hot, the burn would be a thing I chose.*

He read it back, and Lira read it upside down from the bed, and neither of them said anything more about it that night.

---

On the fourth night the number stopped.

He had thought it would go on falling. Eleven, nine, six; the arithmetic seemed to promise a night when no bag touched him at all. Lira's shoulder was rested, and she was fast again, and Brom threw as he always threw. Cael watched the floor and read Lira out of the side of his eye and picked and picked and picked, faster than the night before, and four bags touched him.

The next session, after a rest, four again.

He knew why before Lira said it. He could feel it in his own legs, in the instant before each slip. The framework read the threat and offered him a way out of it, as it always had. Then, in the half-beat while he was still taking it, something in him checked the floor, and the floor said *not that way*. He stopped. He began a second way, and the second way was always a hair too late, and a bag that would have missed him found him.

They sat down in the dirt of the training yard together at the end of it, because neither of them had the legs left to stand. The last light had gone out of the sky. The pump dripped. Brom was walking slowly round the ring picking up bags and putting them back in the flour sack.

"You're still deciding," said Lira, "whether to trust the slip or throw it away. And you're doing it in the same half-beat you're meant to be doing one or the other. You can't do both in there. There isn't room."

"I know."

"So pick faster." She said it flatly, to the frozen earth between her boots. "It doesn't matter which, at first. Pick the wrong one fast and you'll get further than the right one slow. Speed the decision. The correctness comes later, once your feet have learned the floor."

"That isn't how Vell taught it."

"Vell taught you one opponent at a time. One rhythm, one body, one strike to read. You got very good at it." Lira leaned back on her hands. "This isn't one rhythm. It's twenty, all coming due at once, and she's the only one who knows the order. You can't read twenty rhythms the way you read one. You've got to stop asking whether it's right and just go, and trust the floor to have been read already."

He thought about it, sitting in the dirt.

"Watch the quiet part," he said. "Brom said. Everything she does early is where the fight is."

"Brom's right. Brom's always right about the floor." Lira got up, slowly, and held out a hand. He did not need it, and he took it anyway. "You'll get there. You always do, in the end. I've just never once seen you get there against something with this many pieces before you've even—"

She stopped.

She stood with his hand still in hers, in the dark, with her mouth a little open on the next word, and did not say it. Brom, at the far side of the ring, had stopped with a bag in his hand. Nobody moved for a moment.

"Before you've even had a chance to learn it properly," said Lira, and let go of his hand, and went to help Brom with the sack.

Nobody had said the word all week. Not on the wall, or at the scarred table, or in Brom's room with the wet cloth folded on the blanket. Not once in four nights of chalk and peas and Lira's voice saying *point* in the dark. It sat at every meal anyway, like a fifth chair pulled up to the table that nobody looked at. Lira had nearly sat in it just now and had caught herself, and the three of them walked back across the frozen yard to the residence wing in a silence that had the word in the very middle of it.

He wrote that night, at the desk by the window, with his legs aching so badly he could feel his pulse in his calves.

*Lira's lattice. Eleven, nine, six, four, four. Stopped at four. I'm still deciding in the half-beat where I should be moving. Lira: pick faster. Speed the decision. The correctness comes later.*

He looked at that for a long time. Then he wrote one more line, small, at the bottom.

*Nobody has said it all week. I haven't either. I don't think I've let myself think it.*

# Chapter 35 — Fight Me

Quenna posted the conditions two days out, on the Tuesday of the sixteenth week, in her own hand and not the clerk's.

*Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track. Fifth sitting. Supervised training match, formal conditions, under standings rules. Three exchanges on the instructor's count. The presiding assessor may halt the match at any moment, at the faculty's discretion, without stating cause. Panel of three: the presiding assessor; the instructor's seat, Wray; the rotating faculty seat by rota. The outcome will be logged as a demonstration assessment under the provision. Opponent: Karis, Ember Path, Iron Rank Three, by her written consent. Formal yard, the fifth bell, Thursday.*

The rota, Cael saw at the bottom of the sheet, had given them the heat-Paths instructor with the scorched cuffs.

He read it twice in front of the board with students going past behind him toward the second bell. Then he read it a third time, slowly, because Quenna had written it and he had learned that nothing Quenna wrote did only one thing.

*The presiding assessor may halt the match at any moment, at the faculty's discretion, without stating cause.* That was for Karis. It meant that if anything happened on that floor that Quenna did not like, she could stop it without having to say what she had seen, and nobody could ask her afterward why. *Logged as a demonstration assessment under the provision* was for him. It meant that whatever happened in the formal yard on Thursday, it would go into his record in the same column as four quiet mornings with Hobb, and be counted by anybody hostile who ever read it as one more sitting. It did not become a standings bout. It did not become anything new. It fed the category, as everything he did at Greyvane had to feed the category, or the category would starve.

The panel would see the observer tested against Iron-tier at full intensity. The four of them would see something else. The sheet on the board would satisfy both and tell neither everything, and Quenna had signed it, and every word on it was true.

"She's very good at this," said Lira, at his elbow. She had come up without his hearing, with a stolen apple. She read the sheet once, fast, the way she read anything she had already guessed. "*Without stating cause.* That's the best three words on it."

"Why?"

"Because it means she's thought about stopping it." Lira bit the apple. "People who haven't thought about stopping something don't write down that they won't have to explain why."

The Tuesday session in section four was the last before the match, and all three of them knew it, and nobody said so.

Quenna stood at the chalk with her slate. Karis sat behind her line on the borrowed stool with her two pens laid side by side. Brom leaned on the far side of the post. It was exactly as it had been on twenty Tuesdays and Thursdays before, and Cael found that he was grateful to it for being exactly the same.

He ran the framework, and Karis timed his recovery between passes.

"Three counts," she said. "Back to where it was before the drills." She wrote, and turned the page round so that he could read it, as the clause said. *Recovery three counts. Unchanged from the sessions before the sixteenth week. Cause of the earlier slowing stated by subject: Lira. Cause of the recovery: not stated, presumed the same.* She looked at the page and then at him. "That's the last thing I'll write about you before Thursday. I've decided."

"Why?"

"Because anything I write after this I'd be writing about my own match." She capped her pen. "I don't want to have a page in my notebook that I wrote knowing what I'd use it for. It would be a different kind of page."

At the chalk, Quenna did not look up from her slate.

They did the post work. The felt was on, and Brom leaned and shifted on the far side, and the read gave him four in twenty, as it had the week before. When the hour was done Quenna signed the strip and held it out to him, and this time she did not hold on to it.

"Thursday," she said. "The fifth bell."

"Yes."

"Come rested. You know that." She tucked the slate under her arm. Then she looked at Karis, folding her stool, and at Cael, and at Brom, who had come round the post and was standing with his hands behind his back. "Whatever either of you has been doing this month," said Quenna, "and I don't want to know, I'd like you both to walk out of the yard on Thursday on your own feet. That isn't in the conditions. It's mine." She went to the door. "That's all."

"She's frightened," said Karis.

"Quenna?"

"A little. It's in her shoulders. She holds them like Lira does before a bout." Karis tucked the stool more firmly under her arm. "I find I don't mind that somebody is."

He tapered the last three days, as Vell had taught him to taper before the big bouts in Ardenmere, a lifetime ago in a cold city.

Shorter sessions. Earlier nights. No drills with Lira after the fourth night; she had said so herself, flatly, at breakfast. "You've got four in your legs. You'll keep four. You'll lose four if you go out there tired." No work in Brom's room at all. The binder work shifted from gathering to holding. On the Tuesday night he redrew every chart on Karis from memory, without the binder open, on clean paper. Then he checked the new drawings against the old ones, line by line, and found that they matched, and burned the clean sheets in Brom's grate because they were nobody's business.

Her off hand, a quarter of a second, a hair more by the end of an hour. The half-breath through the nose before every sustain. The release that hung a third of a breath when she was fresh and longer as she tired. The rule about cold air. The four words: lay, hold, join, let go. Sixteen, the most she had ever held. A lattice is only frightening once it's a lattice. He knew all of it past memory now. It lived in him where Lira's footwork lived, in the place below thinking.

On the Wednesday afternoon he stood at the rail of section two and watched her train alone.

She had no partner that day and had not asked for one. She ran her point-work in long patient sequences on the instructor's count, lay and hold and join and let go, over and over, the coins of light blooming on the boards and dying on her word. There was nothing new in any of it. There was nothing she had not shown him across six weeks of looking. She was not practising, he understood after a while. She was doing what he had done with the charts the night before. She was checking that it was all still where she had put it.

Halfway through the block she looked up and caught him watching.

Neither of them waved. Neither of them nodded. They had been watching each other for six weeks across a whitewashed room and a formal yard and the rail of this section, and it was the only language they had ever really spoken, and it did not need anything added to it. She looked at him for the length of one held point. Then she let the point go, and its third of a breath of shimmer hung in the cold air between them, and she went back to her count.

Six weeks. He knew her ignition to a quarter of a second. She knew his recovery to a count. Tomorrow both notebooks would be set against each other at full intensity, on a public floor, in front of a panel, and whatever happened in that yard, nobody had ever written it down before.

But I do know how you teach somebody to quit watching themselves long enough to fight. Ternhall taught me that much. They'd be very surprised to hear what I was using it for."

"And if nothing happens? If tomorrow's the same as the quiet room?"

"Then I'll write it down on Friday morning with exactly as much care as I'd write anything else. A null in a formal yard, in front of a panel, at full intensity. Nobody's ever recorded one." She lifted one shoulder. "It would close a door. I like knowing where the doors are."

She stood. She had brought nothing, so there was nothing to gather, and she stood for a moment with her hands at her sides as if she had expected them to be holding something.

"There's one more thing," she said. "It's fair you know it, so I'm telling you." She looked down the long room toward the dark stacks. "I'm going to try to win. Tomorrow. Properly. Not a demonstration. Not a public draft. I want you to know that before you walk out there, so that you don't ever think afterward that I held anything back to help with anything." She looked back at him. "If I lose, I want to have lost to you. If I win, I want to have beaten you. Not a boy who was being careful of me."

"I wasn't planning to be careful of you."

"Good," said Karis. "Then we'll both have been told."

She went to the door. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame, and turned back.

"I've watched you for six weeks," she said. "You catalogue everything except yourself. Tomorrow, for three exchanges, don't catalogue. Just fight."

She went out before he could ask her what she meant by the last part. He suspected she had meant him not to ask. It was a sentence built to be carried into sleep unfinished, so that it would still be sitting there in the morning when the panel called his name.

---

After the door had shut he stayed where he was for a long while. The notebook lay shut on the table in front of him, and for once he left it that way.

The reading room ticked and settled round him the way old buildings do at night, the stone of the waystation letting the day's small warmth go out of it a little at a time. Prynn's lamp at the high desk had been put out. Only his own burned, low, at the end of the long table, and the shelves went back from its light into a dark that held more of other people's recorded lives than he could have read in ten years of evenings.

Six weeks sat behind him, as complete as they were ever going to be. The charts, the tells, the four words, sixteen, the cold air. Lira's chalk, four touched, stopped at four. The drop, two of three, one breath and a strap, and a sentence in the binder about choosing a burn. And under all of it, in the binder's back pages, the other sentence, the one Karis did not have and Lira did: *It takes what's used on me.*

Tomorrow everything that sentence needed would be in one yard, at one time, for the first time in his life with his eyes open. And two people who loved him, and one who had come across a whole country to stand in front of him, had told him in their different words to forget it was there.

He did not know if he could. He did not know if anybody could carry a sentence like that into a ring and set it down at the chalk. He thought about Reydan, in the third exchange of the bout that had ended everything in Ardenmere. He had not been thinking about anything then. He had been losing, and fighting, and losing, and the notice had come at the very last moment it could have come and still been any use to him, and he had not been watching himself at all.

Perhaps that was all it was. Perhaps that was all it had ever been.

He found, turning it over one last time in the low light, that he was not afraid of tomorrow in the way he had been afraid of the quiet room. In the quiet room he had been afraid of nothing happening. Tomorrow he was afraid of losing. That was a cleaner fear. He knew what to do with it. He had been carrying it into rings since he was fourteen.

For the first time since Ardenmere he was going to walk into a match without knowing which version of himself would walk out of it.

He put out the lamp and went up to bed, and slept, which surprised him most of all.

He had nowhere left to go that did not cost him something he could not pay twice. Out of angles. The fight he had come in to fight was over, and he had lost it, and he was standing in the last half-breath of it with his arm beating and nothing left to spend.

Time did what it does in the deepest part of a fight. It went wide and slow. Every fraction of a second was suddenly a room he could stand up in and look round.

He had spent six weeks learning her. Not writing her down, though he had done that too, on more pages than he could count. He knew the shape of her points and the rhythm of her joins and the exact gap between one commitment and the next. He knew the stillness in her off hand, and he could feel it now, through the read, gathering a tenth of a second ahead of the fourth channel's last point. He knew her the way he knew his own handwriting. Until this half-breath he had never once had cause to reach for it.

So he stopped running.

It was not a decision the way decisions usually came to him, weighed and turned over and written down afterward in the square hand. He was awake for it. That was the difference, the whole difference, the way being awake is the whole difference between falling and diving. Something in him that had stood in the quiet room twenty-two times, reaching and watching itself reach, was not watching anything now. There was nothing left over to watch with. There was only Karis, trying with everything she had, and a point about to bloom a pace in front of his foot, and him.

He reached for it.

Not with his hand. He reached into the shape of what she was making, into the point gathering on the earth in front of him the way all her points gathered, small and controlled and certain and entirely hers, and he took hold of it.

He would spend pages afterward trying to say what it was like, and never get it right. It was not like taking. It was not like borrowing, or catching, or any of the words he had tried on since the first notice came. "Did she." Lira almost smiled. "Then we worked it out separately, the two of us. Without clauses. I think that's the first time she's ever arrived anywhere without writing it down first."

"All of it."

She went in. He stayed on the wall a while longer, with his arm against his chest, and found that he was not afraid of anything that night. He was only tired, all the way through, the way a field is tired after harvest.

# Chapter 38 — Inside

Karis woke on the Friday with warm hands, and the first thing she did, before she had sat up or looked at the window, was check whether she was still all there.

She held her left hand out over the blanket, palm up, a hand's width from her face. Her off hand, the right, went still at her side. She counted it without meaning to, the way she had counted it since she was fourteen: a quarter of a second, near enough, a hair less than at the end of yesterday. Then a point bloomed over her palm, small and dim and steady, a coin of light the size of a thumbnail, and its warmth reached her skin.

It was exactly as it had always been.

She held it to a count of twenty. She let it go, and watched its third of a breath of shimmer hang over the blanket and fade. Then she laid another, and joined the two along the bedpost with a thin singing line, and let that go too, and lay back on the pillow and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

Nothing was missing. Nothing in her had been taken away.

She had known that, really, since the yard. She had felt nothing leave her in the third exchange; she had felt something answer her, which was not the same. But she had wanted to check it in the morning, cold, with her own hands, the way she checked any result that mattered to her before she let herself believe it. Whatever he had done, it was not theft. Her Path was exactly the size it had been on Wednesday. It had not been divided. It had been, she thought, carefully, trying the word in her head and not yet trusting it, *copied*. No. That was not right either. She did not have the word yet.

She got up and washed and dressed and sat down at the little table by her window with both notebooks, because that was what she did with mornings.

---

The first notebook was easy.

She wrote the match into it as she would have written any bout. The conditions, the panel, the three exchanges, her point counts in each, the time she held them through the resets, the sustain cost that had come due afterward in her fingertips and gone by morning. She wrote that the candidate had read her near points before they lit, and that she had moved her floor outward in the second exchange to answer it. She wrote that he had crossed a channel, the shortest one, and that she had not believed he would. She wrote that her fourth channel's last point had failed to take, because the place she had laid it was already burning, and that the assembly had lost its sequence and gone out.

Then she stopped, with her pen over the page, at the next line.

She did not write *by what*. Clause five of the first terms forbade it, and clause two of the second, in Brom's square capitals, forbade it twice over. She had agreed to both. She had written one of them herself. She would not have written it anyway. There were things she did not put on paper because a rule said she must not, and there were things she did not put on paper because she would not, and since yesterday this was both.

She closed the first notebook.

The ledger was harder. It was the ledger of the nulls, twenty-two sessions in her small upright hand with the ruled line under them and the sentence about the missing condition. It recorded conditions and results and nothing else. A match was not a session. It had not been run in the quiet room, or under her consent spoken aloud, or by any rule she had written. But it had been the twenty-third time she had stood in front of him while he watched, and she found that she could not leave the ledger without it.

So she wrote it under the barley clause, under everything.

*Supplementary. Not a session. Formal yard, fifth sitting, full intensity, under the presiding assessor's conditions. Source: K., Ember Path, Iron Rank Three. Consent: written, filed. Distance: from eight paces to one. Duration: three exchanges. Result, as reported by the subject: a fragment notice. Ember-adjacent. The field reads: acquisition, directed.*

She looked at it for a long time. It was the first entry in the ledger that did not end in *null*. She found that her hand did not want to write anything under it, and she let her hand have its way, and closed the ledger.

Then she opened the second notebook, the one that held everything the standard model got wrong. She turned to a clean page, and wrote the date at the top, and did not write anything else for an hour.

Quenna stopped her in the yard at the second bell, on her way back from the pump with a cup of water she did not want and had gone to fetch only so that her legs would have something to do.

She did not stop her so much as arrive beside her, in her grey coat with her folder under one arm, and walk with her for a few steps across the frost as if they had always meant to be going the same way. Karis did not slow down. Quenna did not ask her to.

"I'm not going to ask you anything," said Quenna.

"I know you're not."

"I did."

"No." Quenna's voice did not change at all. "I've spent twenty years learning which questions to ask. I've spent nearly as long learning which ones not to." They had reached the door. Eat something today. I don't care what."

She had meant to, because Quenna had told her to eat. She simply found, when the fourth bell rang, that she was still at the table by the window with the second notebook open to a page that held one date and nothing else. She could not imagine sitting at the scarred table with the three of them and saying anything at all about the weather.

At the fifth bell she went to the quiet room, because it was the hour her slate always claimed, and because it was the one place in the academy where nobody would ask her anything. The slate on the nail by the narrow door still said *K. — declaration practice*. It was still true.

She stood at the end of the whitewashed room where she had stood twenty-two times, and laid points.

Single points. Pairs. A channel along the end wall. A sustain to a count of sixty. She did not do it to learn anything. She did it the way some people walk, to keep their bodies busy while their minds got on with something else. And somewhere in the second quarter-hour, with nobody at the far end of the room, she laid a point a fraction early.

She knew why. She had always known why.

For three years at Ternhall there had been a second set of points across the paired floor from hers. They had always been a breath behind or a breath ahead, so that every ignition she laid was half of a conversation and the other half was always coming. She had learned to lay early into that, the way a person speaks a fraction early into a pause where somebody has always replied. She had come to Greyvane and laid her points on a lean old man's count into nothing at all, and found herself still doing it. She corrected it every time. She never wrote the correction down. She had decided long ago there was a small amount of data she would rather carry than file.

She stood in the quiet room with her off hand very still and let herself think about the third exchange.

She had been trying. That was the thing he had read aloud from the back of his binder by a shaded lamp, and it was true, and she had known it was true before he had finished the first sentence. She had been trying with everything she had, for the first time since the paired floor. She had come out of her mark mid-argument and laid sixteen and held them through two resets and never let one go. She had spent more of herself in three exchanges than in any bout of her life, because she had wanted very badly to win, and she had wanted to win against him.

And in the third exchange, at the very end, when she had been certain, when the fourth point was going down and the whole drawing was about to light, something had answered her.

It had answered in her own language. It was not a channel of somebody else's fire, and not a counter, and not anything she had a column for. It was a point. One small, rough, crooked point, laid exactly where hers was going, a breath before hers, so that hers could not take. It was the oldest rule there was, the first rule they taught an Ember child, *nothing can set fire where a fire already is*. She had shown him that rule herself, on the sixth block, for symmetry. He had said it back to her in the middle of her own sentence, in her own words, with a hand on the frozen ground.

She had not been hurt. She had not been diminished. She had been *answered*.

She stood very still in the whitewashed room. She had expected, through the whole of the long cold night, to feel something she would have to put a name to. She had expected to feel taken from. Robbed, or violated, or at the least strange, with some part of the thing she had drilled since she was eleven now lying inside somebody else, for good, because she had signed a sheet and the sheet had worked.

She did not feel any of that. She had gone looking for it, the way she went looking for a fault in a timing table, carefully and on purpose, and it was not there.

What she felt instead was the thing she had not felt since she had walked out of Ternhall's gate nine days before its term. Across a floor, for the length of one breath, she had not been alone in it.

She let the last point go and sat down on the scrubbed floor with her back against the whitewash and her hands in her lap, as she had sat after the long session in the tenth week, and cried. When it was over she wiped her face on her cuff, and noted, because she noted everything about herself, that she felt considerably better, and that she did not know whether that was a finding.

Beside it was a small stone pot with a lid, still faintly warm to the touch, and a spoon laid across the top. She lifted the lid. Barley.

She knew whose they were. Lira stole bread from everyone in the building and gave most of it away again, and she had never once in Karis's sight given anything to anybody in a way that could be thanked for. Brom did not give barley to people. Brom defended barley to people, at length, with real feeling, and ate it in front of them. A pot of it outside somebody's door was not a gift so much as a statement of position.

Neither of them had knocked. Neither of them had left a note.

Karis carried both inside and sat down at the table by the window and ate all of the bread and most of the barley. Then, because she was who she was, she opened the first notebook at the back and wrote, very small, under the last page of timings: *Friday. Bread at the door (L.). Barley at the door (B.). No knock. No note.* She looked at it, and could not think of anything to write under it that would be true and would also be data, and closed the notebook.

Her window looked down across the corner of the training yard to the low wall at the end of the residence wing. Late in the evening, when she got up to draw the curtain, there were two shapes sitting on it in the dark, close together, with their knees drawn up. One of them had a coat pulled round her in a way Karis would have known at any distance. The other sat with one arm held against his chest.

She stood at the window with the curtain in her hand and watched them.

They were not doing anything. They were not talking, as far as she could see; neither of them moved for a long time. She had watched the three of them on that wall from this window on other nights, and she had always noted it the way she noted the hour of the bells, as a fact about the academy. Tonight she found that she did not want to note it. She wanted, quite simply and without any method at all, to be down there on the cold stone with them, not saying anything.

That was new. She examined it, standing at the window, as carefully as she had examined her own hands that morning.

At Ternhall she had never once wanted to be anywhere she had not already earned. She had been first, and first had a place, and she had sat in it. She had not known until this moment, at a window in a minor academy in the cold, that a person could want a place she had not earned and could not rank for. Nor had she known that the wanting could feel so plain, like being hungry.

One of the shapes on the wall stood up, and shook out her coat, and went in. The other stayed. Karis watched him sit there alone with his arm against his chest until the cold must have come right through him. Then she drew the curtain, because watching him any longer without his knowing was a thing she had promised herself on a coach in the autumn that she would never do, and she meant to keep every one of those promises, including the ones nobody else knew she had made.

She did not sleep much. She did not mind.

She wrote it the first time at dawn, at the table by the window. It came out as a report, which was the only shape she knew how to make a thing come out. *Session subject reports no distress. The finding is anomalous and the source is unable to account for it.* It went on like that for half a page. She tore the page out, carefully, along the fold, and set it aside.

She wrote it the second time at the third bell and it was the other way, all feeling and no shape. She could not bear to read it back past the second sentence. She tore that out too.

The third time she tried to say only what was true and nothing more. She went slowly. Twice she stopped and went to the window and looked at the yard and came back. Once she wrote a sentence and then scraped it off the paper with her penknife, very carefully, because she did not want a crossing-out on this page, and the ghost of it stayed in the grain of the paper anyway. When she had finished she read it four times, because she did not trust herself to have read it right the first three.

This much I can say plainly. At no point did I feel examined. Since the third exchange I have not once felt like the thing being tested. At the very end of it something answered me, in my own language, and for that moment I was not alone in it. I have not been alone in it since. I do not know yet what that makes me. I would rather find out standing with the three of you than standing outside, if you would ever have me.*

One paragraph, no clauses, no way out written into it anywhere, asking for the one thing no clause could ever get anybody. She had written the least defended sentence of her life in the same hand she used for boundary conditions. Then she had folded it and carried it two days against her ribs. He thought of Lira on a rooftop in Ardenmere, a long time ago, and of how every time something in his life had changed for good, it had been because someone had decided to hand someone else the unguarded copy.

"I know," said Karis. "That's why I wrote it that way."

She stood. She did not hold out her hand for the page. She left it with him, and that was its own message. She was not going to take it away and improve it. It was done. It was what she had found.

She read it a third time in the light from the residence wing's one lit window, slowly, the way she had read the consent across the scarred table. Then she sat with it in both hands on her knees and looked out at the black yard for a long time.

"Well," she said, half to herself. "There it is, then."

It was not refusal. It was not quite welcome either, not yet. It was the sound of a line moving, an inch, the way lines moved when they moved for real reasons and not because somebody had declared that they had.

"She left you bread," said Cael. "Didn't she. On the Friday."

"Somebody left her bread." Lira did not look at him. "I wouldn't know who. People are always leaving bread about in this building." She folded the page once along its softest crease. "Two days. She did it in two days. It took me two years to read four paragraphs."

"It took you a morning."

"It took me two years to get to the morning." She held the page a moment longer. "Where's Brom?"

Brom was in his room with his boots off and his feet up on the sill and Wray's book open on his chest. He read the page standing, when Cael held it out. Halfway down he nodded, and at the bottom he nodded again. He gave it back, and delivered a verdict Cael suspected he had settled on weeks ago.

"Took her less time than I thought," said Brom.

"How long did you think?"

"Till spring." He sat down again and put his feet back on the sill. "I had barley on spring. With nobody, but I had it."

Lira went down the corridor by herself after that, to Karis's door. Cael did not follow her. He heard her knock, two knuckles, quiet, and heard the door open, and heard nothing after that for a long while, because Lira's voice when she meant something went low enough to stay in a doorway. Then the door shut, and Lira came back up the corridor alone, with her hands in her coat pockets and her face doing nothing at all.

"What did you say?"

"I told her she could stop asking properly." Lira went past him to her own door. "She's in. She can be as rude as the rest of us now. I said she'd need practice." At the door she turned. "And I told her about the binder."

He looked at her.

"That it's still yours," said Lira. "That being in doesn't change that, and nobody's going to pretend it does." She opened her door. "She said she knew. She said she promised in the stable never to ask, and she meant it the day she said it, and she means it now." Lira shook her head. "She's going to be very tiring, Cael. I can tell already."

She went in. He stood in the corridor with the soft-cornered page in his hand, and then he went and put it in the back pocket of the binder, with the papers that proved things. It did not prove anything that would ever stand up in front of a panel. He put it there anyway, behind Karis's consent, because the two of them belonged together, and because the back pocket was where he kept the things he meant to have read again when it mattered.

In the morning there were four bowls at the end of the scarred table, and Karis was sitting at the fourth, and nobody said anything about it at all.

# Chapter 39 — Three Clean Days

He sat up late that Saturday, after Lira's door had shut and the corridor had gone quiet, with the binder open on the desk by the narrow window and the lamp turned low.

Three people had spent the last two days telling him what the match had cost, each in their own language.

Quenna's language was the record's. A record that would stand only as long as nobody unfriendly ever saw him do it again, and a question he had no answer for. *Whether you can not do it.* Karis's language was the odder of the two, and the gentler. She had gone hunting in herself for two days for a cost, as carefully as she hunted for a fault in a table, and had not found one. In its place she had found something she wanted more of, and that was its own kind of dizziness, because it meant whatever was in him did not only take. It joined, in both directions, in ways none of them had a word for yet. And his own language was the oldest he had, the binder's. Two misfires, dated and timed. The thing in him handing him a bill in half-seconds of nothing, for something he had done without ever being shown the price.

He made himself write it out in columns. Columns kept a frightened mind honest better than sentences did. Sentences let you lean.

*Known. A directed acquisition is possible. Once. Under six weeks of close watching, and then a match where she was trying with everything she had, against me, and I had nothing left.*

*Known. It costs the others. About a day. Two misfires in the Wind, this time, and then clean.*

*Known. The panel saw an effect and not how. The record is drafted. It will hold unless I give somebody a reason to open it again.*

*Unknown. Whether I can not do it, if the same things ever line up again without my wanting them to. In a real fight. Against somebody who means it. In front of exactly the people Quenna is afraid of.*

*Unknown. Whether the cost grows, and with what. Whether some later one asks for something the older four can't pay.*

He stopped. He looked at the last column a long time before he wrote in it, and when he did, he wrote slowly.

*Unknown. What it is for. Whether it's for anything.*

He drew a small box round that line. It was his own private mark, and it meant *not yet*. He had drawn it round the session-nine page in the back of the binder, and round the line he had copied out of Vell's archive in a hand that was not his, and round every door he had ever found before he found the key to it. He sat back and looked at the little box. It looked very small for what was in it.

Then he took up the pen again and wrote the sentence that had been gathering in him since the frozen ground of the formal yard, because plain was the only way he knew to hold something this size.

*I need to understand what I'm actually doing before it gets someone killed.*

He read it over. Nothing he had put in the binder since the week of Karis's hypothesis had been as true, and it was only half a sentence. He could feel the rest of it waiting. He sat with the pen in his hand until he could make himself be exact about it instead of brave.

*It isn't me I'm afraid for. I can carry my own bill; I've decided that. It's the three people who keep standing close enough to be in the room when I find out what the next one costs.*

---

Brom came in a while past the hour when the corridor went silent, without knocking, and sat down on the floor across from the desk with his back against the wall and his legs out in front of him.

He did not say anything for a long time. He sat as he used to sit in the Ardenmere market square, saying nothing because nothing needed saying yet, the arbiter of interesting problems arrived at his post and in no hurry at all. The lamp burned down a knuckle's width. Somewhere below, the old building ticked and settled.

When he spoke at last it was not about the misfires, or Quenna's question, or the soft-cornered page in the back pocket of the binder.

"Back in Ardenmere," said Brom, "I told you one day you'd get to pick the word for what you are. Instead of having it picked for you." He tipped his head toward the binder, where the ink had not yet dried. "You just started choosing."

Cael looked at what he had written.

It did not feel like winning anything. It felt like the first honest weight he had ever put a real number on. It was lighter, somehow, for being honest, than a comfortable vagueness would have been. On Thursday he had learned that he could reach. Since then he had learned what reaching cost, and on whom the cost could fall, and that the slow dated unglamorous counting of it was the only way of carrying this that he could live inside. Anybody could take. The choosing was in the ledger.

"It's a heavier word than I expected," he said.

"Most of the good ones are," said Brom. He did not move. He stayed where he was on the floor until the lamp had burned down so far that there was nothing left either of them needed to say.

---

On the Sunday morning, before the first bell, Karis came out to the training yard with them.

She came out with her coat collar up and her hands in her pockets and no notebook, and stood at the edge of the frost a little way off from the pump while Lira and Cael squared up. She did not say anything until Lira looked at her.

"May I count?" said Karis. "It isn't a session. There aren't any terms for this one. I'd only like to know the number."

"You're in," said Lira. "You don't have to ask."

"I know I don't." Karis did not move. "I'd like to anyway. For a while. Until I've got the hang of not."

Lira looked at her for a long moment, and then something in her face gave, very slightly, and she nodded at the frozen earth beside the pump.

"Stand there, then. You'll see his feet."

They ran the old patterns at half speed. Lira came at him with the sequences she had taught him before either of them had known what he was learning, slip and turn and step, and he called on the framework for every one of them. Every one of them came. It was there before he had finished turning toward it, quick and light and his, as it had been every morning of his life before the formal yard. The left turn where it had failed on Friday came clean. The third sequence came clean. He ran forty and every one of them answered.

At the end Karis said, from beside the pump, "A quarter of a breath. The lead. It's back to where it was before the eleventh week."

"Is that good?" said Lira.

"It's what it was," said Karis. "I'd call that good. I'd write it down as unchanged."

"Then write it down as unchanged." Lira picked up the rope from the pump's nail and coiled it. "That's two. Saturday was clean and today's clean. One more."

"Saturday wasn't counted," said Cael. "You didn't say."

"I counted it. I didn't say it. I'm saying it now." Lira hung the rope back on its nail. "Two."

---

At breakfast there were four bowls again, and this time somebody said something about it, and it was Karis.

"I've been thinking," she said, to her porridge, "about how this is supposed to work."

Lira looked up from the loaf she was tearing. "How what's supposed to work?"

"This. Being in." Karis turned her spoon over. "At Ternhall there were rules for everything. Where the first rank sat in the refectory. Who spoke first at a paired drill. Whether you were allowed to borrow somebody's notes without asking in writing." She looked round the three of them. "I've been watching the three of you since the autumn and I can't find any rules at all. I've looked."

"There aren't any," said Brom.

"There must be some."

"There's one," said Lira. She tore the loaf into four, not three, and pushed the pieces round the table like a dealer laying out cards, and the largest piece went to Karis. "I take everybody's bread. That's the rule. Everybody's. Yours too, from tomorrow."

Karis looked at the largest piece.

"You've given me the most."

"That's the other half of the rule," said Lira. "I take it, and then I give most of it back. Nobody knows why. Don't ask."

Karis considered this with the whole of her attention, the way she considered a timing that did not agree with anything she had written before. Then she picked up the bread, and broke it in half, and held out the smaller piece across the table to Lira.

"Then I'll start practising," she said.

Lira stared at the bread. Then she laughed, really laughed, the sound that came up out of her only a few times a term. She took the piece and ate it.

"She's learning," said Brom.

"She's very quick," said Lira, with her mouth full. "I'd forgotten that about her. Watch your bowl, Brom."

Brom moved his bowl a hand's width further from Karis with great dignity, and Karis watched him do it with an expression that was trying very hard not to be delight and failing.

Cael ate his porridge and did not say anything at all. He was watching Karis, because he could not help watching, and so he saw her look once, very briefly, at the corner of the binder showing in the inside of his coat, and then look away, at once, and not look back at it for the rest of the meal. She did not ask. He had not thought she would. It was a long promise, and she had made it in a stable in the fourth week at a table with a clean sheet on it, and she was keeping it as she kept everything, exactly.

---

Oona found him at the board in the main hall on the Sunday afternoon, in front of the calendar, which had been her office far longer now than it had been his.

She was not looking at the calendar. She was looking at his arm. He was wearing his oldest shirt, with the sleeve rolled loose above the linen so that nothing touched it, and she looked at the linen for a long moment with her whole serious face before she looked up at him.

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

He thought about it, because she would know.

"Like a very bad kettle. Less every day."

She nodded, as if that was a good answer and a precise one. Then she looked round the hall. It was half empty on a Sunday afternoon, and nobody was near. She lowered her voice anyway.

"I was on the front bench," she said. "I was close. Closer than the panel. When you went down on one knee." She looked at his right hand, the good one, and then back at his face. "There was a light under your hand. Not hers. Yours. I saw it. Nobody believes me. I told the girls on my landing, and they said it was hers and I didn't know where to look." Her chin came up. "I know where to look."

He stood in front of the calendar and remembered a morning in the first fortnight. He had told her, without knowing what it would cost him later, that he would not lie to her. He had kept it. He was not going to stop keeping it now, in a half-empty hall, because the truth had become heavier.

"It was mine," he said.

Oona did not move.

"It's new," he said. "It came on Thursday. I don't understand it yet. I don't understand most of what I am yet, and that's the truth too." He held her eyes. "When I understand it better, I'll tell you what I can. I'm not going to tell you everything. Some of it isn't safe to say out loud yet, even to people I'd tell anything. But I'm not going to tell you it was hers, because it wasn't."

Oona considered this, the way she considered every sum, with great care and all the way through.

"Is it the strange kind? Like yours was?"

"It's part of the strange kind."

"Will mine be?"

"No," said Cael. "Yours will be ordinary. You decided that in the first week, and you've been right about everything else."

Oona looked at him for a long time. Then she nodded, once, briskly, and wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice. He did not try to read it.

"Six days," she said.

"Six days."

"The hand goes on the other shoulder. Not the one by your arm." She tucked the slate under her own arm. "I worked it out. My mother's going to stand on the left, and if you come down too you'd have to stand on the right, and your right arm's fine." She was already walking away, and she turned back. "I won't tell the girls on my landing. They wouldn't believe it, and it's not theirs."

She went up the hall toward the residence wing with her slate held very tight against her chest.

Gerda was at the floor desk in the training hall when he passed it on his way back from the archive, signing a slip for the sixth bell. She wrote the hour on it in her careful square figures and blotted it, and then she did not look up at the clock above the door. She looked at him instead.

"I didn't look at it," she said. "On Thursday. I watched the three exchanges. Not the clock once."

"That was your advice."

"It was. I thought I'd better take it, if I was giving it." She folded the slip once along a crease that was already there. "I was by the gate. When you crossed the short one, I could feel the heat from where I stood." She looked at the linen on his arm. "That was a hit you took on purpose. Where everybody could see you chose it."

"Lira says that's the only kind worth taking."

"Lira's right." Gerda tucked the slip into her coat. "I'm sparring Hobb again on Tuesdays. Nobody asked me to. I asked him." She said it in her procedure voice, every word set down separately, as if she were reading a clause aloud. "I lay the haze and walk out of it on purpose, every time, before he's even moved. It's the opposite of what I did for a year." She nodded once, formally. "I wanted somebody to know. It isn't in any record."

"It's in mine now," said Cael. "If you don't mind."

Gerda considered that.

"I don't mind," she said, and went off toward the open floor, and did not check the clock on the way.

Lira had the stories at supper, all six of them, laid out at the scarred table between the four bowls like a dealer laying out cards.

"First, Karis threw the match on purpose so that the faculty would stop watching her. That one's mostly the Edge floor, and it's very stupid, and they love it." A second finger. "Second, the observer's secretly Ember and has been hiding it all term. That's near, so I've been squashing it." A third. "Third, Karis's lattice failed on its own because she'd held it too long, and she laughed because she was embarrassed. The heat-Paths second-years are telling that one, and they're very sure, because they understand sustain cost." She looked at Karis. "You're not offended?"

"No. It's a reasonable hypothesis from the evidence they had," said Karis. "It's wrong, but it's reasonable."

"Fourth and fifth are boring. Sixth." Lira held up the sixth finger, which meant the thumb of her other hand. "The observer made fire. Out of his own hand. On one knee." She put the hand down. "That's the true one. Nobody believes it. Oona's been telling it all afternoon on the second floor, and they're teasing her about it."

"I told her it was true," said Cael.

Lira looked at him for a long moment across the table.

"Good," she said at last. "You'd promised her." She took his bread and gave him back the larger half. "Four stories pulling different ways and two true ones nobody believes. Worry when they start to agree."

Hobb passed the end of the table on his way to the hatch with his bowl, and slowed, as he slowed at rails. He looked at the linen on Cael's arm.

"Shortest one?" said Hobb.

"Shortest one."

Hobb considered the arm with a kind of professional sympathy.

"My shins say you should've listened," he said, and went on to the hatch.

---

He went to the archive after supper, because it was where he went, and sat at the far end of the long table with the observation notebook shut and the bad arm resting on the wood. Prynn was at her high desk with her ledger and her spectacles on their cord. She did not look up when he came in.

At the eighth bell, when she would ordinarily have come down off her stool and put out her lamp and stood by the door with her keys until he took the hint, she came down off her stool and went to the pot by her desk instead. She poured two cups. She brought one to the long table and set it at his elbow, and then she did a thing she had never done in fifteen weeks. She sat down across from him with the other.

He did not say anything. He had learned that much about Prynn.

"I heard the yard," she said, after a while. "On Thursday. From here, with the windows shut. I've heard a great many yards in sixty years. I've never heard one go that quiet." She drank her tea. "I'm not going to ask you what made it go quiet. I don't ask readers what they're reading."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've told you before." She set the cup down square to the edge of the table, exactly, as she set everything. "I'll tell you something instead, since you've been sitting at my table every evening for a term and pretending not to notice a thing."

He waited.

"I have not forgotten to lock that door," said Prynn, "once in sixty years." She looked at him over her spectacles. "Not once. Not on the night of the long frost, when I couldn't feel my own hands on the key." She picked her cup up again. "So you may stop being so careful never to mention it. I'd hate you to strain yourself."

He looked at her, and she looked back, perfectly composed, and drank her tea.

"Why?" he said at last.

"Because the shelves want reading," said Prynn, "and they don't much care what hour it is. Most of the people who come in here want one page and go away again. You read the page next to it." She looked at her cup. "Drink your tea. It's getting cold, and I don't make it twice."

He drank his tea. She drank hers. When the cups were empty she took them both back to her desk, and climbed onto her stool, and opened her ledger, and left the lamp burning, and did not look at him again.

He wrote to Hesk on the Sunday evening, at the desk by the window, because Hesk had said to write when something happened, and the last letter had said nothing had.

*Hesk —*

*Something happened this week.*

*I fought the girl from the bigger school. The one who asks before she looks. It was a match in front of the whole academy, with three people writing it down, and she was the best I have ever stood across from. She had been showing me her best for six weeks and it turned out it was only her careful. The real thing was faster. I got burned on the arm doing something stupid that I chose on purpose, and it will be a while healing, and I would choose it again. It was called a draw.*

*I found out something about myself in it. I can't put it in a letter. I'm not keeping it from you; I'm keeping it off paper. I'll tell you the whole of it one day across your bench, if you'll have me on it.*

*She has asked to be one of us. We said yes. Lira left her bread.*

*Brom says I've started choosing. I think he's right. It's heavier than I thought.*

*The shoulder still complains. The arm complains louder. Both fine.*

*Cael*

He read it back. There was nothing in it that was untrue, and nothing in it that a stranger could have made anything of. The whole week was in it all the same, folded small. He wrote the Denvash direction on the outside and set it on the corner of the desk for Lira to take down on the third-day post.

Then he sat a while longer with the lamp low, and let himself turn, carefully, toward the new one.

He did not reach for it. Lira's rule held until the third day, and the third day was not yet. He only attended to it, the way you look at a coin in your palm without spending it. It was where it had been since Thursday, low and warm, not doing anything. It still felt like her. The precision was still there in the warmth, and the patience, the small commitments kept exactly, like a page of handwriting that carries the whole hand that wrote it. He had stopped being startled by that. He had begun, instead, to be glad of it, in a way he did not yet know how to write down. He thought that perhaps he would never write it down. Some things you carried before you filed them.

He thought of all of them, the five, and the people in them. He thought that whatever else it was, whatever it was for, the thing in him had always kept the company it came from.

---

On the Monday of the seventeenth week, at first light, the frost was so thick on the training yard that their boots left prints in it like prints in snow.

Lira came at him at half speed and then, because it was the third day and she had promised herself she would test it properly, at three-quarters. Karis stood by the pump and counted. Brom had come out too this time, without saying why, and stood beside Karis with his hands behind his back and his breath going up white, as solid as a wall somebody meant to keep.

The framework answered every time.

It answered on the left turn and the right. It answered on the third sequence and the eleventh. It answered on the double that Lira threw at the end, two pushes one on top of the other, the way Wray had broken the count at the first sitting, and it read both off her shoulders and slipped him out of both, and there was no empty pocket anywhere in it. It was quick and light and his, the oldest thing he owned, and the quickness in it was Lira's and always had been.

Lira stopped. She stood in the frost with her hands on her hips, breathing white, and looked at him for a long moment.

"Three," she said.

"Three," said Karis, from the pump. "A quarter of a breath. Unchanged."

Brom nodded once, the slow nod.

"Three clean days," said Lira. "That's the rule, and it's met." She took the rope off the pump's nail and began to coil it, and did not look at him while she did it. "You can touch the new one now. Carefully. Small. With somebody in the room." She hung the rope back. "Tomorrow. Not today. Today you've earned a breakfast."

They went in across the frozen yard together, the four of them, leaving four sets of prints in the frost behind them.

He wrote it at the desk by the window before the first bell rang, in the plain hand he kept for the mornings when something had been counted and come out right.

*Monday, week seventeen. Third clean day. The framework answered every call, at three-quarter speed, double included. Lead a quarter of a breath, unchanged. K. counted. Lira's rule met. The new one may be touched from tomorrow, small, with somebody in the room.*

He looked at the page above it, where the misfires were written, and the columns, and the small box round the line he could not answer. He looked at the back pocket, where a consent and a soft-cornered paragraph sat side by side. He thought of Quenna's question, which was not written anywhere, in any record, in anybody's hand, and was the heaviest thing he had.

Then he wrote one more line, small, at the bottom.

*Five fragments. One of them chosen. A question I can't answer yet, filed nowhere. Four bowls at the end of the table. Oona Kindles on Saturday.*

He closed the binder, and went down to breakfast, where Lira had already taken his bread.

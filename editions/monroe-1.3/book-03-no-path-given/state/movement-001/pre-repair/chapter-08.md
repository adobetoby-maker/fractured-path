# Chapter 8 — Vantage

By the time he crossed the yard that afternoon, the rumors had found a face to hang themselves on, and it was his.

He had first noticed Edran three days earlier, from the rail of the open floor, where an observer was allowed to stand and watch as long as he did not step over the chalk. Edran had been the easiest person in the hall to pick out. Everyone else on the floor moved around him the way water moves around a post, without seeming to think about it, and he fought with the settled ease of someone who had been at the top of a board for so long that he no longer checked whether he was still there. Glass Path. Iron Rank Two. First on the standings.

Cael had watched him for most of an hour, filling a page.

Glass was not what he had expected. He had always thought of glass as the thing that broke. Edran's declarations broke, certainly; Cael watched a bright, hard shell of something like crystal form along his forearm to take a strike and then burst apart into a glitter of fragments that hung for a heartbeat in the air before they vanished. But the shell did not break because it failed. It broke because breaking was what it was for. The burst carried the strike's force away with it in a hundred directions at once, and before the glitter had faded, a new shell was already forming in the next place Edran expected to be hit.

By the end of that first bout he had crossed out a page of his own notes about brittleness and started again.

And somewhere in the middle of the second, between exchanges, Edran had looked up at the rail.

His eyes had gone first to the open binder, Cael was nearly sure, and only afterward to the person holding it. He held the look a beat longer than a glance needed. His face showed nothing at all. Then he turned back to his opponent and finished the bout, and the rest of it was sharper than the beginning by some small, definite degree, as if he had tightened a screw.

Cael had written, under the page, *Sharper with an audience.*

Then, because the log was the one place he did not let himself be generous: *Or he minds being taken down in ink. Find out which.*

---

Edran was waiting near the yard's north gate, the way a man waits when he has decided where another man will have to walk.

He was not alone. Three or four students were nearby, doing other things very slowly. One was retying a boot that had not come untied. Two were talking with the particular attention of people listening to a different conversation. Cael saw the shape of it before he was close enough to see faces. It was not an ambush. It was an audience.

"Observers don't demonstrate," Edran said.

He said it to the yard in general, in a clear, level voice that carried without being raised. But his eyes stayed on Cael the whole time.

Cael stopped walking.

"That's not what the category is for," Edran went on. "I've read the provision. I've sat beside observers in lectures since my first year. Observers watch. They take notes. They don't get a panel and a closed hall and a training partner at first bell." He tilted his head, very slightly. "So either the faculty spent a morning watching you do nothing, which I don't believe, or somebody wrote a form that says one thing and means another."

The boy retying his boot had stopped pretending.

"What exactly are you?" Edran asked.

It was not hostile. That was the hard part. Cael had faced hostile questions all his life, and hostile questions were easy, in their way; they told you what the person wanted, and you could refuse to give it to them. This was something else. Edran sounded genuinely curious, almost academic, the way a person sounds asking about a rule he has found a hole in. Anger burned out. Curiosity kept a notebook.

Cael felt the yard around him the way he felt a crowd before a bout. The loose half-ring of listeners. The boy with the boot. Whatever he said in the next breath would be in every common room by the seventh bell, word for word, and improved on by the eighth.

He went through what he had. A form that said a thing the signers knew was not so. A binder in his pack that no one here would ever read. Nothing between the two that he could say out loud and keep.

He would not lie. He had decided that a long time ago, on a different road.

"I'm enrolled," he said.

Every word of it was true, and none of it was the thing Edran had asked.

Edran looked at him for a long moment. Cael watched him weigh it, the way he had watched him weigh an opponent between exchanges. He saw Edran consider pressing, and saw him decide against it, not out of kindness but out of something more like respect for a guard that had been put up properly.

"That isn't an answer," Edran said.

"No."

"It's the one you're giving, though."

"Yes."

Edran nodded once, slowly, as if filing something away for later. Then he turned and walked off toward the training hall without another word. The boy with the boot went after him. The others drifted apart, already talking.

Cael stood alone in the yard with the afternoon light going long across the chalk lines and understood that the question had not been answered. It had only been put down, carefully, where both of them knew it was.

---

He told Brom about it that evening in the stable, over barley, because not telling Brom things had stopped being one of his habits sometime in the last year.

Brom listened the way he listened to an account of a fight, which was what it had been. He asked two questions. Where had the other students been standing? Had Edran raised his voice? Then he ate in silence for a while, turning it over.

"The yard was the point," he said finally. "Not the question. He wanted a dozen people to hear you not answer."

"I gathered that."

"It isn't you he's cross with." Brom pointed his spoon toward the window, at the dark yard and the training hall beyond it. "It's the standings. He's sat at the top of them since he came, and he thinks they're true. Then this morning the faculty spent first bell on somebody who isn't on them at all, and nobody will say why." He went back to his bowl. "If you've climbed a ladder, you don't like hearing it might be leaning on the wrong wall."

"Should I be worried about him?"

Brom considered that with great care.

"Watch him," he said. "Don't fear him. Whatever he does next, he'll do it by the book, in daylight, with his name signed at the bottom. You'll see it coming a week off." He paused. "That's the useful part. The other part is that he'll never cheat you. Remember which is which."

Lira, who had been listening from the end of the bench with her chin on her fist, said, "I like him."

They both looked at her.

"He asked to your face," she said. "In front of people. He didn't go to the provost. He didn't start a rumor. He walked up and asked." She shrugged. "Half the people at Fenmark would have smiled at me in the corridor and written a letter about me the same afternoon. Give me a boy who asks."

---

He wrote the day up that night, by the narrow window, with the lamp turned low.

*First assessment. Function shown: Wind framework, four passes at panel speed, one with the count broken; surface read, four calls, eyes closed on the last. Benefit: assessment satisfied. Wray's words, on the record: Unorthodox architecture. Consistent execution. No safety concerns.*

*Cost: one strike taken, left forearm, third pass. The Compression fragment woke for it and I gave it no road. It let go cleanly. Bruise, about the size of a hand.*

He stopped. Then he wrote the part that mattered.

*Integration note. Today was the first time I have ever chosen not to use a fragment that was already reaching. Not failed to reach. Chosen. It went quietly. I think that is worth more than the quarter-push.*

He sat with that a long moment. It was true, and it frightened him a little, and he could not entirely say why.

Underneath, in the plainer hand he kept for things that were not fragments: *Edran asked what I am, in front of witnesses. I said I'm enrolled. He knows that isn't an answer. So do I. It will not hold forever.*

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper, because he had told Hesk he would write again after the first assessment, and he had meant it.

The second letter was shorter than the first. He told Hesk the assessment had passed. He told him three people had watched, and one of them had asked a good question, and he had answered it with a limit in it, and the man had looked pleased. He did not mention the strike or the fragment that had reached for it. Some things were for the binder. He signed it, folded it, and set it ready for Lira's next trip down the hill.

---

The second week settled into a shape.

He learned the bells. By the third day he no longer looked up when the second rang. By the fifth he could tell the fourth from the seventh with his eyes shut, which was useful, since the fourth meant food and the seventh meant the last chance at it. He learned the latches in the residence wing and which stair step creaked and that the girl across the courtyard practised something with a low humming sound every morning before the first bell. He learned that Prynn's door stood a finger's width wider each time he came to the archive and left everything on the reading table exactly as he had found it. He was not sure she knew she was doing it. He was not sure she didn't.

He went to his lectures. He sat at the back of the taxonomy block beside a row of thirteen-year-olds who had stopped turning around to look at him, and he answered Oona's questions when she asked them, which was often, and he tried very hard not to look at the place on the painted chart where a word was not.

He went to his supervised hours. Twice a week, in section four, with Quenna or a delegated assessor at the chalk with a slate, alone. He ran the framework against no one. He did Lira's conditioning circuits. He stopped shaping the work for the slate, and the slate wrote less, and he found he did not mind.

And in every free hour he had, he watched.

That was what an observer was allowed to do. So he did it, from the rail of the open floor, from the edge of the formal yard during standings bouts, from the back of the defensive branch's drill hall, with the binder open on his knee and his pen moving. He could not fight anyone. He was not eligible for the open bouts where the real learning of the place seemed to happen, the unscheduled matches between friends and rivals that filled the late afternoons. He could not stand on the floor. But nobody had ever said he could not stand at its edge and look.

He found, by the fourth day, that he was building a page format without having decided to.

Across the top went the declaration structure, as far as he could see it from outside: what came up, where, how fast, how it faded. Down the left margin went the tells, the small things a body did before its Path did anything. The middle held timing. And at the bottom of each page, after some days of trying other headings, he wrote a single line under the words *What this Path believes*.

He had come to think every Path held an opinion. Not only a set of abilities, but a settled view of what a fight was for and how one ought to be won, and you could see the view in how its people stood, long before they threw anything.

*Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose.*

*Stone believes that if you refuse to move, the other person has to.*

---

The Stone Path page was the broad student's.

He had watched him three times now, once on Brom's floor, once across a chalk ring at first bell, and once more on the fifth day of the second week, drilling alone in the corner of the defensive hall while Wray worked with someone else. Alone, without anyone to be furniture for, the boy moved differently. Slower than Cael expected. Lower. His feet barely seemed to leave the floor, but every shift of his weight went down through his heels and came back up a fraction stronger, as if the ground were lending him something each time he touched it. Cael realized, watching, that the boy had been solving the same problem Brom solved, how to take a strike and survive it, from the opposite direction entirely. Brom gave and borrowed. This boy simply refused to be moved, and let the ground do the arguing.

The defensive branch posted its drill pairings on a board outside the hall. Cael had walked past it every day for a week without reading it, because it had nothing to do with him.

That afternoon he stopped and read it.

Brom's name was there, three times in the week. Each time, paired beside it in the same clerk's hand, was a single short name.

*Hobb. Stone. Fourth year.*

He stood in front of the board and said it under his breath, once, to fix it. Hobb. A plain name for a plain, patient person who stood where he was told and hit as hard as he was asked and not one ounce harder.

He did not go and find him. Not yet. A thank-you shouted across a drill hall would be worth nothing. He wanted a proper moment, a quiet one, when it would not look like an observer currying favor with the partner who had been lent to him. He would know the moment when it came. He wrote the name in the binder under the Stone Path page, and underneath it, *Owed: thanks. Not yet paid.*

That evening he told Brom.

"His name's Hobb," he said.

Brom put his hand up flat, like a man stopping a cart.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't tell me any more. I'm going to ask him myself." Brom looked faintly embarrassed, and then faintly stubborn. "It's been two weeks. He's hit me harder than anyone in my life. The least I can do is find out his name from his own mouth."

"You already know it now."

"I'll forget it," said Brom firmly. "Watch me."

---

Lira's standings bout fell on the sixth day of the second week.

It was not an assessment, not exactly. The re-certification track required its candidates to fight a set number of recorded bouts each month against students from the standard roster, and the results went into the candidate's file with an assessor's initials beside them. It was routine. It was meant to be routine. The clerk who scheduled it had paired her, by the luck of the roster, with a third-year standing high on the Copper side of the board.

He was a Force Path, tall and long-armed, Copper Rank Eight by his tag, with a clean record and a reputation, Cael learned from the rail, for pushing opponents out of the ring before they had properly set their feet. He had no dot beside his name on any board. He came into the formal yard with the easy, half-bored air of somebody keeping an appointment.

Twenty or so students had drifted in to watch. Not for him. For the dot.

Cael stood at the rail. Gerda came and stood two places along from him, arms folded, her ash-pale hair bright in the cold sun. She nodded to him without speaking. Brom arrived a minute later, still moving carefully on his right side, and leaned on the rail between them.

"She's going to go hard," Brom said quietly.

"I know."

"She shouldn't."

"I know," said Cael. "She's going to anyway."

Gerda glanced at them both and said nothing. Then she said, to no one in particular, "The assessor will initial it either way. The procedure doesn't care how she wins."

"Lira cares," said Brom.

Gerda considered that. Something passed over her face that Cael could not quite read.

"Yes," she said. "I used to."

---

The first exchange went the way a procedural bout was supposed to go.

The Force Path opened at the textbook distance and tested her with two straight pushes. Each one carried a thickening of the air in front of his palm, a shove made solid, and Lira slipped the first and let the second brush past her shoulder without committing to anything. She was measuring him. Cael could see it in her feet, which never quite settled, and in the way her eyes went to his hips and not his hands. The third push she let come all the way in, and stepped off its line at the last instant, the Wind in her moving her a half-pace sideways as easily as a door swinging. The push went past her into empty air, and the Force Path's weight followed it a little too far.

She did not punish it. She stepped back to the centre.

The crowd made a small, satisfied sound. That was what a procedural bout looked like. Careful. Polite. A testing of edges.

Then the second exchange began, and Lira stopped being polite.

She came off her mark before he had finished setting his feet. Not at the textbook distance. Inside it, fast, the way she had fought on the Ardenmere circuit when the rent was due, the way no academy had ever taught her and Fenmark's records had never once managed to describe. The Force Path threw a push to drive her back out, and it was a good push, a real one, the air in front of his palm going thick and hard as a wall. She could have slipped it. Cael saw the space where she could have slipped it.

She didn't.

She took it on the left shoulder.

It knocked her a full step sideways. Cael heard Brom draw breath beside him. But she had turned with it, and the step sideways was a step around, and before the Force Path had brought his arm back she was inside his reach on the side he had just opened, and she hit him twice, short and hard, once under the ribs and once on the point of the shoulder that had pushed. He gasped and went back a pace.

The yard had gone very quiet.

Cael understood what he was watching. He had seen it before, on the circuit, many times. Lira had paid for those two strikes with a hit she could have avoided. She had paid deliberately, in front of everyone, so that everyone would see the price and see that she did not care about it.

The third exchange the Force Path tried to take the fight back to where he wanted it. He reset to the textbook distance and set himself properly, and when she came in again he was ready for her. He threw a push low and wide, at her legs, to take away her footing. She jumped it, the Wind lifting her a hand's width higher than her legs alone could have managed, and landed already moving. He tightened his form. He tried again to slow the fight down to a pace he understood.

She would not let it slow.

Every time he adjusted, she adjusted a half-beat faster. Every time he reset, she was already inside the reset. He began to give ground without meaning to, a half-pace at a time, his pushes coming shorter and more cramped, and Cael watched the chalk at his heels come closer and closer.

The fourth exchange ended with him one step from the edge of the ring, both hands up, breathing hard, and Lira standing in the centre with her shoulder already beginning to swell and not even looking at it.

The assessor called it.

---

She did not celebrate. She collected her water from the bench, drank, and walked off the formal ground toward the rail with the look of someone who has said exactly what she came to say and does not intend to say it twice.

The crowd did not leave at once. Cael heard the talk start as she passed, low and quick, the voices of people who had just watched something and were already trying to work out how to tell it to someone who had missed it. He heard someone say *the one with the dot*, and someone else say *not after that*, and he could not tell whether they meant the mark or the girl.

Lira reached the rail. She toweled her face. She did not quite meet Cael's eyes.

"The file says Copper," she said. "Fenmark's file. It's in a box at the intake desk. It says Copper, and it says I was trouble, and it says the termination stands." She set the towel over her good shoulder. "The floor just watched something else. I'm not asking the file to change. I wanted the floor to have seen it. Now it has."

Brom had waited until she was finished. He always did.

"Second exchange," he said. "The push to the shoulder. You didn't need to take it."

"I know."

"You wanted an audience for it."

"I wanted them to watch it not matter." Lira flexed the shoulder, winced very slightly, and stopped. "You of all people know the difference. You've made a career of it."

Brom considered that for a long moment. Then he dipped his head, conceding.

"Get your initials," Gerda said from along the rail. "Before the sixth bell."

Lira looked at her. Then she smiled, a real one, quick and surprised.

"Already on my way," she said.

She went toward the assessor's table. Gerda watched her go with her arms still folded. After a moment, without looking at either of them, Gerda said, "She's good."

"She is," said Cael.

"It won't matter to the file," said Gerda. "The file only counts. It doesn't watch." She unfolded her arms and pushed herself away from the rail. "But it'll matter to her. That's something."

She left before either of them could answer. Cael watched her walk away across the yard, tall and narrow and very straight, and wondered what Gerda's own file said, and what she had done the last time a floor had watched her and a box had not.

---

They sat on the wall outside the residence wing at dusk, the three of them, as they had sat on walls in a dozen towns.

Lira had a cold cloth from the kitchens folded over her shoulder, where the Force Path's push had landed. The bruise underneath had come up dark and broad. She did not seem to mind it. She sat with her heels knocking against the stone and watched the light go out of the sky over the hill town, and for a long while none of them said anything.

"The assessor initialed it," she said at last. "Before the sixth bell. Gerda was right. He looked at the time when I handed him the slip. He actually looked."

"So it's dated today," said Brom.

"It's dated today. It's in the file." She tilted her head back against the wall behind her. "With a dot next to it."

Cael waited. He had learned, over a year and a half, that Lira's thoughts arrived in their own time and did not like to be fetched.

"I still hate the dot," she said. "I want you both to know that. I'm not going to wake up one morning and like it."

"Nobody asked you to like it," said Brom.

"I know." She was quiet a moment. "But I signed the page. And I'm glad I signed it. I'm glad I'm here. Today I stood on their floor and did what I do, and twenty people saw it, and a clerk wrote it down with the date on it." She turned the cold cloth over on her shoulder. "Both of those are true. I hate the dot. I'm glad I signed. I kept waiting for one of them to win, and this afternoon I realized I'd stopped waiting."

"What happened to the fight between them?" Cael asked.

Lira thought about it with great care, the way she thought about an opponent's footwork.

"I think I just let them both sit down," she said. "There's room on the bench."

Brom made the low sound in his chest. "That's wise."

"Don't," said Lira. "You'll ruin it."

He didn't. He sat beside her in the dusk with his ribs held carefully to one side and said nothing more at all, and after a while she reached over without looking and adjusted the cold cloth so that it covered his ribs as well as her shoulder, which it did not quite, and neither of them mentioned it.

Cael watched the hill town's lamps come on one by one below them and did not write anything down. Some things he wanted to keep exactly as they were, without his handwriting on them.

---

He made the week's inventory on the last night of the second week, sitting cross-legged on his bed with the binder open across his knees and the lamp turned low.

He counted the Path pages first.

Glass, from Edran. Stone, from Hobb. Shield, from Wray, from the way she stood and the way she changed one condition at a time, though he had never seen her use her Path at all. Iron Skin, from Brom, which he had known for a year but had never before watched taken apart by someone better at taking things apart than he was. Wind, from Lira, in a formal yard, with a clerk's dot beside her name. Force, from the third-year she had beaten. Ash, from Gerda, glimpsed once in a supervised block, a low grey haze that seemed to slow everything that passed through it. Blade, from a pair in the training hall who drilled every day in total silence and had plainly stopped needing to tell each other anything. Current, from a second-year girl whose footwork never once stopped moving. Storm, from a fourth-year off the movement floor who left the formal yard smelling of rain. Anchor, from a Copper first-year who planted himself so absolutely that two people together could not shift him a finger's width.

Eleven.

Eleven Paths, watched at close range. Closer than a spectator stood. Closer than the circuit's crowded nights had ever let him get. Nobody's result depended on him, and he was allowed nowhere else, so every scrap of his attention had gone into looking.

He turned to the bottoms of the pages and read down the line he had added to each.

*Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose.*

*Stone believes that if you refuse to move, the other person has to.*

*Shield believes nothing should be spent that doesn't have to be.*

*Iron Skin believes you can borrow anything, if someone gives it to you first.*

*Wind believes the place a strike is going is a place you can leave.*

*Force believes distance is a thing you can take away from people.*

*Ash believes everything is easier to handle when it's slower.*

*The Blade pair believe in each other. I couldn't find a Path for that.*

He stopped there for a while.

He had walked through the gate two weeks ago thinking of the observer category the way everyone did. A patch over a hole in the rules. Bars with a hinge on them. Something to put up with until somebody could argue him into a better one. He had thought the narrowness was the whole of it. He had thought his work at Greyvane would be pushing at its bars.

It was not the whole of it.

The category had shut him out of every open bout on the grounds. It had refused him a partner, in writing. It had put him in a room full of thirteen-year-olds and on the far end of every desk. And because it had shut him out of everything, it had left him standing at the edge of everything, with nothing to do but look.

He had never had so much to look at in his life.

He wrote it slowly, in the careful hand he saved for entries he meant to reread.

*Greyvane, end of week two. Eleven Paths seen closer than the circuit ever let me. They built the observer track to hold me still.*

He looked at the line. Then he wrote the rest of it.

*Holding still turns out to be the best place to see from. It isn't a cage. It's a vantage point.*

---

He read back over the two weeks before he closed the binder, the way he did on nights that felt like hinges.

Brom had a teacher. A woman who wanted to be proved wrong and had said so out loud, and who had found the soft place in him and offered to mend it. He was still holding his right side when he sat down. He was happier than Cael had ever seen him.

Lira had put something down at an intake desk and picked something else up on a formal floor, and she was carrying both now, the signed page and the beaten third-year, and neither of them had won. She was letting them share the chair. She had not said so. Cael had watched her do it.

He himself had a name on a public board in the main hall, written in the same ink as everyone else's, and a hollow circle on his tag, and a refusal in writing filed behind the stranger's note in the back of his binder. He had a first assessment satisfied and a second one coming in a month. He had a question asked in the open by the best fighter in the building, and an answer that was true and was not an answer, and a strong feeling that the question had only been set down, not dropped.

He had a name, too, that he owed a thank-you to. Hobb.

He closed the binder.

Across the corridor he heard Brom say, quite clearly, in his sleep, "Hobb," and then grunt, and turn over.

Cael lay back in the dark and laughed silently at the ceiling until his ribs hurt. Then he lay still, and listened to the building, and found he knew every sound in it now. The stair. The latch. The pipe. The bell, far off, saying it was late.

He knew where he was. He fell asleep knowing it.

# Chapter 4 — One Condition at a Time

He found Brom on the defensive-branch floor, and he could tell how the afternoon had gone before he was close enough to see Brom at all.

There was a crowd. Not a large one, perhaps a dozen students strung along the chalk boundary of the far section, but it had the particular stillness of people who had stumbled onto something worth watching rather than come on purpose. Nobody was talking. Two of them had set their bags down, which meant they had stopped meaning to leave.

Cael found a place at the end of the line and looked over a shoulder.

Brom stood in the middle of the section in his usual stance, low and loose, weight sunk into his heels as if the floor were a slope he had decided to lean against. Across from him stood the broadest student Cael had seen on the grounds so far. The boy was a head taller than Brom and half again as wide, with forearms like fence posts and the calm, patient face of someone who had been asked to stand in front of other people for a very long time and had made his peace with it. His feet were planted shoulder-width apart, and the chalk dust around them had settled into a faint ring, as if he hadn't moved them in some while.

Stone Path, Cael guessed, from the way the boy's weight seemed to go down through his soles and keep going. A fourth-year at least. Iron-tier, probably low in it.

Between them, a little to one side, stood a woman of about fifty.

She was not large. She was not, at first glance, anything in particular, a lean woman with grey-streaked hair tied back and a plain brown training coat buttoned to the throat. But she stood the way Cael had seen only a handful of people stand, perfectly balanced, every part of her body doing exactly the work it needed to do to keep her upright and not one ounce more, so that even at rest she seemed to be holding a shield she wasn't carrying.

Shield Path. It could not have been anything else.

"Wray," the girl beside him whispered to her friend, as if Cael had asked. "Bronze. She's had him out there since the fifth bell."

"Who?"

"The new one. The Iron Skin."

Cael looked at the light through the high windows and did a quick sum. Brom had been on this floor for most of an hour.

---

Wray raised one finger.

The broad student came forward, and Cael understood within three exchanges that he was watching the most orthodox defensive fighter he had ever seen. Everything the boy did came out of a textbook. He pressed, set his angle, pressed again, and each strike arrived from exactly the approved line, at exactly the approved distance, with his weight committed exactly as far as the form recommended and no further. It was not exciting. It was not meant to be. It was the kind of pressure that wore people down by never giving them anything to argue with.

Brom met it from underneath, the way Brom met everything.

He let the first strike come to him. Cael watched it land on Brom's forearm and saw the Iron Skin harden there a fraction before contact, the faint dull sheen that came over Brom's skin when his Path took hold, and then he saw the thing he had spent a year studying and still found remarkable: Brom gave. He let the force push him half an inch, then an inch, finding the line of it the way water finds a crack, and at the end of that inch he turned it, so that the broad student's own weight came back around on him from a direction he hadn't planned for.

The boy staggered half a step.

It was a small thing. In a real fight it would have been an opening. Here, Wray simply lowered her finger, and both of them stepped back to their marks.

She said something to the broad student, too quiet to carry. He nodded.

"Again," she said.

This time she had moved Brom's mark forward. Cael saw it at once, because he had been watching the chalk. Brom's starting line was a full pace closer than before. When the broad student came in, he came in almost at once, and Brom had a great deal less room to give in.

Brom gave anyway. He took the strike on the forearm, sank, and found he had nowhere to sink to. His back heel touched the chalk line behind him. He turned the force short, cramped, and it came back around on the broad student with only a little of its weight.

The boy did not stagger this time. He shifted his feet, absorbed the small return, and pressed again.

Wray raised her finger. Stop.

She watched Brom for a long moment without saying anything at all. Then she spoke to the broad student again, quietly, and moved Brom's mark back to where it had been, and this time she touched the boy's left shoulder.

"From there," she said.

The angle changed. The strikes began to arrive from Brom's left side instead of square on, and Cael felt something in his own chest tighten in recognition, because he had fought Brom often enough to know that his left was the side he trusted less. It was not a weakness, exactly. It was a preference so old that Brom himself might not know he had it. Every redirect Brom had ever shown him had turned to the right.

Brom took the first angled strike and turned it right, as he always did, and the turn carried the force across his own body and left him a little open on the side he had just been hit from.

The broad student did not exploit it. He had not been told to.

Wray's pen moved on the slate she held against her hip.

---

Cael realized, somewhere in the fourth change, what she was doing.

It was what he did with the binder. She was isolating variables. Distance. Angle. Rhythm. She changed exactly one condition at a time, held everything else still, and watched which part of Brom held and which part bent. She was not watching a fight at all. She was taking him apart into the pieces that were structure and the pieces that were habit.

He felt a strange, sharp pleasure at seeing someone else do it, and do it better than he did, with a living person instead of a page.

Wray raised her finger a fifth time. She went to the broad student and spoke to him longer than before, and Cael saw the boy frown slightly, as if he had been asked to do something unfamiliar. Then he nodded, and Wray stepped away.

"Don't finish anything," Cael heard her say, as she passed the chalk.

The broad student came in.

The strikes looked the same at first. The same textbook line, the same approved distance. But they were short. Each one began properly and then stopped, an inch or two before it would have committed, and pulled back, and began again. Jab, withdraw. Press, withdraw. There was force in all of it, real force, but none of it was ever given away.

Brom took the first one on his forearm and reached for it, and there was nothing to take.

Cael saw it happen on Brom's face. A small flicker of surprise. The give-and-return he had built over two years depended on one thing above all others, which was that the person hitting him meant it. A committed strike was a loan. Brom borrowed it, turned it, and paid it back with interest. An uncommitted strike was no loan at all. There was nothing in it to borrow.

The broad student pressed again, short, and Brom gave half an inch to nothing.

Again.

Again.

The crowd had gone very quiet. Somebody behind Cael shifted their weight and the sound of it was loud.

Brom's shoulders changed. Cael knew that change. It was the moment, in a long fight, when Brom stopped waiting for a thing to happen and decided to make it happen.

He stepped in.

It was not his style. He came forward off his back heel into the broad student's space and dropped his guard by a hand's width on the right side, an obvious opening, the kind of opening a textbook fighter is trained to punish. He was baiting. He was offering the boy something worth committing to.

The boy committed.

It was the first full strike in a long while, and it came in hard, the whole weight of a fourth-year Stone Path behind it, and it landed on Brom's ribs before he had finished turning to meet it.

The sound was a flat, heavy thud. Brom's breath went out of him in a grunt. His Iron Skin had come up in time, Cael saw the sheen of it along his side, but Iron Skin did not make a person weightless. Brom slid a full pace back across the chalk and dropped to one knee.

Cael was moving before he knew it. He caught himself at the chalk line with one hand on the shoulder of the student in front of him. The girl beside him made a small sound.

Brom stayed down for two breaths. Then three.

Wray had not moved. She was watching Brom's face, not his ribs.

He got up.

He did it slowly, carefully, one hand pressed flat to his side, and Cael could see from the set of his mouth that it hurt. He walked back to his mark without being told to, and turned, and set himself, and looked at Wray.

She studied him.

"Full," she said to the broad student. "Commit everything."

The broad student came in with a committed strike, the whole of his weight behind it, a fence post of an arm driving straight at Brom's centre, and Brom took it, and gave, and turned it.

This time the turn was clean. The force came around in a short hard arc and went back into the boy along a line he had not braced for, and the broad student's planted feet came unplanted for the first time all afternoon. He went sideways a full step, then another, arms flung wide for balance, and ended up on the far side of the chalk with a startled expression on his patient face.

The crowd made a sound. It was not a cheer, exactly, more a collective breath let out at once.

Wray did not make a sound at all. She wrote on her slate.

---

When she lowered the slate, she walked out onto the floor and stood in front of Brom. He was still holding his ribs.

"Somebody taught you to live through being underestimated," she said. "Maybe you taught yourself."

Brom said nothing.

"It isn't nothing. It carried you here. Everything you do waits for someone to give you too much. Most people do, the first time. They see a Copper tag and a stocky boy with no school behind him, and they put their whole weight into the first strike, and you take it from them." She tilted her head. "You've built a whole fighter out of other people's mistakes."

"It works," Brom said.

"It works until someone respects you." Her voice did not rise or harden. It was the voice of someone reading a measurement off a scale. "You've just seen what happens then. A good opponent who takes you seriously won't give you anything to borrow. Then you have to go and fetch it. And you don't know how to fetch it yet, so you open yourself up and pay for it with your ribs."

Brom was quiet for a long moment.

"Now I'll teach you the other thing," Wray said. "What to do when the person across from you has stopped making that mistake. You don't have it yet. Learning it will make the last two years look like the easy part."

She turned to the broad student, nodded once in thanks, and dismissed him with two fingers. He picked up his bag and left without looking at anyone. Then she turned back to the crowd, who scattered at once as if they had all just remembered urgent business elsewhere.

Cael stayed.

---

Brom was quiet most of the way back to the residence wing.

He walked a little stiffly, keeping his left elbow tucked against his side, and Cael matched his pace without comment. The light had gone long and gold across the yard. Somewhere in the lecture wing a bell rang, the sixth, the one that meant the training floors were closing.

They were nearly at the residence door before Brom spoke.

"I think I have a teacher."

He said it the way he might have set down something fragile on a table, carefully, as if he wasn't yet sure the table would hold it. Cael looked at him. In all the months he had known Brom, he had never once heard him use that word without something sour attached to it. A joke about Velmere's hired instructors. A dry aside about men who taught for money and wanted thanks for it. This time there was nothing attached at all.

"That's good," Cael said.

"Yes." Brom stopped at the door and looked back across the yard toward the training hall. "She watched me for an hour and found the soft place. You saw it. The waiting. I've been hiding that for two years." He shook his head slowly. "Then, instead of leaning on it, she said she'd mend it."

"Nobody's done that before."

"Plenty of people have found things. My family found things. Every instructor they ever paid for found things, and then they used them, to make me fit whatever they'd been paid to make me into." He touched his ribs gingerly. "This is the first one who found a thing and wanted to fix it."

"Are you going to let her?"

"I'd decided before she finished talking." He glanced sideways at Cael, a little wry. "I only noticed afterward. Do you do that?"

"All the time. The binder is mostly me reading what I already chose."

Brom made the low sound in his chest that did him for a laugh, and then winced, and pressed his side.

"The big one," Cael said. "Your partner. What's his name?"

Brom thought about it, and an odd look crossed his face.

"I don't know," he said. "She pointed at him and he came. He never said it. I don't think he says much of anything." He considered the closed door of the training hall. "He took everything she asked him to do and did it exactly. Held back when she said hold back. Hit when she said hit. Didn't once try to win."

"That's a talent."

"It's a rare one," Brom said. "Most people can't stand to lose on purpose for an hour, even when it's their job."

Cael filed it. He did not know yet why. He only knew that a person who could be furniture for an hour without minding it was worth knowing, and that he did not even know the boy's name.

---

Lira was already at a trestle table in the stable when they came down for supper, and she had brought bread.

Not the kitchen's bread. Real bread, a dark round loaf with a crackled crust, wrapped in a cloth and set in the middle of the table like a trophy.

"I found the baker," she announced.

"We guessed," Brom said, lowering himself onto the bench with great care.

Lira looked at the way he sat. "What happened to you?"

"I got a teacher."

"And the teacher hit you?"

"A boy the size of a door hit me. The teacher arranged it."

"Good," Lira said with feeling, and tore him off the biggest piece of the loaf.

She had spent the afternoon in the hill town, between her own scheduled blocks, and she reported on it the way a circuit veteran reported on a new district, quickly and without wasted words. It was even smaller up close than it looked from the hill, she said. One street worth walking, one worth avoiding. A mill, a smithy, a post office in the back of a draper's, and a baker at the bottom of the hill who opened before dawn and was rude to everyone equally, which Lira considered a mark of integrity.

"I'll take your letter down tomorrow," she said to Cael. "I'm going anyway. The post goes out on the third day of the week."

He handed it across. She tucked it inside her coat without looking at the direction, which he appreciated more than he said.

Then she grew quieter, and turned her cup between her hands.

"There are others on my track," she said.

Both of them looked up.

"Re-certification. I saw the roster for the review room. My name, and three others. Not many." She was looking at the cup. "But I'm not the only name on the list. So somebody did this before me, and somebody will do it after me, and there's a desk and a procedure and a clerk who knows how to read the clauses aloud. It's a *track*."

"Is that good or bad?" Brom asked.

She took a long time to answer.

"I've been carrying this as if Fenmark invented it just for me," she said at last. "As if they'd looked at me and decided, specially, that I was the problem." She set the cup down. "It turns out there's a desk for it. A queue. Other people standing in it."

"That's not an answer," Cael said.

"Give it a month," Lira said, and from her that counted as hope.

---

Brom's first full day of coursework was the next day, and he came back from it talking.

Cael heard him before he saw him. He was halfway down the stable stairs at supper, still talking over his shoulder to a second-year who looked faintly alarmed, and he kept talking as he set down his tray, and he went on talking as he sat. Cael took a moment to understand what was different about it. Brom usually arrived at a conclusion in silence and reported only its finished shape. This was the process itself, spilling out of him as fast as he could think it, and it was a register Cael had never heard him use.

"Declaration theory," Brom said, by way of greeting. "Two blocks of it. A whole lecture hall full of Stone and Shield and Iron Skin and Anchor, all of us sitting there while an old man draws curves on a board."

"Curves of what?" Lira asked.

"Efficiency. How much a declaration gives you, rank by rank, as you refine the form." He ate a spoonful of barley without looking at it. "Half of it's solid. Better than solid. He has names for things I found out by falling over. There's a word for the way I turn a strike. There's a diagram of it."

"And the other half?"

"Wrong."

Lira raised her eyebrows. "The other half of a lecture you've attended once is wrong."

"The half about ceilings." Brom set his spoon down, which in Brom meant he was serious. "The theory says that after a certain point, refining your form stops paying. You hit a ceiling. Somewhere in the middle of a tier, past a certain rank, the gains go flat, and after that the only way to get stronger is raw output. More force. Bigger declarations. The form's done all it can."

"And you think that's wrong."

"I know it's wrong. I spent two years past where the ceiling's supposed to be. On my own, with nobody telling me there was a ceiling there. And I kept getting better at the form." He leaned forward. "Every month. Not in great jumps. In small ones. Steadily."

"Then either the lecture's wrong," Lira said, "or you're a freak. Pick one."

"Or there are enough freaks that the rule's no use to anyone." Brom's voice had the intensity he usually saved for a fight. "That's what's wrong with it. It's describing what happens to people who believe there's a limit and stop pushing. It isn't a limit in the body. It's a limit in the belief."

Cael turned that over.

It was exactly the kind of confident thing a boy with no formal training might say about a body of doctrine written by people with far more learning than he had. It was the kind of claim that usually fell apart at the first serious question. It was also, he suspected, true, because Brom's evidence was his own body and two years of improvement the theory said should not have happened, made by someone who had never once been told he was supposed to stop.

"You should tell Wray," Cael said.

"I did."

"And?"

Brom's face did something complicated, as if several expressions were queuing for the same doorway.

"She said, 'Good. Then show me on the floor, not in a lecture hall. If you're right, I'll rewrite the lecture.'"

He said it slowly, word for word, the way a person repeats a sentence they intend to remember.

Lira put down her bread. "She said she'd *rewrite the lecture*?"

"She teaches the ceiling section to the second-years. She said if I can show her the ceiling isn't there, on the floor, in front of her, she'll stand up and tell them she was wrong." Brom shook his head slowly. "I don't think I've ever had a teacher who wanted to be proved wrong."

"Velmere's instructors didn't," said Lira. It was not quite a question.

"Velmere's instructors wanted to be obeyed." Brom said it without heat. The old grievance had worn smooth with distance. "Wray wants somebody to test her. That's a different animal entirely."

He went back to his barley.

Cael watched him eat and thought about what had happened in two days. A tag that hadn't needed a question. A teacher who found a weakness and offered to mend it. A lecture hall full of people who had names for the things Brom had learned by falling over, and one of them willing to rewrite her own lecture if a Copper-tier boy from nowhere could beat her on a chalk floor.

He thought Brom had been waiting for this place his whole life without knowing it existed.

He did not say it. Brom would have been embarrassed, and anyway it was not his to say.

He wrote it down instead, that night, under the heading he had started on the road. *Things that are changing that I want to remember changed.*

*Brom has a teacher. He said the word without a joke in it. She wants him to prove her wrong, and he's going to try.*

He looked at the line for a while. Then he added a second one beneath it.

*His partner today let himself be hit for an hour without trying to win once. Nobody knows his name. I should.*

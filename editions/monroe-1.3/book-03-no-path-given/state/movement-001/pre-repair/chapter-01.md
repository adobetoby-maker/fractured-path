# Chapter 1 — The Last Climb

Brom knelt in the pine needles with his right hand open, like a man about to push a stuck door.

"Ready," he said.

Cael was not ready. He set his left forearm across his chest anyway, braced it, and nodded.

It was the third morning out of Ardenmere. The fire was down to a red eye in the ashes. Lira was still rolled in her blanket at the edge of the clearing, awake, pretending not to be, because watching them fail before breakfast had become one of her small pleasures on the road. Cold air came down off the hills above the camp and smelled of wet stone.

Brom's palm met his forearm.

The push was slow. It always started slow. Brom let a little Iron Skin harden through his hand first, so the contact arrived like a flat stone rather than a palm, and then he leaned. The force came in steady and even, a fraction of a real strike, the kind of weight a man could count.

Cael waited for it to arrive in full.

That was the first thing he had learned on this road. The fragment did nothing with force that was still coming. It only woke when the force was already his, already inside the bone, and the waking had a feel to it now. A tightening, low in the chest. A sense of something gathering.

He reached for it.

The force gathered. It turned. For a quarter of a heartbeat he held a piece of Brom's push the way a person holds water in cupped hands, and then he had to decide where it went.

It wanted to go down.

He felt it slide toward his left knee, the same way it had slid two days ago. The same lazy path. The joint that still remembered being used as a drain. The memory was so exact that his whole body flinched from it before his mind had finished recognizing it.

He let go.

The gathered piece spilled out of him harmlessly. His forearm took the rest of the push the ordinary way, the way any arm takes weight. Brom eased off.

"You dropped it," Brom said.

"It was going to my knee again."

"You're sure."

"Same road as last time." Cael flexed the knee. Nothing. A faint ache that had been there since the first day, fading, nothing new on top of it. "It wants the nearest joint. If I don't give it somewhere better before it moves, it picks for itself."

Brom considered that. He considered most things the way a smith considers a bar of iron, turning it slowly before deciding what it was good for.

"Then give it somewhere better first," he said.

"That's the whole problem."

"No. That's the whole answer. The problem is you're deciding late." He set his hand again. "Again."

---

They did it eleven more times.

The first three failed cleanly. Nothing caught. The fragment sat in him like a word on the tip of the tongue, present and unreachable, and Brom's push simply arrived and left.

On the fourth he caught something and sent it the wrong way. Not into the knee. Into his own shoulder, the right one, the one the Reydan bout had already used hard. It flared. He hissed through his teeth and Brom stopped at once.

"Shoulder."

"Shoulder," Cael agreed. He rolled it. There was the old complaint, the one that announced itself on certain angles, and over it a new hot line that faded as he breathed. "Better than the knee."

"Better how?"

"The shoulder's already paying. It's an account that's open."

Lira's voice came from the blanket. "That is the worst thing I have ever heard a person say about his own body."

"You're awake."

"I'm always awake. Keep going. I want to see you do it right once before I have to walk all day next to someone sulking."

He did it right on the seventh try.

The force arrived. He waited until it was in the bone. Before it could pick its own road, he chose one for it — out, back along the line it had come in, into Brom's own braced arm — and he held the choice while the fragment gathered.

A quarter of the push went home.

It went back into Brom's wrist with a small, solid knock, like a door shutting in another room. Brom's eyebrows lifted. Under the Iron Skin his forearm had felt it. Cael knew he had felt it, because Brom's own read was better than anyone's at telling where weight had gone.

Then the bill came.

It always came the same way. It ran up through the shoulder and across the breastbone, a band of heat and pressure, as if someone had laid a hot iron bar along his collarbones and leaned on it. For two breaths he could not fill his lungs. He waited them out on his knees in the pine needles.

"There," Brom said quietly. "Quarter."

"Quarter," Cael said, when he could.

He did it twice more before they broke camp. One worked. One didn't. The one that worked cost exactly what the first one had, no more, no less, and he found that oddly comforting. A price that stayed the same was a price he could plan around.

The one that failed went nowhere at all. He had reached a breath too early, while the push was still coming, and the fragment had simply not answered. He was almost grateful. A failure that did nothing was the cheapest lesson the fragment offered.

Lira was sitting up now, blanket around her shoulders, eating the last heel of yesterday's bread. She had watched all of it.

"Three in twelve," she said.

"Three in twelve."

"On a push Brom could do while asleep."

"He'd have to be asleep for me to catch more than a quarter of it."

She grinned. "That's honest, at least."

---

He wrote it up while Brom kicked dirt over the fire.

The Power Log sat open on his knees, a binder now, its rings stiff with cold. A year ago it had been a single slim notebook. He had added pages so many times that the original cover was mostly a memory, and he had long ago given up pretending the binder was tidy. It was not tidy. It was honest. Those had turned out to be different things.

Every fragment had its own section. Every section used the same four headings, because the same four questions had turned out to matter every time.

Function: what it did.

Benefit: what it was good for.

Cost: what it took from him.

Integration: how much of it answered when he called.

He had not invented the format so much as surrendered to it. Every other way of writing about the fragments had eventually broken down into either bragging or panic. The four headings did not allow either.

He turned to the newest section and wrote.

*Compression-adjacent. Road session, third morning. Function: catches force already inside the body and sends it back out. Must wait for the force to arrive. Reaching early gets nothing. Benefit: none in a real fight yet. Cost: shoulder and breastbone, two breaths of not being able to breathe. Same price on every success. Integration: partial. Three of twelve. A quarter of a slow push each time.*

He paused. Then he added the line that mattered most.

*If I don't choose the road before the force moves, it chooses the nearest joint. Knee on day one. Shoulder today. Choose first. Always choose first.*

He read it back, then turned to the front of the binder, where the oldest notices were copied out.

There were four confirmed fragments now. He went through them out of habit, the way some people touch a pocket to make sure a coin is still there.

Wind-adjacent was first. Lira's. An evasion framework he had once needed a full second to assemble and now did without thinking, reading a strike off an opponent's body a half-beat before it landed. Its range was the length of a fight. Its price was fatigue, and a choice of direction he had to make before he moved. Against faster opponents, it read less. He had shown it on the circuit a hundred times.

Pressure-adjacent was second. A redirect, borrowed from a Bronze-tier fighter named Feryn who had beaten him fairly. Three clean uses mapped, each paid for in the right shoulder. It worked at contact and nowhere else. Nobody outside the three of them had ever been told it existed.

Iron-adjacent was third. Brom's. A pressure-read along the skin that told him where a person's weight was about to go. It worked at contact and out to a few paces, thinner with distance, thinner still with speed. It was quiet. People who watched him fight saw only that he was rarely surprised.

Compression-adjacent was fourth. Days old. Bad at everything.

Its notice was copied out on the page in front of him, exactly as it had arrived the night after his last circuit bout.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Compression-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: force absorption component. Damage redirect, contact range.
```

He read the words the way he always read them, as if they might have changed overnight. They hadn't. Contact range. Partial. Unknown.

Behind the four sections sat a fifth page, set apart.

It had a date, a short account of an afternoon in Brom's sparring circle, and a single line underneath.

*Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.*

He did not add anything. He never did. He only looked at it, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth, and then he closed the binder and stood.

His knee did not hurt. His shoulder did. That was an improvement, by the log's arithmetic, and he decided to count it as one.

---

The road climbed all morning.

It ran through scrub at first, and then through low oak, and then out into open hill country where the grass had been cropped short by sheep and drystone walls stitched the slopes into uneven squares. The sky was high and pale. Early autumn had put a cool edge on the wind but had not yet taken the green out of anything.

They walked three abreast where the road allowed it and single file where it didn't. They had done this for so long now that none of them noticed the switching anymore.

"How many of them will be Copper?" Lira asked.

She asked it of Brom, not Cael, because Brom was the one of them who had grown up near an academy's kind of thinking. Velmere had been a family that kept tutors.

Brom thought about it for fifty paces.

"Most," he said. "Same as everywhere. Six in ten of anyone who fights for a living is Copper. Most people who Kindle into Copper stay there."

"Most adults," Lira said.

"Most adults. They get to Rank Four or Five and the gains slow down and they stop pushing. They get a trade. They have a family. Copper's enough to work a dock or run a stall. Nobody needs Iron to bake bread."

"I'd like to see Iron bread."

"You'd break a tooth."

Cael listened. He knew the numbers. Everybody in Valdris knew the numbers, the way everybody knew which way the river ran. But he had never heard them said by someone like Brom, who had been raised to think of them as a ladder his family expected him to climb.

Ten ranks in each tier. Rank Ten was the threshold. To cross from one tier to the next, you needed both: the rank earned through use, and a formal evaluation at a registered Arbiter station that confirmed it. Copper. Iron. Bronze. Silver. Gold. After Gold the tiers became history, and then rumor. Most people stopped at the first rung. A quarter made Iron, the journeyman's tier — professional fighters, serious tradespeople, the ones who kept pushing. One in ten made Bronze. Silver was the kind of tier that got your name said in other cities.

"Greyvane's a minor-tier academy," Brom went on. "That means they take Copper and Iron and turn out Bronze, if they're lucky. A Silver graduate would be a good year for them."

"Quenna is Silver," Cael said.

"Quenna is faculty. Faculty is different. They buy faculty." Brom shrugged. "They can't buy students. Students go where the prestige is."

Lira kicked a stone off the road. It bounced down the slope through the sheep, who ignored it.

"Then what are we?" she asked.

She meant herself and Brom. Both were Copper on paper. Both carried an honest rating from Vell's circuit ledger that said something else.

"Iron-equivalent," Brom said.

"Which means what, at an academy?"

It was a fair question. Cael had been chewing on it since Ardenmere.

On the circuit, Iron-equivalent had meant exactly what it said. Vell had rated you by what you did on her floor, against whoever was standing across from you. Beat enough Iron-tier fighters, consistently, and her ledger called you Iron-equivalent, whatever your registry document said. It was a rating of results. The circuit trusted results.

"It means you can beat Iron fighters," he said slowly. "It doesn't mean an Arbiter has said you're Iron. It doesn't give you an Iron rank. It doesn't move you up the ladder."

"So it means nothing."

"It means something. It just means it to people who were watching."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"I'm Copper," she said. "On paper. I'm going to walk in that gate as Copper, and people who never saw me fight are going to look at the word and think they know what it means."

"Yes."

"I hate that."

"I know."

"I'm going to hate it less than I would have a year ago," she said, after a moment. "That's the strange part. I can feel myself hating it less."

Cael glanced at her. She was looking straight up the road, chin a little high.

He thought of himself, by comparison. Iron-equivalent by Vell's honest ledger, because he had beaten an Iron Rank Eight in front of six hundred people. No tier at all on paper. No rank. Not even Copper. The word on his registry file was not a tier. It was a classification the registry did not know what to do with.

He had stopped needing to say this out loud a long time ago. He noticed that he had also stopped needing to think it every hour. That was new. He filed it somewhere in himself to write down later.

---

At the midday rest Lira tried, for perhaps the fifth time since Ardenmere, to get Brom to show her a declaration.

"Just one," she said. "The first one. The one you got at Kindling."

"No."

"I showed you mine."

"You described yours. You never showed me."

"Describing is showing."

"It isn't," Brom said, unbothered. He was eating cold barley from a tin with every sign of contentment. "You know it isn't."

Cael knew it too. He watched the argument the way he watched most arguments between them, with his whole attention and without any intention of joining.

Declarations were the language an Arbiter used. Every practitioner received them, from Kindling on. A small glowing sigil only you could see. Then words, in a form only you could read, telling you what your Path had just given you. A new ability at a new rank. They were the private record of a person's growth, written in a hand nobody else saw.

People did share them. Brothers sometimes. Married couples. A teacher and a student who had trusted each other for years. But you did not ask, any more than you would ask to read someone's letters, and you did not show unless showing meant something.

Lira knew all of this. Lira was asking because she liked watching Brom refuse.

"Tell me the shape, then," she said. "Not the words."

Brom set the tin down.

"There's a header," he said. "It names the Path and the tier and the rank. Then a line with the ability's name in brackets. Then a sentence about what it does. Sometimes two. How far it reaches. When it works. That's all." He picked the tin up again. "It's very plain. People think it'll be poetry. It's more like a receipt."

"A receipt," Lira repeated, delighted.

"A good receipt. It tells you exactly what you paid for."

Cael found himself listening harder than the conversation deserved.

His own notices were nothing like that. He knew the shape of a declaration from books and from Lira's careful descriptions. A Path's name. A tier. A rank. A named ability. His notices began with two words in capitals, *Fragment Acquired*, and gave every ability the same name, which was no name at all. *Unnamed.* Where a declaration would name a Path, his gave an adjacency. Wind-adjacent. Iron-adjacent. Near something, never the thing. Where a declaration gave a tier and rank, his said only *Tier equivalent: unknown.*

A declaration was a receipt. His notices were more like a note left on a table by someone who had been in the room and gone.

"What's yours look like?" Brom asked him suddenly.

Cael blinked. "You've read the log."

"I've read your copies. I've never asked you what it's like when one arrives."

It was, he realized, a very Brom question. Brom had been studying him for most of a year. He had never once asked something he could find out by watching.

"Quiet," Cael said. "No sigil. Nobody's there. It's just suddenly in front of me, like a line someone already wrote. The words don't change if I look away. They're just there."

"Like a ledger."

"Like a ledger."

Brom nodded slowly. "Then you got Vell's version and I got the receipt."

Lira laughed out loud. A pair of sheep looked up at her in mild disapproval.

Cael laughed too, after a moment, more surprised by it than anything. He had spent a year and a half thinking of his notices as a lack. A declaration that had come out wrong. Brom had just set the two side by side as if they were simply two different kinds of record.

He would think about that. Later. Probably in writing.

---

The last stretch of the climb came in the early afternoon.

The road grew better. Somebody had cut the verges back within the month. There was new gravel in the ruts. A wall ran beside them that had been rebuilt in places, the new stones paler than the old. Cael noticed all of it, because maintenance meant somebody whose job was maintenance. A place that kept its road kept other things too.

A hill town showed itself in pieces. Slate roofs. Smoke from cooking fires. A mill wheel somewhere below them, turning slowly, making a deep wooden groan they heard long before they saw it. The wind carried cut grass and wet stone and, under those, bread.

Lira lifted her head like a hound.

"Later," Brom said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You smelled it. I watched you smell it."

The road forked above the town. One branch bent down toward the roofs. The other kept climbing.

"Town or academy?" Lira asked.

"Academy," Cael and Brom said together.

Lira laughed and was ten paces up the hill before either of them moved.

Greyvane came over the brow of the hill a quarter-mile later, and it was small.

That was Cael's first clear thought. Small. Smaller than the word *academy* had promised him in the weeks since Quenna had said it. A wall of old grey stone, plain and weathered, the coursing of a building far older than any school. A gate set into it, newer, oak strapped with iron. Beyond the wall, a few roofs. A training yard with its own wall. A long building with uneven windows. A newer building further back, clean-cut, three floors high, the only thing in sight that looked as if it had been built on purpose for what it was now.

That was all.

A bell rang somewhere inside. Once. Slow and unhurried, marking some hour of the day that had nothing to do with them.

Cael stopped walking.

He did not mean to. His feet simply stopped, and Lira and Brom went on two paces and then stopped too and looked back at him.

"What," said Lira.

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing."

"I'm not cataloguing." He was. He knew he was. The wall was old waystation stone. The gate was two generations newer at most. There was a single clerk's booth set into the gatehouse instead of a guardroom, which meant this place logged people far more than it stopped them. "I'm looking."

"Those are the same thing for you."

"Not always."

She waited. Brom waited. Below them the mill wheel groaned.

He tried to name what he was feeling, and could not, and decided that was all right. The afternoon light was coming in low across the wall. It made the old stone almost gold. A year and a half ago he would not have stopped for that. Ardenmere had taught him to look at a place before deciding what it meant.

"It's small," he said finally.

"It's small," Lira agreed.

"I like that it's small."

Brom looked at the wall for a long moment.

"It's the size it is," he said. "It isn't pretending. That's rarer than big."

They walked the last quarter-mile together.

---

The gate clerk had a list.

She was a woman of about forty, with ink stains on the first two fingers of her right hand and the patient face of someone who copied things all day. She did not stand up. She ran a finger down a page on her desk, found a place, and read.

"Three of you," she said. "Cael. Unclassified observer track. Lira. Re-certification candidate. Brom. Standard enrollment." She looked up. "You're expected. Intake's the first door past the yard. Someone will meet you."

That was all she said.

It was enough.

Cael stood at the booth a half-second longer than the moment needed. He knew it was a small thing. Every institution in the world kept lists. Denvash had kept one. The certification office had written his name on a schedule before he'd ever asked to be written down. The Compact had his name in a registry that ran the length of the continent.

But those lists had all been the same kind. A door opening because a rule said it had to. This was a different kind. Someone in this building had looked at his name, weeks ago, and decided it belonged on the other side of the gate. Had written it down in ink before he arrived. Had trusted that he would come.

Most of his life, he had been handled as paperwork. He had walked into rooms where he had to prove he existed before anyone would hand him a chair.

His name was already here.

"Cael," Lira said quietly, from inside the gate.

He realized he had stopped again.

"Coming," he said.

He walked through behind them. The gate's shadow passed over him and was gone. On the far side, the yard opened up, swept and empty, chalk lines faint on the packed earth. A bell rang again, a different pitch from the first.

He did not know what that bell meant. He would learn. He noticed, with something close to surprise, that he wanted to.

For the first time in a long time, he did not reach for the notebook. He only noticed the feeling, and let it sit there unwritten.

Lira would have called it a miracle.

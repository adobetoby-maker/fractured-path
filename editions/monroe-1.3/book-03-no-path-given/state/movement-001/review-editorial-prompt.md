# Chapter 1 — The Last Climb

Brom knelt in the pine needles with his right hand open, like a man about to push a stuck door.

"Ready," he said.

Cael was not ready. He set his left forearm across his chest anyway, braced it, and nodded.

It was the third morning out of Ardenmere. The fire was down to a red eye in the ashes. Lira was still rolled in her blanket at the edge of the clearing, awake, pretending not to be, because watching them fail before breakfast had become one of her small pleasures on the road. Cold air came down off the hills above the camp and smelled of wet stone.

Brom's palm met his forearm.

The push was slow. It always started slow. Brom let a little Iron Skin harden through his hand first, so the contact arrived like a flat stone rather than a palm, and then he leaned. The force came in steady and even, a fraction of a real strike, the kind of weight a man could count.

Cael waited for it to arrive in full.

That was the first thing he had learned on this road. The fragment did nothing with force that was still coming. It only woke when the force was already his, already inside the bone, and the waking had a feel to it now. A tightening, low in the chest. A sense of something gathering.

He reached for it.

The force gathered. It turned. For a quarter of a heartbeat he held a piece of Brom's push the way a person holds water in cupped hands, and then he had to decide where it went.

It wanted to go down.

He felt it slide toward his left knee, the same way it had slid two days ago. The same lazy path. The joint that still remembered being used as a drain. The memory was so exact that his whole body flinched from it before his mind had finished recognizing it.

He let go.

The gathered piece spilled out of him harmlessly. His forearm took the rest of the push the ordinary way, the way any arm takes weight. Brom eased off.

"You dropped it," Brom said.

"It was going to my knee again."

"You're sure."

"Same road as last time." Cael flexed the knee. Nothing. A faint ache that had been there since the first day, fading, nothing new on top of it. "It wants the nearest joint. If I don't give it somewhere better before it moves, it picks for itself."

Brom considered that. He considered most things the way a smith considers a bar of iron, turning it slowly before deciding what it was good for.

"Then give it somewhere better first," he said.

"That's the whole problem."

"No. That's the whole answer. The problem is you're deciding late." He set his hand again. "Again."

---

They did it eleven more times.

The first three failed cleanly. Nothing caught. The fragment sat in him like a word on the tip of the tongue, present and unreachable, and Brom's push simply arrived and left.

On the fourth he caught something and sent it the wrong way. Not into the knee. Into his own shoulder, the right one, the one the Reydan bout had already used hard. It flared. He hissed through his teeth and Brom stopped at once.

"Shoulder."

"Shoulder," Cael agreed. He rolled it. There was the old complaint, the one that announced itself on certain angles, and over it a new hot line that faded as he breathed. "Better than the knee."

"Better how?"

"The shoulder's already paying. It's an account that's open."

Lira's voice came from the blanket. "That is the worst thing I have ever heard a person say about his own body."

"You're awake."

"I'm always awake. Keep going. I want to see you do it right once before I have to walk all day next to someone sulking."

He did it right on the seventh try.

The force arrived. He waited until it was in the bone. Before it could pick its own road, he chose one for it — out, back along the line it had come in, into Brom's own braced arm — and he held the choice while the fragment gathered.

A quarter of the push went home.

It went back into Brom's wrist with a small, solid knock, like a door shutting in another room. Brom's eyebrows lifted. Under the Iron Skin his forearm had felt it. Cael knew he had felt it, because Brom's own read was better than anyone's at telling where weight had gone.

Then the bill came.

It always came the same way. It ran up through the shoulder and across the breastbone, a band of heat and pressure, as if someone had laid a hot iron bar along his collarbones and leaned on it. For two breaths he could not fill his lungs. He waited them out on his knees in the pine needles.

"There," Brom said quietly. "Quarter."

"Quarter," Cael said, when he could.

He did it twice more before they broke camp. One worked. One didn't. The one that worked cost exactly what the first one had, no more, no less, and he found that oddly comforting. A price that stayed the same was a price he could plan around.

The one that failed went nowhere at all. He had reached a breath too early, while the push was still coming, and the fragment had simply not answered. He was almost grateful. A failure that did nothing was the cheapest lesson the fragment offered.

Lira was sitting up now, blanket around her shoulders, eating the last heel of yesterday's bread. She had watched all of it.

"Three in twelve," she said.

"Three in twelve."

"On a push Brom could do while asleep."

"He'd have to be asleep for me to catch more than a quarter of it."

She grinned. "That's honest, at least."

---

He wrote it up while Brom kicked dirt over the fire.

The Power Log sat open on his knees, a binder now, its rings stiff with cold. A year ago it had been a single slim notebook. He had added pages so many times that the original cover was mostly a memory, and he had long ago given up pretending the binder was tidy. It was not tidy. It was honest. Those had turned out to be different things.

Every fragment had its own section. Every section used the same four headings, because the same four questions had turned out to matter every time.

Function: what it did.

Benefit: what it was good for.

Cost: what it took from him.

Integration: how much of it answered when he called.

He had not invented the format so much as surrendered to it. Every other way of writing about the fragments had eventually broken down into either bragging or panic. The four headings did not allow either.

He turned to the newest section and wrote.

*Compression-adjacent. Road session, third morning. Function: catches force already inside the body and sends it back out. Must wait for the force to arrive. Reaching early gets nothing. Benefit: none in a real fight yet. Cost: shoulder and breastbone, two breaths of not being able to breathe. Same price on every success. Integration: partial. Three of twelve. A quarter of a slow push each time.*

He paused. Then he added the line that mattered most.

*If I don't choose the road before the force moves, it chooses the nearest joint. Knee on day one. Shoulder today. Choose first. Always choose first.*

He read it back, then turned to the front of the binder, where the oldest notices were copied out.

There were four confirmed fragments now. He went through them out of habit, the way some people touch a pocket to make sure a coin is still there.

Wind-adjacent was first. Lira's. An evasion framework he had once needed a full second to assemble and now did without thinking, reading a strike off an opponent's body a half-beat before it landed. Its range was the length of a fight. Its price was fatigue, and a choice of direction he had to make before he moved. Against faster opponents, it read less. He had shown it on the circuit a hundred times.

Pressure-adjacent was second. A redirect, borrowed from a Bronze-tier fighter named Feryn who had beaten him fairly. Three clean uses mapped, each paid for in the right shoulder. It worked at contact and nowhere else. Nobody outside the three of them had ever been told it existed.

Iron-adjacent was third. Brom's. A pressure-read along the skin that told him where a person's weight was about to go. It worked at contact and out to a few paces, thinner with distance, thinner still with speed. It was quiet. People who watched him fight saw only that he was rarely surprised.

Compression-adjacent was fourth. Days old. Bad at everything.

Its notice was copied out on the page in front of him, exactly as it had arrived the night after his last circuit bout.

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Compression-adjacent. Duration: sustained. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
Note: force absorption component. Damage redirect, contact range.
```

He read the words the way he always read them, as if they might have changed overnight. They hadn't. Contact range. Partial. Unknown.

Behind the four sections sat a fifth page, set apart.

It had a date, a short account of an afternoon in Brom's sparring circle, and a single line underneath.

*Session nine. Could not reproduce. Still don't know what that was.*

He did not add anything. He never did. He only looked at it, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth, and then he closed the binder and stood.

His knee did not hurt. His shoulder did. That was an improvement, by the log's arithmetic, and he decided to count it as one.

---

The road climbed all morning.

It ran through scrub at first, and then through low oak, and then out into open hill country where the grass had been cropped short by sheep and drystone walls stitched the slopes into uneven squares. The sky was high and pale. Early autumn had put a cool edge on the wind but had not yet taken the green out of anything.

They walked three abreast where the road allowed it and single file where it didn't. They had done this for so long now that none of them noticed the switching anymore.

"How many of them will be Copper?" Lira asked.

She asked it of Brom, not Cael, because Brom was the one of them who had grown up near an academy's kind of thinking. Velmere had been a family that kept tutors.

Brom thought about it for fifty paces.

"Most," he said. "Same as everywhere. Six in ten of anyone who fights for a living is Copper. Most people who Kindle into Copper stay there."

"Most adults," Lira said.

"Most adults. They get to Rank Four or Five and the gains slow down and they stop pushing. They get a trade. They have a family. Copper's enough to work a dock or run a stall. Nobody needs Iron to bake bread."

"I'd like to see Iron bread."

"You'd break a tooth."

Cael listened. He knew the numbers. Everybody in Valdris knew the numbers, the way everybody knew which way the river ran. But he had never heard them said by someone like Brom, who had been raised to think of them as a ladder his family expected him to climb.

Ten ranks in each tier. Rank Ten was the threshold. To cross from one tier to the next, you needed both: the rank earned through use, and a formal evaluation at a registered Arbiter station that confirmed it. Copper. Iron. Bronze. Silver. Gold. After Gold the tiers became history, and then rumor. Most people stopped at the first rung. A quarter made Iron, the journeyman's tier — professional fighters, serious tradespeople, the ones who kept pushing. One in ten made Bronze. Silver was the kind of tier that got your name said in other cities.

"Greyvane's a minor-tier academy," Brom went on. "That means they take Copper and Iron and turn out Bronze, if they're lucky. A Silver graduate would be a good year for them."

"Quenna is Silver," Cael said.

"Quenna is faculty. Faculty is different. They buy faculty." Brom shrugged. "They can't buy students. Students go where the prestige is."

Lira kicked a stone off the road. It bounced down the slope through the sheep, who ignored it.

"Then what are we?" she asked.

She meant herself and Brom. Both were Copper on paper. Both carried an honest rating from Vell's circuit ledger that said something else.

"Iron-equivalent," Brom said.

"Which means what, at an academy?"

It was a fair question. Cael had been chewing on it since Ardenmere.

On the circuit, Iron-equivalent had meant exactly what it said. Vell had rated you by what you did on her floor, against whoever was standing across from you. Beat enough Iron-tier fighters, consistently, and her ledger called you Iron-equivalent, whatever your registry document said. It was a rating of results. The circuit trusted results.

"It means you can beat Iron fighters," he said slowly. "It doesn't mean an Arbiter has said you're Iron. It doesn't give you an Iron rank. It doesn't move you up the ladder."

"So it means nothing."

"It means something. It just means it to people who were watching."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"I'm Copper," she said. "On paper. I'm going to walk in that gate as Copper, and people who never saw me fight are going to look at the word and think they know what it means."

"Yes."

"I hate that."

"I know."

"I'm going to hate it less than I would have a year ago," she said, after a moment. "That's the strange part. I can feel myself hating it less."

Cael glanced at her. She was looking straight up the road, chin a little high.

He thought of himself, by comparison. Iron-equivalent by Vell's honest ledger, because he had beaten an Iron Rank Eight in front of six hundred people. No tier at all on paper. No rank. Not even Copper. The word on his registry file was not a tier. It was a classification the registry did not know what to do with.

He had stopped needing to say this out loud a long time ago. He noticed that he had also stopped needing to think it every hour. That was new. He filed it somewhere in himself to write down later.

---

At the midday rest Lira tried, for perhaps the fifth time since Ardenmere, to get Brom to show her a declaration.

"Just one," she said. "The first one. The one you got at Kindling."

"No."

"I showed you mine."

"You described yours. You never showed me."

"Describing is showing."

"It isn't," Brom said, unbothered. He was eating cold barley from a tin with every sign of contentment. "You know it isn't."

Cael knew it too. He watched the argument the way he watched most arguments between them, with his whole attention and without any intention of joining.

Declarations were the language an Arbiter used. Every practitioner received them, from Kindling on. A small glowing sigil only you could see. Then words, in a form only you could read, telling you what your Path had just given you. A new ability at a new rank. They were the private record of a person's growth, written in a hand nobody else saw.

People did share them. Brothers sometimes. Married couples. A teacher and a student who had trusted each other for years. But you did not ask, any more than you would ask to read someone's letters, and you did not show unless showing meant something.

Lira knew all of this. Lira was asking because she liked watching Brom refuse.

"Tell me the shape, then," she said. "Not the words."

Brom set the tin down.

"There's a header," he said. "It names the Path and the tier and the rank. Then a line with the ability's name in brackets. Then a sentence about what it does. Sometimes two. How far it reaches. When it works. That's all." He picked the tin up again. "It's very plain. People think it'll be poetry. It's more like a receipt."

"A receipt," Lira repeated, delighted.

"A good receipt. It tells you exactly what you paid for."

Cael found himself listening harder than the conversation deserved.

His own notices were nothing like that. He knew the shape of a declaration from books and from Lira's careful descriptions. A Path's name. A tier. A rank. A named ability. His notices began with two words in capitals, *Fragment Acquired*, and gave every ability the same name, which was no name at all. *Unnamed.* Where a declaration would name a Path, his gave an adjacency. Wind-adjacent. Iron-adjacent. Near something, never the thing. Where a declaration gave a tier and rank, his said only *Tier equivalent: unknown.*

A declaration was a receipt. His notices were more like a note left on a table by someone who had been in the room and gone.

"What's yours look like?" Brom asked him suddenly.

Cael blinked. "You've read the log."

"I've read your copies. I've never asked you what it's like when one arrives."

It was, he realized, a very Brom question. Brom had been studying him for most of a year. He had never once asked something he could find out by watching.

"Quiet," Cael said. "No sigil. Nobody's there. It's just suddenly in front of me, like a line someone already wrote. The words don't change if I look away. They're just there."

"Like a ledger."

"Like a ledger."

Brom nodded slowly. "Then you got Vell's version and I got the receipt."

Lira laughed out loud. A pair of sheep looked up at her in mild disapproval.

Cael laughed too, after a moment, more surprised by it than anything. He had spent a year and a half thinking of his notices as a lack. A declaration that had come out wrong. Brom had just set the two side by side as if they were simply two different kinds of record.

He would think about that. Later. Probably in writing.

---

The last stretch of the climb came in the early afternoon.

The road grew better. Somebody had cut the verges back within the month. There was new gravel in the ruts. A wall ran beside them that had been rebuilt in places, the new stones paler than the old. Cael noticed all of it, because maintenance meant somebody whose job was maintenance. A place that kept its road kept other things too.

A hill town showed itself in pieces. Slate roofs. Smoke from cooking fires. A mill wheel somewhere below them, turning slowly, making a deep wooden groan they heard long before they saw it. The wind carried cut grass and wet stone and, under those, bread.

Lira lifted her head like a hound.

"Later," Brom said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You smelled it. I watched you smell it."

The road forked above the town. One branch bent down toward the roofs. The other kept climbing.

"Town or academy?" Lira asked.

"Academy," Cael and Brom said together.

Lira laughed and was ten paces up the hill before either of them moved.

Greyvane came over the brow of the hill a quarter-mile later, and it was small.

That was Cael's first clear thought. Small. Smaller than the word *academy* had promised him in the weeks since Quenna had said it. A wall of old grey stone, plain and weathered, the coursing of a building far older than any school. A gate set into it, newer, oak strapped with iron. Beyond the wall, a few roofs. A training yard with its own wall. A long building with uneven windows. A newer building further back, clean-cut, three floors high, the only thing in sight that looked as if it had been built on purpose for what it was now.

That was all.

A bell rang somewhere inside. Once. Slow and unhurried, marking some hour of the day that had nothing to do with them.

Cael stopped walking.

He did not mean to. His feet simply stopped, and Lira and Brom went on two paces and then stopped too and looked back at him.

"What," said Lira.

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing."

"I'm not cataloguing." He was. He knew he was. The wall was old waystation stone. The gate was two generations newer at most. There was a single clerk's booth set into the gatehouse instead of a guardroom, which meant this place logged people far more than it stopped them. "I'm looking."

"Those are the same thing for you."

"Not always."

She waited. Brom waited. Below them the mill wheel groaned.

He tried to name what he was feeling, and could not, and decided that was all right. The afternoon light was coming in low across the wall. It made the old stone almost gold. A year and a half ago he would not have stopped for that. Ardenmere had taught him to look at a place before deciding what it meant.

"It's small," he said finally.

"It's small," Lira agreed.

"I like that it's small."

Brom looked at the wall for a long moment.

"It's the size it is," he said. "It isn't pretending. That's rarer than big."

They walked the last quarter-mile together.

---

The gate clerk had a list.

She was a woman of about forty, with ink stains on the first two fingers of her right hand and the patient face of someone who copied things all day. She did not stand up. She ran a finger down a page on her desk, found a place, and read.

"Three of you," she said. "Cael. Unclassified observer track. Lira. Re-certification candidate. Brom. Standard enrollment." She looked up. "You're expected. Intake's the first door past the yard. Someone will meet you."

That was all she said.

It was enough.

Cael stood at the booth a half-second longer than the moment needed. He knew it was a small thing. Every institution in the world kept lists. Denvash had kept one. The certification office had written his name on a schedule before he'd ever asked to be written down. The Compact had his name in a registry that ran the length of the continent.

But those lists had all been the same kind. A door opening because a rule said it had to. This was a different kind. Someone in this building had looked at his name, weeks ago, and decided it belonged on the other side of the gate. Had written it down in ink before he arrived. Had trusted that he would come.

Most of his life, he had been handled as paperwork. He had walked into rooms where he had to prove he existed before anyone would hand him a chair.

His name was already here.

"Cael," Lira said quietly, from inside the gate.

He realized he had stopped again.

"Coming," he said.

He walked through behind them. The gate's shadow passed over him and was gone. On the far side, the yard opened up, swept and empty, chalk lines faint on the packed earth. A bell rang again, a different pitch from the first.

He did not know what that bell meant. He would learn. He noticed, with something close to surprise, that he wanted to.

For the first time in a long time, he did not reach for the notebook. He only noticed the feeling, and let it sit there unwritten.

Lira would have called it a miracle.

# Chapter 2 — Three Lines

The intake room had three desks, and that told Cael most of what he needed to know before anyone spoke.

It was a low old room built from the same grey stone as the wall. The ceiling beams were close enough that Brom ducked under the first one out of caution and then looked faintly embarrassed when he saw he hadn't needed to. A bench ran along the back wall. Four other arrivals sat on it with bags at their feet, waiting their turns. A window let in the afternoon, and the light lay across the floorboards in a long pale square.

Three desks. Three clerks. Three lines, each with a small painted board hung above it.

*Standard Enrollment.*

*Re-certification.*

The third board was blank.

"That's you," Lira murmured.

"It might be for transfers."

"There's no transfer board because transfers go in the standard line. I asked the gate woman while you were having your moment." She nudged him. "Blank board. That's you."

He looked at it. A board somebody had hung up and then not known what to paint on.

It was, he thought, the most accurate sign anyone had ever made about him.

---

Brom went first, because his line was empty.

His clerk was a brisk young woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. She took his papers, scanned them, and turned them face up on the desk in a single smooth motion. Copper-tier certification from a registered Arbiter station. Iron Skin Path. A clean record of two seasons' documented circuit work, with Vell's stamp at the bottom of every page.

"Standard," she said. "Good."

She wrote. She stamped. She asked him two questions — his age, and whether he had any injuries the floor instructors should know about — and wrote the answers in a neat column. She checked something on a list. She stamped again.

Cael, waiting on the bench, counted the time without meaning to. He counted most things without meaning to.

Eleven minutes.

At the end of the eleven minutes the clerk reached into a drawer and set a small metal disc on the desk in front of Brom. It was the size of a coin and stamped with the academy's crest. A tower above a road, very simply cut.

"Your enrollment tag," she said. "It opens the training hall, the lecture wing, the residence wing and the library. Keep it on you. Supper's at the seventh bell. Orientation for new students is tomorrow after the first. Your room is in the residence wing, upper corridor, third door on the left. Your name's on it."

Brom looked at the tag.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it."

He picked it up and turned it once in his fingers. His face did something Cael had not seen it do before. On anyone else it would have been a smile. On Brom it was a kind of quiet astonishment, as if he'd put his weight on a step expecting it to creak and it had simply held.

He came over to the bench. He held the tag up between two fingers.

"Eleven minutes," Cael said.

"You counted."

"I count everything."

Brom sat down beside him. He looked at the tag again.

"Two years," he said, "I've been the complicated one. Every room. Every hall. My family. The circuit. You can't just be a Copper Iron Skin from a good estate who walked away from the estate. There's always a question after." He shook his head slowly. "She didn't ask the question."

"Maybe she didn't know there was one."

"Maybe there isn't one here."

He put the tag in his pocket. A moment later he took it out again and looked at it, as if it might have changed its mind. It hadn't. He put it away properly.

"I'm going to find my room," he said. "I've never had a door with my name on it."

He left. Cael watched him go and found that he was smiling.

---

Lira's line took most of an hour.

Her clerk was an older man with half-moon spectacles and the slow, careful manner of someone who had handled difficult paper before. He did not hurry. He took a thin file out of a box at his elbow — her Fenmark file, Cael realized. It had arrived here before she had. Academies talked to one another about the students they had thrown out.

The clerk laid the file open beside a printed sheet of rules. He compared them, line by line, moving a finger down each in turn. Sometimes he made a small mark. Sometimes he stopped and read a line twice.

Lira stood in front of the desk with her arms folded.

She did not sit, although there was a chair. She did not look at the file. Cael knew that stance. He had seen her take it across a sparring circle a hundred times, waiting out an opponent she hadn't finished measuring. Weight even. Shoulders loose. Giving nothing away.

The bench emptied and filled again. A boy with a Blade Path crest on his collar went through the standard line in nine minutes. A tall girl was sent away to fetch a missing letter and came back flushed.

At last the clerk set a single sheet on the desk in front of Lira and turned it to face her.

"I'm required to read this aloud," he said, not unkindly. "Each clause. You'll confirm you understand it."

"I can read."

"I know you can. It's the procedure."

She nodded once.

Cael was close enough to see the heading without meaning to. *Acknowledgment of Prior Standing.*

The clerk read it out in a flat, even voice. It said that her enrollment at Fenmark Academy had been terminated. It said that the termination stood as recorded. It said that her candidacy for re-certification at Greyvane was granted on the understanding that she accepted that record as accurate, and did not dispute it as a condition of entry.

"Do you understand the clause?"

"Yes."

"There is a separate process for a formal dispute, should you wish to pursue one later. It is not connected to your enrollment here. It would go through channels outside this academy." He tapped a small box at the bottom of the sheet, unmarked. "You may note an intention to dispute. It doesn't affect your candidacy."

"I understand."

He turned the sheet toward her a little further and laid a pen beside it.

She read it again herself. Slowly.

Her jaw did most of the talking. Cael watched a small muscle work at the hinge of it, tightening and easing and tightening again. Her face was otherwise perfectly still. Two years of a thing were being held down, by force, to a size that fit an intake desk.

She had spent those two years refusing to say *expelled* without saying *wrongly* right after it. Cael had heard her do it in Ardenmere to strangers who had not even asked.

She picked up the pen.

She signed. She did not mark the box.

The clerk blotted the signature and put the sheet in the file and the file in the box. Then he gave her a disc like Brom's. It carried the crest too. Under the crest, small and neat, someone had stamped a single raised dot.

She looked at the dot for a moment, then put the tag in her pocket without a word.

When she came over to the bench she sat down beside Cael and stared at the opposite wall.

"Well," she said.

"You didn't mark the box."

"No."

"You could have."

"I know." She breathed out. "I could have marked it and fought about it later. I could have stood there and told that nice man why Fenmark was wrong. I've got the speech. I've had the speech for two years." She turned her head and looked at him. "I want a place here more than I want to win an argument on paper about something that happened when I was fourteen."

He waited.

"That's new," she said. "For me. Wanting the one thing more than the other."

"How does it feel?"

She was quiet a long time.

"I thought it would feel like losing again," she said. "Like that day. Standing in a room while somebody reads you what you are." She looked down at her hands. "It doesn't. It feels like setting a pack down at the end of a road. I didn't know how much it weighed until it was on the ground."

She stood up abruptly, the way she did when a feeling threatened to become a conversation.

"Go and see what they make of you," she said. "I'll be on the wall outside."

---

Nobody called him forward. There was no line for him to stand in.

Instead, as the old clerk finished stacking Lira's box, a door at the side of the room opened and Quenna came through it.

She was exactly as he remembered her from the Ironyard. About forty. Dark hair cut short and plain. A grey coat with no ornament on it at all. She carried a leather folder under one arm and the faint air of a woman who had been arguing with someone in another room and had won, but only just.

"Cael," she said. "Come and sit."

She did not hand him to a clerk. She led him to the third desk, the one under the blank board, and sat on the far side of it herself.

He registered that. It could be read two ways. A courtesy — she had brought him here and meant to see him through it. Or a warning — he was the kind of case that could not be trusted to a clerk.

He decided, after a moment, that it was both, and that both were reasonable.

"This will take some time," she said. "I'm going to be honest with you about why. There isn't a form for you."

"I noticed the board."

The corner of her mouth moved. "The carpenter asked what to paint on it. Nobody could tell him."

The afternoon went in pieces.

Papers came to the desk in twos and threes. A letter of acceptance. A copy of his circuit record, with Vell's stamp. A form that asked for his Path and tier and rank, which Quenna read, frowned at, and set aside. Another form that asked the same questions in different words. A third, older, with a line for "registry document attached," which led to a long, quiet search through her folder.

His registry document was in there. She had obtained a copy from somewhere. He saw the corner of it as she leafed past. He saw the word in brackets before she covered it again.

Twice she excused herself and went through the side door. Through it, faintly, he heard her voice and another voice, low and patient, going back and forth. The words did not carry. The tone did. It was the tone of two people trying very hard to fit a large thing through a small opening without breaking either.

A boy from the kitchen brought tea on a tray. Cael thanked him and held the cup in both hands and forgot to drink it.

The square of light moved off the back wall and onto the floor. It slid toward the desk legs. It thinned.

The other clerks finished. The man with the half-moon spectacles stacked his last box and nodded to Quenna on his way out. The young woman who had enrolled Brom nodded too, and gave Cael a look he read easily. *Better you than me.* The bench emptied. Somebody, somewhere, lit a lamp.

He did not mind.

That surprised him. He had braced to mind. He had imagined this afternoon a dozen times on the road, and in every version he had been angry by now, or frightened, or both.

He was neither. He watched Quenna come back from her second argument with a fresh sheet in her hand and her mouth set, and he understood what he was looking at. Not a wall. Not someone trying to keep him out. It was a building straining, in good faith, to find words for him.

He had been described in bad faith before. In Denvash. In the market square. In a hundred small moments on the circuit before Vell's ledger gave people something better to go on.

Good faith was slower. It was worth a whole afternoon.

---

"Here," Quenna said at last.

She turned a long printed form toward him. It had his name at the top in a clerk's careful hand. It had a seal pressed into the bottom corner. It had a signature line, blank, with a pen laid neatly beside it.

Above the signature line was a box labeled *Category of Enrollment*.

The box said *unclassified observer.*

He read it.

Beneath the category, in smaller type, a printed note explained what the category was. It was reserved for any prospective practitioner who *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment*. The note went on. Such students were usually children whose families had asked for early schooling before their certification appointments. The category granted lecture attendance and training under supervision. It granted academic standing. It did not grant a Path, a tier, a rank, or an Arbiter-issued record of any kind.

He read the category line again.

*Unclassified observer.*

He reached for the tea. It had gone lukewarm. He set it down without drinking.

He read the category line a third time. He read the gloss under it. *Has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.*

The tea was colder when he looked at it again. He did not reach for it this time.

"I underwent it," he said. His voice came out quieter than he meant it to. "I was fourteen. Denvash. There's a record."

"There is," Quenna said.

"Then this is wrong."

"There's a record with a classification on it that the Compact's own schedule doesn't define." She said it evenly. She did not soften it. She had never softened anything she'd said to him. "This category exists for anyone without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter. The provision's language says exactly that. A child who hasn't Kindled has no standard classification. Neither do you. Yours isn't standard. It isn't anything the schedule accounts for at all."

"So you found a gap."

"I didn't make it. It was already there. I'm only using it."

He looked at the line again.

"It says something that isn't true," he said. "In ink."

"It's an accurate use of the words," Quenna said. "Every word on that page will hold up, exactly as written. That matters. That's what protects you." She leaned forward slightly. "What doesn't hold up as well is the purpose. The people who drafted that provision were thinking of twelve-year-olds whose parents wanted them to sit in on lectures. They were not thinking of you. Someday someone will read this form more carefully than a clerk does. They will ask about the purpose."

"So someone will come and read it properly."

"Yes."

She did not hesitate over it. He was grateful.

"And then?"

"Then we need to be established fact before they get here." She laid one finger on the seal in the corner. "A student with a monthly assessment record. A demonstration record. Public, dated, signed by people who watched. Every month you are simply here, doing well, on the record, it gets harder for anyone to argue that you shouldn't be."

"And if they argue anyway?"

"Then they'll have to argue their way past a stack of dated pages first." She sat back. "I've backed worse odds than these, Cael. I don't back anything I expect to lose."

He looked down at the form for a long time.

He thought of the four other people the registry had ever written his word against. Four cases. All of them gone within weeks. He did not know their names. He knew the number the way he knew his own heartbeat. He had carried it since he was fourteen.

This form would not make him a fifth. It would make him something else. Not a known problem with a file. An error the registry had never been asked to hold.

It was a strange kind of progress. He wasn't sure it was better. He was fairly sure it was the only door open.

He picked up the pen.

He signed in the careful hand he used for the Power Log. The hand that had learned that things which were meant to last deserved precision, even when precision couldn't fix what was underneath.

The ink dried.

Nobody at the desk said anything about the distance between the words and the truth. Quenna knew. He knew. The empty room seemed to know. Saying it again would not have made the page more honest.

Quenna took a disc from her folder and slid it across to him.

It was the same size as Brom's. It had no crest. Where the crest should have been, someone had stamped a hollow circle, a ring with nothing in the middle.

"Provisional access," she said. "It opens what Brom's opens. Anyone who checks it will learn that the reason isn't theirs to ask."

He turned it over. The back was blank.

"Welcome to Greyvane," Quenna said. There was something under the flatness of it that was almost gentle. "Supper's at the seventh bell. You've missed the sixth. I'd eat."

---

Lira and Brom were on a low wall outside the intake building, in the last of the light.

They sat a little apart from each other, and the gap between them was exactly his width. He saw it from twenty paces off. It did something to his chest. They had sat on walls like this in more towns than he could count, and he had never once had to ask where to sit.

He sat.

"Well?" said Lira.

"I'm an unclassified observer," he said. "In writing. With a signature."

Brom considered that the way he considered a new kind of hold. "That isn't you."

"No."

"Do you mind?"

Cael thought about it honestly. It was the only way he knew how to think about things that mattered.

"Less than I expected," he said. "The words hold up. Quenna's right about that. I've spent so long being something the paperwork had no name for that one more wrong form hardly shows." He paused. "I'm still going to write about it."

"You'll write two pages," Lira said. "Possibly three."

"For what it's worth," Brom said, "the form isn't the interesting part."

"What's the interesting part?"

"That they built anything at all." Brom looked across the yard at the old wall going grey in the dusk. "Most places don't build you a door. They let you walk around the outside until you figure out there isn't one. This one's the wrong shape. Fine. You've been getting through wrong-shaped things since I met you."

Lira stared at him. "Did you just say something wise before supper?"

"I'm hungry. It loosens me up."

"Show me the tag," she said to Cael.

He held it out. She took it and tilted it toward the last of the sky. The hollow circle caught the light.

"Huh," she said. "A ring with nothing in it."

"Quenna says it means the reason isn't theirs to ask."

"It means *please don't ask*." She handed it back. "Mine has a dot. Brom's has a tower. You have a hole." She sounded, very slightly, pleased. "We're a set."

Brom took his own tag out, as if to check the tower was still there. It was.

A bell rang somewhere beyond the training yard. It was not the seventh bell. Cael could tell by the pitch. Something earlier, some signal in a rhythm he had not yet learned.

He got the binder out anyway.

He wrote it plainly, without decoration, in the hand he saved for things he meant to keep.

*Enrolled. The word is doing a lot of work.*

He looked at the line. Then he added a second one beneath it, smaller.

*It isn't doing it alone.*

Lira read it over his shoulder without asking, the way she always had. She didn't say anything. She bumped his arm with hers, once, and then the seventh bell rang, and the three of them got up and went to find out what an academy fed people.

---

It was barley.

Barley and onions and a little mutton, ladled out in a long low room that had clearly been a stable once. The old hay door was bricked up but you could still see its outline in the wall. Students sat on benches at trestle tables in loose groups. Most had already eaten and were talking over empty bowls. A few looked up when the three of them came in. Most didn't.

Brom ate two bowls with an expression of deep contentment.

"It's honest food," he said, when Lira looked at him. "It doesn't pretend to be anything."

"It's porridge with ambitions."

"It's barley. Barley has never once lied to me."

"Neither has a rock," Lira said, "and I don't eat those either."

Cael ate. It was hot and plain and there was enough of it. He was more tired than he had known. Three days of walking and a whole afternoon of sitting still had left him hollow in a way the barley filled quite well.

Afterward, the three of them climbed to the residence wing.

The corridors bent where no builder would have bent them on purpose. They followed the shape of whatever the building had been before it was a school. The doors were of six or seven different ages and woods. Somebody had painted names on small boards beside each one.

Brom's was across the corridor from his. Lira's was two doors down.

Cael noticed this. He also noticed, as they passed the stair, a roster pinned to the wall that showed rooms empty in two other halls. Someone had put the three of them together. Someone had decided, before they arrived, that they were a unit.

Quenna, almost certainly.

He did not mention it. He suspected the others had noticed too, and that none of them wanted to say it aloud and make it smaller.

"Good night," Lira said at her door.

"Good night."

Brom stood in front of his own door for a moment before opening it. He read his name on the board. Then he went in and shut the door softly behind him.

---

The room was narrow and whitewashed.

A bed. A desk. A shelf. A single window looking out on the training yard's wall, which was blank and grey and asked nothing of anybody. Cael set his pack down and sat on the bed and listened.

A building he didn't know. Feet on a stair. A latch somewhere. Voices through the wall, too faint for words. A door closing. Water in a pipe.

He would learn all of these sounds. In a week he would know which latch was which and whose feet those were. He had done it in Ardenmere. He had done it at Hesk's, long before that, without ever thinking of it as work.

Tonight he did not know any of them. Tonight they were simply the sounds of a place that had decided to have him.

He lit the lamp. He took out a sheet of paper.

He had made a promise. *I'll write when I arrive.* He had made it in a boarding house in Ardenmere with Hesk's letter open beside him. He had arrived. That was all there was to it.

He wrote slowly.

*Hesk —*

*I'm here. Greyvane is small. Smaller than I imagined. There's an old wall, older than the school, and a hill town below it with a mill and a baker Lira has already smelled from a quarter-mile away.*

*The gate clerk had our names on a list. All three of them. I don't know how to explain why that mattered so much. I think you'll understand without my explaining it.*

He paused. He thought about leaving out the form.

He did not leave it out. Hesk had never once asked him to make anything smaller than it was.

*They've enrolled me under a category meant for children who haven't Kindled yet. It's called unclassified observer. It isn't true. Everybody at the table knew it wasn't true. The woman who brought me here says the words will hold up, and I think she's right. I signed it. I want you to know I signed it with my eyes open.*

*Brom got through intake in eleven minutes. He didn't know what to do with himself. Lira signed a paper that said Fenmark was right about her, and she says it felt like putting something down. I'm not sure what it felt like for me yet. I'll tell you when I know.*

*You told me to find more people who have seen me clearly and haven't left. I haven't found new ones yet. But I came through the gate with two, and there's a woman here who argued with someone in a back room all afternoon to find a word for me. That might be the start of a third.*

*I'm well. The shoulder still complains on certain angles. The new thing I wrote you about is coming along slowly, and it hurts, and I think that means it's working.*

*I'll write again after the first assessment.*

*Cael*

He read it over. It was not long. Hesk's letters were never long either. He folded it, wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the desk to post in the town below.

Then he opened the binder to the back pages, where he kept the things that were not fragments.

He looked at what he had written on the wall. *Enrolled. The word is doing a lot of work.*

He thought about the board above the third desk. The blank one. A carpenter asking what to paint, and nobody knowing.

He thought, for the first time with no fear in it at all, that he would rather be a blank board somebody had bothered to hang than a painted one in a building that wanted him gone.

He wrote that down too. Then he blew out the lamp and lay in the dark, listening to the building he didn't know yet, and fell asleep before he had learned a single sound.

# Chapter 3 — The Ledger

He worked on the question all through breakfast.

It was not a hard question to ask. It was a hard question to ask well. He had learned that the first shape a question took was usually the shape that made it easiest to answer badly.

*Why did you take me?* That was too small. It invited a small answer. A kindness. A shrug. Something that would not survive a bad month.

*What do you get out of this?* That was too sharp for a first meeting with the man who ran the building he slept in.

He tried four more versions in the margin of the binder while Lira stole the crust off his bread and Brom ate his own porridge with slow, serious attention. None of them were right. Each one leaned too far toward himself.

He found it on the walk across the yard.

*Why did this academy take the risk?*

Not *me*. The risk. A man could tell a kind story about a boy. It was harder to tell a kind story about a business decision with sixty years of ledgers behind it. If Cael asked about the decision, he might get the ledgers.

He wanted the ledgers.

---

He had to sit through orientation first.

It was held in the main hall after the first bell, for the eleven students who had arrived that week, and it was run by a senior instructor with a carrying voice and a long wooden pointer he never once used to point at anything. He used it instead to tap the floor at the end of each rule, as if nailing it down. Cael sat at the end of the second bench with Lira on one side and Brom on the other, and wrote.

The bells came first. Then meals. Then lamps. Then the rule about the residence wing after the eighth bell, which was that there was no residence wing after the eighth bell, only your own room, and anyone found in the corridors would be asked politely where they were going and then not so politely why.

Then the standings.

"Greyvane keeps a board," the instructor said, and tapped the floor. "You'll find it in the formal yard. Every student with full standing is on it. You are ranked by your record this term, within your cohort. Not by your tier. Not by your rank. By what you do on our floors, against our students, in bouts we schedule and record." Tap. "A Copper student with a good term can sit above an Iron student with a poor one. It happens. It happens often enough that the Iron students learn to take it seriously."

A boy on the front bench put up his hand. "Who's first?"

"An Iron Rank Two in his third year. Glass Path. He's been first for some time." The instructor's voice did not change, but something in it suggested that he had been asked this question by every intake for a while now. "You'll meet him. If you want his place, the board is open."

Lira made a small, interested sound under her breath. Cael glanced at her. She was looking at nothing, with the face she wore when she was setting something aside for later.

"Advancement," the instructor went on. Tap. "Some of you will reach Rank Ten in a tier while you're with us. When you do, your instructor will certify the rank, and you will walk down the hill to the certification station in the town, which is a registered Arbiter station, and you will be evaluated. If the evaluation confirms it, you cross. If it doesn't, you come back and keep working, and nobody here will think less of you for it." Tap. "Rank is earned on our floors. Tier is confirmed down there. We do not confuse the two, and neither should you."

Cael wrote that down carefully. *Rank is earned. Tier is confirmed.* He underlined *confirmed*.

He knew there was a station in every town of any size. He had walked into one in Denvash at fourteen, with Hesk's hand on his shoulder, and walked out with a word in brackets. He had not known until this moment that this hill town had one too, a half-hour's walk below the wall, where children from the observer track would go to be Kindled and students from the standard track would go to be measured, and where he would never, so far as he could see, have any reason to go at all.

He found that he was looking at his own tag. The hollow circle. Nothing to certify. No tier to confirm.

Brom shifted on the bench beside him, very slightly, so that their shoulders touched. He did not look over. He just stayed there, a solid weight, until the instructor had moved on to the rules for the library.

At the end the instructor read out the tracks. Standard. Re-certification. Transfer. And then, after the smallest pause, "Observer," and two names: a girl Cael did not know, and his own.

Somebody on the front bench turned around to look. Then a second person did.

Cael looked back at them, pleasantly, until they turned away again.

"You enjoyed that," Lira murmured.

"A little."

"Good," she said. "You'll need to."

---

Provost Naveth kept his office at the top of the old building, up a narrow stair worn into a dip at the center of every step.

The door stood open. Cael knocked on the frame anyway.

"Come," said a voice. "Sit. The chair is for you. It's for whoever comes in, but this morning that's you."

The office was small and very orderly. One desk with nothing on it but a blotter, a pen, and a stack of three letters squared to the corner. Two chairs, one on each side. No third. A man who rarely saw people in groups, then. A tall window behind the desk looked out over the training yard rather than the road.

Cael noted the window before he noted the man. It told him which way this man paid attention.

Naveth was in his sixties. Lean, grey, with a long face that had been weathered by some larger place before it came here. His hands rested flat on the blotter. He did not stand.

"You're the observer," he said.

"Cael."

"I know your name. I signed the acceptance." He gestured at the chair again, and Cael sat. "You've come with a question. I can see it on you. You've been carrying it up the stairs like a bucket you don't want to spill. Ask it."

Cael asked it.

"Why did this academy take the risk?"

Naveth did not answer right away.

He looked at Cael for a long moment. It was not the look Cael was used to from assessors and officials. That look measured him against a category and found the fit wrong. This was more like the look Hesk gave a bracket before deciding whether to hang real weight on it.

"That's a good question," Naveth said at last. "Better than the one most people ask. Most people ask why I took them. You've asked about the institution. That tells me you want to know whether I'm a gambler or a fool."

"I didn't say that."

"No. You were too careful to say it. It's still the right thing to wonder." He leaned back. The chair took his weight with the worn creak of furniture that had been sat in exactly this way for many years. "Let me tell you where I come from. Then I'll show you something. Then you can decide which I am."

---

He talked the way some men walked: steadily, never in a hurry, always certain of the next step.

"Eleven years I ran a mid-tier academy," he said. "A good one. Silver prospects every few years. Iron-tier graduates who went on to Bronze within a decade. Every year, two or three of my best would leave for somewhere bigger. We hadn't failed them. We'd done the job too well. When you do it well at that level, somebody larger notices and makes a better offer. I became very good at raising things for other people to keep."

"So you came here."

"Not at once. Then ten years elsewhere, which I won't inflict on you. A fair part of them I called being between posts. The true name for it was not knowing what I wanted to build." He said it plainly. There was no polish on the story. It had not been told so often that its edges had worn smooth. "But yes. Eventually, here. And it took me most of a year to see why it fit me better than anywhere larger ever had."

He raised one finger.

"This academy will never win a student the system has priced right. A Silver-tier prospect with a clean file and a good family is going to a school with better floors and better placement. That door is closed to us. It always will be."

A second finger.

"So we go looking for the ones it has priced wrong."

He turned in his chair, reached behind him without looking, and took down one of a long row of thin bound books from the shelf. They were plain books, each the width of two fingers, all the same dull brown.

He laid it on the blotter and opened it toward Cael.

"Our alumni record," he said. "Read me one."

Cael leaned forward.

The page was ruled into columns. A name. A year. A tier and rank at intake. A tier and rank at leaving. A short line in a small, neat hand under each.

He read the first entry aloud. "Copper Rank Two at intake. Kindled at sixteen."

"Late," Naveth said. "Two years late. Nobody at the bigger schools would look at a sixteen-year-old Kindler. They assume something's wrong. Go on."

"Bronze Rank Three at twenty-four."

"Bronze. On a schedule nobody predicted. Next."

Cael ran his finger down. "Classification disputed at intake. Re-evaluated in fourth year. Changed from Stone Path to Anchor Path."

"Overturned," Naveth said with quiet satisfaction. "Because someone here believed the paperwork was wrong before the registry did. It took four years. She's teaching at a coastal school now. Next."

"Re-certification candidate. Expelled from—" The name of a school was written there. Cael stopped himself before reading it out. It wasn't his business. "Copper Rank Nine at intake. Iron Rank Four at leaving."

"Someone thought she was a discipline problem. She was a girl with a temper and a better eye than her instructors." Naveth closed the book gently. "There are sixty years of these on that shelf. Late Kindlers. Disputed classifications. Re-certification cases no one else would take, because they're a nuisance to process. A clerical error is the cheapest thing in Valdris to take on and the most expensive thing to have been wrong about. This place has lived sixty years on the difference."

He set his hands flat on the closed book.

"Every student in this building is someone's clerical error, Cael," he said. "You're simply the largest one we've ever had the nerve to sign for."

---

It was not warmth.

Cael sat in the plain chair and understood that very clearly. He could have wanted it to be warmth. An old man moved by a boy's hard luck. A kindly school. He had wanted that kind of thing once, in the year after Denvash, and it had always ended the same way.

This was something else. This was a man showing him a book of numbers and saying, *here is why you are worth it to us.*

It was more reassuring than warmth would have been.

He had learned, across a year and a half, to be careful of anything that wanted him out of kindness. The certification office in Denvash had been kind on the day of his Kindling. Gentle voices. Apologetic about the classification even while they stamped it. That kindness had lasted exactly as long as the appointment, and not an hour longer.

Kindness was a loan anyone could call in without warning.

An incentive was a contract with its terms written down. You could read it. You could see when it ran out.

"One more question," he said, before he could talk himself out of it.

"Go on."

"What happens if I stop being a good bet?"

Naveth nodded slowly, as if Cael had passed something.

"Then we're wrong, and we live through it," he said. "We have before. Some of the people in those books didn't go anywhere. A few washed out in a season. We adjust the ledger. We keep going." He tapped the book. "That's the other half of the model. We bet on the ones the system prices wrong, and we accept going in that some of those bets won't pay. The ones that fail don't sink us."

"And the ones that pay?"

"Those carry us."

Cael thought about that. Then he asked the question underneath it.

"Where does Quenna sit? In the ledger."

Something shifted in Naveth's face. Not a smile. The look of a man watching a question land exactly where it was supposed to.

"Quenna is the one who priced you," he said.

Cael waited.

"She came to me with a file. An argument. And her professional reputation, laid on this desk as collateral." Naveth's palm pressed lightly on the blotter, as if the reputation were still lying there. "She has been staking that reputation on irregular cases for as long as I've known her, which is a long time. On the merits, I have never once seen her wrong."

He paused. Cael could see him deciding whether to say the rest. He said it.

"What she has misjudged, now and then, is the bill for being right. You should know that. If this goes badly, it goes badly for you first. Then for her. Then for me. That's the order of the ledger." He met Cael's eyes. "Better you hear it in this chair than add it up alone one night and wonder who kept it from you."

Cael sat very still.

The order of who paid. He had known it already, in a way. Quenna had walked into the Ironyard and spent months watching before she said a word. She had built her offer like an argument, because she knew it would one day have to survive an argument. He had seen her stake. He had not let himself add it up.

Now somebody had added it up for him. Out loud. In a plain office with a window that faced the yard.

"Thank you," he said.

It came out smaller than he meant. He meant it more than the two words could carry.

"Thank me with your assessments." Naveth stood. Apparently that was how meetings ended here. No ceremony. A man with other work. "Come back when you have a question that earns the stairs. I suspect you'll know which ones do."

He wrote it on the stair, sitting on a worn step halfway down, because he could not wait.

*Naveth. Showed me the ledger instead of his heart.*

He looked at it. Then he added a line underneath.

*A man who shows you his numbers can be checked. Ask anyone what happens if they're wrong, and listen to whether they answer.*

He thought for a moment more, and added a third line in smaller writing.

*Order of who pays: me, Quenna, Naveth. Quenna knew that when she walked into the Ironyard. I didn't. Now I do.*

He realized, closing the binder, that he had decided something. He had not meant to decide it. It had happened somewhere between the alumni book and the stair.

He was going to pass every assessment this building gave him. Not for himself. He had been doing things for himself for a year and a half, and he was good at it. This was different. A woman had laid her name on a desk for him. He would not be the bet that cost her it.

---

The tour began at the second bell.

A junior clerk ran it. She was perhaps twenty, quick and bored, with a slate under one arm and the practiced patter of someone who had given this tour more often than she found interesting. Six new students followed her. Cael walked at the back with the binder open, filling a page in his densest shorthand.

The training hall came first.

It was a single long floor under a high roof, smaller than the Ironyard by a wide margin. Chalk lines divided it into six sections, each big enough for two fighters and an instructor and nothing else. Two were in use. In one, a Blade Path pair ran a drill in total silence. # Chapter 4 — One Condition at a Time

Not a large one, perhaps a dozen students strung along the chalk boundary of the far section, but it had the particular stillness of people who had stumbled onto something worth watching rather than come on purpose. Cael found a place at the end of the line and looked over a shoulder.

Brom stood in the middle of the section in his usual stance, low and loose, weight sunk into his heels as if the floor were a slope he had decided to lean against. Across from him stood the broadest student Cael had seen on the grounds so far. The boy was a head taller than Brom and half again as wide, with forearms like fence posts and the calm, patient face of someone who had been asked to stand in front of other people for a very long time and had made his peace with it. His feet were planted shoulder-width apart, and the chalk dust around them had settled into a faint ring, as if he hadn't moved them in some while.

Stone Path, Cael guessed, from the way the boy's weight seemed to go down through his soles and keep going. A fourth-year at least. Iron-tier, probably low in it.

Between them, a little to one side, stood a woman of about fifty.

She was not large. She was not, at first glance, anything in particular, a lean woman with grey-streaked hair tied back and a plain brown training coat buttoned to the throat. Shield Path. It could not have been anything else.

"Who?"

"The new one. The Iron Skin."

Cael looked at the light through the high windows and did a quick sum. Brom had been on this floor for most of an hour.

Wray raised one finger.

The broad student came forward, and Cael understood within three exchanges that he was watching the most orthodox defensive fighter he had ever seen. Everything the boy did came out of a textbook. It was not exciting. It was not meant to be. It was the kind of pressure that wore people down by never giving them anything to argue with.

Brom met it from underneath, the way Brom met everything.

He let the first strike come to him. # Chapter 5 — The Edges of the Room

The Path taxonomy lecture met on the ground floor of the lecture wing, in a room full of thirteen-year-olds.

A chart hung on the wall behind the lectern.

Bronze in warm gold. Silver, bright as a coin. Gold, deep amber. Platinum, white touched with blue, with a small note beside it that said *historical*. And at the far end a seventh box with no color in it at all, only the word *Void* and a question mark.

Down the left side ran the Paths themselves, grouped into families and painted in neat capitals. Movement: Wind, Current, Storm. Hardening: Stone, Iron Skin, Shield, Anchor, Wall. Edge: Blade, Glass. Then Ember and Ash, Force and Pressure, Mire and Tide, and more below them, a long column of names, some of which Cael had only ever seen written.

"This chart," the instructor said, "is copied from the Compact's own schedule. The list the registry uses. Every Path an Arbiter can assign is on it. The families are ours, for teaching. The list is theirs. If your Arbiter tells you a Path, it will be one of these. If it isn't on this chart, it isn't a Path."

He tapped the top row with a pointer.

"Tiers first. You all know the colors. Who can tell me how many ranks are in a tier?"

A dozen hands went up. "Ten," said a boy near the front, without waiting.

"Ten. Rank One to Rank Ten. You start at Copper Rank One, almost all of you. You earn rank by use, by practice, by working your Path until it grows. And when you reach Rank Ten—"

"You go up," said the same boy.

"You *may* go up. Rank Ten is the threshold, not the door. To cross from Copper into Iron you need two things. The rank, which you earn yourself. And an evaluation at a registered Arbiter station, which confirms it. Both. Rank without evaluation stays where it is." He pointed down the Copper column. "Most people who Kindle into Copper never reach the threshold at all. They find a level that suits their life, and they stay there. Six of every ten working practitioners in Valdris are Copper. Most adults plateau somewhere in the middle of it."

"My father's Copper Rank Five," said a girl by the window, a little defiantly.

"Then your father is like most fathers," the instructor said mildly. "Copper Rank Five is a good, honest rank. It works a forge. It builds a house." He moved the pointer along. "A quarter of practitioners reach Iron. These are your journeymen. Professional fighters. Master tradespeople. One in ten reaches Bronze. You'll meet a few Bronze instructors here. Silver, perhaps four in a hundred. Gold, fewer than one. Platinum—"

"Nobody," said Oona.

"Nobody living," the instructor agreed. "And Void is a word in stories. You may write it down, but you will not be tested on it."

Cael wrote in two columns, the way he had started doing in Ardenmere without ever quite deciding to. On the left, what was being taught. On the right, what the teaching assumed.

On the left he wrote: *Ten ranks per tier. Rank Ten is the threshold. Advancement needs rank and a station evaluation, both. Most adults plateau in Copper.*

On the right he wrote: *Assumes an Arbiter. Assumes a station that will evaluate you. Assumes your Path is on the chart.*

He looked at the right-hand column for a moment, then went on listening.

"Declarations," the instructor said, a little later, "are how your Arbiter tells you what your Path has given you."

He described them the way Brom had on the road. A header naming the Path, the tier, the rank. An ability's name in brackets. One or two plain sentences saying what the ability did and how far it reached. He drew the shape of one on the slate board, with blank lines where the words would go, and several of the children copied it carefully, as if it were a door they were memorizing the shape of.

"Your first declaration comes at Kindling," he said. "Your Arbiter gives you your Path and your first ability together. After that, new declarations come as you climb. Not every rank brings one. Some do. You'll learn the rhythm of your own Path."

Oona's hand went up.

"Yes?"

"Do you have to tell people what they say?"

The instructor paused. The room had gone quiet in a different way, a listening way, and Cael understood that half the children had wanted to ask the same thing.

"No," the instructor said. "You never have to. Your declarations are yours. Your Arbiter shows them to you and no one else. Nobody, not a parent, not a teacher, not an assessor, has the right to make you share one."

"But people do."

"Sometimes. Between people who trust each other a great deal." He set the chalk down. "Showing someone your declaration is a little like letting them read a letter you wrote to yourself. It's intimate. You'll know, when the time comes, whether a person is someone you'd do that for. Most people do it once or twice in their whole lives, if they do it at all."

Oona considered that. Then she wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice.

Cael found that he was thinking of Brom's face at the midday rest, refusing Lira for the fifth time with perfect calm. *It's very plain. It's more like a receipt.* He thought of Lira, back in Ardenmere, describing her own first declaration to him because he had asked and she had decided to answer. She had not shown it. She had only described it. Even that, he understood now, had been a gift he hadn't known enough to recognize.

And he thought of his own notices, which no Arbiter had ever shown him because no Arbiter had ever been there. The rule did not reach him. There was no one to keep his notices private from except the people he chose to tell. He had told two.

He wrote, on the right-hand side of the page: *Assumes your declarations are given to you by someone.*

---

Near the end of the block, the instructor invited questions about the chart.

There were a great many. Could a Path change families. (No.) Could a person have two Paths. (No.) Was it true that Storm and Wind were really the same Path with different tempers. (A long, patient no, with a diagram.) Why was Glass in the same family as Blade when glass broke. (Because a thing that breaks well still cuts, and the instructor had a story about a broken bottle he declined to finish.)

Oona put her hand up last.

"Where's shattered?"

The room did not go quiet at once. Most of the children had no idea what she meant. They kept rustling, kept whispering. But the instructor's face went very still for half a moment, and in the back row Cael stopped breathing.

"I'm sorry?" said the instructor.

"Shattered. My uncle says there's one called shattered. He works for the Blade Guild and he says it's in the registry." She frowned at the chart. "It isn't on there."

"No," the instructor said carefully. "It isn't."

"Why not?"

He took a moment before he answered. Cael watched him choose the words.

"Because it isn't a Path," the instructor said. "It's a registry matter. A classification the registry has used, very rarely, in its records. It isn't one of the Paths on the schedule, so it isn't on the chart. The chart only lists Paths." He picked up his chalk again. "It's not something you'll need for your Kindling, Oona. I wouldn't worry about it."

"I'm not worried," Oona said. "I just wanted to know where it went."

"It doesn't go anywhere on this chart."

She accepted that the way she seemed to accept most things, by writing it down.

Cael sat with his pen above the page and did not write anything at all for a long moment.

His face felt hot. That surprised him. He had heard the word said aloud before, by officials, by clerks, once by a man in a market square who had meant it as a warning. He had not expected to hear it in a classroom from a thirteen-year-old, asked as easily as a question about mud. He had not expected the instructor to answer it with the word *isn't*.

*It isn't a Path.*

He knew that. Of course he knew it. He had known it since Denvash. He had a binder full of evidence that whatever he had, it was not a Path in any way this chart would recognize.

It still stung to hear it said, in a kind voice, to children, as if it were the answer to a question about where something had been put away.

He made himself write, on the right-hand side, in very small letters.

*Shattered is not on the chart. Registry matter, not a Path. Said kindly.*

Then he closed the binder, because the bell was ringing, and he did not want to think about it any further just then.

Oona caught him at the door.

She stood in his way without seeming to mean to, holding her slate to her chest, and looked up at him with frank curiosity.

"You're on the board," she said. "Next to me. Observer track."

"I am."

"You're old for an observer."

"I know."

She looked at his tag. Then she held up her own, which hung on a string around her neck. It was the same size as his. In the middle of it was a hollow circle, a ring with nothing in it.

"Same," she said.

"Same," Cael agreed.

He had known since orientation that she was on the track. He had not, until that moment, thought about what that meant. The category had been built for someone. It had been built for her. A guild family's daughter who wanted to sit in lectures before her Kindling, so that when her Arbiter came she would already know the shape of the chart. That was the real use of the hollow circle. He was the one borrowing it.

"Did you Kindle late?" she asked. "My mother says some people Kindle late."

He thought about how to answer. He did not want to lie to her. He found, rather to his surprise, that he cared a great deal about not lying to her.

"It's complicated," he said.

She weighed that answer with the same seriousness she had given the instructor's.

"That's what grown-ups say when they don't want to tell you."

"Sometimes it's what they say when it's actually complicated."

"How do you know which?"

"You wait and see if they ever tell you."

Oona thought about it, and seemed to find it fair. She nodded once.

"I Kindle in winter," she said. "I'll be fourteen. Then I won't be an observer anymore." She said it as if announcing the date of a journey. "Will you still be one?"

"I don't know," said Cael, and it was the truest thing he had said all day.

The registry-history lecture met on the third floor, and Cael had picked it off the schedule almost at random.

He stayed with it because of the instructor.

She was a thin, quick woman of perhaps sixty with grey hair cut very short. She lectured without notes and walked the length of the room while she did it, and every few minutes she said something about the Compact's paperwork that made Cael want to stop her and ask where she had learned it.

"The registry records a Kindling within the week," she said, early on. "Every Kindling, in every district, everywhere in Valdris. It has never, in its whole history, unrecorded one. Think about that. It is a ledger that has never closed an account."

A little later: "A form is an argument somebody won so long ago that nobody remembers there was an argument. When you sign one, you are agreeing with the winner."

Later still, while explaining how the guild charters had been drawn up and bound together under the Compact: "Every amendment to a code is a confession. It tells you what somebody was afraid of, in the year they wrote it. Read the amendments and you'll know the history better than the people who lived it."

Cael wrote all of it down on the left side of his page.

On the right side, after a while, he wrote: *Assumes the registry is the record. Assumes that what it doesn't record didn't happen.*

He thought of Vell's ledger, folded into his pack. Every bout he had fought at the Ironyard, every rating change, in her own hand. *The records know you existed here*, Vell had said. The Compact's registry had never heard of most of it. It had happened anyway.

The instructor paused by his bench at the end of the block, on her way out, and glanced at the open binder without any pretense of not reading it.

"Two columns," she said. "What's the second one?"

"What the lecture assumes."

She looked at him for a moment longer, with an expression he couldn't quite read.

"Keep it," she said, and left.

His first supervised session fell at the fifth bell that afternoon.

He reported to the training hall with his tag and was told, at the desk, to go to section four. Quenna was there already, standing at the chalk line with a slate. There was no one else in the section. There would be no one else.

"Twice a week," she said. "One hour. Under supervision. You may train anything you like within the section. You may not use the open floor. You may not spar with another student unless an assessor arranges it."

"Has an assessor arranged it?"

"No."

He set his bag down against the wall.

It turned out to be a strange hour. He ran the Wind framework against an imagined count, moving through the evasion patterns at half pace and then full pace, reading strikes off a body that wasn't there. It was harder than it should have been. The framework did not like empty air. It was built to read an opponent, and without one it had nothing to read, so he had to supply the strikes himself, imagining each one, and every imagined strike came from his own expectations and so never surprised him. He did conditioning circuits Lira had built him in Ardenmere, legs and core and the slow shoulder work that kept the Reydan joint from stiffening. He tried the surface read and found, as he had known he would, that there was nothing for it to do. Iron-adjacent needed a body within a few paces, someone with weight that was about to move. Alone, it was a sense with nothing to sense.

All the while, Quenna stood at the chalk line and wrote on her slate.

He found that he kept shaping the work for her. Moving a little more cleanly than he needed to. Finishing patterns with a flourish he would never have bothered with alone. Making it look like something.

It took him most of the hour to notice he was doing it, and the last ten minutes to stop.

When he stopped, and simply worked, she wrote less.

He noticed that too. It was either coincidence or the most useful thing she had ever told him without saying it.

At the end of the hour she initialed the session record and handed him the slip.

"You could ask for a partner," she said, without looking up.

"Would I get one?"

"Not yet."

"Then why ask?"

"Asking costs you nothing. Being seen to have asked, early and often, may be worth a great deal one day." She finally looked at him. "Files get read from the front page. I'd like yours to show you knocked from the start."

He wrote the request that same evening, at the desk in his narrow room, in his most careful hand. *The undersigned, enrolled under the demonstration provision, requests that a training partner be assigned for supervised sessions, in order that practice may be conducted against a live opponent.* He read it over twice. He signed it. He left it at the assessors' desk before the eighth bell.

He did not expect it to be granted. He found that he did not mind. A refusal on paper would at least carry a date and a signature. Most of the doors he had ever stood in front of had offered neither.

---

On the fourth morning, he watched Lira sign in.

He had not meant to. He had come into the training hall early to look at the open-floor roster, and she came in a minute behind him, and he saw her before she saw him.

There were two lines at the floor desk. The standard line ran from the near end, where a clerk sat with the big roster and a stamp. The other line was at the far end of the same desk, past a small wooden marker. It had its own sheet. It had its own clerk, who did nothing but wait for the few names that belonged there.

Lira went to the far end.

She did it well. That was the thing that stayed with him afterward. She did not glance at the standard line. She did not hesitate at the fork. She walked past the marker as if it weren't there, signed her name, took the slip that would need an assessor's initials before the day was out, and turned toward the floor with her chin level and her stride unbroken. If he had not known her, he would have thought the whole business meant nothing to her at all.

He knew her. He saw her shoulders, a half-second after she passed the desk, come down by about an inch.

It was perfect. It was also labor. He did not say anything.

There was one other person at the far end of the desk. A woman of perhaps twenty, tall and narrow-shouldered, with ash-pale hair cut short and a training coat that had been patched at both elbows. She signed in after Lira and then, as Lira passed her, said something Cael didn't catch.

Lira stopped.

They spoke for a minute. Cael, at the roster, could not hear, but he could read the shape of it. The older woman pointing at the slip in Lira's hand. Lira looking at the slip, then back at her. A short, dry exchange. Lira almost laughing.

When Lira came over to him a few minutes later, she was still half smiling.

"You saw that," she said.

"I saw some of it."

"That's Gerda. Ash Path. She's on the track. She's been on it for most of a year." Lira held up her slip. "She told me to get the assessor's initials before the sixth bell, every time, or the clerk dates the result the next day, and if you get three results dated a day late, somebody writes a note in your file asking why you can't keep a schedule."

"That seems unfair."

"That's what I said. She said, 'It isn't unfair. It's procedure. Unfair would be if they hid the rule.'" Lira shook her head slowly. "She's from somewhere south. A different academy. She didn't say which, and I didn't ask."

"Do you like her?"

"I don't know yet." She considered. "She talks to the procedure like it's a dog that might bite. Very calm. Very careful. Never turns her back on it." She glanced toward the far end of the desk, where Gerda had gone out onto the floor. "I think she's been bitten before."

---

At the second bell he went to hear Wray teach the ceiling.

He had found it on the lecture schedule the night before, after Brom's argument over the barley: *Declaration Efficiency, Hardening Family, Second Years. Instructor Wray.* An observer could attend any lecture. He had not quite believed, until he walked into the room, that Brom would be there too.

Brom was in the back row, sitting very straight, with his notebook open and his ribs held carefully to one side.

"You're a first-year," Cael said, sliding in beside him.

"I asked." Brom did not look up. "She said I could sit in as long as I kept my mouth shut until the end."

"Are you going to?"

"I'm going to try very hard."

The room was on the hardening floor of the lecture wing, and the twenty students in it were all from the same family of Paths: Stone and Shield and Anchor and Iron Skin and one tall boy whose skin went faintly grey and grainy when he was nervous, which was apparently always. Wray stood at the front with a slate board behind her. She did not use notes. She drew.

She drew a curve. It started low at the left side of the board, at a mark labeled *Copper One*, and rose steeply through the early ranks, and then began to bend. By the middle of Copper it was rising more slowly. By the upper ranks it had nearly flattened.

"Form efficiency," Wray said. "How much more you get out of a declaration, rank by rank, by refining the way you use it. Not by getting stronger. By getting better." She tapped the steep part of the curve. "Early on, every hour of form work pays. You learn where your weight goes. You stop wasting motion. Your first declaration does twice what it did at Kindling, and you haven't gained an ounce of raw output."

She moved the chalk along the bend.

"Then it slows. Somewhere around the middle of a tier, for most people, form work stops paying the way it did. You've learned the big lessons. The small ones are smaller. And here—" She tapped the flat part. "—here is what this school, and every school I know of, calls the ceiling. The point past which refining your form gives you so little that you're better off spending the hours on raw output instead. More force. More endurance. Bigger declarations, earned the slow way through rank."

A Shield girl near the front raised her hand. "Is it the same ceiling for every Path?"

"Roughly. The curve bends at different places for different families. Hardening Paths tend to flatten a little later than movement Paths. It's in your reading." Wray wrote a short list of ranks beside the curve. "That's what the doctrine says. That's what I've taught for a long time. That's what you'll be examined on."

Cael glanced sideways. Brom's jaw had set, and his pen had stopped moving.

Wray laid down the chalk.

"There is a first-year sitting in the back of this room," she said, in exactly the same voice she had used for the curve, "who believes the flat part of this line is not real."

Twenty heads turned. Brom went very still.

"He believes," Wray went on, "that the curve flattens because the people on it believe it will, and stop pushing. He says he has spent two years past where the ceiling ought to be, on his own, without anyone to tell him it was there, and that his form has kept improving every month." She looked at Brom down the length of the room, without any expression at all. "I have told him to prove it on the floor. If he does, I will stand here and tell you I was wrong, and I will redraw this line."

The grey-skinned boy had gone a shade greyer. Somebody near the window whispered something.

"Until he proves it," Wray said, "this is the lecture. Copy the curve."

Twenty heads turned back. Twenty pens moved.

Cael looked at Brom. Brom was staring at the board with an expression Cael had never seen on him, a kind of fierce, astonished stillness, as if someone had just set a heavy weight in his hands and told him it was his.

He did not say anything for the rest of the block. He copied the curve, carefully, exactly as she had drawn it. Then, underneath it, in small square letters, he drew a second line that did not bend.

When the bell rang, Wray caught his eye on the way out. She did not stop. She only said, as she passed, "I meant it," and walked on.

"Meant what?" Cael asked, on the stairs.

"The line. If I can show her, she'll redraw it." Brom was holding his ribs and smiling, which seemed to hurt, and he did not stop. "She told twenty people. In her own lecture. She told them I might be right."

"She told them you think you're right."

"She told them she'd listen," Brom said. "Nobody's ever told a room that about me before."

Cael thought, going down the stair behind him, that he had just watched something he would want to remember exactly. He wrote it in the binder that night under the heading for things that were changing. *Wray put Brom's argument in front of her own class, by name, and told them she might be wrong. Brom drew a straight line under her curve. He's going to spend the whole term trying to make his line the true one.*

Under that, after a moment: *She wants to be tested. I didn't know teachers came like that.*

---

He found Lira again at the notice board that afternoon.

She was standing in front of the training-floor roster with her arms crossed, reading it for longer than reading it could possibly take. He knew what she was looking at before he reached her.

Her name was on the roster. Beside it, in the clerk's small hand, sat a tiny mark. A raised dot, the same as on her tag. The provisional marker. It sat beside her name and beside three others, Gerda's among them, and beside no one else on the whole board.

"Before you say anything," she said, without turning, "I know it's a clerk's mark. I know it only means somebody checks my results before they count. I've read the rule. Twice."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something kind and sensible, and I'd have had to agree with it." She kept her eyes on the dot. "Here's the trouble. For two years I've told anyone who'd stand still that Fenmark got me wrong. Then I walked in here and put my name to a page that says Fenmark's record stands. I did it so I could train. And now there's a mark by my name every morning to remind everyone that my wins need a second signature." She breathed out through her nose. "I was right about Fenmark. I also signed. Both of those are true, and I can't make one of them sit down and let the other have the chair."

"Maybe neither of them has to sit down."

"Maybe." She uncrossed her arms and crossed them again. "I come and look at it every day. As if, if I stare at it long enough, it'll stop being able to sting."

"Does it?"

"Not so far," said Lira, and almost laughed. "I'm very stubborn, though."

She left after that, toward the yard and the floor and the work she trusted more than any board. Cael stayed a moment longer and looked at the dot beside her name.

He thought, for the first time with any clarity, that he had been wrong about something. On the road he had assumed that his own door would be the hard one. The blank board. The borrowed category. He had braced himself for being the complicated case. He had not thought much about what it would cost Lira to walk through a door that was simple on its face and marked on every single morning after.

He wrote it down that night. *Lira's door looks easier than mine. It isn't. Mine is strange once. Hers is marked every day.*

---

The notice was under his door when he came back from supper.

It was a single folded sheet, slid in flat across the boards, with no envelope and no seal. It had been delivered with exactly the ceremony of a laundry list.

He picked it up and unfolded it standing in the doorway, one hand still on the latch.

*Demonstration Assessment. Unclassified Observer Track. First sitting. Training hall, first bell, the eighth day. Closed session. The candidate will demonstrate at his own election.*

He read it twice.

He had seen the date on the public board already. He had known it was coming. But there was a difference between a line on a board in the hall and a sheet of paper on his own floor with his own name at the top of it, and the difference went through him like cold water.

This was the hinge. Not the signature. Not the category. Not the hollow circle on his tag. All of that only held a door open. This was the first time anyone would walk through it and look at what was on the other side.

He had four days to decide what to show them.

He closed the door, sat on the bed, and opened the binder to a clean page.

# Chapter 6 — Panel Speed

Quenna gave him the rules standing up, in the corridor outside the archive, because she had somewhere else to be and plainly believed the rules were simple enough to survive the format.

"There'll be three chairs," she said. "Mine in the middle. Wray has the instructor's chair this month. The third goes round the senior faculty in turn, and nobody tells the candidate whose turn it is. We do that on purpose. Spend your four days on yourself, not on guessing a stranger."

"All right."

"The format is yours to fill. You'll have a training partner, assigned by the instructor's seat, who will strike when he's told to strike and stop when he's told to stop. Nowhere does the provision ask you to name a Path."

"Good. I haven't got one."

"The drafters seem to have foreseen you." If there was any irony in that, she did not spend breath on it. "What you are required to show is consistent, observable practice."

She let the three words sit in the cold corridor air for a moment, as if she wanted him to hear each of them land separately.

"Those three words are the whole law of the morning," she said. "Attend to them. *Consistent* means the panel sees it more than once, with the same structure and the same result. Once is an accident. Twice is a pattern. *Observable* means it happens in the room, at a scale a person holding a slate can see and write down. Nothing internal. Nothing they have to take your word for. And *practice*—" She paused, a fraction longer than she had paused on the others. "*Practice* means it is something you do on purpose. Not something that happens to you."

Cael heard, inside that last pause, everything she wasn't saying. He had told her nothing about how the fragments arrived. She had never asked. But she had watched him fight for months before she ever spoke to him, and she was not a fool, and she had just drawn a line on the floor between *a thing he did* and *a thing that happened to him* with the precision of a woman marking a boundary she did not intend to step over.

"Read the words again tonight and notice what isn't in them," she went on. "Nothing about your best. Nothing about all of it. Whoever wrote that provision knew how far a loose phrase will stretch, and left it loose."

"And whatever I show goes on the record."

"Whatever you show goes on the record, in three hands." She shifted the folder under her arm. "Think about that half harder than the first."

She went off down the corridor without saying goodbye. Cael stood outside the archive door for a while after she had gone, looking at the iron bands on the dark wood without seeing them.

*Consistent, observable practice.* He could show anything. He simply had to show it the same way twice, in a way a person could write down, and it had to be something he did.

That was a gift.

By evening he saw the other side of it. Anything he was free to show, he was also free to show too much of, and whatever he showed would be written down and kept.

He spent the first night doing what he always did with a decision that mattered. He made a list, crossed most of it out, and made a smaller list.

Then he made himself do something harder.

He imagined the record being read.

Not by Quenna. Not by Wray. They would be in the room, and they would write what they saw, and whatever they wrote would be honest. He imagined it being read a year from now, in some office he had never seen, by somebody who had never been in the room at all.

He made himself build that reader carefully, because a careless version would be useless. Not a villain. A villain would make mistakes out of spite, and spite could be planned for. He built a clerk instead. Patient, thorough, correct in every procedure, with a long file of notes on him and no particular feeling about him either way. Someone who would sit at a plain desk under a plain lamp and read the panel's entry line by line, not looking for what it said, but for what it implied. How fast had the observer grown? What was his ceiling? What could he do that a child who had not yet Kindled could not possibly do?

What, in short, made the words *unclassified observer* harder to say with a straight face?

He had met people like that. A man in a market square with careful questions and a careful voice. Officials who had looked at him across desks and written things he never got to read. He let them blur into a single patient figure under a lamp, and he sat down across the desk from that figure in his imagination, and he went through what he could show, one capability at a time, and watched what the reader did with each.

He hated every minute of it. He was fairly sure it was the best use of his time all week.

*Show enough to pass. Bank everything that matters.*

Then he went through the binder, section by section, and decided.

Wind-adjacent first. Lira's framework, a year and a half old now, drilled through every bout he had fought since. Its range was the length of a fight. It read a strike off an opponent's body a half-beat before the strike arrived, and it let him step, slip or turn into the space the strike would not be. Its price was fatigue over long exchanges and one structural weakness: he had to choose his direction before he moved, and a sharp opponent who saw the choice could close the door behind it. Against faster opponents, it read less. He had shown it on the Ardenmere circuit a hundred times in front of crowds, judges, and at least one man from the Compact. It was not a secret anymore. It was simply skill.

*Show.*

Iron-adjacent next. Brom's gift. A pressure-sense along the skin that told him where a body's weight was about to go, at contact and out to a few paces. Its range was short, and speed thinned it, so that against a fast opponent at distance it gave him almost nothing. Its price was close attention, a kind of listening with the whole body that tired the mind more than the muscles. It had been on the circuit too, quieter, visible to anyone watching only as a boy who was very rarely surprised.

*Show.*

Pressure-adjacent. Feryn's redirect. Three clean uses mapped across a long, careful year, each one paid for in the right shoulder with a price he knew to the ounce. Contact only. It took force aimed at him and turned it, the way a door on a good hinge turns a shove into a swing. It had never been shown to any judge or panel or official. Lira and Brom knew. No one else.

*Bank.*

Compression-adjacent. Days old. A quarter of a slow push, caught on a good try, paid for through the shoulder and breastbone, with a habit of draining into the nearest joint whenever he forgot to choose a road for it first. It was the youngest thing he owned and the least understood. If he showed it badly, the record would say *erratic*. If he showed it well, the record would say something far worse.

*Bank.*

And under all four, the thing that was not a fragment at all. How any of them had come to him. The fact that each had arrived after he had watched someone use their Path, closely, under real stakes. The fact that the binder had a section for each new one, and a page for each notice, and a growing suspicion between its lines that the list was not finished.

That was not something a person demonstrated. It was the ground every demonstration stood on. The provision asked for practice. It said nothing about digging.

*Never.*

He looked at the page. Two shown. Two banked. One thing never.

It looked, written down that way, almost tidy.

He added one more line at the bottom, because the log was the one place he never lied.

*Iron-equivalent by Vell's ledger. That means I can beat Iron-tier fighters. It does not mean I have a tier. It does not mean the panel will believe either.*

Quenna had told him to read the words again, so on the fifth afternoon he went to the archive to find them.

Prynn's door stood at its usual angle. He knocked on the frame. She looked up from a ledger at a high desk just inside, took off her spectacles, and looked at him for exactly as long as it took him to wonder whether he should leave.

"The observer," she said.

"I'd like to read the demonstration provision. The academy's copy."

"Would you." She did not move. "Most people ask their assessor to tell them what it says."

"My assessor told me to read it."

Something changed very slightly at the corner of Prynn's mouth. She put her spectacles back on, came down off her stool, and opened the door perhaps two fingers wider than its usual angle. It was not an invitation. It was the absence of a refusal, which from her seemed to be the same thing.

The archive was colder than the corridor and much quieter. The shelves ran back in narrow aisles, one person wide, and the light came from three high windows and a single lamp on the reading table. Prynn walked him to the end of the nearest aisle, which held the academy's own records, newer than anything else in the room, and took down a thick bound book with *Statutes and Provisions* stamped on the spine in faded gilt. She laid it on the table and opened it, without looking, to the right page.

"You've done that before," Cael said.

"Three times in thirty years." She set a weight on the facing page to keep it flat. "Twice for Quenna. Once for a man who wanted to know if he could use it for his nephew. He couldn't." She looked at him over the spectacles. "Tea?"

He had not expected that. "Yes. Thank you."

She went away between the shelves. He sat and read.

The provision was short. He had expected something longer, after an afternoon of arguments in a back room. It was barely half a page. It said that a candidate without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter might be enrolled for academic exposure and supervised training. It said such a candidate would be assessed monthly by demonstration before a panel of three. It said the candidate must demonstrate *consistent, observable practice*, and that the panel's finding should be entered in the candidate's record. And that was very nearly all.

He read it twice, slowly, the way Quenna had told him to, paying attention to what was not there.

There was no word about best. No word about all. No word about naming anything. No word, he noticed, about how a candidate had come by whatever he practiced. The provision asked what he could do. It did not ask where it came from.

He sat back and let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

Prynn came back with a cup on a saucer and set it at his elbow, well away from the open book. The tea was very strong and very hot and had clearly been made in a pot that had been stewing since the morning.

"Don't spill it on the statutes," she said.

"I won't."

"Everyone says that." She returned to her high stool and her ledger and did not look at him again.

He drank the tea. It was terrible. He drank all of it anyway, because she had brought it, and because the room was cold, and because he had a feeling that refusing it would be noticed and remembered for a very long time.

When he left, he closed the book and set it square on the table exactly where she had laid it, with the weight beside it, and carried the cup and saucer to the end of her desk.

Prynn looked at the cup. Then she looked at the book on the table, square and closed. Then she looked at him.

"Hm," she said, and went back to her ledger.

He found out two days later that she had left the book out for him. It was still on the table, closed, with the weight beside it, when he next came in. Nobody had reshelved it. He was fairly sure nobody in that room did anything Prynn had not decided they should.

He had not meant to stop there. He kept doing it anyway, the way a hand goes back to a bruise. His line was still there, in the same ink as everybody else's. *Eighth day. First bell.*

Oona stood beside him with her slate under her arm and read the line too, moving her lips.

"That's yours," she said. "The demonstration."

"Yes."

"I don't have one of those."

"No."

"Why do you?"

He thought about how to answer. He had decided at the classroom door that he would not lie to her, and he found that it was much harder to keep a promise like that to a thirteen-year-old than to anyone else, because she asked the questions everyone else was too polite to.

"Because I'm older," he said, "and they want to see what I can do."

"Can you do things?"

"Some."

She studied him with great seriousness. "Are you scared?"

He opened his mouth to say *no*, the way he would have said it to Edran or to the girls on the stair. Then he looked at her, small and solemn with her slate, and found he did not want to give her a grown-up's answer.

"A little," he said.

Oona nodded, as if he had confirmed something she had suspected.

"I'm scared about my Kindling," she said. "Not very. A little. My mother says it doesn't hurt. My uncle says it's like being hit by a door you didn't know was there." She frowned. "I don't know which one is lying."

"Neither of them, probably," Cael said. "It's different for everyone."

"What was yours like?"

He was quiet for a moment. He thought of the certification office in Denvash, the scrubbed floor, the clerk's gentle voice, the silence that had gone on far too long, and the word in brackets afterward that had followed him across half the continent.

"Short," he said at last. "And then very long."

Oona considered that answer carefully, turning it over the way she turned over the instructor's answers in class. Cael braced himself for the next question.

It did not come. She looked up at him with something that, on an adult, he would have called sympathy.

"That sounds like a bad one," she said.

"It wasn't a good one."

"Mine will be ordinary," she announced. "I've decided."

"I hope it is."

"It will be." She tucked her slate more firmly under her arm. "Good luck on the eighth day. I hope you're not boring."

He stood in front of the board a while longer, and found that he was smiling, and that the small cold knot he had been carrying under his breastbone since the notice slid under his door had loosened by about a thread.

The refusal came on the sixth day.

It was waiting in the narrow wooden box with his name on it at the assessors' desk, folded once, in a clerk's hand under a printed heading.

*Request for assignment of training partner, supervised sessions, demonstration provision. Request declined at this time. The provision does not presently authorize regular partnered practice for candidates in the observer category. The request has been entered in the candidate's file.*

At the bottom, two sets of initials. One of them was Quenna's.

He read it twice in the corridor. Then he went up to his room, opened the binder to the pocket inside its back cover, and slid the refusal in behind the stranger's note from Ardenmere and Vell's folded sheaf.

He was surprised by what he felt. It was not disappointment. It was something closer to satisfaction.

It said *no*, and *not yet*, and *we have written down that you asked*. He had spent most of his life in front of doors that said nothing at all. A no with a date and two sets of initials was better company than any silence.

He wrote a single line under the request's entry in the log. *Refused in writing, day six. Filed. Keep asking.*

Brom opened it already in his shirtsleeves. He looked at Cael's face and stepped back without a word to let him in.

Brom's room was the mirror of his own, narrow and whitewashed, with the same bed and the same desk. The only difference was that Brom had already arranged everything he owned in neat rows on the shelf, as if he expected an inspection, and had set his enrollment tag in the very centre of the row, crest up.

"Compression," Cael said.

"Before an assessment?"

"Not for the assessment. It isn't going anywhere near the assessment." He sat on the floor with his back against the bed. "But it's the only one that's still getting worse when I leave it alone. Two days without practice and the timing slides."

Brom lowered himself onto the floor opposite, slowly, keeping his right side stiff. The ribs Wray's partner had found three days ago had gone a deep, spreading purple. He had shown them to Lira, who had said something sharp about Stone Path students and then fetched him a cold cloth from the kitchens.

"Left hand," Brom said. "Right side still doesn't like leaning."

"Left's fine."

They worked quietly, the way they had on the road. Brom set his left palm against Cael's braced forearm and hardened it, a little Iron Skin coming up under the skin like frost forming on a window, and then he leaned.

Cael waited until the force was in the bone.

He chose the road before it could choose for itself. Out. Back along the line it came in.

He reached.

The first one failed. Too early. The fragment simply did not answer, and the push came and went like an ordinary push. The second one caught, and he sent it back, and a quarter of Brom's weight knocked against Brom's own wrist with that small solid sound, a door shutting in another room. Then the bill arrived, the hot bar laid across his collarbones, the two breaths he could not take, and he sat with his eyes shut and waited them out.

Brom waited with him.

"Four in ten," Brom said, at the end. "On the road it was three in twelve."

"Same size, though. A quarter. Every time."

"The size is a different problem. You've fixed the timing." Brom turned his left hand over and looked at it, as if it might report something. "When you started, you were reaching early and hoping. Now you wait. You wait well. I could feel you waiting."

"What does waiting feel like, from your side?"

Brom thought about it seriously.

"Like pushing on a door," he said, "and knowing someone's standing on the other side of it, deciding whether to open it."

Cael wrote that down. He did not know yet what it was evidence of. It felt like evidence.

He did not reach for the Pressure redirect, though he thought about it. His right shoulder had spent its credit on the road, and it would be asked for nothing in two days' time, and he wanted it quiet and unbothered and boring. Some things were banked in the binder. Some things, he decided, were banked in the body.

When they were done, Brom sat back against the desk leg and looked at the ceiling.

"She had me on the floor again today," he said. "Wray."

"With the same partner?"

"With the same partner. He still hasn't told me his name. I haven't asked." Brom's mouth twitched. "It's started to feel like a rule. As if asking would ruin something."

"No," Cael said. "It isn't nothing."

She had found a way around the refusal. Cael was not entirely sure how. Demonstrator: Lira, re-certification track.* It was a different form from the one he had filled out. It said nothing at all about a partner.

"I'm not your partner," Lira said, signing in at her end of the desk while he signed in at his. "I'm a *demonstrator*. I'm here to demonstrate strikes so you can demonstrate getting out of their way. It's completely different."

"It's exactly the same."

"It's on a different form," she said serenely, "which makes it different."

"Framework," she said. "Half speed."

He ran it. She struck, clean and simple, from a dozen angles, and he read each strike off her body a half-beat early, the way he had for a year and a half, and stepped where the strike would not be.

"Again. Demonstration speed."

He ran it faster.

"Stop," she said, after the fourth pass. "Second strike. You went out too far."

"I corrected."

"You corrected halfway through the step. You were making up your mind on the move." She reset her feet. "Listen to me. In a fight, deciding while you move is the whole point. That's what the framework is for. But in that room tomorrow, a choice made mid-step reads as reflex. Reflex reads as something that happens to you. That's the last thing you want on their slates."

He stopped. He thought of Quenna in the corridor. *Practice means it is something you do on purpose.*

"Let them see a skill somebody chose," Lira said. "A choice made beforehand looks finished. So finish every choice out here, tonight, where the only slate belongs to him, and he doesn't count."

The assessor by the wall did not look up.

"How fast?" Cael asked.

Lira considered. Then she invented a speed on the spot, the way she invented most useful things.

"Panel speed," she said. "Quick enough that nobody thinks you're pretending. Slow enough that a pen can keep up."

---

They ran it eleven more times.

The first three were bad. He kept speeding up in the middle, because his body knew the framework at full speed and wanted to use it that way, and Lira stopped him each time with a single raised hand and a flat look. On the fourth he found the pace. It felt wrong in his legs, slower than anything he had ever done for real. On the fifth it felt less wrong. By the seventh he had stopped thinking about it at all.

Somewhere around the eighth, something changed in the work.

It stopped being a framework he was holding back, and became a framework that had been built to a certain height beforehand. He was not throttling it anymore, not catching himself mid-step and reining in. The limits were already there, set before he moved, like the depth of a channel cut before the water came. There was nothing left to flex. Nothing for a watching eye to catch adjusting.

He felt the change before he understood it. He understood it on the ninth pass, and he nearly laughed out loud, because it was the same lesson the Compression fragment had been teaching him all week. *Choose first. Always choose first.* Lira was teaching it to him in a completely different language, and he had not noticed they were the same sentence until now.

On the eleventh pass, Lira lowered her hands and stepped back.

"There," she said.

She tossed him his water. He drank while she watched him with her head on one side.

"That's a fence," she said. "You've built it to the provision's height and not a finger over." She took the water back and drank herself. "Whatever's on the other side of it, keep your eyes off it tomorrow."

"That's your professional advice?"

"I'm a demonstrator," said Lira. "It's on the form."

---

Brom had appeared at the rail somewhere around the eighth repetition. Cael had not seen him arrive. He was simply there, leaning on his good side, watching with the patient interest he gave to most things worth watching.

He fell in beside them as they left the hall.

"The surface read," he said. "Do the last part with your eyes closed."

Cael looked at him. "Why?"

"It's true, and it reads as restraint." Brom shrugged, carefully, minding his ribs. "You don't need your eyes for it. You never did. But they don't know that. If you shut them at the end, they'll see you aren't watching the partner's hands or his face or his feet. They'll know you're reading something they can't see you read."

"That sounds like the opposite of what I want."

"No. Listen." Brom stopped on the path and turned to him, which was how he signalled that a thing mattered. "Somebody with a slate is always hunting for one word, and it's the word that tells them you won't hurt anyone. *Controlled.* Give them a reason to write it and it goes with you everywhere that file goes." He started walking again. "Shut your eyes for the last one."

Lira looked at Brom with open admiration.

"When did you get good at evaluators?"

"Velmere," said Brom shortly. "I spent my whole childhood learning what evaluators liked so I could stop giving it to them." He considered. "Turns out it's still useful the other way round."

---

He lay awake that night in the narrow room and ran the morning through, the way he used to run fight preparation the night before a circuit bout.

Framework first. Four times. Panel speed. Same structure each time, varied only in the angle. Then the surface read. Four calls. Eyes open for the first three, closed for the last. Nothing else. No Pressure. No Compression. Nothing about where any of it had come from.

Small. The same every time.

Dull, with any luck.

He turned over on the hard mattress and looked at the faint grey square of the window. Dull would get him a second month. Dull would get him a third. He had spent a year and a half on the circuit learning to make crowds lean forward. Tomorrow, the whole skill was to make three people with slates sit back in their chairs.

Across the corridor he heard Brom shift in his sleep and grunt at his ribs. Two doors down, nothing at all, which meant Lira was awake and lying very still.

He realized he was not afraid. He checked, the way a person checks a pocket. He was nervous, which was different. Afraid would have meant he did not know what was going to happen. Nervous only meant he did.

He thought of the sentence on the clean page. *Show enough to pass. Bank everything that matters.*

He thought of the line on the board in the main hall with his name on it, written in the same ink as everyone else's.

He fell asleep somewhere between the two, and did not dream.

# Chapter 7 — Consistent, Observable Practice

The training hall at first bell was a room he had never seen before, though he had walked through it every day for a week.

Someone had cleared it. The chalk lines of the six sections had been swept away, leaving only faint pale ghosts on the boards, and a single fresh ring had been drawn at the centre of the floor. The high windows let in a thin grey morning. The air smelled of new chalk and cold stone and, faintly, of the lamp oil someone had used before dawn to see by while they swept.

At the far end, three chairs.

They stood in a row facing the ring, each the same distance from the next, and a faint chalk tick on the boards beside each leg told him somebody had used a rule. A small table sat in front of the middle chair with an inkwell on it. Nothing else. No benches. No rail. No crowd.

He had fought in front of six hundred people at the Ironyard. He had never once walked into a room this quiet to be looked at.

Quenna sat in the centre chair with a slate on her knee. To her left sat Wray, upright, still, her brown coat buttoned to the throat, watching him come in with the same level attention she had given Brom on the defensive floor. To Quenna's right sat a man Cael had never met. He was older than both women, white-haired and spare, and he sat the way a heron stands in a shallow, as if he could wait all morning for one thing worth noticing. He had a slate too. He had already written something on it.

The rotating seat, Cael thought. The one he could not prepare for.

He did not try. He looked at the man once, noted his age and his stillness and the fact that he held his pen very loosely, as if he had held a great many pens and broken none of them, and then he let him go.

The training partner was waiting in the ring.

Cael knew him at once. It was the broad student from Brom's floor, the Stone Path fourth-year with forearms like fence posts and a face of perfect patience. He stood easily at his mark with his feet a shoulder-width apart, and he looked at Cael without any curiosity at all, the way a wall looks at weather.

Wray rose, crossed to him, and bent to his ear for a moment. The boy nodded once. Wray returned to her chair.

The partner settled his weight and waited. He did not glance at the panel. He did not glance at Cael's tag. He simply stood there being furniture. Whatever he had been lent for, he meant to be exactly that and nothing more.

Cael felt a rush of gratitude toward him so strong that it embarrassed him.

---

"For the record," said Quenna.

Her voice had changed. It had dropped into a slower, flatter cadence, each word set down separately, and Cael understood that from this moment everything she said was being written as she said it.

"Demonstration assessment. Unclassified observer track. First sitting. Present: the presiding assessor, the instructor's seat, the rotating faculty seat. The candidate will demonstrate at his own election. The panel observes. The instructor's seat may direct the exercise partner as needed."

She looked up.

"Whenever you're ready," she said.

She said it the way a clerk says *next*. He suspected she had practiced that.

He walked into the ring and took his mark across from the partner, at the distance Lira had drilled into his legs the night before. He set his feet. He let his shoulders drop.

He chose.

That was the whole of the preparation now, and he did it before anything moved. The framework would run at panel speed. Four passes. The same structure each time. Its ceiling was already built, its height already set, the channel already cut before the water came. Whatever happened in the next quarter of an hour, he would not decide it while moving.

"Framework," he said, to the panel. "Evasion. Four passes, same structure, varied angle."

Wray lifted a finger to the partner.

The partner struck.

---

It was an honest strike. Nothing theatrical in it. The boy came in on a straight line from the left with his weight committed exactly as far as a textbook recommended, a solid, workmanlike blow aimed at the centre of Cael's chest, and Cael read it off his body before it had properly begun.

The read came the way it always came. Not from the fist. From the hip, from the small settling of weight into the boy's front foot, from the way his shoulder dropped a quarter-inch to load. A half-beat before the strike had any speed in it, Cael already knew where it was going to be, and the framework moved him out of that place and into the space beside it.

He did not hurry. That was the hard part. Every muscle that had fought on the Ardenmere circuit wanted to slip the strike at full speed and come back around inside it with a counter, the way he had a thousand times against fighters who meant to hurt him. Instead he moved at the pace Lira had built for him, fast enough to be real, slow enough that a pen could follow, and stepped half a pace to his right with his weight already set for the next strike.

The second strike came from the right.

He read it. He moved.

The third came low, a short hooking blow at the ribs. He turned his hip away from it a half-beat before it arrived and let it pass through the air where his side had been.

The fourth came high.

He sank under it, rose behind it, and stood still.

Four strikes. Four reads. The same structure from beginning to end, only the angles different. He reset to his mark and waited.

Three pens moved at the far end of the hall.

"Second pass," Quenna said.

He ran it again. The partner came in from a new set of angles, each of them called by Wray with a small movement of her fingers that Cael could see from the corner of his eye and did not try to read. It made no difference what she called. The framework did not care where a strike came from. It only cared that a body was about to send one.

Read. Move. Read. Move.

When he came back to his mark, he was breathing a little harder than he would have liked. It was not tiredness. It was the effort of holding a thing at a pace it did not want to go.

---

Between the second pass and the third, Wray raised a finger and held it there.

She did not speak. She looked at the partner, and something passed between them that Cael could not read, and then she lowered her finger halfway, and held it again, and lowered it the rest of the way at a moment that was not on the rhythm she had used before.

The partner struck early.

He was a quarter-beat ahead of where the count would have put him, and then on the next strike half a beat behind, and on the third almost no gap at all between two blows. Wray had broken the count. Whatever she had told him in those three quiet sentences before they began, she had told him to do this when she gave the sign: abandon the pattern and see what happened to a fighter who had been fed it.

Cael knew within the first strike what she was testing.

If his framework ran on the count, if he had learned the panel's rhythm over two passes and was moving on the beat rather than on the body, then breaking the count would break him. He would step into a strike he had expected to come later, or step away from one that came sooner than he thought.

The framework did not run on the count. It never had. It ran off the boy's hips and shoulders and the weight in his front foot, and those told the truth no matter what rhythm Wray chose. The first early strike came at him and he read it off the boy's body as easily as the others and moved at panel speed into the space beside it.

The late one he read too.

The double came fast, two blows almost on top of each other, and for a fraction of a second he felt the old circuit hunger leap up in him, wanting full speed, wanting to slip both and come back around. He did not let it. The ceiling was built. He moved at the speed he had chosen the night before and took the second strike, not cleanly, on the outside of his left forearm.

It landed solid. A Stone Path fourth-year, even holding back, hit like a dropped sack of grain.

And something in Cael's chest woke up.

It was low and tight and familiar, the gathering he had felt every morning on the road and every evening in Brom's room. The force of the blow was already in the bone of his forearm, already his, and the Compression fragment had felt it arrive and was reaching toward it, gathering, the way a hand closes on something dropped into it.

For one breath, a road opened. He could feel exactly where it went. Out. Back along the line the strike had come in. A quarter of the boy's own weight, sent home into the boy's braced arm, with that small solid sound like a door shutting in another room.

Three people with slates would hear that door shut.

He chose.

He chose nothing. He gave the gathered force no road at all, and he let it go. It spilled out of him harmlessly, the same way it had on the first road morning when he felt it sliding toward his knee, and his forearm took the blow the ordinary way, the way any forearm takes a blow, with a dull flat ache that would be a bruise by supper.

He finished the pass. Read. Move. He came back to his mark.

His heart was going hard. He kept his face still and his breathing slow and his hands loose at his sides. Nothing had happened. That was the whole point. Nothing at all had happened, and anyone at the far end of the hall would have seen a boy take one strike on the forearm out of a dozen and keep his form.

Wray lowered her finger. She wrote.

The fourth pass he ran on her ordinary count again, and it landed the same as the first, the same structure, the same four reads, as if the broken rhythm and the strike and the road he had not taken had never happened at all.

The rotating seat spoke for the first time.

"Your lead," he said. His voice was mild and dry and addressed to the room in general, as if he were asking about the weather. "The half-beat. Is it the same against any partner? Or does it change when the partner is faster?"

Cael had prepared for many questions. He had not prepared for that one, and he was glad, because it meant he had to answer it honestly instead of well.

"It changes," he said.

Then, because precision was the language this room spoke, and because he wanted the record to hold precision under his name: "Within limits. It reads the opponent's body. Faster opponents give me less body to read, not more. Their weight shifts later and for less time. Past a certain speed the read gets thinner. I haven't found where it stops working. I know which direction that is."

The rotating seat wrote for a long time.

When he finally looked up, he looked faintly, modestly pleased, like a man who has been brought exactly the right tool without having to describe it twice. Cael understood why after a moment. He had handed the man an edge. An edge was something you could measure, and a thing that could be measured sounded studied rather than bragged about. Bragging was exactly what got underlined later, by people under lamps.

"Thank you," the rotating seat said.

"Second demonstration," Cael said. "Surface read."

It was simpler to show and stranger to watch.

He asked the partner, through Wray, to stand at the edge of reach and shift his weight in any direction he chose, whenever he chose, and to do nothing to hide it. Then Cael stood still in front of him, two paces off, and let the read open.

It came in through the skin. It always did. Not through his eyes, though he kept them open, but as a kind of pressure along his forearms and the backs of his hands and the front of his body, as if the air between them had grown thicker on one side than the other. A big body had a great deal of weight, and weight that was about to move leaned on the world a moment before it went.

"Left," Cael said. "Heel."

The partner's weight went to his left heel.

"Forward. Right foot."

It went forward onto the right foot.

"Back. Both."

The partner rocked back onto both heels. He did it a fraction late this time, as if he had begun to suspect something and wanted to see whether he could beat the call. He couldn't. Cael had named it before the boy had properly decided.

For the fourth call, he closed his eyes.

He did it the way Brom had told him to, simply and without any show, the way a person closes their eyes to listen harder to a sound in another room. The hall went dark. The pressure along his skin did not change at all. It had never needed his eyes. It had only let him think it did.

The big body in front of him leaned, very slightly, toward the right, and then went still, and then leaned toward the right again with more weight in it, as if gathering itself.

"Right," Cael said. "Toes."

He opened his eyes in time to see the boy's weight settle forward onto the ball of his right foot.

The partner huffed through his nose, startled, like a man who has just heard someone finish his sentence. For the first time all morning he looked at Cael properly, a long, flat, measuring look. Then he looked back at Wray and waited for his next instruction.

Wray gave him nothing. She wrote.

Cael stepped back to his mark.

"That's the demonstration," he said.

The panel withdrew to the far end of the hall to deliberate.

They stayed inside the hall, at the end farthest from him. They stood close together by the end wall with their slates and spoke in voices too low for him to follow.

He read what he could anyway. He could not help it.

Wray spoke first, and briefly, and then folded her arms. The rotating seat spoke longer, and used his hands, sketching something in the air with one finger that might have been a curve. The partner stood easy in the ring throughout.

He did not sit down, though there was nothing to stop him. He did not wander or stretch or look curiously at the panel. Cael looked at him for a long moment.

He had spent a week watching Brom be hit by this boy, and now he had been hit by him himself, once, on the forearm, and he still did not know his name. Brom had said it had started to feel like a rule. As if asking would break something.

Cael did not think it would break anything. He thought the boy had probably been asked to stand in for other people's tests so often that nobody had thought to ask him anything at all.

That was not a rule. It was just a habit everyone had fallen into without noticing.

He would write that down later, the distance between the room he had feared and the room he got. It seemed like a lesson worth keeping.

The door closed.

Quenna stayed.

"I want you to understand what today was, though. Before you let yourself relax about it."

"Today proves you're real." She folded her hands on the table. "That's all. It establishes the first line of the record. It tells the file that a candidate exists under this provision who can demonstrate consistent, observable practice, exactly as the language requires, with nothing in it a hostile reader can seize on. He waited. He could hear that she was not finished.

"The assessments that matter," Quenna said, "come later. They're the ones that will be read by someone who wants you gone. Today we were writing the file. One day someone else will be reading it, someone who isn't sitting in one of these chairs, and every line we write from now on has to be ready for that person."

He thought of the patient figure he had built under a lamp on the fifth night. He thought of the road he had not taken in the third pass, the small door that had nearly shut in a room full of pens.

"I'll be ready," he said.

"I know you will." She stood and picked up her slate. "You being ready has never been what keeps me up."

She did not explain. He did not ask her to. Hesk did that. Vell did it. Some things were handed over to be held, not opened.

She left.

He would write it in the log as a cost. A bruise for a road he had not taken.

A smear where Cael had sunk under the fourth strike of the first pass. It was the only mark the morning had left on the world, and it would be swept clean before the fifth bell.

Everything else that had happened here existed only on paper now. In a file. In a building full of files.

He understood, standing in the cold grey light, that he had spent the morning being written down. He had chosen every word they were allowed to write. Two things shown, two things held back, one thing never even approached. It had gone well. He meant it to keep going well.

Before he left, he crouched by the ring and pressed two fingers into the smear where he had sunk under the first pass's last strike, the way a person touches the doorframe of a house they are leaving. Then he stood and went to find the others.

"The partner broke the count. I took one."

She studied the mark with the professional eye of a woman who had given and received a great many bruises.

"You took it on purpose," she said. It was not a question.

He thought of the road opening in his chest, and the small door, and three pens.

Lira looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, slowly, the way she nodded when an opponent did something she would have done herself, and she pushed the rest of the bread back across the table to him.

"Eat," she said. "You've earned the crust."

# Chapter 8 — Vantage

By the time he crossed the yard that afternoon, the rumors had found a face to hang themselves on, and it was his.

He had first noticed Edran three days earlier, from the rail of the open floor, where an observer was allowed to stand and watch as long as he did not step over the chalk. Edran had been the easiest person in the hall to pick out. Everyone else on the floor moved around him the way water moves around a post, without seeming to think about it, and he fought with the settled ease of someone who had been at the top of a board for so long that he no longer checked whether he was still there. Glass Path. Iron Rank Two. First on the standings.

Cael had watched him for most of an hour, filling a page.

Glass was not what he had expected. He had always thought of glass as the thing that broke. Edran's declarations broke, certainly; Cael watched a bright, hard shell of something like crystal form along his forearm to take a strike and then burst apart into a glitter of fragments that hung for a heartbeat in the air before they vanished. But the shell did not break because it failed. It broke because breaking was what it was for. The burst carried the strike's force away with it in a hundred directions at once, and before the glitter had faded, a new shell was already forming in the next place Edran expected to be hit.

By the end of that first bout he had crossed out a page of his own notes about brittleness and started again.

And somewhere in the middle of the second, between exchanges, Edran had looked up at the rail.

His eyes had gone first to the open binder, Cael was nearly sure, and only afterward to the person holding it. He held the look a beat longer than a glance needed. His face showed nothing at all. Then he turned back to his opponent and finished the bout, and the rest of it was sharper than the beginning by some small, definite degree, as if he had tightened a screw.

Cael had written, under the page, *Sharper with an audience.*

Then, because the log was the one place he did not let himself be generous: *Or he minds being taken down in ink. Find out which.*

---

Edran was waiting near the yard's north gate, the way a man waits when he has decided where another man will have to walk.

He was not alone. Three or four students were nearby, doing other things very slowly. One was retying a boot that had not come untied. Two were talking with the particular attention of people listening to a different conversation. Cael saw the shape of it before he was close enough to see faces. It was not an ambush. It was an audience.

"Observers don't demonstrate," Edran said.

He said it to the yard in general, in a clear, level voice that carried without being raised. But his eyes stayed on Cael the whole time.

Cael stopped walking.

"That's not what the category is for," Edran went on. "I've read the provision. I've sat beside observers in lectures since my first year. Observers watch. They take notes. They don't get a panel and a closed hall and a training partner at first bell." He tilted his head, very slightly. "So either the faculty spent a morning watching you do nothing, which I don't believe, or somebody wrote a form that says one thing and means another."

The boy retying his boot had stopped pretending.

"What exactly are you?" Edran asked.

It was not hostile. That was the hard part. Cael had faced hostile questions all his life, and hostile questions were easy, in their way; they told you what the person wanted, and you could refuse to give it to them. This was something else. Edran sounded genuinely curious, almost academic, the way a person sounds asking about a rule he has found a hole in. Anger burned out. Curiosity kept a notebook.

Cael felt the yard around him the way he felt a crowd before a bout. The loose half-ring of listeners. The boy with the boot. Whatever he said in the next breath would be in every common room by the seventh bell, word for word, and improved on by the eighth.

He went through what he had. A form that said a thing the signers knew was not so. A binder in his pack that no one here would ever read. Nothing between the two that he could say out loud and keep.

He would not lie. He had decided that a long time ago, on a different road.

"I'm enrolled," he said.

Every word of it was true, and none of it was the thing Edran had asked.

Edran looked at him for a long moment. Cael watched him weigh it, the way he had watched him weigh an opponent between exchanges. He saw Edran consider pressing, and saw him decide against it, not out of kindness but out of something more like respect for a guard that had been put up properly.

"That isn't an answer," Edran said.

"No."

"It's the one you're giving, though."

"Yes."

Edran nodded once, slowly, as if filing something away for later. Then he turned and walked off toward the training hall without another word. The boy with the boot went after him. The others drifted apart, already talking.

Cael stood alone in the yard with the afternoon light going long across the chalk lines and understood that the question had not been answered. It had only been put down, carefully, where both of them knew it was.

He told Brom about it that evening in the stable, over barley, because not telling Brom things had stopped being one of his habits sometime in the last year.

Brom listened the way he listened to an account of a fight, which was what it had been. He asked two questions. Where had the other students been standing? Had Edran raised his voice? Then he ate in silence for a while, turning it over.

"The yard was the point," he said finally. "Not the question. He wanted a dozen people to hear you not answer."

"I gathered that."

"It isn't you he's cross with." Brom pointed his spoon toward the window, at the dark yard and the training hall beyond it. "It's the standings. He's sat at the top of them since he came, and he thinks they're true. Then this morning the faculty spent first bell on somebody who isn't on them at all, and nobody will say why." He went back to his bowl. "If you've climbed a ladder, you don't like hearing it might be leaning on the wrong wall."

"Should I be worried about him?"

Brom considered that with great care.

"Watch him," he said. "Don't fear him. Whatever he does next, he'll do it by the book, in daylight, with his name signed at the bottom. You'll see it coming a week off." He paused. "That's the useful part. The other part is that he'll never cheat you. Remember which is which."

*First assessment. Function shown: Wind framework, four passes at panel speed, one with the count broken; surface read, four calls, eyes closed on the last. Benefit: assessment satisfied. Wray's words, on the record: Unorthodox architecture. Consistent execution. No safety concerns.*

He was not sure she knew she was doing it. He was not sure she didn't.

He had come to think every Path held an opinion. Not only a set of abilities, but a settled view of what a fight was for and how one ought to be won, and you could see the view in how its people stood, long before they threw anything.

*Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose.*

*Stone believes that if you refuse to move, the other person has to.*

They sat on the wall outside the residence wing at dusk, the three of them, as they had sat on walls in a dozen towns.

Lira had a cold cloth from the kitchens folded over her shoulder, where the Force Path's push had landed. The bruise underneath had come up dark and broad. She did not seem to mind it. "The assessor initialed it," she said at last. "Before the sixth bell. Gerda was right. He looked at the time when I handed him the slip. He actually looked."

"So it's dated today," said Brom.

"It's dated today. It's in the file." She tilted her head back against the wall behind her. "With a dot next to it."

Cael waited. He had learned, over a year and a half, that Lira's thoughts arrived in their own time and did not like to be fetched.

"I still hate the dot," she said. "I want you both to know that. I'm not going to wake up one morning and like it."

"Nobody asked you to like it," said Brom.

"I know." She was quiet a moment. "But I signed the page. And I'm glad I signed it. I'm glad I'm here. "What happened to the fight between them?" Cael asked.

Lira thought about it with great care, the way she thought about an opponent's footwork.

"I think I just let them both sit down," she said. "There's room on the bench."

"Don't," said Lira. "You'll ruin it."

He made the week's inventory on the last night of the second week, sitting cross-legged on his bed with the binder open across his knees and the lamp turned low.

He counted the Path pages first.

Glass, from Edran. Stone, from Hobb. Shield, from Wray, from the way she stood and the way she changed one condition at a time, though he had never seen her use her Path at all. Iron Skin, from Brom, which he had known for a year but had never before watched taken apart by someone better at taking things apart than he was. Wind, from Lira, in a formal yard, with a clerk's dot beside her name. Force, from the third-year she had beaten. Ash, from Gerda, glimpsed once in a supervised block, a low grey haze that seemed to slow everything that passed through it. Blade, from a pair in the training hall who drilled every day in total silence and had plainly stopped needing to tell each other anything. Current, from a second-year girl whose footwork never once stopped moving. Storm, from a fourth-year off the movement floor who left the formal yard smelling of rain. Anchor, from a Copper first-year who planted himself so absolutely that two people together could not shift him a finger's width.

Eleven.

Eleven Paths, watched at close range. Closer than a spectator stood. Closer than the circuit's crowded nights had ever let him get. Nobody's result depended on him, and he was allowed nowhere else, so every scrap of his attention had gone into looking.

He turned to the bottoms of the pages and read down the line he had added to each.

*Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose.*

*Stone believes that if you refuse to move, the other person has to.*

*Shield believes nothing should be spent that doesn't have to be.*

*Iron Skin believes you can borrow anything, if someone gives it to you first.*

*Wind believes the place a strike is going is a place you can leave.*

*Force believes distance is a thing you can take away from people.*

*Ash believes everything is easier to handle when it's slower.*

*The Blade pair believe in each other. I couldn't find a Path for that.*

He stopped there for a while.

He had walked through the gate two weeks ago thinking of the observer category the way everyone did. A patch over a hole in the rules. Bars with a hinge on them. Something to put up with until somebody could argue him into a better one. He had thought the narrowness was the whole of it. He had thought his work at Greyvane would be pushing at its bars.

It was not the whole of it.

The category had shut him out of every open bout on the grounds. It had refused him a partner, in writing. It had put him in a room full of thirteen-year-olds and on the far end of every desk. And because it had shut him out of everything, it had left him standing at the edge of everything, with nothing to do but look.

He had never had so much to look at in his life.

He wrote it slowly, in the careful hand he saved for entries he meant to reread.

*Greyvane, end of week two. Eleven Paths seen closer than the circuit ever let me. They built the observer track to hold me still.*

He looked at the line. Then he wrote the rest of it.

*Holding still turns out to be the best place to see from. It isn't a cage. It's a vantage point.*

---

He read back over the two weeks before he closed the binder, the way he did on nights that felt like hinges.

Brom had a teacher. A woman who wanted to be proved wrong and had said so out loud, and who had found the soft place in him and offered to mend it. He was still holding his right side when he sat down. He was happier than Cael had ever seen him.

Lira had put something down at an intake desk and picked something else up on a formal floor, and she was carrying both now, the signed page and the beaten third-year, and neither of them had won. She was letting them share the chair. She had not said so. Cael had watched her do it.

He himself had a name on a public board in the main hall, written in the same ink as everyone else's, and a hollow circle on his tag, and a refusal in writing filed behind the stranger's note in the back of his binder. He had a first assessment satisfied and a second one coming in a month. He had a question asked in the open by the best fighter in the building, and an answer that was true and was not an answer, and a strong feeling that the question had only been set down, not dropped.

He had a name, too, that he owed a thank-you to. Hobb.

He closed the binder.

Across the corridor he heard Brom say, quite clearly, in his sleep, "Hobb," and then grunt, and turn over.

Cael lay back in the dark and laughed silently at the ceiling until his ribs hurt. Then he lay still, and listened to the building, and found he knew every sound in it now. The stair. The latch. The pipe. The bell, far off, saying it was late.

He knew where he was. He fell asleep knowing it.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

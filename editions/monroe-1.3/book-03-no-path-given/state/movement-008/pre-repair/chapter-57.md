# Chapter 57 — Shall Mean

Prynn brought the code herself.

Cael saw her come in at the side door of the assembly room a little before the first bell, while the benches were still filling, and for a moment he did not understand what he was looking at. She was carrying the first volume of the charter against her chest in both arms, as she had carried it to the long table on the first full morning of the three weeks, as if it might break. Behind her came Hobb, with a stack of dark-spined volumes balanced on his forearms from his elbows to his chin, and the bundled fascicles with their faded tapes hanging over the top of the stack like the roots of something dug up whole. Behind Hobb came Brom with the rest.

They set it all down on the respondent's table, the charter's three volumes and the schedule's seven and the fascicles in their order, and Prynn squared each one against the edge as it went down, with the side of her hand, until the bare table of yesterday had a low dark wall along its far side. Then she stood back and looked at it.

"Mine," she said. "Not theirs. If you're going to read the code to a magistrate you'll read it off a copy that's been kept properly." She looked at him over her spectacles. "I've counted them. Every fascicle. Don't drop anything."

"I won't."

"See you don't." She turned to Hobb. "Thank you. You can go and sit down." Hobb went. She turned to Brom. "You too. The front bench. I've kept it."

She had. Cael looked round at the gallery and saw that the whole middle of the front bench was empty, five places of it, in a room where people were already standing in the passage, and that nobody had dared to sit there. Prynn went and sat down at the end of the empty places with her arms folded, and Brom sat down beside her, and a moment later Karis came along the bench from the aisle and sat beside Brom, and Lira beside Karis. That left one place, between Lira and the next person along, an old woman from the town who had plainly been warned. On the empty place Prynn had laid a slip of paper, folded once, of the narrow kind she put in a gap on a shelf where a volume had been taken down.

Nobody asked her what it was for. Cael did not ask either. He looked at it a moment, and then away.

The room was fuller than yesterday, if that was possible. The people who did not belong to the hill had multiplied overnight. There were two more grey coats, and a man who had the look of an academy's provost about him and sat beside the woman in dark blue, and a cluster of people from the town at the back who could not have said a word about the charter and had come anyway, because somewhere over the winter the town had decided that the observer was its own. He saw the draper, who let a room over her shop to people's mothers, standing by the back wall with her shawl over her head. He saw the station clerk from the grey building at the corner, without his ledger. He did not see the old woman in black with the cushion, and then he did; she had moved nearer the front.

Oona was on the front bench at the left by the aisle, in the same place as yesterday, with her slate on her knees. She was not writing. She was looking at the respondent's table with her whole face.

Quenna came in by the side door and did not come to the table. She went to the row of chairs behind it, set a little back from it, where the academy's counsel and Naveth already sat, and took the chair at the end nearest the table, and sat down in it, and put her slate on her knee. She did not look at him. Cael felt her there exactly as she had said he would.

He sat down alone at the respondent's table, in front of the low dark wall of books, and put his hands flat on the wood for a moment, and took them off again.

---

The panel came in at the bell, and the room stood and sat.

Yorlan had the thin book under his arm. The grey-bearded assessor had his pad. And Ilsev, coming last, carried in her two arms a set of dark-spined volumes of her own, tied with a strap, which she had not brought the day before. She set them down on the bench in front of her chair and unbuckled the strap and laid them out in a row, and opened the first of them at a place near its beginning, and put one finger on the page.

Cael saw Coss see it. He saw the Warden's eyes go to the row of volumes in front of the senior evaluation seat, and stay there for a breath, and come away.

Yorlan opened his book.

"This tribunal sits under the charter, in public, and on the record. The matter is named in the notice. The parties are named in the notice. The panel will hear the challenge first, and the answer after. The panel will hear what is put to it and no more. The panel will not hear argument for which it has not been shown the ground. The respondent will state his name."

It was the same. Every word was the same as yesterday, at the same pace, in the same order, and somewhere behind him in the gallery Cael heard a small sound that might have been Karis, and might have been a laugh.

He stood. "Caelen Hesk-ward."

"The panel notes it." Yorlan did not tell him to sit. He turned a page in the thin book, and read from it in the same voice. "At the close of yesterday's sitting the respondent put a question to this panel about the reach of its own hand. A tribunal may always be asked how far its hand goes. It may never decline to look." He turned another page. "The panel has looked. The question is before it. The respondent will be heard standing, and while he is heard nobody will break in on him, from either table or from this bench, except the panel for order." He looked up. "The floor is the respondent's."

He sat back in the high-backed chair.

Cael stood alone in the middle of two hundred people who had stopped breathing.

He knew this quiet. It was the quiet of the main floor at the Ironyard in the instant before Vell lifted her hand for the first exchange, when the rails have stopped shouting their odds and six hundred people are only waiting. It was the formal yard with the frost still white in the cracks of the flags and Karis across the chalk, laying her first point. He had never once stood in it with less to lose in his body and more to lose in his life. He laid both palms on the table for the space of a heartbeat, as he had laid them on the edge of every ring since Ardenmere, and felt the grain of the wood, and through it, very faintly where he leaned, the press of a folded page and a pencilled slip against his chest.

*This is real. Start from that.*

He took his hands off the wood and began.

"The Warden is right," he said.

He let the four words go out into the room by themselves and stand there, because they had cost him three weeks and they were worth a breath of quiet.

"Everything he showed you yesterday is so. The provision is a kindness written for children who haven't yet been to a station, and his drafting records show it, signed, and when the committee was asked whether it should reach somebody like me it said no and wrote down why. I've got nothing to say against any of that, and I won't waste the panel's patience pretending I have. I went to a station. I went in Denvash, at fourteen, on a day the registry holds. So the form describes somebody I'm not, and an enrollment that stands on that form doesn't stand. I give the Warden all of it. Every part."

Nothing came from the gallery at all.

He kept his eyes on the bench, because he had promised himself on the stair that he would look nowhere else. But he could feel the room behind him, and he felt it change, the way a crowd round a ring changes when the fighter it came to cheer lowers his hands. Half the benches, he knew without turning, had just decided he had come in to surrender.

Yorlan leaned a very little way forward, and did the next thing the procedure gave him to do. Afterward Cael thought that this, more than anything he had said himself, was what made it real.

"A thing given on this record," said Yorlan, "is given for good. Nobody takes it back afterward, at any sitting, for any reason. From this moment the panel will hold every part of the challenge as proved. The respondent will say that he understands it."

"I understand it, Magistrate. Every part, proved, for good." He kept his voice flat, the way Karis had made him say it eleven times behind the bench of books. "I'm not giving it you as a move. I'm giving it you because it's so, and nothing I've come here to say needs it to be otherwise. If my argument needed one true thing to be false, it'd be a wrong argument."

Across the boards, Coss did not move at all.

Cael had watched that man be still in a good many ways by now. There was the stillness of a borrowed desk in Ardenmere. There was the tidy patience he had worn through the whole of the first sitting, laying stone on stone. There was the other kind, which Cael had seen only once, in the swept yard by the pump at dusk, when Coss had stopped walking on the far side of the chalk. It was that one now. A man who has built a wall nobody can push over has nothing left to do with it when the other side walks up and sits down on top of it. Yesterday's whole case lay across the floor between the two tables, every stone of it true, and Cael was standing on it, and Coss knew it, and did not move a finger.

"So I won't ask the panel whether the enrollment is good," said Cael. "We've settled that it isn't. I'd like to ask something that has to be settled first, before anybody gets to that. To void my enrollment the Compact has to do something to me. Doing something to a person needs a right to do it, and a right in a code needs a line in the code that gives it. I'm asking the panel to show me where it's written." He paused. "I've looked for three weeks. I don't think it's there. I don't think the Compact was ever given the right to do this to me, by its own words, and I'd like to show the panel how I came to think so."

---

He did not reach for any of the books yet.

"I read the code the way I'd read anybody I had to fight," he said. "Not for what each part can do to me. For what each part is standing on." He held his left hand out flat, palm down, at the height of his chest, and set his right hand flat beneath it. "The Compact's powers over a practitioner are all in the enforcement chapters: every sanction, every order, every removal. But the enforcement chapters can only make you answer for an obligation, and the obligations only fall on you once you've been classified. So the powers rest on the obligations, and the obligations rest on classification." He lowered the left hand onto the right, and then moved both down to the table. "And classification rests on one sentence. One. In the whole of the code there's only one place that says what the word means, and it's the first article of the charter. If you want to know how far the Compact can reach, there's no point starting with the enforcement chapters, where the reaching is. You start at the foot of it."

He drew the first volume of the charter out of the low wall of books by its spine, as Karis had made him do eleven times, and opened it at the first article after the preamble, and laid his finger on the line.

He read it slowly.

"*Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.*"

He did not look up. He read it again, at the same pace, so that the roll and the room would both have it whole, and on the second reading he stopped after *shall mean* and let the two words stand on their own in the high quiet hall.

Then he laid the volume open on the table and looked up at the bench.

"*Shall mean,*" he said. "The drafters had softer words to hand, and they knew how to use them; the code's full of *includes* and *may be taken to cover*. They didn't use them here. This sentence isn't showing the panel an example of the thing so that it can go and find others like it. It's drawing a line round the thing and shutting it. There's no second door in that sentence. A practitioner is classified if the designation he holds is written in the schedule, and in no other way, *for all purposes*. If a word isn't in the schedule, then by the Compact's own first article it isn't a classification. Not a nearly one. Not one for most purposes. It's simply not that kind of word at all."

At the bench, Ilsev's finger had not moved from her own copy. He was nearly sure it rested on the same line.

"So the panel has a fact to find, and it's a small one," said Cael. "Anybody with the books can check it. It's strange that it seems never to have been checked on any record I could find. The registry calls me [SHATTERED]. The Warden's papers call me that on every page he showed you yesterday, and his challenge rests its whole weight there, on my status. So the panel only needs to know whether that designation is written down where the charter says a classification has to be." He laid his hand on the first volume of the schedule, closed, at the end of the row. "I'll ask the panel for its help. Help me find [SHATTERED] in the schedule."

He opened the first volume, and began to read it aloud.

---

The recording officer turned the brass glass at the first word, because a quarter-hour was a quarter-hour, and he had been taught to mark them all.

Havel had recorded a great many things since the summer. He had recorded depositions and petitions and once, at a district sitting on the coast, a dispute between two fishing guilds over a boundary stone, which had run for three days and turned on the meaning of the word *seaward*. He had never before recorded a man reading a schedule. Nobody read a schedule aloud. People cited it, by section and entry, in a breath; they did not open its first volume and begin, as the boy at the respondent's table had just begun, at the beginning.

"*The primary enumeration,*" read the boy. "*The Path families, in the schedule's order.*"

Havel wrote it.

He wrote it in the service's short hand, the little hooks and bars that let a recorder keep up with the fastest witness, and he found within a quarter of a page that he hardly needed them. The boy did not read fast. He read at the pace of somebody who meant every word to be written down, clearly, a little slower than speech, and he stopped at the end of every entry for exactly as long as it took a pen to finish it. Havel noticed that by the fourth entry. By the tenth he understood that the boy had noticed the pen first, and had set his pace by it.

Every Path family had its name, and under it its ladder of tiers, Copper to Gold and the rank within each, and under that the notes of its admission: the year it was entered, the amendment that had entered it, any condition that had been attached. The boy read all of it. He read Stone with its four old notes and its one new one. He read Wind. He read Iron Skin, and Havel heard the stocky boy on the front bench shift his weight at the name and go still again. He read Glass and Blade and Ash and Anchor. He read the compound designations, which Havel had not known there were so many of, and the lineage variants, each with the district that had argued for it and the year it had been allowed.

At the end of the first quarter-hour Havel turned the glass and made his mark in the margin, and found that his hand had begun to ache in the place between the thumb and the first finger where it always ached on a long day, and that the day had barely started.

He looked up at the room once while the sand ran.

The gallery was restless. He had sat at enough recording desks to know the sound of it, the rustle of people who had come to hear an argument and were being read a list. A boy on the third bench had his chin on his fist. Somewhere at the back two people were whispering, and the clerk at the rope had turned his head toward them twice. The woman in the dark blue coat at the end of the bench had stopped writing. In the middle of the front bench the old archivist sat with her arms folded and her eyes shut, and Havel could not tell whether she was listening or asleep, and suspected she was doing both.

At the delegation's table the Warden sat with his hands folded on his closed cases and his eyes on the boy.

Havel bent to his roll again.

It was some way into the second quarter-hour that the room began to change, and he heard it change before he saw it.

He could not have said when. There was no single moment. The rustling simply grew less, the way rain grows less on a roof, so gradually that you notice only when it has stopped. The whispering at the back had ended. The boy with his chin on his fist had taken it off his fist. And the quiet that came in to fill the place where the restlessness had been was a different quiet from the one at the start. The first had been people waiting for something interesting. This one, Havel thought, listening to it with his pen moving, was people who had begun to understand that the interesting thing was already happening, slowly, in front of them, one entry at a time.

The boy had finished the primary enumeration and turned to the provisional annex.

"*Contested-Standing,*" he read. "*Entered under the fourteenth amendment, with terms of review.*" He read the terms of review. "*Interim-Assessed.*" He read its conditions. "*Dual-Registered.*"

Every category the Compact had ever tried before it made up its mind was in the annex, with the amendment that had let it in and the conditions on which it was let, and the boy read every one, and every one had a name. Havel wrote each name. He had written perhaps four hundred names since the first word, and they lay along his roll in a long dark ladder, every Path and every tier and every provisional standing the Compact had ever put its hand to. It came to him somewhere in the annex, quite suddenly, with the pen still moving, what it was that he was making.

He was writing the whole of the Compact's word for what a person could be onto a single roll, in order, under the date. And there was one word he would not be writing.

It was in the subject's file, in the field where the file's flag was entered. It was in the challenge, on its first page. Havel had seen it in the routing papers of the autumn request on Wednesday afternoon, under the line Ilsev had read three times. It had gone down the hill on Monday morning in the senior evaluation seat's referral, in the delegation's bag, on the first courier of the day, while the magistrate was reading the notice into the record and the boy was saying his name. And it was not on Havel's roll. It would not be. The boy was reading every name the schedule held, and every name the schedule held was going down on Havel's roll in his own hand, and not one of them was that.

A recorder marked silences. He made a short dash for every count of quiet, so that a magistrate reading afterward would know where a room had held its breath. There was no mark for an absence. There was no mark for a word that should have been on the roll and was not. Nobody had ever needed one.

He went on writing. His hand ached, and he let it.

---

The Warden stood up at the turn of the third quarter-hour.

Havel saw him rise before anybody spoke, and his own pen stopped of itself, a finger's width above the roll, waiting.

Coss's voice was as level as it had been all day yesterday. "The Compact will stipulate, Magistrate, that the respondent's designation has no entry in any part of the schedule. It will put that in writing. To spare the panel the rest of the morning—"

"The respondent's primary presentation is not to be interrupted," said Yorlan.

He did not say it with any heat. He said it as he said everything, in the voice that gave every word the same weight, and Havel wrote it down in that voice.

Then Yorlan went on, and Havel, who had sat at three of the magistrate's sittings before the grade and had never once heard him add anything to a ruling on order, felt his pen go cold in his fingers.

"And the panel declines the stipulation," said Yorlan. "A stipulated absence is an agreement between parties. The respondent appears to be offering the panel a demonstrated one. They are not the same object, and the panel prefers the better one." He did not look at the Warden. "The respondent will proceed."

Coss sat down.

He sat without any sign of having been sat down, folded his hands on his cases again, and looked at the boy as he had been looking at him before. Havel, writing the magistrate's words onto the roll in his best short hand, thought that he had just watched the best advocate in the regional service try the only door left in the room and find it shut, and take it as calmly as he would have taken the weather.

The boy had not looked round at any of it. He waited until the magistrate had finished, with his finger on the line where he had stopped. Then he went on from the next word, at the same pace, as if he had only paused to breathe.

On the bench, Ilsev closed her book.

Havel saw her do it. She had been following in her own copy since the first entry, with one finger moving down the page at the boy's pace, turning when he turned. Now, without any change in her face, she closed the volume in front of her, and laid her hand flat on its cover, and sat back in her chair. She did not take up the next. She looked instead at nothing in particular, at the air over the respondent's table. Havel had seen that look on examiners at the end of a long assessment, when they had stopped checking the work for mistakes because they had decided there were none, and had begun to check it for the one thing rarer than being right, which was being complete.

He turned the glass, and marked the margin, and went on.

After the annex the boy read the appendix of disputed standings. Every category in it had been forced on the Compact by one of its member guilds, and the Compact had let each one in and then set down beside it, in the guild's own words, that it had objected. The schedule kept even its own reluctance on the page, as if it could not bear to forget that it had once minded something. Then came the amendment schedules, one for every revision, and in them, struck through but left standing in their places, the entries the Compact had once made and later taken back. The boy read the struck entries as carefully as the standing ones. He said *struck* before each, and the year of the striking, and then the name, and Havel wrote *struck* before each on the roll and the name after it.

The light was moving.

Havel noticed it on the floor. When the sitting had opened, the light from the high windows on the south side had lain across the scrubbed boards between the two tables in a long pale bar. Now it lay across the foot of the respondent's table, and climbed a little way up the low dark wall of books, and the gilt on the spines of the nearest volumes had begun to shine. It had moved the length of a bench. He had not seen it move, any more than he had seen the gallery go quiet.

He looked at the gallery now, and it was not the same room it had been at the first bell.

Nobody was restless. Nobody was whispering. Two hundred people and more sat on the benches and stood in the passage and along the back wall, and every one of them was facing the same way, and the quiet they made was so complete that Havel could hear the boy turn a page from where he sat, and hear the sand run in the glass. It was not a waiting quiet any more. It had a shape. It had grown as the list grew, as if the room were holding the whole length of the list in its two hands. Every Path and every tier, every provisional standing and every protest and every struck name, generation upon generation of the Compact's own words for what a person could be, entry after entry after entry; and every person in the room was holding all of it, and waiting at the end of every entry for the one word that every paper in the room said the boy was.

It did not come.

The stocky boy on the front bench had both hands flat on his knees. The girl beside him with the wind-burned face had her chin up and her eyes shut. The girl beside her, the one with ink on her fingers, had her pen in her hand over an open notebook and had not written anything for half an hour; Havel had seen her stop. And the old archivist at the end of the bench had opened her eyes. She was watching the boy turn the pages of her books with an expression Havel did not have a word for, and did not try to find one. Beside her, on the empty place, the folded slip lay where she had put it.

At the far end of the front bench a girl with a polished tag had her slate on her knees and her pencil still, and her lips were moving very slightly, and Havel understood after a moment that she was saying the names with him.

He looked down at his roll. Four hundred names. Six hundred. He had lost count, which a recorder should never do, and he did not mind. He turned the glass again, the fourth time, and marked the margin, and his hand did not shake, and he watched it not shake from a little way off, as he had once watched it enter a query word for word in a day-book.

The boy reached the last of the fascicles. He untied its faded tape, and opened it, and read the last amendment the Compact had ever made to its schedule, and the last designation in it.

Havel wrote it down.

Then the boy closed the fascicle, and laid it on the others, square, and put his hand flat on the pile for a moment, and the room heard the sound of it, a small dry sound like a book set down on a shelf at the end of a day.

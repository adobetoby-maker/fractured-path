# Chapter 31 — Twenty-Two

There were eleven people at the rail for Gerda's supplementary bout, which was six more than had come to her review, and Cael counted them as he came through the gate because he could not help it, and because he thought that Gerda, who counted hours, would want somebody to have counted this.

Lira was at the front, with her shoulder unstrapped at last and a purple-yellow bruise showing at the edge of her collar. Brom was beside her, and Karis, with her hands in her pockets and no notebook. Hobb was at the far corner, as always, with his hands in his pockets too. The other seven Cael had not expected. Two of them were Current Path second-years from the movement floor, one of whom was the girl whose footwork never stopped, who had lost to Gerda at the review and seemed to have come to see what she had lost to. Three were from the heat-Paths floor, Ash and Ember first-years, standing together with the shy bright look of children who have heard that somebody from their own floor is going to do something and do not want to miss it. One was the second-year tout, who had no wager on it, because nobody was taking wagers on a re-certification candidate's supplementary bout at first bell, and who had come, he told Cael with great dignity, out of professional interest.

The last was Edran. He stood by the standings board with his arms folded, as he had stood for Lira's review. He did not look at the board.

The panel sat at the north end: Quenna in the middle with the review ledger open and Gerda's request beside it, Wray on her left in the brown coat, the heat-Paths instructor with scorched cuffs on her right. The same panel, as the provision required.

Gerda came through the gate as the first bell finished. She did not stop to look at the clock above the main hall door. Cael watched her walk past it with her chin level and her stride unbroken and her eyes on the ring, and he saw her not look, and he knew it had cost her something, and he knew she had decided to pay it.

"For the record," said Quenna. "Supplementary recorded bout, elected by the candidate under the reconsideration clause of the re-certification provision. The candidate: Gerda, Ash Path. The bout will be considered together with the candidate's formal review. The candidate takes the south mark."

The opponent was the Blade fourth-year with the very fast hands. Cael did not know whether the panel had chosen him on purpose, and suspected Wray had, and suspected that Gerda had been told and had said nothing but *Good*. He took the north mark and stood light on his feet with his fast hands loose and his face listening to whatever it always listened to.

Wray lowered her finger.

Gerda laid her haze. It went out from her across the swept earth, low and grey, two paces in every direction, as it always had. The Blade fourth-year stopped at its edge and waited, as he had waited before, light and patient, outside the slowness, with nothing to make him go into it.

Gerda came out.

She came out at once, before he had settled, two long strides out of her own grey onto clean ground. She went straight at him with nothing slowing anybody down at all, and his fast hand flicked out and caught her on the shoulder, exactly where it had caught her a week ago, and she took it. Cael saw her take it. He saw her see the hand coming and see the space where she could have turned from it, and not turn, and let it land on the point of her shoulder where everyone at the rail and all three chairs could see it land.

"Touch," said Wray. "Opponent."

Gerda went back to the south mark with her shoulder held a little away from her side and her face white and absolutely calm. She did not look at the rail. She did not look at the clock. She looked at the Blade fourth-year, and Cael, who had watched her for two months, saw something in her face he had never seen there before. It was the look Lira wore on a circuit floor after a hit she had chosen. *That one cost me. Now you owe me.*

"She took it," said the tout behind Cael, in a reverent whisper.

"She bought it," said Lira, without turning round.

The second exchange she won with Hobb's lesson.

She laid the haze again and came out of it again, and this time when the fast hand came she took it on the forearm, turned from it, and the turn put her in under his arm, close, closer than a Blade fighter ever let anybody get. She put her shoulder into his side with the whole of her weight and the whole of her height, the way she had put it into Hobb on the fourth evening. A Blade fourth-year was not a Stone fourth-year. He went three steps sideways and nearly off his feet, and her two fingers were on his breastbone before he had them back under him.

"Touch. Candidate."

At the far corner of the rail Hobb took his hands out of his pockets.

The third exchange went by slower. He had learned; she had shown him twice now that she would come out, and he met her at the edge of the haze instead of waiting outside it. For a long moment the two of them circled on the border of the grey, his fast hands against her height, neither committing. Cael held his breath. He could see the old weight on Gerda's back foot, the pull toward the haze, the relief that waited for her two paces behind her. He saw her feel it, and he saw her leave it there. She stepped forward onto clean ground, took his next strike on the outside of the forearm without slipping it, and turned under it. Her fingers were on his breastbone. Wray called it, and called the bout.

Two touches to one.

The yard did not cheer, because it never did. But the three heat-Paths first-years at the rail made a sound of pure delight they could not keep in, and the Current Path girl who had lost to Gerda at the review clapped twice before she remembered where she was and stopped, and Lira at the front of the rail put both hands flat on the cold wood and leaned on them and let her head drop between her shoulders, and Cael heard her breathe out for what he thought must have been the first time since the first bell.

The panel did not go to the far wall. They talked at the table, low, for no longer than it took the Blade fourth-year to walk across the ring and shake Gerda's hand, which he did, formally and in silence, as he did everything. Then Quenna wrote, and lifted her pen.

"Supplementary recorded bout. Won by the candidate, two touches to one. The panel considers it together with the formal review." She read off the ledger in the slow flat cadence of the record. "The candidate came out of her own declaration under live opposition and committed fully, at cost, twice. The recommendation of reconsideration is withdrawn. Continued track."

She looked up from the ledger at Gerda, across the frosted earth.

"Initials," she said. "And the candidate's signature."

Gerda walked to the table and signed. She signed carefully, every letter separate. Then she stood and read the line Quenna had written, as Lira had stood and read hers, and Cael saw her lips move once.

She came off the ring toward the rail. She stopped in front of Lira, who had straightened up and folded her arms and arranged her face into something she clearly hoped looked unconcerned, and did not.

"Answered," said Gerda.

"Correctly?" said Lira.

Gerda considered the word, as she considered every word, as if it were a clause she had been handed to read aloud.

"Correctly," she said. "And the right way round, this time."

Then she did look at the clock above the main hall door. She looked at it for a long moment. And she laughed. It was a short, surprised, rusty sound, as if it had not been used in a long time and had had to be found at the bottom of a box. Lira, who had kept her face still through two bouts of her own and one of Gerda's, did not manage it through that, and put her good arm round Gerda's shoulders, bruise and all, and held on.

Hobb passed them on his way to the gate. He did not stop. He slowed, as he had slowed at the rail after the exhibition, and looked at Gerda once.

"Didn't go back," he said, in his deep slow voice, and went on out of the yard.

---

The twenty-second session was that evening, and all four of them came, because it was the last one written into the plan in the back of Karis's ledger and Lira had said, at the midday meal, that she was not going to let a thing end without being in the room for it, especially a thing that had produced nothing.

Lira sat on the floor by the door with her knees up and Gerda's laugh still visible somewhere at the corners of her mouth. Brom sat beside her with a heel of bread he did not take out of his coat. Karis stood at the far end of the whitewashed room with the ledger closed on the boards at her feet, and Cael stood at three paces from her, as close as the room had ever brought them except for the one morning at a single pace.

"I consent," said Karis, "to this session," and wrote the hour.

She had told him what the last session would be, at breakfast, in the voice she used for proposing a variable. It would not be a variable. It would be all of them. Everything the twenty-one before it had tried, put into one hour, at the nearest distance and the longest duration she could sustain at demonstration intensity without her points going dirty. Single points and paired, held and released, laid in the air and on the floor, announced and unannounced, her Drawn Channel at every length the room would take. She would run it fresh, rested since the morning. And he would reach every way he had ever reached, in turn and then all at once, with the read open on his skin and every channel of attention he owned turned toward her like lamps.

"If it doesn't close in that," she had said, "it doesn't close in this room. And then we'll know that, which is the finding."

So she began, and he reached.

He knew her now. That was the thing he understood, standing three paces from her in the last hour of the series, while the first point bloomed at the height of her shoulder and held, steady as a coin of lamplight. He knew her better than he had known anybody he had ever watched, better than he had known Reydan at the top of the bout and better, in this one narrow way, than he knew Lira. He knew that her off hand would go still a quarter of a second before every ignition, and a hair longer by the end of the hour. He knew the half-breath through the nose before every sustain. He knew how long her release would hang in the air at the start of a session and how much longer it would hang by its end, so exactly that he could have told the time of evening by it. He knew where she would lay the next point before she laid it, and twice in the hour, with the read open, he felt the air at that place grow faintly heavier before the light came, as it had on the long afternoon. He could have shut his eyes and rebuilt any one of twenty-two sessions from the sounds alone, the small hiss of a point, the kettle-song of a channel, the click of her pen. He had learned her past memory, down into the place below memory where Lira's footwork lived in his feet.

And every way he reached, and all of them at once, nothing came.

There was no settling, and no space being filled. Nothing arrived anywhere in him, and no line appeared in anybody's handwriting. There was a boy standing three paces from a girl in a whitewashed room, wanting, and the girl's small exact fires blooming and going out, and the light in the high window going from grey to dark. Lira and Brom by the door did not move once in the whole hour.

At the end Karis let her last point go and watched its shimmer hang and fade, very slowly, because she was tired now, and then she bent and picked up the ledger.

"Null," said Cael.

"Null." She wrote it, in the same careful hand, at the same size, as she had written the first. *Session twenty-two. All conditions of sessions one to twenty-one, combined. Result: null.* Then she did not close the ledger. She stood and looked at the page for a long moment, and then she drew a line across it under the last session, straight, from one side to the other, the way a person draws a line under a column of figures before writing the total.

Under the line she wrote one more sentence. She did not read it aloud. She turned the ledger round and held it out across the three paces so that he could read it under the fourth clause.

*Twenty-two controlled sessions. Twenty-two nulls. We are missing a condition.*

"I don't know what it is," said Karis. Her voice was perfectly level. "The pattern's real. I'd stake both notebooks on it. You watch, and then something comes. But watching isn't closing it, not at demonstration intensity, not at any distance or for any length of time I can sustain, not however hard you reach. Something else closes it, and whatever it is, it isn't in this room." She lowered the ledger. "I can't think of anything I could bring into this room that would be it, without breaking the line at the bottom of our page."

Cael said nothing. He stood with his hands at his sides and the hypothesis in the binder under his arm in his own square hand, and he felt Lira's eyes on him from the door, steady, and did not look round. *I'm keeping it off her.* He had said it on the wall, and he meant it, and it was still the honest thing. It was also the hardest thing he had done in twenty-two sessions, harder than any of the reaching.

Karis looked at him for a moment as if she knew there was something behind his silence. Perhaps she did. She had a page, after all, called *Not asked*. She did not ask.

"So that's the end of the plan," she said. "But I want to say one more thing, with all three of you here, so that it's witnessed."

Brom took his hand out of his coat.

"Whatever it is that closes it," said Karis, "someday there may be a way to find out that doesn't break that last line. An honest way, that nobody has to arrange in secret. I don't know what it would look like. I've asked Quenna once whether there could ever be anything more than watching, inside the rules, and she said not as the provision's written, and I asked her to keep the question." She held Cael's eyes across three paces. "If that day ever comes, I want it to be me. I've done the hard part already. I've stood in front of it twenty-two times on purpose, knowing what it might do. If it's going to be tested, I'd rather it were tested on someone who knows what she's standing in front of, and has written down everything she knows about it first."

The quiet room was very quiet.

"You don't have to decide that," said Lira from the door. Her voice was not quite steady. "Not now. Not ever, if you don't want."

"I know I don't." Karis did not look away from Cael. "I've decided it anyway. I'm only telling you, so that it's on the record somewhere honest, which is this room, with the three of you in it."

Cael found that he could not say anything at all for a moment. He thought of the line at the bottom of the second page of terms, *if anything at all happens to the source that the source did not choose*, and of the girl who had written it, standing in front of him now with her ledger in her hand and her face perfectly calm, choosing.

"Noted," he said at last, because it was the only word he had that was big enough and it was hers.

Something moved at the corner of Karis's mouth, and she did not correct it.

Then Brom cleared his throat.

"Is there," he said, "now that it's over, any chance at all of a clause about barley?"

Karis looked at him. She looked at the ledger. Then she uncapped her pen and wrote, under the line, under the sentence about the missing condition, in her small upright hand, and turned it round so that Brom could read it from the door.

*Witness's barley clause: granted, after the fact, in recognition of service.*

Brom read it twice. He sat back against the wall with his bread in his hand and an expression of such deep and peaceful satisfaction that Lira, beside him, laughed until she had to put her head down on her knees.

---

There was a note under his door when he came up from supper, folded once, with no envelope and no seal, delivered with the ceremony of a laundry list.

*After the fourth sitting. My room, the third bell. Bring Karis. She has the same note. Q.*

He read it standing in the doorway with one hand on the latch, twice, and then a third time, and found it said exactly the same thing all three times.

The fourth sitting was on the calendar in the main hall, posted a week ago in the clerk's small even hand in the same ink as everybody else's line: *Demonstration Assessment, Unclassified Observer Track. Fourth sitting. Training hall, first bell. Eighth day.* He had walked past it every morning that week and not let himself stop, because it was a routine sitting and he meant to keep it routine. Show enough to pass. Bank everything that matters. Hobb would be there, and Wray would say something with nothing new in it, and Quenna would write *Assessment satisfied*. Nothing in it would be anything a reader under a lamp somewhere could make a new word out of.

It was after that sitting that something would happen.

*About something I'd like to do*, Quenna had said at the chalk. *Something with structure*, she had said in the formal yard after the exhibition. And Karis, folding her stool, had said she thought Quenna had kept the question.

He folded the note and put it in the back pocket of the binder, with the refusal and the stranger's note and Vell's sheaf and the fair copy of his own third sitting, among the papers that proved things. It did not prove anything yet. He put it there anyway.

Then he sat down at the desk by the narrow window and took out a clean sheet of paper, because the third-day post went down the hill in the morning, and Hesk had written that he read both kinds of letter the same, and this week had been mostly the second kind.

*Hesk —*

*Nothing happened this week. I'm writing because you said to.*

He looked at that, and decided it was true in the way that mattered to Hesk and not true in any other way, and went on.

*I've been working at something in my training that won't come. I tried it twenty-two times over three weeks and it failed every one of them. A girl here helped me try. She wrote down every failure in a ledger as carefully as anybody ever wrote down a win, the distance and the hour and what I tried, and at the bottom of each one she wrote the word null, the same size every time. I didn't know you could be failed at that carefully. It's better company than you'd think.*

*Lira had her review. The academy wrote down, in its own words, that her old school's paper about her doesn't add up. She's keeping both papers. She'll want to tell you the rest herself one day, so I won't.*

*Brom found out from his teacher that there's a gap in the way he turns a strike that only somebody very fast could use, and nobody here is fast enough to teach him to close it. He took it the way he takes everything, by eating more barley.*

*There's a woman on Lira's track who fought a bout today and came out of her own corner for the first time in a long while. She laughed afterward. I don't think she'd laughed since she came here. I wanted you to know that, though you've never heard of her.*

*The shoulder still complains. The bench sounds good.*

*Cael*

It was not long. He read it back and found nothing in it that was untrue, and a great deal left out, the whole push and the quarter of a strike and a mark in a ledger beside a page he never added to. Some things were for the binder. But he had told Hesk about the ledger, and the word *null*, and the woman who had laughed, and he thought that was nearer to the whole of the week than it looked. He folded it, wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the corner of the desk for Lira to take down in the morning.

---

Lira was on the wall in the dark after supper, alone, and he went out to her because he would have gone out anyway.

The frost had come back after two days of rain, and the top of the wall was rough with it under his hand. Below them the hill town's lamps were coming on one by one down the slope, along the street worth walking and then along the street worth avoiding, and the corner where the grey building stood with its board over the door, where Oona would go down in a little under seven weeks with somebody's hand on her shoulder. Lira had her knees drawn up and her coat round her and Fenmark's four paragraphs, he knew without seeing them, folded inside it against her ribs.

"Gerda's asleep," she said. "Before the eighth bell. I watched her go in. I don't think she's been asleep before the eighth bell since she came here."

"You did that."

"Hobb did that. And she did. I only stood at the rail and shouted." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Quenna's sent you a note."

"How do you know?"

"Karis has one. She showed me at supper. She held it out at arm's length so I'd know she wasn't hiding it." Lira looked out at the yard. "She's very proper about notes."

They sat for a while without saying anything. Somewhere down the hill a dog barked twice and stopped.

"You didn't tell her," said Lira. "Tonight. When she wrote the line."

"No."

"It was in your face the whole time. I could see it from the door."

"I know."

"I'm not saying you were wrong." Lira was quiet a moment. "I'm saying she stood there and told the three of us she'd stand in front of it if it ever came to a test, and you were holding the one thing that would tell her what kind of test. And you held it." She turned her head and looked at him. "I think that was right. I think it was the hardest thing I've watched you do since Ardenmere. Both of those."

"Both of those," he agreed.

She looked back at the yard.

"Whatever Quenna wants, after the sitting," she said, "you'll tell me the day you know it."

"The day I know it."

"Same rule."

"Same rule. Heavier load."

"It'll hold," said Lira, and stood, and put her hand on his shoulder for a moment, light and brief, and took it away, and went in.

He stayed on the wall a long time after, as he had on the night she renewed the covenant, while the cold came through his coat and the town's lamps went out below him one by one. Then he took the binder out and wrote by the light of the residence wing's one lit window, slowly, in the square hand he kept for the lines he meant to keep believing.

*End of week eleven. Twenty-two sessions with Karis as source, at every distance and length and kind of declaration she could give me, every way I know how to reach. Twenty-two nulls. Not one notice. Not one thing arriving. She wrote at the bottom: we are missing a condition. She doesn't know what it is.*

*I've written down what I think it is. Watching is necessary. It isn't enough. It takes what's used on me, and the stakes are part of how. I haven't told her. It isn't tested, and nobody can test it honestly, and I won't go looking for a way. Lira knows. Brom knows the shape.*

*Session nine is kept. She marked it real, unexplained, keep. It's still exactly what it was.*

He stopped there and looked at it for a long while. Then he wrote the last part, because it was true, and because the binder was the one place he never made anything smaller than it was.

*I know her better than I've ever known anybody I've watched. I could close my eyes and see every tell she has. She's said that if there's ever an honest test, she wants it to be her. Quenna wants to see us both after the fourth sitting.*

*Part of me is still waiting. I still don't like it. I'm not going to pretend it isn't there.*

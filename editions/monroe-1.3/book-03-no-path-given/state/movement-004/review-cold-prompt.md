# Chapter 24 — A Version of One

The word came at breakfast on the first morning of the ninth week, folded once and carried by the same junior clerk who had fetched Cael up Naveth's stair twice that term, and it was not for him.

She stopped at the end of the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, where the three of them sat with porridge and the last of yesterday's dark loaf, and held the note out to Lira without a word. Lira took it, read it, and folded it again along the clerk's crease. Then she went on eating, which was how Cael knew it mattered, because Lira had never in her life gone on eating through good news.

Brom had seen it too. He kept his eyes on his bowl and his spoon moving at its usual pace, the pace of a man who meant to finish his porridge whatever the morning brought, and he did not ask.

The week had begun quietly. The frost had come every night since the exhibition and lifted every morning by the second bell, and the yard outside the long window was grey and hard and full of students going to assembly with their hands in their sleeves. Over the last two days Cael had kept a promise he had made to himself on the wall, which was that he would carry nothing of his own across to Lira until her week was done. He had decided to learn to aim the thing in him, and he had not tried. He had written that he did not know how, and that was still true. It could wait five days, because she had waited two years for hers.

"Fenmark," Lira said at last, to the bread.

Brom set his spoon down.

"The full record of the finding. The provision says the old academy has to send it before a formal review can sit, and it came up the hill on Saturday under their seal." She turned the note over on the table as if there might be more on the back, and there was not. "Quenna has it. The annex by the archive, the fourth bell. I can bring whoever I like, or nobody."

"Who are you bringing?" said Brom.

Lira looked at him, and then at Cael, and something went across her face that was not quite a smile and not quite anything else.

"Him. He can stand by the door and keep his mouth shut, which he's good at." She tore the heel of the loaf in two and put the larger half in front of Brom, which was an apology in advance. "Not you. You'd help."

"I would," said Brom with dignity.

"I know. That's why." She finished her porridge. "I'll want you after. At the midday meal. Talking about something."

"About what?"

"Anything at all. Barley. Wray's lecture. The honey thief." She stood and took her bowl. "I'll want noise, and you're the only person I know who can make noise sound like it's been thought about first."

She went to the hatch with her bowl and her chin up, and Cael watched her go and found that the porridge in front of him had gone cold, and that he did not want it anyway.

Brom picked his spoon back up.

"Did you know it was coming?"

"I knew the review was. Five days from Saturday." Cael turned the bowl a quarter-turn on the table. "I didn't know the file had to come first. She didn't either, I think. She never reads the rules for things she's decided not to think about."

"She'll read this one."

"She'll read it," Cael agreed. He had watched Lira decide things across a great many tables, and he had seen her face when she folded the note, and he knew that whatever else happened at the fourth bell, she had already decided that much.

---

The annex was a narrow room at the end of the archive passage, where the old waystation had kept something nobody remembered, and the academy now kept the binders for its irregular tracks. Lira had been past its door a dozen times without once looking in. It had one window high up in the wall, two chairs that did not match, and a table with a pale ring on it where somebody, years ago, had set down a hot cup without a saucer and been forgiven by nobody. Through the open door at the far end she could see the corner of Prynn's high desk and the old woman's grey head bent over a ledger, and she could smell the archive, paper and glue and cold stone, the smell of everything that had ever been written down about anybody and kept.

The envelope was already on the table when she came in.

She saw it before she saw Quenna, which annoyed her, because she had meant to walk in and look at the person first like a civilised human being. It was long and stiff and the colour of weak tea, with her name on the front in a clerk's round hand and, on the back, a blob of green wax pressed with a crest she had last seen at the top of a letter telling a twelve-year-old girl from the river quarter that she had been awarded a place. Green wax. She had forgotten the green. She had not forgotten anything else about the place, and somehow the green had gone.

Quenna was sitting at the far side of the table with her folder closed on her knee. She did not get up, and she did not say good morning, and Lira liked her for both.

Behind her, Lira heard Cael settle against the doorframe, a little to one side, out of the light. He made no sound after that. She had told him to stand by the door and keep his mouth shut, and he was doing it the way he did everything people asked of him, completely, as if being asked were a kind of rope.

"Sit, if you'd like," said Quenna. "Stand, if you'd rather."

Lira stood.

"That's Fenmark's record of the finding." Quenna did not touch the envelope; she only looked at it, the way a person looks at a dog she has not yet decided about. "What came here with you at intake was the notice that your enrollment had ended, and your standing record. That was enough to put you on the track. It isn't enough for a formal review. The re-certification provision says the panel must have the originating academy's full record of the finding before it sits, so I wrote to them for it three weeks ago, when your review went on the calendar." A pause, very small. "They took their time."

"They always did."

"It came up on the Saturday coach under their seal. Nobody here has broken it." Quenna laid one hand flat on her folder. "You're entitled to read it before the panel does. You're also entitled not to read it at all. Some candidates prefer not to."

Lira looked at the green wax.

"And if I don't?"

"Then on Thursday the panel reads it without you, as the provision requires, and it goes into your file here with everything else. You'd find out what it says when anybody finds out what's in a file." Quenna's voice did not soften and did not harden. "Afterward. From people who'd already read it."

"So that isn't really a choice."

"No," said Quenna. "It's a version of one."

Lira almost laughed, and did not, because laughing would have meant she was going to do the thing, and she had not decided yet. She stood with her hands at her sides and looked at the envelope, and the room was very quiet. Out in the archive Prynn turned a page with a sound like a dry leaf.

She had not read the file at intake. She had stood in front of the clerk with the half-moon spectacles for most of an hour while he compared her thin folder to a sheet of rules, and she had looked at the opposite wall the whole time, because looking at the file would have meant admitting it was a real object with real words in it and not a thing that lived in her chest. For two years she had known exactly one word that was in it, and she had built the whole of the rest out of that one word, the way you build a house on a single stone you happen to own because it's the only stone you've got.

She knew what Cael would do in her place, because she had watched him do it with a category line and a cold cup of tea. He would read it three times and then write it down. She was not Cael. She was the person who walked into a ring first so that she could choose which way the sun fell.

She picked the envelope up. It weighed nearly nothing.

She broke the seal with her thumb.

---

It was one sheet.

That was the first thing, and it went into her like the wrong end of a stick. She had braced for a lot of things over two years of not reading it, and she had braced well, the way she braced for a bout against somebody bigger. She had braced for contempt, some examiner's opinion of river-quarter scholarship girls written down in the long careful words that clever people used for being cruel. She had braced for lies, her correction turned into a tantrum and the room turned into a riot and three adults turned into witnesses to something she had never done. Most of all she had braced for weight, for pages and pages of it, testimony and argument and the long grinding work of an institution deciding what to do with her, something so heavy it would have needed two whole years to carry up a hill.

What she had in her hand was one sheet, written on one side.

She turned it over. The back was blank except for a filing stamp in faded purple, a date and a number. She shook the envelope, and a small slip fell out onto the table, a transmittal form addressed to Greyvane's provost, and nothing else came out after it. She looked into the envelope anyway, the way you look into an empty purse in case the coin you know isn't there has somehow come back.

Four paragraphs.

She read them standing up.

*Record of finding. Candidate: Lira. Path: Wind. Tier: Copper. Enrollment: scholarship.*

*During a Wind Path evaluation the candidate corrected the examining assessor's technique aloud, without deference to the assessor's authority.*

*Finding: insubordination, under the disciplinary code, section four, subsection two.*

*Enrollment ended. The finding stands as recorded. Formal hearing not required: the finding falls below the severity threshold set for scholarship students of Copper tier.*

There was a signature under the fourth paragraph, sloping and quick. It belonged to nobody she knew.

She read it again, slowly, the way she read an opponent the second time round, looking for the thing that had been hidden behind the obvious thing. There wasn't one. That was all of it.

She counted the second paragraph without meaning to. She did not count things as a habit; that was Cael's sickness, and he'd been giving it to her a little at a time for so long that sometimes she caught herself at it and wanted to throw something at him. But she counted it. Nineteen words. Her whole afternoon was in there, the afternoon that had sat at the middle of her life for two years like a stone in a boot, and somebody had made it nineteen words long, and not one of the nineteen said whether she had been right.

She had been right. She knew that the way she knew her own feet. The examiner had been teaching the third turn of the evasion form to a room of Wind candidates, and he had been teaching it off the front foot, and you could not leave a strike in time off the front foot; your weight was in the wrong place, and you were leaving late, and against anybody quick you'd be leaving with a bruise. She had said so. She had said it out loud, because she was fourteen and it was true. When he told her to show him, she had shown him, his own demonstration strike going past her shoulder into empty air while her back foot did the work, and the room had gone so quiet you could hear the windows tick in the cold.

*Corrected the examining assessor's technique aloud.* Yes. That was true. That was the whole truth, if you were the kind of person who thought the truth stopped at the edge of the paper.

She read the fourth paragraph a third time.

*Formal hearing not required.*

---

"There wasn't a hearing."

She said it to the window, or to the room, or to nobody. Quenna did not answer, and Lira was grateful, because an answer would have been a door she'd have had to walk through, and she was not ready to walk anywhere yet.

"I remember a room." She heard her own voice, flat as a plank. "Four days after. They sent for me at the end of a lecture. There was a long table with a green cloth on it, and a jug of water that nobody drank, and three of them sitting behind it, and a man standing up at one end with a paper." She stopped. "I thought that was the hearing. I thought the three sitting down had decided."

She had spent two years furnishing that room. That was the thing she had not understood until this minute, standing in a narrow annex with one sheet in her hand. She had furnished it like somebody setting up a house she meant to live in. She had added the jug and the cloth and the cold coming off the windows, and then, night after night, she had added the three faces, one by one, until she knew them better than her own. She had decided the man in the middle had wanted her gone from the start, because he had looked at her boots when she came in. She had decided the woman on the left, who had once moved Lira's grip on a training staff with two kind fingers in her first month, had argued for her and lost. She had needed that. She had needed somebody in that room to have argued for her and lost, because if somebody had argued, then there had been an argument, and if there had been an argument then she had been a question, and a question can be answered wrong.

She had argued with that room every night for two years. She had rehearsed better answers for it in the dark on the Ardenmere road, and on the walls of a dozen towns, and across the rings of a hundred bad circuit nights. She had told it she was sorry, and she had told it she wasn't, and she had told it the truth about the third turn so many times that the telling had worn a groove in her.

The room had never been listening, because it had never been a room for listening. Three people had sat behind a table because that was where people sat, and a man had stood up and read out a thing that was already finished, because a form said it was finished, and a form said it did not need to be heard. *Below the severity threshold.* She was small enough to be dealt with on one sheet, and there was a rule that said so in advance, written for girls like her before she had ever been born.

And the words he had added, the man standing at the end of the table, when he had read out the one word and then looked up at her over the paper, to make sure she understood.

*It means you have no control of yourself.*

She read the four paragraphs one more time, all the way down, looking for it.

It wasn't there.

He had made it up. He had stood at the end of a long table with a green cloth on it and added a sentence of his own to the finding, out loud, to a fourteen-year-old, because he could, and then he had folded the paper and the sentence had gone into her and stayed there for two years, and it had never once been written down anywhere. Nobody had thought it worth the ink.

Lira found that her hand wanted very badly to crumple the sheet into a ball and throw it at the high window. She watched her hand want it. Then she laid the sheet down on the table, flat, beside the pale old ring the cup had made, with exactly the care she would have used on a page of Karis's notebook, and stood looking at it with her arms at her sides.

"They didn't even bother," she said. "Two years. Every night, with myself, about what I did wrong and what I ought to have said and whether I had it coming. All of it built round a thing they didn't think was worth a second sheet of paper."

"No," said Quenna. "They didn't think it was."

Lira looked at her. Quenna was sitting exactly as she had sat when Lira came in, the folder closed on her knee, her face doing nothing at all, and Lira understood that this was a kindness, a very large one, the kind that cost the person giving it some effort and was meant to look like no effort whatever.

"Procedures written for people who aren't expected to object are always short," Quenna said. "That isn't a judgment of you. It's a judgment of the people who wrote it, and the paper doesn't know the difference."

Lira laughed then, once, a short ugly sound with nothing behind it.

"I don't know whether that's better or worse."

"It isn't either. It's simply true." Quenna turned her head very slightly toward the door, where Cael still stood against the frame, and back. "What you do with it is yours. On Thursday the panel will ask you a different question. Not what you did at fourteen. What you are now, on our floor, against our students, in front of three people who have read that sheet and are about to watch you."

Lira stood with that for a while.

Then she picked the sheet up, squared it against the edge of the table, folded it once along the clerk's old crease and slid it back into the envelope with the transmittal slip, and she set the envelope down.

It was a strange thing to feel happen in her own body. She had been carrying a question for two years, carrying it in her shoulders and her jaw and the back of her neck, and question-weight was the heaviest kind because you could never set it down for long without it asking you again. What she held now was an answer. It was a stupid answer, and a small one, and she did not like it at all. But an answer weighed what it weighed, and no more, and she could feel exactly what that was. It was one sheet.

"What time Thursday?"

"First bell, as posted. The formal yard." Quenna rose at last, and picked up the envelope, and put it inside her folder. "Eat something today. I don't care what."

Lira turned to go. Cael was still in the doorway, still exactly as she had left him, and his face had the look it got when he had been told not to help and was finding it the hardest thing he had ever done. She went past him and knocked her shoulder against his on the way, once, the old knock, and kept walking, and after a moment she heard him come after her down the passage.

---

She ate.

She ate barley at the midday meal, which she hated, and a heel of the kitchen's bread, which she also hated, and an apple she took off a second-year's tray without asking, which she did not hate at all. Brom sat across from her and talked. He talked about Wray's lecture on how a hardening Path carries load through the heel, and then about the honey thief, who had been seen near the kitchen garden with a spoon and a guilty face, and then, at great length and with real feeling, about barley, and why it was the most misunderstood grain in Valdris, and how the kitchen at Velmere had served it with cream to make it pretend to be something else, which in his opinion was the beginning of most of what had gone wrong with Velmere.

She knew what he was doing. He knew that she knew. Neither of them said so. It was noise with a shape to it, and the shape was a man sitting across a table from her for as long as it took, and she let it wash over her like warm water and was more grateful than she would ever have told him.

Cael ate beside her and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of noise.

At the end of it she pushed her bowl away and looked at Brom.

"The cream thing. Is that true?"

"Every word."

"That's disgusting."

"I know," said Brom. "I was six. It marked me."

She laughed, a real one this time, and the second-year whose apple she was eating looked over from the next table in alarm, and she waved the core at him in thanks.

In the afternoon, at the sixth bell, she went to the open floor. She went because the floor was the only place she had ever been sure of, and because she had a review in three days and two years of rust in her shoulders, and because she wanted to find out what the drills felt like with no question sitting on top of them.

She ran the Ardenmere sequences in the near corner, the ones she had run every morning for a year in a cold yard with Cael watching her across the circle and neither of them knowing what he was doing with it. Slip and turn and step and slip. She had run them yesterday and the day before, and the day before that, and every time she had felt them come out of her pulled tight, like a rope with something heavy on the end. Today they came out loose. She did not try to make them loose; she would have hated that, it would have been a performance, and she had never forgiven anybody for a performance in her life. They simply came out that way. On the third set her back foot did the work on the third turn so cleanly that she laughed out loud, alone in the corner, and two Current Path first-years at the next chalk looked round at her as if she had gone mad.

Perhaps she had. It felt a great deal like having gone sane.

Gerda was in the far corner laying her haze across the boards for a first-year, that low grey slowness that made everything passing through it look like it was walking through honey. She looked up once, across the hall, and checked the clock above the door, and looked back at Lira.

"Thursday," Gerda said, when they passed each other at the floor desk afterward with their slips.

"Thursday. Friday for you."

"Friday." Gerda wrote the hour on her slip in her careful square figures and looked at the clock to make sure the hour was still the hour. "You look different."

"I read something."

Gerda considered that for exactly as long as it took to blot her slip.

"Was it bad?"

"It was short," said Lira.

Gerda looked at her properly then, with her pale level eyes, and Lira saw her understand a great deal more than had been said, which was a thing she had not known Gerda could do.

"Short is often worse," Gerda said, and handed her slip to the clerk, and went.

Lira stood at the desk with her own slip in her hand and watched her go, tall and narrow and very straight, and thought about the box at the bottom of the Acknowledgment of Prior Standing that she had not marked at intake, the little square that would have said she meant to dispute. There was still a process for that, through channels outside this academy, slow and expensive and full of more people who had never been in the room. She could start it tomorrow. She could stand up in front of somebody and tell them about the third turn and the man who had added a sentence that wasn't written anywhere.

She thought about it all the way back across the yard in the cold, and found, at the door of the residence wing, that she did not want to start it tomorrow. She wanted Thursday first. She wanted a floor to see her before anyone read her again.

The man at the end of the table had made the sentence up. She held that thought in her hand on the stair like a stone she had found and decided to keep. Fenmark had not written it down. Fenmark had not even thought it.

It was the lightest thing she had carried in two years.

---

He did not ask her anything that evening, and she did not offer.

At supper she sat in her usual place and stole half his bread in her usual way and listened to Brom finish the history of the cream, and nothing in her face would have told a stranger that anything had happened at all. Cael was not a stranger. He watched her shoulders, as he had watched them at the floor desk on the fourth morning of the first week. They were down. They had been down since the annex, not by an inch but all the way, and they stayed down through supper and up the stairs, and he did not think he had seen them sit like that since Ardenmere.

He had stood in the doorway of the annex for the whole of it, because she had told him to, and he had kept his mouth shut, because she had told him to do that too, and it had been one of the hardest quarter-hours he had ever spent standing still. He had not seen the words on the sheet. He had not tried to. He had seen her read them three times, and he had seen her hand want to throw something and decide not to, and he had seen her set the envelope down in a way he did not have a heading for in the binder. She had not put it away. She had put it down, the way you put down a pack at the end of a road, and then she had walked out from under it.

He was at his desk with the lamp low and the binder open when the knock came, two knuckles, quiet. He opened the door, and she was in the corridor in her stockinged feet with her coat over her nightshirt and her arms folded.

"Four paragraphs," she said.

"Four?"

"Four. One sheet. Not even both sides." She looked down the corridor toward Brom's door, and back. "I'll tell you the rest on Thursday night. Not before. I want Thursday first."

"All right."

"I'm not keeping it from you. I'm keeping it from me, for three days. There's a difference."

"I know there is."

She looked at him for a moment longer, and then she nodded once, and went back down the corridor to her own door without another word. He heard it close.

He sat back down at the desk and looked at the clean page for a long time. Then he wrote what he could, which was what he had seen and nothing else, because the rest of it was hers.

*Fenmark's record came. Lira read it in the annex with Quenna and me at the door. Four paragraphs, one sheet. She came out lighter than she went in. I don't know what's in it. She'll tell me Thursday, after the floor.*

He read it back and added a line underneath, because tomorrow was Tuesday, and Tuesday was the first session since he had told himself on a frozen wall that he would find out, and he had not once in two days let himself think about it.

*Tuesday tomorrow. Section four. I still don't know what trying would even be.*

# Chapter 25 — The Other Direction

The felt was still on the post when he came into section four on Tuesday, grey and thick and wound three times round the old oak, exactly as Karis had left it a week ago, as if the post had been waiting all that time to be asked something.

Quenna stood at the chalk with her slate. Karis sat behind her line on the borrowed stool with her two pens laid side by side on the boards, and Brom had come as witness again under the second clause and was leaning on the far side of the post with his sleeves rolled, ready to be useful. Nobody had told him to bring anything, and he had brought a heel of bread in his coat pocket anyway, in case the hour ran long.

They began with the framework, because they always began with the framework, and Karis timed his recovery between passes at the working speed against the mark on her page.

"Three counts," she said, when he had finished. "Last week it was four. The ribs have stopped minding."

"The ribs still mind on the stair."

"The stair isn't in my notebook." She wrote anyway, a short line, and he was nearly sure it said *stair* somewhere in it. "The lead is unchanged. A quarter of a breath. It hasn't moved since the first session, through the exhibition and the ribs and the week after. Whatever else changes in you, that doesn't."

Then the post. He laid his palm flat on the felt and shut his eyes, and on the far side Brom leaned and shifted and leaned again, doing his level best to be unreadable, and for a long time there was nothing in Cael's hand but cloth and wood and the cold. Then, low on the right of the post, a faint thickening came through the felt, like a breath held on the far side of a wall, and a moment later Brom's weight went right.

"Right," said Cael, a fraction late.

"Right," Brom agreed, sounding aggrieved about it.

It came three times in twenty that afternoon, once more than last week, and every time Karis wrote it down and read it back to him in his own words, and every time he listened to her read it and changed nothing, because she had heard him exactly. When the hour was done Quenna signed the strip and tore it off and handed it to him, and went out with her slate under her arm, and Brom went with her as far as the door and then stopped there, because Karis had not stood up.

She was squaring her notebook against her knee. She squared it once, and then a second time, and Cael had learned what the second time meant. It meant her mind had finished arriving somewhere and her hands were catching up.

"Brom, would you stay?"

Brom came back from the door and stood at the rail without a word.

"I want to put something to you," Karis said to Cael, "and I'd like a witness to the asking, because what I'm going to ask isn't covered by any of the seven clauses, and I don't want there ever to be a question of what I said or how I said it." She set the notebook down on the boards beside her, closed. "I want to offer myself as a source."

He did not understand her for a moment. Then he did, all at once, and stood very still by the post with his hand still on the felt.

"Controlled conditions," she said. "My own declarations, at demonstration intensity, sustained, on a schedule we agree. You watch, as closely and as long as you like. And while you watch, you try. On purpose. To take something." She said it plainly, without any softening, every word set down whole. "If what you have can be aimed, that's the cleanest test anyone could design. If it can't, we'll know that too, and it will still be worth knowing."

Brom, at the rail, had gone so still that he might have been one of the posts himself.

Cael took his hand off the felt.

It was not a small thing she was offering, and he did not let himself pretend it was. He knew what it was from the other end, from the end where he had always stood without knowing he was standing there. Every name at the front of the binder had been there when something came to him, and not one of them had been told, beforehand or after, by anybody, that it could happen. Lira had not been asked. Brom had not been asked; he had held out his arm to be read because Cael had asked him to stop being careful, and that was not the same thing at all. Karis was offering to walk up to the front of that line on purpose, knowing what she had already worked out stood at the far end of it, with her notebook open.

"Why?"

She had expected the question. He saw that she had expected it, and that she had decided long before she sat down which of her answers was the true one.

"Because whatever you've had so far, nobody stood in front of it on purpose. Whoever was there when it happened, it happened to them as much as to you, and nobody asked them first. Not Lira. Not Brom." She did not look at the rail when she said Brom's name, and Brom did not move. "I can be asked. I'm asking to be. They wrote the terms at the scarred table that evening, all four of them, after the tile players by the fire had packed up and gone.

Karis had brought a clean sheet and both her pens, and she laid them squared in the middle of the table and did not touch them until everyone had sat. Lira took the corner with her back to the wall. Brom took the end. Cael sat across from Karis, and before anybody said anything at all, he told her what Lira had said on the wall he ought to tell her.

"Each of them came after I'd watched somebody for a long time. That much you'd already worked out, from outside. I'm telling you it from inside, so that you know what you're offering to stand in front of."

Karis did not move for a long moment. Her hands lay flat on either side of the clean sheet, and he saw the fingertips of the left one press, very slightly, against the wood.

"Thank you," she said. "It isn't going on paper. It isn't going anywhere."

Then she picked up a pen.

They built it in under an hour, because they had done it once before and knew now where the joints were. Lira wanted the first clause and got it, and Karis wrote it in her small upright hand: *1. Consent for each session is given aloud at its start, and may be taken back with one word at any moment, without reason.* Brom wanted the second, because it had been his kind of clause the first time, and he wrote it himself in his large square capitals, three lines where anybody else would have taken one: *2. NOTHING ABOUT HOW IT WORKS GOES IN ANY RECORD THAT ANYBODY OUTSIDE THESE FOUR CAN READ.*

"Is there going to be a clause about barley," Brom asked, when he had finished.

"No," said Lira.

"I'm only asking. Every time."

The third clause was Lira's again: that she and Brom would know the general shape of what was happening, and could come to any session as witnesses under the same rule as before, without notice. The fourth was Cael's, and Karis wrote it the way he said it, word for word: that her ledger for these sessions would record the conditions and the result and nothing else, and that clause six of the first arrangement held, and he would read every line first and keep a copy.

"What counts as a result?" Lira asked.

"What happens," said Karis. "Or doesn't. If nothing happens, the result is nothing, and I write nothing, exactly."

"There's one more. It's mine."

She turned the sheet round so that it faced him.

"That one's right," said Lira quietly.

"It's the most important line on the page," said Karis. "If I didn't write it, I'd be asking you to trust that I'd stop. I don't want trust in this. I want a rule."

The quiet room was at the far end of the training hall, behind a narrow door Cael had walked past fifty times without ever reading the board beside it. It was one of two small rooms the academy kept for students practising a new declaration in private, because a new declaration was nobody's business but the person it had been given to, and the board said only that. A slate hung on a nail by the door, and on the slate, in Karis's hand, it said *K. — declaration practice, sixth bell*, which was true.

It was a whitewashed box six paces wide and nine long, with a scrubbed floor and a single high window and nothing else at all. Karis stood at one end of it. Cael stood at the other. Lira had come, under the third clause, and sat on the floor against the wall by the door with her knees drawn up, because tomorrow was her review and she had said that watching something that wasn't her review was the best possible way to spend the night before it.

"I consent," said Karis, "to this session," and wrote the hour.

Then she began.

He had watched her Ember a dozen times from the edge of a section and twice in a formal yard, and he still found that he had to make himself breathe when she worked close. She did not throw heat about the way circuit Ember did, in sheets and gouts that cleared a ring. She set it. A point bloomed in the air at the height of her shoulder, a dim warm light the size of a coin, like a coal glowing through paper, and it stayed exactly where she had put it, steady as a lamp, until she let it go on her count, and then it was gone with that faint third of a breath of shimmer after it, like heat over a summer road. Then two at once, at the same height, a pace apart. Then two again, and this time she joined them, and a thin line of heat ran along the air between them with a sound like a kettle a moment before it sings, and held while both points held, and the smell of warm stone came across the room to him and the hair on his forearms stood up.

*Drawn Channel*, he thought. *Iron Rank Three.* He had read the receipt once, on a card on the stable table, and he was watching it be spent.

And underneath the watching, for the first time in his life on purpose, he reached.

He did not know what reaching was. He discovered that in the first minute, standing in a whitewashed room with Lira by the door and a line of heat singing between two coins of light. For a long time he had written down what happened after a fragment came, the sense of something settling into a place he had not known was empty, the notice in front of him like a line somebody else had already written. He had never once made any of it begin, and wanting a thing to happen and knowing how to make it happen turned out to be two entirely different skills, and he had only ever had the first.

So he tried what he had.

He tried attention first, all of it, the whole of his looking laid on her at once the way he had once laid it on Reydan in the third exchange, every scrap of him pointed at the place where her off hand went still a quarter of a breath before each bloom. Nothing came of it but the timing, which he already had.

He tried something like invitation. He found the place in himself where the four sat, if they sat anywhere, and held open the space beside them, absurdly, like a hand held out across a room to someone who has not seen it. He felt foolish doing it and kept doing it until she had laid six points and let them go.

He tried wanting. That was the easiest of all to produce, plain and bare, and he was ashamed of how easy, and he let himself feel the shame and kept wanting through it, because it was data whether he liked it or not.

Last of all, near the end of the hour, he tried to rebuild the stillness he remembered from the moments that had mattered most, the strange quiet that had sat in him at the top of the Reydan bout before anything changed. He built it the way a person tries to remember exactly how falling asleep feels, carefully, piece by piece, knowing all the while that the remembering was the thing in the way, and stood inside it while she laid a sustained point and held it to a count of thirty, steady, and let it go.

Nothing happened.

There was no settling and no sense of a space being filled, and no line appeared in front of him in anyone's handwriting. There was a boy standing at one end of a whitewashed room, watching a girl at the other end set small exact fires in the air, and wanting, and nothing else.

"Nothing," he said at the end, more to the floor than to her.

"A null result is data." Karis was already writing, her pen going in its short economical runs, as unbothered as if he had told her the time. "We now know that demonstration intensity, sustained observation at nine paces, and deliberate intent on your part are not, together, sufficient. That isn't nothing. That's a boundary, and before tonight nobody knew where it was."

"It feels like nothing."

"It feels like nothing because you were hoping." She looked up at him then, and on anyone else he might have called the look kindness. On Karis it was nearer to respect, the plain respect of one worker for another who has just tried something hard and got no result and told the truth about it. "Hope is not a research methodology."

By the door, Lira made a sound that might have been a laugh into her knees.

"Tomorrow?" said Karis.

"Not tomorrow," said Cael, and looked at Lira. "Tomorrow's hers."

"Of course." Karis capped her pen with its small definite click and wrote one more line under the first, and turned the ledger round so that he could read it across the room under the fourth clause, and he went down to her end and read it.

*Session one. Source: K., Ember, Iron Rank Three, demonstration intensity. Consent given aloud. Distance nine paces. Duration one hour. Declarations: single points; paired points; one channel; one sustained point, count thirty. Subject's reach: attention, invitation, want, rebuilt stillness, in that order. Result: null. No change in source.*

"Saturday," she said. "After both reviews are done."

"Saturday," he agreed, and she went out with her ledger under her arm, and Lira got up off the floor and stretched until her back cracked.

"Well," she said. "That was the dullest thing I've ever watched." She looked at him sidelong. "I loved it. Walk me to my door. I've got a floor in the morning, and I want to go to sleep thinking about somebody else's nothing."

# Chapter 26 — Priority Review

The formal yard at first bell on Thursday was grey with frost everywhere except inside the ring, where somebody had swept the earth bare before dawn and laid the chalk fresh, as they had for the exhibition, and as they did, Cael supposed, for anybody whose name was on the calendar in the main hall in the same ink as everyone else's.

It was not the exhibition. Nobody had climbed onto the roof of the tool shed. The benches held perhaps thirty, which for a re-certification review at the first bell was, Brom said, a crowd, and the low wall along the open side held a dozen more with their hands in their sleeves. The rumor market had not been told anything, because Lira had told it nothing, but the rumor market at Greyvane did not need to be told things to know them. It knew that the girl with the dot who had walked a Force Path off the chalk in the second week was being formally reviewed this morning, and it knew that something had come up the hill on Saturday under a green seal, and that was enough to fetch people out of bed early in the cold.

Cael stood at the rail on the open side with Brom on his left. Karis was a few places along, and he noticed, because he could not help noticing, that her hands were in her coat pockets and that she had not brought her notebook at all.

Gerda was at the far end of the rail, alone, with her arms folded and her patched coat buttoned to the chin. Tomorrow at this hour it would be her. She had the look of a person standing at the edge of cold water, watching someone else go in first to find out how cold it was.

Further along, by the standings board, Edran stood with his arms folded, looking not at the board for once but at the ring. Hobb was beside him, the two of them saying nothing, as companionable as two posts.

The three chairs stood at the north end with their chalk ticks and the little table. The rotating seat was the broad-faced woman from the movement floor who had sat Cael's second sitting, and Cael thought that was a piece of luck or of somebody's quiet arranging, because she taught the movement Paths, and Wind was hers to read.

The originating academy's record of finding has been received and read by the panel. The candidate's recorded tier is Copper." Quenna did not look up from the ledger as she said it, and did not slow down, and did not hurry. "The panel will assess advancement against the recorded tier. First bout. The candidate takes the south mark."

Lira took the south mark.

Cael watched her take it and knew, from the way she set her feet, that she had read the sheet in the folder three times on Monday and every night since in her head, and that she had come out here to make three people who had read it too sit and watch a floor disagree with it.

The first opponent was a third-year from the movement floor, Current Path, Copper Rank Seven by the board, a solid square boy with a reputation for doing everything the way the lecture said to do it. Current moved like water down a slope, a long gliding step that seemed to carry a body further than its legs had any right to, and the boy had a textbook advance built on it: two glides in, the second one curving, and a strike off the end of the curve while his opponent was still working out which way the water was going.

Wray lowered her finger.

Lira gave him the first exchange. She always gave the first exchange; Cael had watched her give it to fighters in Ardenmere who had not deserved the courtesy. She gave ground in short neat steps and let him show her everything. He glided in, and in again with the curve, and struck off the end of it, and she was not there, and he glided back out to the textbook distance and set himself and came again in exactly the same shape. She let him. Cael watched her watching. Her eyes were on his hips, where the glide began, and on his back foot, where it began a moment before that.

Wray called the exchange with no touch.

The third-year came off his mark into his first glide, and Lira came off hers at the same moment, straight at him, not to the side as the textbook said an evading fighter must but along his own line, inside it. It would stay. That was the thing he knew better than almost anybody alive. Things written down in a ledger like that one had a way of staying, long after the people who wrote them had forgotten the morning, and his whole life at Greyvane was standing on that one fact like a house on a single stone.

"Initials," said Quenna. "And the candidate's signature that she has heard the notation."

Lira walked to the little table. She took the pen and signed, quickly, in her slanting hand. Then she stood a moment and looked down at the ledger, at the line Quenna had written, and Cael saw her read it, once, the way she had read nothing else that morning. Her face did not change. Her shoulders did.

She came off the ring toward the rail with her water flask, and stopped in front of him and Brom, and did not quite meet either of their eyes.

"I'm going to put cold on this shoulder," she said, "and then I'm going to sit somewhere by myself for an hour. Don't come and find me."

"All right."

"I mean it." She drank. "The wall, at dusk. Both of you. Not before."

She went out through the gate toward the residence wing, holding the shoulder, and Gerda watched her go from the end of the rail with an expression Cael could not read at all, and then looked up at the clock above the main hall door, and then went in.

He spent the fifth bell in section four with the post, as Thursday required, and Karis did not say a word about the morning. She timed him and wrote and read back, and once, near the end, when he was getting his breath by the felt, she looked up and said, "She took both of those on purpose," and he said, "Yes," and she nodded and wrote nothing at all, and that was the whole of it.

At the sixth bell he went to the defensive floor, because Brom had said at the midday meal, with a careful face, that Wray had asked him to stay late, and Brom never said anything with a careful face unless he did not yet know what it meant.

The far section was nearly empty. Hobb stood at the edge of the chalk with his hands in his pockets, plainly told to wait and not told why. Brom stood in the middle in his low loose stance, his weight sunk into his heels, and across from him stood Wray, with her brown coat off for the first time Cael had ever seen, folded on the boards at the edge of the section.

She was going to strike him herself.

Cael understood it as he reached the rail, and understood too that he had never seen her do it. In two months he had watched her measure Brom against Hobb, against the fetch drill, against two partners at once and against a Stone fourth-year told to commit everything. He had never once seen her stand across the chalk from him. Shield Path, Bronze. He had written on her page that Shield believed nothing should be spent that didn't have to be, and he had written it from the way she stood, because he had never seen anything else.

"Redirect," said Wray. "Whatever I send, take it and turn it. No more than that."

She struck.

It was not fast, the first one. It was a flat hard shove off her forearm, and as it came a faint pale plane went along the outside of her arm from wrist to elbow, like a pane of glass seen edge-on, so that the shove arrived as a wall arrives, all at once along its whole width. Brom took it on the forearm and gave, the inch and then the second inch, and turned it, and it went back round on her along a line she plainly had not braced for, and she took it on her own planted feet without moving at all, as if she had been expecting it to the last ounce.

"Again."

She struck again, a little faster.

Cael watched her count. She did not count aloud. He could see it in the stillness of her face between strikes, the way he had seen Karis count, and he understood that every strike was a measurement, each one a fraction quicker than the last, one condition changed and everything else held still. She went up the speeds the way a person goes up a stair in the dark, one step at a time, feeling for the next before putting weight on it.

On the seventh strike she stopped.

It had been fast, faster than anything Cael had seen Hobb throw or Edran either, a Bronze-tier strike from a woman who had not spent a single unnecessary motion since he had known her. Brom had taken it and given and turned, and the turn had been clean. But Cael had seen something in it that he had seen before without ever once seeing it as a thing, and when Wray lowered her hands and stood still he knew she had seen it too.

"Again," she said, quietly. "Same speed. Brom, I want you to do nothing different."

She struck. Brom took it, and gave, and waited, and turned.

The wait.

It was the thing Karis had asked him about in the first week, the second half that he held longer than anybody taught. Cael had written it on Brom's page as the best thing about him, the patience that let a strike commit itself fully before Brom took it away, because if he turned too soon the other fellow felt it and kept his weight back. It was the whole of what made his redirect work against people who meant it. And at this speed, for the first time, Cael could see the shape of it as a shape. Between the instant Brom's body had read the strike and the instant his body began to turn it, there was a gap. It was very small, small enough to live inside a breath with room to spare. At Iron speed it was invisible, and at Copper speed it was a gift.

Wray stepped back and put her hands down at her sides. She was not breathing hard. Cael had never seen her breathe hard.

"There's a gap," she said, "between the moment you've read a strike and the moment you begin to turn it. You hold it on purpose. It's the best thing you do." She looked at Brom steadily. "And it's a gap. Against anybody you've ever fought, it's worth holding, because they can't use it. I can see it. I'm Bronze, and I'm not quick enough to use it, but I've stood in front of Silver, and I can tell you what a fast Silver would do with it. He'd be through it before you'd started your turn."

Brom stood very still in his stance.

"Can you fix it?"

"I can show it to you. I've just done that." Wray bent and picked up her coat and put it on and buttoned it to the throat, one button at a time. "Closing it is another matter. You'd close it by meeting strikes fast enough to punish it, every day, for months, until your body learned to start the turn before the wait was done without losing what the wait gives you. That needs somebody faster than the gap." She buttoned the last button. "I haven't got anybody faster than the gap. Not me. Not Edran, not the Ternhall girl, not anybody on this academy's roster. Greyvane doesn't have one."

Hobb, at the edge of the chalk, took his hands out of his pockets.

"So it's a ceiling," said Brom slowly. "After all."

"No." Wray said it at once, and firmly, and Cael saw that she had been waiting for him to say it so she could say that. "You've made me careful with that word, and I'm not going to waste the care now. A ceiling is a place people stop because somebody drew a line on a board and told them to. Your column says I drew it in the wrong place, and I'm watching your column." She looked him in the face. "This is a wall. It's a real thing in the road, in your body, not in anybody's belief. You can see it now. Seeing a wall isn't the same as getting over it, and you'll not get over this one here. I'm sorry for that. I'd rather tell you today than let you find it on a floor somewhere with somebody fast on the other side."

Brom looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, slowly, the nod he gave a fight that had gone the way he thought it would and he had not wanted it to.

"A wall."

"A wall," said Wray. She turned to Hobb. "Thank you. You were here in case I needed something slow and heavy to show him the difference, and I didn't. Go and eat." Hobb nodded and went, and passing Brom he stopped, and put one broad hand on Brom's shoulder for a moment, and took it away, and went on without a word.

Wray stopped at the rail where Cael stood.

"You saw it," she said. It was not a question.

"On the seventh."

"So did I." She looked at him with her level measuring eyes, and he could not tell what she was measuring. "Write it down properly. He'll want somebody to have seen it besides me."

---

Lira was on the wall at dusk, as she had said she would be, with a cold cloth folded under her coat over the shoulder and her knees drawn up, and when they came out she moved over so that the gap beside her was exactly the width of two people.

She did not wait for them to ask.

She told them what was on the sheet. She told it flatly and in order, the four paragraphs, the one signature that belonged to nobody she had ever met, the nineteen words. She said the nineteen words aloud, all of them, and Cael counted them as she said them and hated himself a little for counting and could not stop. She told them about the threshold rule, *formal hearing not required*, and the room with the green cloth that had not been a hearing at all, only a reading. Then she told them about the sentence the man at the end of the table had added out loud, *it means you have no control of yourself*, and how she had looked for it on the sheet and it was not there.

"He made it up," she said. "Two years I've had that sentence in me like a splinter, and it isn't even written down anywhere. They didn't think it was worth writing."

Brom said nothing. He put one hand flat on the stone between them.

Cael thought for a long time before he said anything, because it mattered, and because the things that mattered most to say to Lira were the ones she would know at once if he had not thought them through.

"When Vell gave me the sheaf," he said slowly, "the last night in Ardenmere. Her copy of my whole record off the Ironyard ledger, every bout. She said, the records know you existed here. I didn't understand what she meant. I thought she meant it as comfort, because the registry had me down as a word and nothing else, and her ledger had me down as everything I'd done." He looked out at the yard going dark. "I think she meant it as a fact. Whatever anyone writes somewhere more important, the people who were there and wrote it down honestly, their page stays. It knows what happened. Nobody gets to argue it away by writing something different in a better building."

Lira was very still beside him.

"The records know what happened here," she said, slowly, as if she were testing the weight of each word before she put her own on it. "Nobody gets to say otherwise."

"That's the one I'd keep."

She sat with it a while. Then she did a thing he had not expected, which was to laugh, really laugh, a sound that came up out of her as if it had been kept waiting on a stair for two years and had just been told it could come down. She took the four paragraphs out of her coat. She had got them back from Quenna, then; he had not known. She folded them once more along the old crease and put them back inside her coat, against her ribs, on the side away from the bruise.

"Evidence," she said. "For when it's obvious."

"When what's obvious?"

"That they were wrong." She said it with no heat in it at all, the way she said the score at the end of a bout. "I'm not filing a dispute. Not now. Maybe not ever. This morning's line does more for me here than any argument with Fenmark would, and I'd have to stand up in front of more people who were never in the room." She patted the coat. "But I'm keeping these. For the day it stops being me proving something to some office, and starts being some office admitting something to me."

She turned to Brom. "You've been sitting there with a face on since you came out. I noticed. I've had my turn. Say it."

Brom looked at the yard.

"Wray found my wall," he said.

He told it as plainly as he told everything, the gap between the read and the turn, the wait that was the best thing he did, a fast Silver going through it before he'd started. Nobody at Greyvane quick enough to teach him to close it. Not Wray, not Edran, not Karis.

"She called it a wall," he said. "Not a ceiling. She was very particular. A ceiling is a thing somebody draws. A wall's a thing in you." He was quiet a moment. "It's an honest wall. Nobody's fault. Just the sort of thing you hit if you get good enough to hit it."

Lira reached over and knocked her shoulder against his, and winced because it was the bruised one, and did it again with the other on principle, and Brom made the low sound in his chest that did him for a laugh.

The three of them sat a long while after that without saying anything, while the yard went from grey to dark and the hill town's lamps came on below them. Cael thought about the two things that had been found that day, one in a ledger and one in a friend, and how differently they sat. Lira's had been paper all along, and paper could be answered with a floor. Brom's was bone and habit and two years of being right, and there was no ledger anywhere that could be argued with about it.

He wrote it that night in the binder, in the plain hand he kept for evenings that changed the shape of things.

*Lira's review. Two bouts, two won. She spent two hits in the second like coins and finished with the barrow turn from the Ardenmere market yard. The notation, read aloud, in their words: Advancement inconsistent with recorded tier. Recommend continued track with priority review. First paper anywhere that says her file might be wrong. She's keeping Fenmark's four paragraphs. No dispute.*

*Brom's wall. Wray struck him herself and found it on the seventh strike, the gap between the read and the turn. She called it a wall and not a ceiling and meant the difference. Nobody here is fast enough to close it.*

He looked at the two paragraphs side by side, and then wrote the line underneath them that he had been carrying all the way up the stairs.

*Some walls come down when you finally push. Some stand because nobody ever built a door in them. I think learning to tell which is which might be most of what this place is for.*

Nobody fetched the rumor market out of bed for Gerda.

The yard at first bell on Friday was colder than it had been on Thursday, and emptier, and the frost lay on the benches unbroken because nobody had sat on them. Somebody's re-certification review at the first bell on the last day of the week was not a thing anybody at Greyvane had a reason to come and see, and so nobody had come.

The panel sat at the north end, Quenna in the middle and Wray on her left. The rotating seat this morning was the lean old instructor with scorched cuffs who took the heat Paths, Ember and Ash together. He had the look of a man who had read the file in front of him and had already been told by it most of what he expected to see.

Gerda came through the gate as the bell finished, tall and narrow and very straight, with her ash-pale hair cut close and her patched coat folded over her arm and her hands wrapped. The finding is termination of enrollment for failure to meet the standing requirement." She turned a page. "The panel will assess advancement against the recorded tier. First bout. The candidate takes the south mark."

Lira's hand had closed on the rail.

Cael had seen it twice from the rail and once in the near corner of the open floor with a first-year walking slowly through it, and it was still strange to watch at close range. It came off her low and grey and spread along the swept earth like smoke that had decided to lie down, ankle-deep and then knee-deep, two paces out from her in every direction. Anything that moved inside it moved as if through cold honey. A strike thrown into it arrived late. A foot set down in it came down slow. She stood in the middle of it with her hands loose and waited.

The Blade fourth-year did not come in.

He had watched her. Cael saw it in the way he stopped a pace outside the edge of the grey and stood there, light on his feet, his very fast hands loose at his sides, looking at the haze the way a swimmer looks at cold water. He had no reason to go into it. A standings bout scored touches, and a touch had to be made by somebody, and if neither of them made one the exchange was empty and so was the next. He circled the edge of the haze, and she turned inside it to keep him in front of her, and he circled the other way, and she turned again, and the first exchange went by like that, two people walking round a patch of fog.

*Correctly.*

Nothing had gone wrong. That was the whole of it. Nobody had cheated her, or lied, or made a sentence up out loud at the end of a table. The procedure had done exactly what it was written to do. # Chapter 29 — What Gets Used

He worked it out that night, alone, at the desk by the narrow window, with the lamp turned up past the oil allowance and the binder open at the front, where the four sections began.

Then he sat a while longer with the pen in his hand, and made himself look at the last thing, because he had promised himself on the wall that he would stop not-knowing what he was doing, and this was a thing he was doing.

Some part of him was waiting for the test. He could feel it, now that he had written the sentence down, the way he could feel the bruise under his breastbone when he breathed. It was not dread. He did not like it. He made himself write that down too, because the binder was the one place he never lied.

*Part of me is waiting for it. Not afraid. Interested. I don't like that part, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't there.*

# Chapter 30 — Real, Unexplained, Keep

Lira came to Brom's room for the fast catch, because she had read the page about the whole push and had told him at breakfast, cutting his porridge into squares, that the next time he did something new to his chest she was going to be in the room for it with a cold cloth and a very bad temper.

She did not speak. She had said she would not, and Lira kept her word about speaking even less often than she kept it about anything else, so that when she did keep it, it meant something.

"It's different," Cael said, kneeling on the boards with his left forearm braced across his chest. "Everything so far has been a push. A push arrives slow. It comes into the bone and sits there and I've got time to choose the road while it's sitting. A strike doesn't sit. It arrives and it's gone."

"So you can't wait for it," said Brom.

"So I can't wait for it. I have to choose the road before it lands. Before I know the size, or the line, or anything." He flexed the left hand. The elbow the whole push had found was nearly quiet. "Not much. A quarter. If I can catch a quarter of a strike and send it home, that's a thing I've never done."

"How fast?"

"Fast. Not your fastest. Fast enough that it's a strike and not a lean."

Brom considered him for a long moment with his chalked hands on his knees. Then he looked across at Lira on the bed, and Lira looked back at him and said nothing, with great force, and Brom looked back at Cael.

"Quarter," he said. "Fast. I'll call it."

He brought up the Iron Skin along his right palm, the faint dull sheen coming over the skin, and drew the hand back a little, and struck.

It was not his fastest. It was a short flat palm strike at the braced forearm, with weight behind it and speed in it, the kind of strike Brom used on the floor when he meant a thing to land. Cael had chosen the road before Brom's hand moved, a quarter's width out and back along the line it would come in, and he held it, and the strike arrived. It was in the bone and gone again before the fragment had finished waking, and the fragment reached for the place where it had been and found the bruise and nothing else.

"Nothing," said Cael.

"Nothing. Again."

Seven times it was nothing. The fragment was too slow for a strike; it woke for force that was already his, and a strike was never his for long enough. On the eighth he did something he had not known he could do. He did not wait for the fragment to wake when the force arrived. He woke it first, before Brom moved, the gathering low in his chest begun and held half-ready like a breath drawn and not let out, with the road laid open in front of it, so that when the strike landed the thing in him was already reaching. It closed on the strike in the instant it was in the bone, a quarter of it, and sent it home.

The quarter went back into Brom's palm with a crack like a dropped plate.

And the bill did not wait for anything.

With a push it had always come a moment afterward, rising through the shoulder and across the breastbone in its band of heat. He could have answered narrowly. It was a question that stood with its toes on the very line of the terms. "Yes," he said. "Once."

Karis went very still, the way she went still before an ignition, all of her listening at once.

"If it was anything. That's the only word I've got that's anywhere near it."

"Did you try to make it happen again?"

"A dozen times. More. The same circle, the same time of day, the same work, as near as my notes could make it. Nothing."

"Tell me everything you remember about the conditions."

He told her. It did not take long, which was the worst of it. There had never been much to tell, and he had told it to himself so often that the telling had worn smooth. She listened without moving. When he had finished she took him through it again, from the other end, starting from the moment itself and working backward to the morning, and then a third time from the morning forward. "It exists."

"I know it exists. That's the trouble with it." She said it without heat. I can't tell which from here. I don't think anybody could."

He said nothing. He thought, privately, of the page in the binder with his own hypothesis on it, *it takes what's used on me*, and of the afternoon in Brom's circle where nobody had been using anything on anybody. It broke his rule too. He did not say so. It was not for saying yet, and it changed nothing about the second in Brom's circle, which was exactly what it had always been.

"So what do we do with it?" he said.

"We keep it."

She opened the ledger again. She did not turn to the nulls. She turned past them, past the plan she had drawn for the last sessions, to a page near the very back that he had never seen, with a single short line already on it in her small upright hand that she must have written in the few minutes he had been talking. He could read it from across the room only because she turned the ledger toward him. *Session nine, Ardenmere, with B. One reading, Tide-like, under a second. Never repeated.* Beside it she made a small mark with her pen. "I have one category in this whole notebook that gets that mark," she said. "Real, unexplained, keep. It isn't a failure, and it isn't a hypothesis waiting to be proved. It's a fact I don't understand, written down as a fact I don't understand, instead of squeezed into some explanation that would comfort me and teach me nothing." She looked at the mark. "Most people can't bear to leave a thing like that alone. They explain it, because an explanation is more comfortable to carry than a question. Then they've got an explanation, and it's wrong, and they build on it."

He looked at the mark for a long time across the scrubbed floor.

He had carried session nine since the road the way a person carries a stone in a boot that nobody else can see, a weight nobody else would credit because it was a weight with no shape. He had never once been able to set it down in front of anyone who would neither explain it away nor shrug at it. Lira had believed him and been frightened; Brom had believed him and been puzzled. Neither of them had known where to put it. Karis had just put it, in front of him, in the most careful notebook he had ever seen, under the most exacting mark that notebook had, and left it there exactly as it was. It was not answered. Nothing about it had been answered. But it had been witnessed, properly, by somebody who knew what witnessing was, and he had not known until this moment how much of the weight of it had been the being alone with it.

"How many other entries have that mark?" he asked.

"None," said Karis. "Until tonight." She closed the ledger. "I can't tell yet whether that's a compliment to you or a warning. I'll let you know when I've worked it out."

"Whatever Quenna wants, after the sitting," she said, "you'll tell me the day you know it."

"The day I know it."

"Same rule."

"Same rule. Heavier load."

"It'll hold," said Lira, and stood, and put her hand on his shoulder for a moment, light and brief, and took it away, and went in.

He stayed on the wall a long time after, as he had on the night she renewed the covenant, while the cold came through his coat and the town's lamps went out below him one by one. Then he took the binder out and wrote by the light of the residence wing's one lit window, slowly, in the square hand he kept for the lines he meant to keep believing.

*End of week eleven. Twenty-two sessions with Karis as source, at every distance and length and kind of declaration she could give me, every way I know how to reach. Twenty-two nulls. Not one notice. Not one thing arriving. She wrote at the bottom: we are missing a condition. She doesn't know what it is.*

*I've written down what I think it is. Watching is necessary. It isn't enough. It takes what's used on me, and the stakes are part of how. I haven't told her. It isn't tested, and nobody can test it honestly, and I won't go looking for a way. Lira knows. Brom knows the shape.*

*Session nine is kept. She marked it real, unexplained, keep. It's still exactly what it was.*

He stopped there and looked at it for a long while. Then he wrote the last part, because it was true, and because the binder was the one place he never made anything smaller than it was.

*I know her better than I've ever known anybody I've watched. I could close my eyes and see every tell she has. She's said that if there's ever an honest test, she wants it to be her. Quenna wants to see us both after the fourth sitting.*

*Part of me is still waiting. I still don't like it. I'm not going to pretend it isn't there.*

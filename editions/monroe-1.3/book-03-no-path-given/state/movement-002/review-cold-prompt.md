# Chapter 9 — Two Notebooks

The rumor arrived at breakfast on the first morning of the third week, carried in by Lira along with half a loaf she had certainly not paid for.

"There's a transfer."

Brom did not look up from his porridge. "There are always transfers."

"Not like this one." Lira sat, tore the loaf in three and pushed the pieces across the table like a dealer laying out cards. "Ternhall. She came in at the gate yesterday afternoon with a trunk and a letter with the Ternhall seal on it, and the clerk at the standard desk read the letter twice and then looked at her rank for so long that the girl behind her in line started to worry the clerk had died."

"What rank?" Cael asked.

"Iron Rank Three. Ember Path." Lira let that sit for exactly as long as it deserved. "Sixteen years old. She walked out of a first-rank academy nine days before its autumn term began, and came here, and nobody can work out why. I've heard three reasons already and I'm not happy with any of them."

"Let's have them," said Brom, who pretended not to enjoy Lira's market and had never once asked her to stop.

She counted on her fingers. "One, she struck an instructor and Ternhall let her leave quietly instead of throwing her out. Two, she lost one bout at Ternhall, just one, and couldn't bear to stay. Three, her family lost their money and Greyvane is cheaper." She frowned at the third finger. "That one's mine. I made it up on the stairs to see if it would spread, and it already has, so now I don't trust any of them."

Brom considered the matter seriously while he ate. "Ternhall doesn't lose people. People leave other schools to go to Ternhall."

"Exactly," said Lira, pleased. "So it's a mystery. I like a mystery before the first bell."

Cael saw her for himself at the fourth bell, when he crossed the formal yard on the way to the stable and found someone standing in front of the standings board with a notebook.

She was dark-haired and neat in the way of a person who had decided long ago what she would wear and had not given it a thought since, and she was copying the board. That alone would not have made him stop, because new students read the board all the time; he had done it himself on his second day. What made him stop was the order she did it in. She had started at the bottom. The last names on the board, the Copper first-years with two recorded bouts and a loss beside each, were already copied out in a small, upright hand, and she was working steadily upward, one row at a time, without once glancing at the top, where Edran's name had sat for longer than anyone could remember.

He stood at the yard's edge for perhaps half a minute, watching her pen. She did not look round. If she knew she was being watched, she gave no sign of minding, and she did not hurry or slow.

That evening, in the binder, under the page for the day, he wrote one line.

*Ternhall transfer. Ember, Iron 3. Copies the standings from the bottom up.*

He looked at it, decided it was the kind of fact that meant nothing yet, and let it be.

The rest of the third week belonged, more than he liked, to Edran.

Since the afternoon at the north gate the question had not come up again, not in words. Edran had not repeated it in the yard or the stable or the lecture wing, and he had certainly not gone to the provost about it; Brom had been right about that, as Brom was right about most things that involved people standing still. But something had changed in the way the best fighter at Greyvane moved through the building. He had begun, quite openly, to watch back.

Cael noticed it first at the rail of the open floor. He was filling a page on the Storm fourth-year who left the yard smelling of rain, and when he looked up between bouts Edran was at the far rail, not watching the Storm fighter at all, but watching Cael watch. He did not look away when he was seen. He simply held the look for a breath, without any expression, and then turned back to the floor as if he had been looking at the floor all along.

It happened again two days later in the stable, at supper, and again at the formal yard during a Copper standings bout, where Edran stood at the end of the benches with his arms folded and his attention not on the ring but on the binder open on Cael's knee. By the end of the week Cael had begun to feel it before he saw it, the faint pressure of being looked at by someone who was reading him, the way he felt a crowd turn without needing to look up.

"He's charting you," said Lira, when he mentioned it on the wall.

"I know."

"How does it feel?"

He thought about it honestly. "Fair," he said at last. "I've been charting him for a fortnight. I've got three pages on him. He's allowed one."

"Have you got a line for him? The bottom line. What his Path believes."

"Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose."

Lira considered that. "And what does he believe?"

Cael did not have an answer. He wrote the question at the bottom of the Glass page that night, under the line about breaking, and left a space beneath it for whenever he found one.

Karis Dellenmoor had kept two notebooks since the winter she turned eleven, and nobody at Ternhall had ever asked to see the second, which she had come to regard as one of the most useful things about Ternhall.

The first notebook was the one everyone expected a serious student to keep. It held bout logs and timing tables, declaration notes and drill counts, the slow honest record of a girl becoming very good at something the world had agreed to measure. When she Kindled at fourteen and her Arbiter named her Path as Ember, the first notebook simply grew a new section and went on; it had been waiting for that section for three years. It was the notebook of a top-ranked student, and in time it made her one. By sixteen she was Iron Rank Three, which was early for anyone and unheard of at Ternhall for a student only two years past Kindling, and her name sat at the head of Ternhall's standings in a hand that the standings clerk had stopped bothering to rub out between terms.

The second notebook was the reason she kept the first.

It held everything the standard model got wrong. Not her own mistakes; the model's. She had started it after her first year of pre-Kindling drills, when she noticed that a Current Path courier who carried letters past the drill yard ran faster downhill than any table in the Ternhall library said a Current Path could run on any slope at all, and that nobody but her seemed to find this interesting. After the courier came a Shield instructor whose shell held a full third longer against cold than against heat, though the formula said the two should be equal. After him came a long procession of small refusals, places where a Path had declined to behave as the books promised, each one noted with a date and a source and, wherever she could get it, a measurement. Every instructor she asked put them down to careless counting. Karis had never yet met a careless count that explained itself so consistently. What she believed in was models nobody had finished.

The first entry in the second notebook that she could not explain was her own off hand. It went still a quarter of a breath before every ignition she laid, as reliably as a clock, though nothing in Ember doctrine said it should and no other Ember student she watched did the same. She had charted it for two years. She still did not know what it meant. She had stopped minding that, which she counted as the most important thing the second notebook had ever taught her.

Rank, she had understood very early, was not the point. Rank was access. A higher rank bought better instructors and older libraries, opponents whose behavior was worth recording, and eventually, if a person kept climbing, a seat near the assessorial records and registry digests where the second notebook's small refusals might add up into something with a shape. She had worked this out at thirteen, as other children worked out which instructors marked gently, and from then on she was excellent on purpose. The system paid its best students by letting them stand closer to the works, and the works were what she had wanted to see all along. A few classmates had sensed this about her over the years and found it unsettling, as people are unsettled by a runner who wins every race and leaves the ribbons on the grass.

Ternhall trained its Ember students in pairs. It paired them at the first drill and kept them paired, on the theory that a fire learned against another fire learned faster, and the theory was correct; her curve proved it. So did the curve of the person across the paired floor, which had climbed beside hers week for week for three years, the first of them before either of them had Kindled. Karis did not write that person's name in either notebook. She had found, quite early, that there was a small amount of data she would rather carry than file.

---

The rumor reached her by way of a joke, which was how most interesting things reached Ternhall.

Master Marlowe lectured the second-years on Path history, and he liked an audience of second-years better than any other audience, because they still laughed at the right places. He was a dry, clever man who collected stories from the provinces the way some men collect bad coins, for the pleasure of showing how bad they were. One afternoon in late winter, between the founding of the registry and the reform of the station fees, he paused and told them about the south.

"Down in Ardenmere, I'm told, the circuit has a boy who beats Iron fighters with no Path at all." He let the room settle. "I expect by spring he'll be doing it with no arms. The provinces are tremendously ambitious."

The hall laughed. Karis, in the third row, wrote *Ardenmere. Circuit. Unranked boy. Beats Iron. No Path* before the laughter had finished, and underneath it, smaller, *Marlowe heard it third-hand and improved it at least once.* Marlowe was an excellent lecturer and a very poor witness.

It took her three weeks to find a real thread and four months to stop finding contradictions along it. She worked as she always worked. She ranked her sources by their distance from the event: first, anyone who had stood at a rail and watched; second, anyone who had spoken to someone at a rail; third, taverns; and last, lecturers. She tagged every claim with who had made it and what they might have gained by making it. A cousin of her mother's traded wool through Ardenmere twice a season, and she wrote to him and got back three paragraphs of carters' gossip, which she ranked third and kept. She paid a copying house, at a price she chose to leave out of both notebooks, to transcribe the Ardenmere circuit's public bout ledgers, and when the copies came they were incomplete and out of order and worth more than everything else she had put together, because a ledger did not exaggerate. A ledger only disagreed with the world in ways that someone had to explain.

The rumor was wrong about everything that did not matter. His name was spelled two ways. His age was given as anything from thirteen to sixteen, and the list of fighters he had beaten changed from teller to teller. It was right, stubbornly and consistently, about the things that did matter: an unclassified fighter, a string of recorded wins against ranked opponents, and, as far as she could rebuild them from the scraps, more than one distinct combat signature in a single body.

She made a page in the second notebook and titled it, with a restraint she considered a professional duty, *Candidate explanations*. Then she spent a winter killing the entries one at a time.

A concealed second Path in the family line died first, because the signatures in the ledgers sat too far apart for any two Paths she knew to cover; there were at least three families in them, and possibly four. Fraud died next, because too many unconnected witnesses agreed on too many small details, and because the circuit's ledger keeper initialed every bout in an ink that changed colour twice in the period and changed at exactly the right entries. Exceptional range within a single Path died hardest. She pieced together a bout he had lost, to a Bronze-tier Pressure fighter, out of four incomplete accounts, and the timing chart she made of it lay on her desk for a week and seemed to sneer at her, because nothing in any single Path's grammar did what the chart said he had done in the last three minutes before he lost.

Nothing survived. That was the finding.

The last ledger copy arrived at the start of the summer and included one more result. An Iron Rank Eight, a fighter of real standing, had been beaten in front of something like six hundred people by a boy with no rank on the board at all. Karis read the entry, set it beside the others, and went for a long walk around the Ternhall reservoir, and when she came back she wrote the sentence she had been trained for five years never to write.

*The model is missing a category.*

She did not underline it. She considered underlining a form of shouting, and she did not shout in either notebook. She only sat and looked at it for a while, the way a person looks at a door that has been painted over for so long that everyone has forgotten it opens.

---

The registrar's office at Ternhall received a weekly digest of new enrollments from every chartered academy in the region. It was meant for recruiting, so that Ternhall could see which promising children had gone elsewhere and occasionally invite them to reconsider, and almost nobody read it. Karis had asked the registrar's junior clerk, in the spring, to set aside any digest that carried a certain name. She had not explained why. The clerk was used to Karis asking for things without explaining, and he had found that when he did what she asked, his own errands seemed to go more smoothly, though he could never quite work out how.

The digest came on a grey morning nine days before Ternhall's autumn term. The name was on the third page, in the list for a minor-tier academy she had to look up on a map. Beside it, in the column for category, someone had written *observer*.

She laughed aloud in the library, once, and a second-year at the next table jumped.

It was the word that did it. A boy who had beaten an Iron Rank Eight in front of six hundred people had been enrolled, in ink, under a category built for children who had not yet Kindled. Either the clerks at this small academy had made the worst mistake she had ever seen in a registry digest, or someone there had looked at the same gap she had been staring at all winter and decided to use it. She did not know which. She wanted, more than she had wanted anything since the morning her Arbiter first named her Path, to find out.

She filed the transfer request before the midday bell.

Her advisor took a full hour to talk her out of it, in his office with the door shut, with the weary patience of a man who had talked a great many promising students out of a great many things. He set out the sums for her, as though sums were what stood in her way: where Ternhall's graduates went and where a minor academy's went, the guilds that read the name of a first-rank school on a letter and the guilds that would not read the name of a small one at all. At the end of the hour he folded his hands.

"A first-rank student at a minor academy," he said, "is a good lamp lit in a cellar."

Karis admired the sentence. It was compact and quotable and almost certain to be repeated to the next student who sat in that chair. It was also wrong, and wrong in a direction he had no way of seeing, because a cellar was precisely where a person carried a lamp when she meant to find something. She thought about showing him the second notebook, and then remembered an instructor she had shown three pages of it at thirteen, who had read them politely and handed them back and called them a charming hobby. She thanked her advisor for his time. She did not change the request.

Her parents were harder, because their worry was about her and not about her prospects, and she had no arithmetic that answered it. Her mother sat with her at the kitchen table on the last evening and asked one question.

"And if he turns out to be only a boy, and the whole mystery is in the paperwork?"

Karis gave the honest answer, because her mother deserved nothing less. "Then I'll have spent a year proving it properly, with everything documented. It would be the best negative result I ever produced, and I'd come home and be proud of it."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. "You've already decided."

That was true. If Karis was strict with herself, and she always tried to be, it had been true since the walk around the reservoir. Her father said nothing at all that evening, and in the morning he packed her trunk with great care, folding each thing as a man folds what he expects to see again.

There was one person at Ternhall she told after the papers were filed rather than before. She did not record, in either notebook, why she had chosen that order.

The coach south took four days. She spent most of them doing something she had never done for any subject in either notebook, which was writing a list of the questions she would not ask.

She began it as an exercise and found by the second morning that it was the most important page she had written all year. Every subject in the second notebook had been a thing, a timing or a curve or a shell that held too long against cold, and none of them had been able to object to being written down. This one could. If he was what the ledgers said, he had spent his whole life being written down by people who never asked his leave, and she had read enough of those people's work, at second and third hand, to know exactly what it looked like from outside. She did not intend to add a page to it.

So she wrote, in a column, the questions she would not ask him unless he offered first. Where it came from. How it worked. Whether it hurt. What the registry had called him, if it had called him anything. What he could do that he had not shown on the circuit. She wrote eleven of them, and then a twelfth, which was simply *anything he hasn't chosen to show*, and she drew a line under the column and wrote beneath it the only question she would ask.

*Whether I may ask.*

Then, because she was honest with herself in both notebooks, she wrote one more line under that.

*If he says no, the finding is no. Record it and go home.*

Greyvane, when she came over the brow of its hill on the last afternoon of the second week of its term, was smaller than its letters and better kept than its reputation. The gate clerk had a list, and Karis's name was on it, entered in the same plain hand as every other name, and she found she liked the academy a little for that before she was through the gate.

She went to the standard desk, because transfers went to the standard desk, and the brisk young clerk there read the Ternhall letter twice, then looked at Karis's rank in the margin for long enough that the girl behind her shifted her feet. Karis waited. She had been looked at in exactly that way at every desk since she was fourteen, and she had learned that the look passed sooner if one gave it nothing to push against.

She was given a room in the residence wing, a schedule, and a block on the training floor at the fifth bell in section two. She unpacked that night. She registered for lectures the next morning, including the declaration theory block, which she did not need and attended anyway, because the way a school taught its basics told her more about the school than its prospectus ever had. On the second afternoon she demonstrated for the floor instructor who took the heat Paths, Ember and Ash together, a lean old man with scorched cuffs who watched her lay six points on the boards and let them die on his count and said only, "Ternhall," in the tone of a man identifying a bird by its song. She showed him the standard set at the standard size and nothing more. A transfer demonstrated to be placed, not to be admired.

It was harder than she had expected, and not for the reason she would have guessed. The points themselves were easy; she had laid ten thousand of them. What was hard was the space across the section from her, where for three years another set of points had always answered hers, a breath behind or a breath ahead, so that every ignition she made had been half of a conversation. Here there was only chalk and a lean old man with a slate. She laid her six and let them die on his count, and she noticed, because she noticed everything about herself, that she was laying them a fraction early, the way a person speaks a fraction early into a silence where someone has always replied. She corrected it on the fourth point. She did not write the correction down in either notebook. She only stood a moment afterward with her off hand very still at her side, and then went to sign her slip.

The next name on the instructor's list was a tall, narrow woman of perhaps twenty with ash-pale hair and a training coat patched at both elbows, who had been waiting by the wall throughout without watching. Her tag carried the academy's crest and, beneath it, a small raised dot. Karis had read the floor roster by then and knew what the dot meant. She watched the woman lay a low grey haze across the boards that slowed everything passing through it, as an Ash Path should, and then watched her sign her own slip at the desk afterward with great care, checking the hour against the clock above the door before she wrote it. Karis filed her under *re-certification track; handles procedure as if it might bite.* It was a thing she recognized. She had not yet decided whether it was a thing she liked.

She catalogued the grounds in a single afternoon. She listened to the rumor market for three days. There were four theories about the observer and, by the second evening, three about herself, and she ranked them all as evidence and found that every one was worthless about its subject and very useful about the academy. Nobody's theory about the observer was frightened. They were curious and silly and competitive, a third-year insisting on a northern scandal and a first-year insisting on poetry, but not one of them was afraid of him. She wrote that down. It told her how this place held him, and it was more than any interview would have told her.

She saw him on the second day and twice more after that.

The first time he was at the rail of the open floor during the fifth bell, with a binder open on his knee, writing while a Blade pair drilled in front of him in absolute silence. He did not look up when people looked at him, and people looked at him a great deal. The tag on his coat was not a crest but a hollow circle. She noticed, because she could not help noticing, that his pen moved in long passages while the pair worked and stopped when they stopped, and she recognized the habit with a jolt, because it was her own.

The second time he was at the edge of the formal yard during a standings bout, watching a third-year Force Path lose. The third time he crossed the yard with a stocky boy in an Iron Skin crest and a girl with a dot on her tag, the three of them walking in step without seeming to arrange it, and the girl said something that made the stocky boy put his hand flat over his own face.

Each time, Karis wanted very much to cross the floor and introduce herself. Each time, she did not.

She had decided on the coach that she would spend one full week at Greyvane as an ordinary transfer before she spoke to him. She would unpack and register, demonstrate for her instructors and fight her first scheduled standings bout, and do all of it with her own name, as a student among students. Later, when anyone asked, and she was certain someone eventually would, she wanted the record to show that Karis Dellenmoor had come to Greyvane as a student and not as a hunter with a question. It would matter which. In her experience a difference kept carefully enough, for long enough, always came to matter in the end.

Her first standings bout fell on the fifth day, against a Copper Rank Nine who had heard the rumors and came at her far too hard. She finished it in two exchanges and did not look at the rail to see who had been watching.

On the last afternoon of the week she went to the archive, because she had a rule about reading an instrument before she studied what it held. The observer was enrolled under a demonstration provision, and she had not yet read it.

The archivist was a small, straight old woman with spectacles on a cord, and she held the door open at an angle so exact that Karis noticed it and filed it at once.

"I'd like to read the demonstration provision," Karis said. "The academy's copy."

The archivist's eyes went past her shoulder, into the room, and then came back.

"It's out," she said. "Somebody's reading it."

He had read the provision three times that week already, and he was reading it a fourth when the door opened, because the second sitting was coming and because Prynn had left the statutes out on the long table for him and it seemed ungrateful not to use them.

He heard the archivist's voice at the door, low and dry, and another voice answer it, and he did not look up at once. Then he felt someone cross the room. It was not the way students usually came into the archive, drifting and uncertain, peering down aisles they did not mean to enter. Whoever this was walked as if she had already measured the floor and knew exactly how many steps it would take. He looked up.

It was the girl from the standings board. She stopped at the end of the long table, not at his elbow and not across the room, but at the one distance that was neither crowding nor caution, and stood there with her hands at her sides and nothing in them. Behind her, at the high desk by the door, Prynn had put her spectacles on and was watching the newcomer with the frank attention of an official who has just seen someone walk straight past the checkpoint and is deciding whether to mind.

"You're Cael," the girl said.

It was not a question, and he noticed that before anything else.

# Chapter 10 — A Finding, Not a Premise

"I am," said Cael.

"Karis Dellenmoor. Ember Path, Iron Rank Three. Until last week, Ternhall." She said the rank the way some people show a pass at a gate, not to impress anyone but because it was the quickest way through. Then, without changing her voice at all: "I came to Greyvane because you did."

He set the statute book down on the table, square to the edge, with Prynn's weight beside it. He did it slowly, because he wanted a moment, and because he had a feeling Prynn was watching how he treated her books.

"Because of me."

"I've spent eight months rebuilding your circuit record from other people's accounts, and almost none of the accounts agree with each other about anything except the outline." She had clearly said this to herself many times; it came out smooth, with the drama worn off it like the shine off a coin. "I'd like to study what you are. Properly, on the record, as a formal piece of research. With your permission, on terms that you set, and you would read every word I wrote about you before it existed anywhere but my own hand. I'm not asking you to explain yourself today. I'm not asking you to explain anything, ever, that you'd rather keep. I'm asking whether you'll let me ask."

He looked at her for a long moment.

He had been looked at all his life. That was the plain truth of it, and he had written it in the binder more than once in more than one form. An Arbiter had looked at him at fourteen and produced a word in brackets. A certification clerk had looked at the word and apologized and stamped it anyway. A man in a market square had looked at him with careful questions, and a patient official whose name he knew had looked at him for a long time afterward, in reports Cael would never be shown. Even the people who loved him had looked closely, Hesk across a workbench and Lira across a sparring circle, close enough to see what did not fit before he had words for it himself. Being watched was the most constant thing in his life. He could not remember a season without it.

Nobody had ever asked.

Not one of them had stopped before the looking and wanted to know whether he would agree to it. The Arbiter had not asked, and neither had the clerk. The patient official had held a file on him as a matter already decided since before they ever met, and Cael doubted it had once occurred to the man that the file's subject might have an opinion about being in it. Whenever anyone had studied him hard, it had been because some office told them to.

"Why should I trust you?"

She did not answer at once. He watched her actually consider it, which was not the same as appearing to; he saw her take his question and hold her own answer up against it, the way a carpenter holds a board against a frame before cutting, and only then speak.

"You shouldn't. Not yet." She said it without any apology in it. "Trust is a finding, not a premise. Nobody ought to begin with it just because someone asked politely. I expect to earn it the way I'd earn any conclusion, from small claims first, where you can check them. I'll be right about little things, again and again, until being right about little things is evidence you can use." She paused. "And when I'm wrong, I'll show you the page where I was wrong. A hidden mistake spoils everything that's built on top of it. An admitted one only spoils itself."

He sat with that.

It was a good answer, and it was good in a way he had not expected. It did not try to comfort him, though it did, a little, in a dry way. It was good because it told the truth about how the thing was built. She had not offered him certainty. She had offered him a method, told him plainly how the method could fail, and promised what she would do when it did. It reminded him of Quenna on his first afternoon, laying the truth on the desk with the hard edges still on it.

"I haven't said yes."

"No. I'd think less of you if you had."

"What would it look like?" He wanted it in practice, because how a thing worked had always mattered to him more than what it was called, and so far she had only told him what it was called. "An ordinary day of it. If I agreed."

"Duller than you're imagining." She answered at once, which told him she had designed it long before she walked in. "Sessions, scheduled when you choose and not otherwise. I watch. I write. I don't touch you, I don't test you, and I never ask you to do anything you haven't already chosen to show. Afterward I may ask questions, and you can refuse any one of them or all of them, without a reason. I write up what I saw. You read it before anyone else does, and you keep a copy of everything, so that if a page of it ever turns up somewhere you didn't send it, you'll have the proof in your own room."

"And if I want to stop?"

"Then it stops. Any day, with one word, and you owe nobody an explanation." She leaned on the last part, very slightly. "That isn't a courtesy. Nobody had ever offered to protect him on the grounds that it kept the measurements clean. "No drinks in the reading room."

Karis glanced at the cup. "He has tea."

She did not stay. She told him she was not expecting an answer that day, or that week, and that she would rather he took a month than gave her a quick one he later regretted. At the end she looked down once at the statute book, open to the provision, and then back at him.

"Most people at this school have been looking at you for three weeks and talking about you for two," she said. "As far as I can tell, not one of them has asked you anything. I wanted the question to exist, out loud, whatever you decide. I think that should count for something before you decide."

Prynn waited until the door had shut.

"She looked at the shelves on the way in."

Cael turned on the bench. "Is that good?"

"Most of them look at you." Prynn turned a page in her ledger. "Drink your tea."

He drank it. It was as bad as it had been the first time, strong enough to stand a spoon in and stewed since dawn, and he finished every drop and carried the cup back to her desk, and then he walked out across the yard with no particular place to go. He did that when he needed his feet to move faster than his thoughts, and he walked the long way round the training hall twice before he noticed he was doing it.

There was a careful answer available, and it would have been the sensible one. He had learned over a long time that people who wanted to study him seldom wanted exactly what they said, and that the space between a stated purpose and its eventual use was where he usually got hurt. She would write things down. She had said so herself, plainly, as if it were the least alarming part of the offer. Written things travelled. A brown envelope had gone down this hill three weeks ago with his name inside it, and a grey card had come back, and he did not know who had read the line in between.

But beside the caution sat another fact, and he made himself hold both rather than let the louder one win by being louder. She had asked. Every eye that had ever studied him had done it as procedure, something done to him under cover of a rule that never stopped to wonder whether he would have agreed. She had walked into an archive and made the choice plain and handed him the refusal before she had written a single line. She had even told him how she expected to fail.

He did not know yet whether that made her someone to trust. He knew it made her unlike anyone else who had ever wanted to know what he was, and in his experience a difference that large was worth studying before deciding what to do with it.

So he did what he always did when he needed to understand a thing before committing to it. He watched her.

Her block was the fifth bell in section two, and it was posted on the roster in the same ink as everyone else's. An observer barred from the open bouts had every right to stand at the edge of a section during open hours, and Cael stood there four afternoons running, a few paces back from her chalk, with the binder on his knee.

On the first afternoon her floor instructor had paired her with the second-year Current Path girl whose footwork never stopped, and for most of the hour Karis did not fight at all. She laid points.

He had seen Ember on the circuit, and he had a page for it in the binder already. Circuit Ember was broad and hot and theatrical. It came off a fighter in sheets and gouts and long ropes of heat that cleared space and made crowds shout, and its practitioners tended to finish a bout red-faced and running with sweat, as if they had been standing too near their own forge. This was not that. Karis worked in small, exact ignitions, each the size of a coin, placed on the chalked boards or in the air at the height of a knee, held for a counted moment and let go. Each one showed as a dim warm bloom, like the glow of a coal seen through paper, and each one faded on a timing so regular that by the second quarter of the hour he had stopped watching her hands and started counting in his head, and found that his count and her points agreed.

The Current girl moved through them as she was told to, one drill after another. Karis placed a point where the girl's left foot was about to land and let it go a breath before the foot came down. Once she placed a point and did not let it go, and the girl's heel came down on it, and there was a small sharp hiss and a yelp. He noticed one other thing, near the end of the hour, and he nearly did not write it down because it seemed too small. Before every point she laid, her off hand went still. It was not much, a quarter of a breath, the free hand stopping wherever it happened to be as if it were listening. Then the point came. He watched for it on the next ten and found it on all ten. On the second afternoon she saw him.

Of course she saw him. He had not hidden, and she was, by her own account, someone who noticed things for a living. Between one drill and the next her eyes came up and found him at the edge of the section with the binder open, and rested on him for perhaps a breath, and went back to the boards.

Then she did nothing differently. That was the part he wrote twice.

Edran had sharpened when he found an audience at the rail; Cael had watched him tighten a bout like a man tightening a screw. She did not angle her best work toward the edge of the section or lay her points a fraction cleaner. She did not even stop laying the late one that had hissed under the Current girl's heel the day before; she laid it late again, on purpose this time, and wrote the result down. *She knows people change when they're watched,* he wrote that night. *She seems to have trained it out of herself, which would mean she decided at some point that clean results were worth more than looking well, and then trained herself until it was so.* He looked at the entry. The two more said it was not a performance. Nobody held themselves that steady for four afternoons running for the benefit of one boy at the edge of a section. On the fourth afternoon she did not look at him at all, and he understood that she had simply stopped needing to.

*Ember, the way she does it, believes a fire doesn't need to be big if it's in exactly the right place.*

"I read her transfer papers. I read every transfer's papers." She handed him the strip. "Most people who want to write about you will never show you a page of what they write," she said. "If she means to, that's worth something. He told Lira on the wall outside the residence wing, the evening after the fourth session, because that was where he told her things.

"Well," he said at the end.

Lira thought about it for longer than he expected.

"She asked." She turned the words over as if testing their weight. "Everybody who's ever wanted anything from you wanted it without asking. She asked. That isn't nothing." A pause. "It isn't everything, either."

"No."

"You don't have to decide tonight. She told you that herself, apparently."

"She said she'd rather I took a month."

"Then take a month." Lira pulled one knee up and wrapped both arms around it, settling in, as she did when a talk had run past the length she had planned for it. It's the category." She was looking out at the yard, not at him. "People who put you on paper. Every bad thing that ever happened to you began as something somebody wrote down."

He did not answer, because there was no answer to that; it was simply true.

"I know about writing things down," Lira went on, more quietly. "There's a file on me in a box at the intake desk. Fenmark's file. I've never read it. Not once. But I know who wrote it, and I know not one of them ever asked me a single question before they did. They watched me for a year and wrote what they decided they'd seen, and then a clerk read it aloud to me in a room, and that was what I was." She breathed out. "So when somebody says *I'd like to study you*, my shoulders go up before I can stop them. I'm not arguing. That's the scar, and I can tell a scar from an argument. I'm only warning you it's there, so that when I pull faces about her later you'll know which bit of me is pulling them."

"All right," he said, and meant it in the full weight the binder gave the words.

"And Cael." She waited until he turned to look at her. "If you do it, do it the way she offered. Terms. Written down. Real ones. Not because I think she'll cheat you. He sat with that longer than with anything else anyone had said to him that week. It was so like Lira to take a question about one person and turn it, in a single evening, into a question about every day after.

"Sit with it," she said, and bumped his shoulder with hers, the old easy knock from more walls than either of them could count. "You're good at holding a thing until you know what you actually think about it, instead of what you're meant to think. Go and be good at it."

---

Brom's view arrived the next morning, unannounced, between spoonfuls, in the voice of a man who had been thinking about it all night and saw no reason to say so.

"The Ternhall transfer."

Cael looked up from his bread. Lira, who had stolen the other half of it, looked up too.

"She watched my floor block two days ago. The whole hour, with a notebook, sitting on the bench at the end of the section like it was a lecture." Brom did not look up from his bowl. "Afterward she came over and asked me one question. Just one. She asked where I'd learned to hold back the second half."

"The second half of what?" said Lira.

"The turn." Now Brom did look up, and there was something careful in his face, as if he were handling an object he had not expected anyone to pick up. "When I take a strike, there are two parts. I give, and then I turn it. The giving is the first commitment. The turn is the second. I wait on the second, longer than anybody teaches, because if I turn too soon the other fellow feels it and takes his weight back." He set the spoon down. "Nobody's ever asked me that. Wray found the waiting, the first day. She never asked about the second half. It took me two years on my own to understand there even was a second half to ask about."

"What did you tell her?" Cael asked.

"The truth. That I learned it by getting hit a great deal in Velmere by people who were paid to make me give up, and I didn't want to give them the satisfaction of feeling me turn." He picked the spoon up again. "She wrote that down. All of it. Then she thanked me and went away." He ate a mouthful, thoughtfully. "It was the right question. When you weigh her up, put that in the scales."

Cael put it in the scales.

That night he sat on his bed by the low lamp and wrote it up in the binder, slowly, in the square hand he saved for entries he meant to keep.

*Ember, Iron 3, Ternhall. Walked away from a better school to ask me a question. She is the first person who has ever asked before looking.*

He read it back. A verdict, he thought, was something that happened to a person. It arrived sealed, and the most you could do with it was survive it. A question was something you could stand inside and answer. He had been a verdict since he was fourteen: a word in brackets, four known cases, a file closed before it was opened. Everyone since who had surprised him into trusting them, Hesk and Lira and Vell and Brom, and Quenna in her cold, careful way, had done something like the same thing: they had refused to take the verdict as proved. This girl had gone one step further than any of them. She had treated it as unasked.

He did not know yet what that was worth to him. He wrote a second line under the first, smaller, because it was the honest one.

*Weighing it.*

# Chapter 11 — Terms

Somewhere in the last year he had stopped deciding things alone, and he could not have said on which day. There had been no moment of choosing. It had felt like a habit forming in the way his habits usually formed, quietly and by repetition, until one morning he looked up and saw the shape of it and understood it had been there for months.

Karis seemed to understand this about him before he had said a word of it.

She was waiting on the landing of the lecture wing's stair the next morning, not on the step he would have to pass, and not in the corridor where he would have had to stop or walk around her, but on the landing itself, to one side, where he could come up to her or go by as he liked. He noticed the placing. He was beginning to suspect that very little about her was accidental.

"I'm not here for an answer," she said. "I'd like to change the question, if you'll let me."

"Change it how?"

"Before you decide, I'd like to put it to the people you'd ask anyway." She shifted the strap of her bag on her shoulder, which was the first unnecessary movement he had ever seen her make. "All of them, together, in one room, where they can object to me directly instead of to your account of me. Your account would be fair. I'm sure of that. It still wouldn't be me." She met his eyes. "If the answer's no, I'd like it to be no on the primary evidence."

He said yes before he had worked out why it mattered so much that she had asked to do it that way. It did matter. That night he added a line to a much longer entry: *She could have kept pressing me on my own. She asked for the table instead. She knows we have a table, though nobody told her.*

The table was the last trestle at the far end of the stable, under the outline of the old bricked-up hay door, and it had plainly hosted every private conversation Greyvane's students had needed to have for a very long time. Its edges were cut and scored with initials and dates going back decades, some of them so old the letters had worn to soft grooves, layer on layer of other people's alliances. By the end of supper the long room had mostly emptied. Two second-years were playing tiles by the fire with the careful deafness of people who knew that not listening was the stable's most respected rule.

Brom arrived first and took the seat in the corner where the wall would be behind him, a habit from the circuit Cael doubted he had ever noticed in himself. Lira arrived second and dropped into the seat directly across from the empty chair, before the person who would sit in it had even come in, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of remark.

"You're braced," said Cael.

"I'm informed." Lira folded her arms on the table. "Brom told me what she asked you on the stairs. I've had a whole day to think."

"And?"

"And I'm going to hear her out first. That's kinder than it sounds, because I've already made up my mind." She looked at the empty chair. "I want to hear her say it herself."

"I'm in favor."

"I've been trying to work Cael out by instinct for most of a year. Watch what he does, guess why it works, fix the guess when it's wrong. That's how I've learned everything I know. "That," said Lira, "is exactly the trouble."

She said it evenly, with no heat in it at all, and that made it land harder than shouting would have. She turned in her seat to face Karis directly, not hostile and not performing anything for the room, simply giving the moment her whole attention because she had decided it had earned that much.

I've watched you in the yard and on the floor for a week, and you work like someone who actually wants to know what the answer is, not someone who's already picked it and is hunting for evidence to fit. "I understand why. I even think your reasons are good ones. But I've watched a lot of people look closely at Cael, and each of them was collecting evidence for something. The two second-years by the fire had stopped pretending to play tiles. Neither of them looked over.

"We're a family," said Lira.

Cael watched Karis take it. He had seen enough of her by now to know how she handled a hard result. She did not flinch, and she did not hurry to make it softer. She turned it over the way he had watched her turn over a late point that hissed under someone's heel, checking it from every side before she decided what it meant.

"Yes," she said at last. "You're right. I want data."

"Brom."

"I'm only saying," said Brom, and subsided, looking pleased with himself.

Quenna read the terms standing up.

"Where do the notes live?"

"In my room, in a box with a lock on it," said Karis. "His copies live in his."

"Not in the archive. Not in any academy record. Not attached to anything with a seal on it."

"No."

"Good." Quenna turned the page a quarter-turn on the table, so that it faced Cael. "And you've read the sixth clause properly. You understand that it gives you every word before anyone else sees one."

"Yes."

"Then I have one condition, and it's mine, not theirs." She laid a finger on the margin. "The sessions happen inside your supervised hours, in section four, with me at the chalk. Karis was nodding before Quenna had finished. Cael saw what the condition was for, and saw that Karis had seen it too. It was not a restriction at all. It was a coat Quenna was putting over the whole arrangement, cut in advance to the shape of a question nobody had asked yet.

Karis did not quite manage to keep the pleasure off her face. It was a small failure, a flicker at the corner of the mouth that she corrected almost at once, and Cael enjoyed it more than he would have admitted. For a moment the two of them looked at each other across the table, the assessor of irregular development and the student of broken models, and he had the odd sense of watching two people across a wide gap in age and standing recognize the same appetite in each other: a taste for language that would carry weight.

"Thursday," said Quenna. "Fifth bell. Bring your own stool. Section four doesn't have one."

He ran the evasion framework, the Wind-adjacent patterns that had carried him through every bout he had fought since Lira taught them to him in Ardenmere, without either of them knowing what she was teaching. He knew them so deeply by now that he hardly had to think his way through them. He ran the whole sequence three times. The first pass was slow, the joints loose and every transition exaggerated, so that the shape underneath stood out the way a skeleton stands out from a drawing of a body. The second was the working pass, the speed he had used against Reydan and a dozen circuit fighters before him, fast enough that someone without training would see only movement and no method in it at all. The third was slow again, at Quenna's request from the chalk, so that the same structure could be watched at two speeds and compared for what changed and what did not.

Karis wrote throughout.

He learned something about her method from the sound alone. While he moved, her pen moved with him in long runs that never broke, and when he stopped it stopped, every time, and lay still on the page until he started again. She was not describing what he did, he realized; anyone with eyes could describe it. She was tracking something that ran on, something that only existed while the framework was live, and when the framework stopped there was nothing left to track. She did not ask him to repeat anything. She did not interrupt to clarify. Once or twice he saw her pen lift and hover over the page, as if a question had arrived, and then come down again without her saying a word, as if she had answered it herself. There was something almost restful in it. Nobody was judging him. She did not seem to care whether his footwork was beautiful or whether his framework belonged to any Path on any chart. She was not deciding what he was for, or whether he should be allowed to stay, or what to write in a box. She was simply recording him, all of him, as information worth the trouble of getting exactly right.

Twenty minutes in, he caught himself adjusting.

It was a small thing. He had come out of a turn a fraction squarer than he needed to, holding the line of his shoulders a moment longer than the movement required, so that the shape of it would read cleanly from the stool. He was not performing. It was not the old circuit urge to make a crowd lean forward. It was something else, and it took him the rest of the pass to put a name to it. He wanted her to see it correctly. He did not want to impress her. He wanted to be understood right, for once, by someone who was actually trying.

He made himself stop doing it on the third pass, because it was not honest data if he shaped it for the page. But he wrote it down that night as its own small finding. *I wanted her to get it right. Not to be impressed. To have it understood. Those aren't the same, and I couldn't explain why to anyone who hasn't been misread for years.*

When the third pass was done, he stood still in the middle of section four with his breath coming a little faster than he liked, and Karis closed her notebook and capped her pen. "That's the arrangement."

She looked up at him. "When you use it, does it feel borrowed?"

He stood there with his breathing still not quite settled and understood at once that this was not a casual question.

Brom, who read him by feel better than anybody living, had never put a question in that shape, as if the movement itself might carry some memory of where it had come from, and might feel, in the doing, like a thing that had belonged to somebody else first. Even Quenna, whose whole profession was the study of what he was, asked only questions with answers you could see. This one had no answer anyone could see. It could only be answered from one place in the world, the inside of him, where nobody else could ever stand, and Karis had walked straight up to that place on her first afternoon and knocked politely on the door.

# Chapter 13 — Subject Fourteen

At the regional registry office, on the fifth day after a brown envelope left a hill town on the third-day post, a clerk with a head cold opened it, read the sheet inside, and entered one line in a book.

It was a routine line. The sheet was headed *Registry Copy, for Notation*, and it came from a minor-tier academy that sent perhaps a dozen of them a year, and the clerk entered it in the same hand and the same ink as the eleven lines above it. She copied the reference number onto a grey card, stamped the date across one corner in purple, initialed the margin, and dropped the card in the tray for the outgoing post, so that the academy would know its sheet had arrived. The academy always knew. The office acknowledged what it was sent. It was one of the few things the office did quickly.

The book was not the only place the line went.

Every evening, after the clerks had gone home, the day's entries were copied into the long index that the registry kept against its standing files, and every name in the copy was run against every name in the index, the way every name was always run. In a queue that handled several thousand of these comparisons a night, one of them came back with a match. Nothing rang. Nobody stood up. The match was set on a pile of other matches, and for six hours it sat there under a weight of ordinary paper while the night went on outside.

Then a person looked at it, and it moved from the bottom of a long pile to the top of a very short one. The six hours it had waited became, afterward, the least important six hours in everything that followed.

---

Coss read the notice three times before he let himself close the folder.

He had learned, over a monitoring assignment that had produced almost nothing worth reporting, to be careful about the space between what a piece of paper said and what he might want it to say. So he read it once for the facts and once for the language and a third time from the top, slowly, as if he had never seen the subject's name before. *Notice of index correspondence. Subject: Cael. Event: enrollment, chartered institution. Institution: Greyvane Academy. Category of enrollment: unclassified observer, demonstration provision. Notation entered.* Beneath that was a line he had never seen on a notice of this kind: *Acknowledgment returned to originating institution, Received for Notation*, and a reference number, and a date that was already three days old.

So the academy had a card. Somewhere up a hill he had never visited, someone was holding a grey card with a purple stamp on it, and believing, quite correctly, that a clerk had filed a sheet.

Below the reference number was the field that always came last on any notice to do with this file, and it was the one he had stopped expecting to read. Where the supervisory designation should have been there was a short ruled bar, as neat as a line drawn under a sum, and beneath it, in small type, *Field restricted. Recipient clearance insufficient.*

He looked at the bar for a while. He had seen it on every notice for this file since the first, and he had written upward about it once, in the plainest language the form allowed, asking only what it was, so that he could do his work properly. He had not asked to be given more authority, or a larger budget, or anything that could be mistaken for ambition. He had asked what was on his own file. No answer had come back. He had waited a season, and then another, and then had put the silence away in the drawer he kept for whatever the work produced that the forms had no place for, and gone on.

He closed the folder and sat with his hands flat on it in the narrow district office he had worked from for more years than he cared to add up.

The difference that mattered was not personal. It was jurisdictional, and he made himself think it through in exactly those terms, because they were the terms he would have to write in. A subject with an irregular registry designation, living quietly in an unranked district or fighting on an open circuit that belonged to nobody, generating no formal complaint and no registered incident, was a matter for patient observation. Coss could watch such a subject. He could report on him. He could do nothing else, and he had done nothing else, honestly, for a long time. A subject with the same designation, enrolled by name in the roster of an academy that held a charter from the Compact, was something else entirely. On the day that line was written in the regional book, the subject had walked out of a category where a Warden could only watch and into one where a Warden's writ had teeth. Compliance was a domain with procedures. Monitoring was a domain with patience, and very little else.

He thought, before he wrote a word, about what the file had cost him so far.

It had cost him season after season of reports that were really one report, its three sentences dressed differently each time. The subject remained outside any chartered institution. The subject had generated no formal complaint. The office recommended continued monitoring at the current level. It had cost him a superior who almost never wrote, and he had learned young not to take that for neglect. The Compact did not staff its patient assignments with officers it meant to check on every week. It gave them to officers it trusted to tell an empty season from a quiet one, and to report which it was without dressing it up.

He had reported which, every time. Whatever else came of the file, that much of his record he would defend to anyone. He had never once made a report sound larger to keep the assignment alive, and never once made one sound smaller to let it close. The boy had simply, for a long time, done nothing that crossed the line.

Now he had. Not by fighting anyone. By signing a form.

---

That evening he wrote out the request for assignment, in the flat language the form demanded. *Request for field assignment, compliance review, chartered institution. Grounds: index correspondence, enrollment of subject under monitoring.* He wrote it in one sitting, read it once, signed it and put it in the evening bag before he could begin to improve it, because he had noticed over the years that the improvements he made to a request at night were seldom improvements by morning.

It came back approved within the day.

He read the approval standing at the window of the district office with his coat still on, and he read it twice, because the speed of it was information. A request of that kind usually took a week to come back and often took three, after travelling up and down a ladder of desks that each needed to initial it. This one had gone up and come back down in less time than it took the evening coach to make the round trip to the regional town, which meant it had not gone up a ladder at all. It had gone to one desk, a desk somewhere above his, and the person at that desk had been waiting for it.

He allowed himself a single unprofessional minute over that.

The file was live at a level he could not see. It had been live there for some time, perhaps from the beginning, and in all the seasons of his quarterly reports nobody at that level had ever sent so much as a clarifying question back down. Someone had been happy to leave the file in his hands alone, for the plain reason that he handled it well. From where he stood, that was either a compliment or a use, and a long career had taught him that from below the two looked exactly alike. He thought of the ruled bar on every notice, and of his one plain question about it, and he understood that whoever had approved his request in less than a day could read what was under the bar, and that he still could not.

The minute ended. He put it away in the same place as the unanswered question and sat down to the first piece of work the assignment required, which, as with every assignment, was to ask for paper.

The records request took him two hours. It was not difficult to write; the form was a standard one, and every item on it was something a chartered academy was obliged by its charter to produce when asked. *Documentation of enrollment, complete. Citation of the provision under which the subject is enrolled. Assessment records to date, with the names of the assessors.* He wrote each item precisely, without adjectives, and attached no urgency to any of it, because urgency in a request invited a defence in the reply, and he wanted the reply to be complete, not careful. He addressed it to the provost of Greyvane, by office, and routed it through the regional channel as the procedure required, so that it would be logged and stamped and carried up the hill by whatever means the regional office used for such things.

Then he took a bag out of the cupboard and began to pack.

It came to him flatly, as most facts about himself came to him, that it was the first time he had packed for this file since Denvash. That was a long way back now. He had packed then in a Denvash lodging with the thin file open on the bed beside the bag, for a journey across to a district of Ardenmere where a boy he had never seen was living on Unranked access. He had expected a week's errand. He had come back with a summons stamped closed-pending, a page of notes in his own hand, and a file he had not known, at the time, he would be carrying for years. Since then the file had asked nothing of him but reading and writing. Tonight it asked for a bag on the floor of a room he had lived in for longer than any assignment should keep a man in one place, and for a document case lying open beside the bag with nothing in it yet. The case would hold the challenge. He had not drafted the challenge. He knew already, in its outline, that he was going to.

---

He left before dawn. Nothing in the assignment required it; it was his own habit. In a long career he had never once found that travelling with the sun already up improved anything about the travelling, and he had found many times that the first hours of a road were the only quiet ones it had.

The route ran first to the regional office, where any assignment to a chartered academy had to begin with a formal briefing and a challenge packet, and neither had been prepared. After that, when the packet was ready and not before, it would run on to the academy itself. He did not have a date for that second road. He had learned not to want one until the first road was finished. A challenge filed early on paperwork that was not yet sound was worse than a challenge filed late, and he meant this one to be sound in every joint.

The coach was half empty and cold. He sat by the window with the file on his knees and read it again from the first page, which was his custom whenever a case was worth the travelling.

He did it in the hope that a file read fresh would yield some detail that a file read by habit had hidden, and it rarely did. This one yielded nothing new on the first day or the second. What the reading actually did, he understood well before the regional town came in sight, had less to do with the file than with himself. It was a way of arriving already settled, so certain of every fact he carried that whatever happened when he finally reached the boy's gate, nothing he learned there would take him unawares. Only once in his career had a case taken him unawares, very early, by a case he had judged wrongly in a way he still thought about more often than the job required. The long re-reading had begun after that case, and he had never once regretted the hours it cost.

The file was thick. It held the quarterly reports, all of them, in his own hand, each one a little shorter than the last, as if the sentences had grown tired of being said. It held the circuit summaries from Ardenmere, written by a correspondent who did not know why anyone wanted them and wrote them with the cheerful thoroughness of a man paid by the page. It held the circuit's own ledger extracts, initialled by the woman who ran the Ironyard in an ink that changed colour twice. It held a short account, from a junior assessor, of an afternoon in a market square that Coss had read many times and never quite liked. And it held, near the back, the report of a bout fought only a few weeks ago, in front of some six hundred people, in which the subject had beaten an Iron Rank Eight of real standing. The correspondent had described the crowd at length and the fight hardly at all. Coss had filed it under *no registered incident*, because there had been none. A fight was not an incident. A fight was only a fight.

On the second night, at a waystation where the road from the south joined his, a carter sat down across the long table from him with a bowl of stew and the air of a man who had been alone on a box seat for three days and intended to talk to somebody before he slept. Coss let him. It was easier than not, and carters knew the roads.

The man had come up from Ardenmere. He talked about the price of feed and the state of the bridges and a wheelwright in the market district who had cheated him once and would not get the chance again, and then, because the stew was hot and the room was warm and Ardenmere was full of it that season, he talked about the circuit.

"Saw the big one," he said. "End of the season. The Ironyard. You'd have had to be dead in a ditch to miss it, the noise they made." He pointed his spoon at Coss. "Iron Rank Eight, the big fellow was. Real rank, off a real station. And a lad with nothing on the board at all, nothing, not even Copper. Everybody had their money on the big fellow, and I don't blame them. I had a coin on him myself."

Coss kept his face still. "And?"

"And the lad took him." The carter shook his head slowly, as men do at weather they do not understand. "Not lucky, either. I've seen lucky. This wasn't. He got knocked about something cruel in the middle of it. I thought he was done. And then he got up and did something I couldn't follow, and the big fellow was on the boards." He went back to his stew. "Funny thing was his face. Six hundred people yelling, and that lad never once looked frightened. Not once. Calm as a clerk."

Coss thought of a line in his own handwriting on the oldest page, and of a boy's pale knuckles pressed together on his knees across a borrowed desk.

"Perhaps he'd stopped showing it," he said.

The carter shrugged. "Perhaps. Couldn't say. I only saw the outside." He wiped his bowl with his bread. "That's all any of us see, isn't it."

Coss went up to bed soon after. He did not sleep for some time.

He read every page. On the third night, at a waystation table with a candle that leaned and a supper he did not finish, he came to the oldest page, as he always did last, and stayed on it.

It was not the oldest sheet in the file. The oldest sheets were Denvash's, a registration entry and a notice and a flag, as thin now as they had been on the afternoon he first read them. But it was the oldest page in his own hand: his notes from the summons in Ardenmere, the first time he had sat across a desk from the subject. Most of it was the form. Name, date of summons, the reference, the designation in brackets, the hour the subject arrived, which was early, and the hour he left. And at the bottom, in the space for the officer's remarks, a single line.

*Subject fourteen. Cooperative. Frightened, controlled it well.*

He had written the last part against the form's convention. Assessorial style discouraged anything that sounded like a description of character, and a senior officer had once told him, mildly, that *frightened* was a word for novels. Coss had never once considered striking it. In all the time since, it had been the single most accurate sentence in the file. All that the boy had done since that afternoon could be found folded inside it. Frightened, controlled it well, and better at both halves every year.

He remembered the afternoon more clearly than he would have chosen to. He remembered the cramped office he had borrowed for two days, and the boy coming in on time with a folded document in his hand, and sitting with his hands pressed together on his knees so tightly that the knuckles had gone pale. The document had been a written challenge to the summons itself, with the statute, the subsection and the appendix all cited exactly, found by a fourteen-year-old in a district archive in two days. It had been correct. Coss had read it three times and conceded it, because it was correct, and he had stamped the summons closed-pending with more force than the stamp required. Through all of it the boy had answered every question he was asked, quietly and completely, and had not once raised his voice. When it was over, he had looked up from the chair and said *Thank you*, as if a man doing his job properly were a courtesy.

Coss had thought about that *thank you* for a long time afterward. He had a daughter at home much the same age, and he had found himself, on the long coach back, imagining her in that chair with her hands pressed white on her knees, and had stopped imagining it because it did not help him do the work. He thought about it again now, at the waystation table, with the candle leaning and the coach horses shifting in the yard outside.

He did not feel sorry for the boy. Sorrow was not his job, and he distrusted it in himself, because a sorry officer made soft reports, and a soft report did no one any good, least of all its subject. But he had been doing this work for a long time, and he had learned that a person could do a hard thing honestly or do it carelessly, and that the difference mattered more to the person on the other side of the desk than almost anything else in the room. He meant to do this one honestly. He meant to do it as gently as the procedure allowed and not one degree more gently, because a gentler challenge would be a weaker one, and a weak challenge would simply be followed by a stronger one in someone else's hand.

He thought, too, of the bar on the notice.

Someone above him could read it. Someone above him had been waiting, perhaps for years, for the boy to sign his name on a chartered form, and had approved the assignment the day it arrived. Coss did not know who that was. He did not know what the bar concealed. He did not, if he was honest, entirely like that he did not, and he was a man who tried hard to be honest with himself at waystation tables when there was no one else awake to be honest with.

Before he put the file away, he took a clean sheet from the document case and tried the first line of the challenge, because he had found that the first line of a thing told him more about its difficulty than a week of thinking about it.

*The respondent is enrolled at Greyvane Academy under the category of unclassified observer, a category reserved by its own terms for candidates who have not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.*

That was true. He wrote the next line.

*The respondent underwent formal Kindling assessment at the certification station in Denvash, on the date entered in the registry, and was classified.*

That was also true, and he could prove it from the oldest sheets in the file. He sat looking at the two lines side by side and felt the shape of the case settle under his hands, simple and strong. A category for those who had not Kindled. A respondent who had. The enrollment was plainly invalid on its face, and any tribunal in Valdris would see it in a sentence.

Then he went back to the enrollment category's gloss, which the notation copy attached to the notice carried in full, and read it again, and stopped.

The category was not, strictly, reserved for those who had not Kindled. The gloss said so in its second sentence, but its first sentence said something wider. It covered any candidate *without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter*. And the subject did not have a standard classification. He had a designation in brackets that sat on no schedule Coss had ever been trained on, and that designation was the whole reason the file existed.

So the words held. Somebody at Greyvane had read them very carefully. The words held, and only the purpose did not.

He wrote *purpose* in the margin, looked at it, and drew a line through it. A tribunal did not rule on purpose by being told about it. It would want the drafting records of the provision, and precedent, and a clear statement of what the people who wrote those words had meant them to cover, and all of that would take weeks to gather from offices that were in no hurry to be gathered from. He found, a little to his own surprise, that he did not resent it. A case that had to be built properly was a case that would stand once it was built. And the person at Greyvane who had read those words so carefully was, whoever they were, someone he would rather argue against than someone he would rather not have to.

He put the sheet in the document case, alone, with its two true lines and one crossed-out word.

He closed the file, blew out the leaning candle, and sat for a while in the dark.

In the morning the coach would carry him the rest of the way to the regional office, to a briefing and a packet that did not exist yet. Somewhere beyond that, at a date nobody had set, there was a gate on a hill and a boy behind it who had signed his name under a category everyone at the table must have known was not true. Coss did not yet know what his challenge would say. He knew exactly whom it would be about. For tonight, that was as much as he needed.

The records request reached Naveth's desk in the fourth week, by the standard channel, under a standard header that told the provost nothing except that somebody at the Compact had begun to pay attention.

Cael knew because Naveth sent for him. A junior clerk found him in the stable after the midday meal and said the provost would like a word, in the tone of someone who had been told to make it sound less important than it was, and Cael climbed the worn stair to the top of the old building with his stomach doing something it had not done since the morning of his first assessment.

The door stood open. Cael had learned to read that. When Naveth wanted a conversation private he shut the door on it, and when he did not mind who heard, he left it wide, and this was evidently one he did not mind.

"Sit. You'll want to see this, and I'd rather you saw it in that chair than heard about it on the stairs."

He turned a single sheet around on the blotter. It was printed on heavy paper, with a seal at the top and a list down the middle.

"Enrollment documentation, complete," Naveth read aloud, running one long finger down the items. "Citation of the provision. Assessment records to date, with assessors' names." He looked up. "They're being careful. Careful isn't quick. Quick is the only thing that would worry me."

Cael read the list over the provost's hand. It was long. Every item on it was, strictly speaking, something the academy was bound by its charter to produce, and none of it was phrased with any urgency at all. He could not tell whether that was reassuring. Naveth seemed to think it was more informative than urgency would have been.

"You're going to comply," Cael said. It was not quite a question.

"I'm going to give them every single thing the regulation says I must, and I'm going to give it at the last moment the regulation allows." Naveth set the request aside and reached into a drawer for a pad of blank requisition slips. "There's a way of obeying that looks from a distance like dragging your feet, and that the code, read word by word, can't tell apart from care. I've spent a long career learning the difference. The difference is patience."

He began to work as he talked, and Cael watched him do it.

He took the request apart. Each item on the Compact's list became a separate slip, and each slip was addressed to the office that had made the original document. The enrollment form had been made at the intake desk, so its copy had to be certified by the intake clerk, and the certification countersigned by the clerk's supervisor. The supervisor kept countersigning hours on two afternoons a week and no others. The provision's citation had to be copied from the bound statute book, not from any loose sheet. The bound statute book lived in the archive, and Prynn certified archive copies on the second and fourth afternoons, by lamplight, in her own hand, and had never once in her life been hurried. The assessment records belonged to the panel, and each assessor had to certify his or her own entries. The white-haired man who had sat the rotating chair at the first sitting was away in the north until the end of the week. When every slip had come back certified and countersigned, the whole bundle had to be assembled, checked against the request line by line, and sealed in the presence of two officers of the academy.

"How long does the regulation give us?" Cael asked.

"Seven days from receipt, for a chartered institution, to produce what it's asked for." Naveth wrote a date on the last slip and underlined it. "We'll produce it on the seventh, in the last hour that's still the seventh. Every word of it will be correct."

"How long could you make it take, if you wanted?"

"Exactly that long. Not one hour longer." Naveth almost smiled. "I didn't write these rules, Cael. I've just never seen why I should hurry through them faster than they require."

Cael watched him for a while. The pad of slips grew into a small orderly stack, each one perfectly legal, each one sending a single piece of the request on a slow, honest walk round the building. He recognized the shape of it, the longer he looked, because it was the shape of the thing he had spent his whole life learning to do with his body. Vell had taught him to read a fight for its gaps before he ever threw a strike. Naveth read a regulation like that, looking past what it forbade to the honest gap between what it required and what it only permitted, and living in that space with the calm of a man who had learned long ago that it was roomier than most people believed.

"You've done this before," he said.

Naveth raised his eyes, and his long face softened for a moment into something close to fondness, the look of a man being recognized at last for a skill he had polished in obscurity for decades.

"This academy's whole way of admitting students makes paperwork disputes, lad. Late Kindlers whose families quarrel with the station. Re-certification candidates whose old schools drag their feet over a file. Larger academies that dislike losing a student to a place with worse floors and a better idea." He set his pen down. "I've been walking slowly in front of the Compact since before you Kindled. It's the most practised skill on these grounds. You happen to be the first case important enough to need all of it at once."

"Does it work?"

"It buys time. In my experience it has never once made a determined opponent go away." He picked the pen up again. "What it does is turn their timetable into yours, which is worth more than it sounds. A hurried defence loses to a patient prosecution nearly every time. Patience against patience is at least a fair contest."

"And if they're patient too?"

Naveth paused over the slips. For a moment the easy authority he had carried through the whole conversation went out of him, and what was left underneath it was plain honesty, which Cael trusted more.

"Then it's a matter of whose time runs out first, and I won't pretend to know which. The Compact has more patience than any single office inside it. But offices are made of people, and people get moved to other posts, and lose interest, and retire, and get promoted to somewhere the file stops mattering." He tapped the request. "If the person handling this on their side is a person and not simply a process, that's better news for us than it sounds. Processes never get tired. People do."

"You're hoping for a person."

"I'm hoping," said Naveth, "that whoever it is has been at this long enough to tell a quarrel worth pressing from one better left to go cold. Most people who last a long time on a quiet file come to have opinions about which is which." He squared the stack of slips against the edge of the blotter. "We'll find out whether this one does. Go to your lectures. Pass your assessment tomorrow. That's still your whole part, and it's still the part that matters most."

Cael went down the worn stair slowly, one hand on the cold wall. At the bottom he stopped and stood for a moment in the passage with the request's careful list running through his head, *assessment records to date, with assessors' names*, and understood that tomorrow's sitting would be the first one he had ever walked into knowing for certain that somebody far away had already asked to read it.

Karis was crossing the yard when he came out of the old building, with her notebook under her arm and her borrowed stool in the other hand, on her way back to the lecture wing to return it. She looked at his face once and changed direction.

"Something's happened."

He told her. It did not occur to him until afterward that he had not decided to; it was simply the next thing he did. He told her what the request asked for and how Naveth meant to answer it, and the seven days, and the last legal hour.

She listened without interrupting, and when he had finished she was quiet for a moment.

"May I note it?" she said. "That an institution has formally asked for your records. Only the fact and the date. Nothing about what's in them."

"Why?"

"Because it's an event that happened to you, and it'll change what I see in the sessions whether I note it or not. You'll be different for the next fortnight. Anyone would." She shifted the stool in her hand. "If I don't write down why, I'll be tempted to explain it some other way later, and the other way will be wrong."

He thought about the sixth clause, and the struck line with the correction beside it, and the honest care in the question.

"Note it," he said.

She nodded, and did not open the notebook there in the yard, where anyone might see. She only said, "Thank you. I'll show you the line," and went on toward the lecture wing with the stool.

He watched her go across the yard with the stool held out a little from her side, and found that the list in his head had grown quieter. It had not gone. It had only, for the first time since the provost turned the sheet round on the blotter, stopped being something he was carrying alone.

# Chapter 14 — The Last Legal Hour

The second sitting looked exactly like the first, and that was the most frightening thing about it.

The training hall at first bell had been swept again, the six sections rubbed back to pale ghosts and a single fresh ring drawn at the centre. Three chairs stood at the far end with the same chalk ticks beside their legs and the same small table with its inkwell in front of the middle one. Quenna sat in the middle with her slate. Wray sat on her left in the brown coat buttoned to the throat. The rotating seat this month was a broad-faced woman Cael had seen only once, crossing the movement floor with a pointer under her arm, and she looked at him as he came in with a pleasant, unhurried interest, as if he were a page she had been told would be worth reading.

Hobb was in the ring. Wray bent to his ear, said something, and went back to her chair, and Hobb settled his feet and waited, exactly as before, a broad, patient shape against the grey light from the high windows.

Everything was the same. Cael knew, walking to his mark, that the only thing that had changed was in his own head, and that it was enormous. Last month he had imagined a reader under a lamp at the end of the road, a careful clerk he had built himself out of officials he remembered, and he had shaped every movement for that imagined reader. Yesterday afternoon, in Naveth's office, the reader had stopped being imaginary. *Assessment records to date, with assessors' names.* Whatever happened in this ring this morning would be copied out by three certifying hands, sealed in the presence of two officers, and carried down the hill to somebody who had asked for it by name.

He set his feet and chose, before anything moved, exactly as Lira had taught him.

"Framework," he told the panel. "Four passes, same structure, varied angle."

He ran it at panel speed. Hobb struck and he read the strike off Hobb's hips and shoulders and stepped into the space beside it, and the next, and the next. Wray did not break the count this time. Instead, on the third pass, she raised two fingers instead of one, and Hobb came in from the left twice in succession, the second strike following the first so closely that the read for it began before the first had finished. Cael felt the framework stretch to cover it, a half-beat of reading laid over another half-beat, and he moved at the pace he had chosen and not a finger faster, and nothing landed. He finished the pass and came back to his mark, breathing evenly, and three pens moved at the end of the hall.

The surface read went as it had before. Four calls, the last with his eyes closed, every one of them right. When he named the fourth, Hobb's weight going forward onto the ball of his left foot, Hobb did not huff this time. He only nodded very slightly, as if confirming something he had decided a month ago, and waited for his next instruction.

The panel went to the far wall and talked for less time than last month. When they came back, Quenna glanced to her left, and Wray spoke as if reading the line off a gauge.

"As previously recorded. No safety concerns."

Quenna wrote it down word for word.

Cael stood in the ring and counted the words without meaning to. Last month there had been seven. This month there were six. Wray had looked at a second sitting and found nothing in it that needed a new word, and he understood, standing there, that this was the highest compliment her dialect could offer. Nothing new to write was the best thing a record could say about you, when the person reading it was looking for something new.

"Assessment satisfied," said Quenna.

Wray rose, nodded to him once, and went out with Hobb a step behind her, and once again Cael watched the broad back go through the door without having found the moment he was waiting for. He let it go. The moment would come. He was beginning to suspect it would come at a time of its own choosing, and that Hobb, of all people, would not mind waiting for it.

Quenna stayed, as she had stayed last month. She turned her slate face down on the little table and waited until the door had closed behind the broad-faced woman.

"The first sitting proved you were real," she said, in the low voice that was not for the record. "This one proves you're the same. That's what *consistent* means to someone reading a file. Once is an event. Twice is a pattern." She looked at him. "You know where this record is going."

"Down the hill. In a bundle."

"In a bundle, in seven days, in my best handwriting." She stood and picked up the slate. "You did well this morning. You did exactly the same as last time, and you made it look as though doing the same thing twice was the easiest thing in the world." She paused at the door. "It isn't. The second time is always harder, because the first time you're only afraid of the room."

She went out. He stood in the swept ring a moment longer and found that she was right, and that he had not known it until she said it.

The sitting was closed, and nothing she could have written about it was anything she had been allowed to see, and she seemed to have decided that the right thing to do with a closed door was to stand beside it and not look through the keyhole.

"Satisfied," he told her, because she had not asked.

She nodded once. "Good." Then, after a moment: "Your shoulders were up when you went in. They're down now. I'm not writing that down. I only noticed."

"You notice everything."

"Nearly everything," said Karis. "I'm working on the rest."

The days after the request arrived passed, on the surface, as Greyvane's days always passed. There were lectures and floor blocks and bells, barley at the seventh and lamps at the eighth, and the ordinary rhythm of an institution that had absorbed far stranger interruptions than one records request in its sixty years. The rumor market had largely moved on. The current scandal involved a third-year and the disappearance of the kitchen's honey, and Lira reported on it every morning with the grave delight of a historian.

Underneath, something had changed, and Cael noticed it in himself the way he would have noticed it in an opponent. That week the binder took more pages from him than any week since he had come through the gate. "I know you're not. You're worried about the reading." She leaned back. "Worry, by all means. Just don't pay for the worry before the bill comes. You'll need it later, and it doesn't come back as fast as you'd like."

He wrote that down that night, under a heading that was slowly becoming a whole section of the binder: things Quenna told him that sounded like management at the time and turned out afterward to be true.

Karis found him an hour after Quenna left, in the same corner of the archive, and did not sit across the table from him. She took the bench beside him, turned her own notebook to a fresh page, and did not say a word.

She worked. He could hear her pen going in its long, even passages, and the small pause and click when she capped it and uncapped it again, and the turning of a page. She did not look at what he was writing. She did not ask him what was wrong. When she spoke at last, it was not about the request.

"You're making the wrong kind of list." She kept her eyes on her own page. "I can see it from here. Your handwriting gets smaller when you're counting dangers instead of findings."

"I hadn't noticed that about my own handwriting."

"I have a page on it." She turned her notebook a little toward him, and he saw at the top of a sheet, in her small upright hand, *Handwriting, size of, under load*, and a column of dates. "Clause six. You're entitled to read it."

He read it. "Is there anything you don't keep a page on?"

"Very little." She uncapped her pen again. "For what it's worth, as a matter of research, this is a good problem to have. An institution asking formally for your records is a data point. It means you matter enough now to have a procedure. Most people never do."

"That's a strange sort of comfort."

"It's the only sort I really have." She looked at him then, and something in her face softened the clinical edge of what she had just said. "I don't do reassurance well. I do documentation." She paused. "But in the ordinary sense as well, for what that's worth: Naveth wouldn't be pouring this much of his own patience into papers he thought were a lost cause."

She went back to her page. He went back to his, and after a while he noticed that his letters had grown again, a little, back toward their usual size.

On the fifth day Lira took him down the hill, because she said that a person who had lived in a place for a month and never seen the town below it was not living there but visiting, and that she had not walked three days from Ardenmere to watch him visit.

He had never been down. There had always been something to do above the wall, and he had found, if he was honest, that he did not much want to go. The hollow circle opened every door Brom's crest opened, including the gate, and nobody would have stopped him. He simply had not gone, and he had not examined why.

The road down was steeper than it looked from the top, and the town at the bottom was smaller than it looked from anywhere. There was one street worth walking and one worth avoiding, as Lira had reported in the first week, a mill with its great slow wheel groaning over the race, a smithy with its door open and its fire showing, and a draper's with a narrow counter at the back where the post came and went. The man at the counter knew Lira by name already and shook his head before she asked.

"Nothing from Denvash," he said. "Not yet."

"It's early," said Lira, to Cael, not to the man. "He writes slowly. You told me so yourself."

"I know."

"And he'll have to write about the assessment, and the form, and the girl who argued in the back room all afternoon, and he'll want to get every word right." She bumped his shoulder. "He'll take a month just choosing the paper."

Cael laughed, and found that he meant it, and they went on down the street.

The baker at the bottom of the hill was exactly as rude as promised. He looked at Lira's coin as if it had insulted his mother, looked at Cael's tag as if it had insulted him personally, and wrapped a dark round loaf in a cloth with such contempt that Lira went out of the shop glowing.

"He's wonderful," she said. "He hates everyone exactly the same. It's the most honest shop in the valley."

The building was small, two storeys of the same grey stone as everything else in the town, with a narrow door and a single window and nothing over the door but a plain board. He knew what it was before he read the board. *Registered Arbiter Station. Certification and Evaluation.* The orientation instructor had told the new students, with his pointer tapping on the floor, that this was where they would come down the hill to be measured, and where the observer children would come down the hill to be Kindled.

A family was waiting outside it. A man and a woman in their good coats, the kind kept for weddings and funerals, and between them a boy of fourteen or so, scrubbed pink, with his hair flattened down with water and his hands clasped in front of him so tightly that Cael could see the whiteness of the knuckles from across the street. The woman kept touching the boy's shoulder, lightly, every few breaths, as if to make sure he was still there.

Cael stood on the corner and did not move.

He remembered a scrubbed floor in Denvash, and a clerk's gentle voice, and Hesk's hand on his own shoulder, warm and heavy and steady, in exactly that place. He remembered the silence that had gone on too long, and the word that had come after it. For a moment the whole of it was very near, as near as the boy across the street, and he waited for the old cold feeling that came with it.

It came, a little. It did not stay. What stayed, instead, was the boy's face, and the mother's hand, and the plain hope in all three of them, and he found that what he mostly wanted was for that boy's morning to be ordinary. That was all. He wanted somebody to come out of that narrow door in an hour with a Path and a rank and a first declaration, an ordinary receipt for an ordinary afternoon, and go home with his parents and eat too much at supper.

"Cael," said Lira quietly.

"I'm all right."

"I know you are. I just wanted you to hear your name." She did not take his arm or hurry him. She stood beside him on the corner with the loaf under her arm until he was ready, and then they walked back up the hill together.

Halfway up, he looked back once. The family had gone inside. The door was shut. Oona would come down this road in winter, with her slate under her arm and her own hands clasped, and he would be at the top of it, waiting. He decided, on the steep part of the road with the wind in his face, that whatever else he was doing in winter, he would be there.

"Sit," she said. "You'll read it to me."

"Read it?"

"Aloud. Slowly. While I write." She set a sheet of heavy paper square to the table's edge, uncapped a pen, and dipped it. "A copy that's certified has to be exact. Every word, every comma, nothing added and nothing lost. My eyes are seventy-odd years old and they've read this half page so often that they'd happily tell me what it says without looking. Ears are more honest. Go on."

So he read it to her, the provision he had now read five times on his own, one phrase at a time, waiting at each comma while her pen caught up. *A candidate without a standard classification issued by an Arbiter.* The pen scratched. *May be enrolled for academic exposure and supervised training.* The pen scratched. She wrote in a small, exact, unhurried hand, and the only other sounds in the archive were the lamp and the cold settling of old paper on the shelves behind them.

Halfway through he read *the panel's finding shall be entered*, and she stopped him with one raised finger.

"Again."

He looked at the book. "The panel's finding shall be entered in the candidate's record."

She looked at him over her spectacles for a long moment. "You read *shall*."

"It says *shall*."

"It does. Most people who read this to me say *may*, because they expect a provision to be polite. It isn't. It's a command." She dipped her pen again. "You read what's written, not what you expect. That's rarer than you'd think. Go on."

He went on. When he reached the end she read her copy back against the book herself, silently, word by word, with one finger moving down each, and then she signed the certification at the bottom in the same small hand and blotted it.

"How many of these have you done?" he asked.

"Certified copies of that half page?" She considered. "Over sixty years, perhaps forty. Most of them for nothing. A family who wanted to know whether a younger son might sit in on lectures. "And this one?"

Prynn looked at the copy drying on the table, and then at him.

"This one is for you," she said. "That isn't nothing."

The slips had all come back. Each one had made its slow, legal walk around the grounds and returned certified and countersigned. Prynn's copy of the provision lay on top, in her small exact hand, and Naveth had read the pencilled note on its cover twice with great satisfaction before rubbing it out. The white-haired man had come back from the north and certified his entries in the hall at the first bell, on his way to breakfast. "Enrollment documentation, complete."

"Present."

"Citation of the provision."

"Present, certified by the archive."

"Assessment records to date, with the names of the assessors."

"Two sittings. Present, certified by all three seats."

The transmittal had a small ruled box near the top marked *Requester's routing annotation, to be copied exactly*. A clerk had copied into it, very neatly, a short string of letters and numbers broken by a slash, taken from the margin of the Compact's own cover sheet, where some office at the other end had stamped it when the request was first logged. Cael had seen codes in that shape before, on a handful of official papers over the last year, and he knew what they were for. They told an office how urgently to treat a thing. The Compact's cover sheet, which Naveth had let him copy, carried a printed key at its foot listing the priority tiers in use, each with its code and a word or two of meaning.

He laid the transmittal beside his copy of the key and went down the list. The request itself was marked as a standard monitoring matter, and the key had a code for that. The code in the routing box was not that code. It was not any of the codes. It was formatted like them, the same length and the same slash, and it did not correspond to a single line in the key.

He took it to Naveth the next morning, mostly out of the habit that made him bring everything to someone who knew more about institutions than he did.

Naveth studied the string for perhaps ten seconds. He turned the page sideways once, as if a different angle might settle it, and set it back on the blotter with the ease of a man who had met more odd marks on official paper than he could remember and had long ago stopped wondering about most of them.

"Registry systems make noise," he said. "Old ones worst of all, and the Compact's routing was old before either of us was born. Half the codes on the papers that cross this desk correspond to nothing in any key I've been sent. Retired categories. Sub-processes nobody's bothered to close. Some clerk's slip of the pen thirty years ago that went into a template and got copied forward ever since." He slid the transmittal back across the desk. "If it meant something, somebody at their end would have corrected it. Nobody has. I wouldn't spend archive hours chasing it."

Cael let it go. Naveth's reading was reasonable, and there were at least four other things on his mind that mattered more than a few characters in a box. He wrote it in the binder anyway. That habit had outlasted every attempt he had ever made to be more selective about what earned a line. He gave it one line, with no theory attached, in the back pages he had started keeping for things that were probably nothing.

*Routing code on the transmittal doesn't match any tier in their own key. Naveth says noise. Writing it down because writing things down is cheaper than being wrong about which ones mattered.*

---

He asked Prynn two days later anyway, because Prynn's whole working life was built around noticing things that did not fit their own filing, and a second opinion cost him nothing but a cup of terrible tea.

She held the transmittal out at the full length of her arm, which was how she held anything she meant to read properly, and she looked at the routing box for a good deal longer than Naveth had.

"I don't know the format," she said at last. "That isn't unusual by itself. The Compact changes its internal codes more often than it changes anything a student would ever see, and most of the changes never reach any key I'm allowed to hold." She laid the page on the desk between them and put one finger beside the box, not on it, as if the ink might be wet. "If it were something made for your file in particular, I'd expect it to match the kind of record it travels with. Provision files have their own codes. I've seen those. This isn't one."

"So it means nothing."

"Or it means something that neither of us has the reference to read." She said it exactly, with no weight added and none taken away, the way she said everything. She handed the page back. "Keep the page. Don't build anything on it."

He kept the page. He built nothing on it. He put the whole conversation in the back section with the rest of the things that were probably nothing, and reminded himself, not quite convinced, that *probably nothing* and *nothing* were not the same, and that the gap between them was worth keeping in mind even when there was nothing to be done about it.

---

That evening the three of them took the wall outside the residence wing again, and while the sky over the hill town went from gold to grey he thought about the bundle.

It would be on a road by now, wrapped in oiled cloth, in a courier's bag. It would reach a desk somewhere, and somebody would cut the string, and read Wray's six words and Quenna's two, and the rotating seat's long careful paragraph about an anticipation that thinned against faster opponents.

"They've known my name ever since Denvash," he said, mostly to himself. "Every single day. Nothing about that changed this month."

"So what did change?" asked Lira.

He sat with the question before he answered, because it deserved that.

"Where I am," he said. "Denvash never had walls round it that belonged to anybody but Denvash. The circuit was open ground; nobody owned it, not really, not in a way that let them knock. This is the first place I've ever stood that belongs to an institution they can make demands of. They didn't find me. They always knew where I was. I've just finally stood somewhere with a door they're allowed to knock on."

Brom had been quiet, watching the last colour go out of the sky with the patience he gave most things now. He spoke without turning his head.

"When I was small, my family had a steward who handled the debts. The ones owed to us, I mean." He considered the hill town's first lamps. "He used to say that a man who sends a letter before he sends a cart expects to be paid in the end, and wants to be paid without trouble. He said the ones who worried him were the ones who sent the cart first."

"Which is this?" asked Lira.

"A letter." Brom shrugged, carefully, the small economical movement Cael had learned meant the sentence underneath it had been considered for a long time. "Somebody who writes for paper first wants it done by the book. That means there's a book. And Cael reads books."

Lira snorted, and then laughed, and bumped Brom's knee with hers. "That's the most comforting thing you've ever said, and it was about debt collection."

"Velmere was very good at debt collection," said Brom, without rancour. "It's one of the few things I learned there that I still use."

Cael laughed too, and sat between them in the dusk for a long while without speaking. Then he got the binder out, while there was still enough light to see by, and wrote the entry slowly, in the square hand he kept for things that would last, because a thing that was going to stay written deserved to be written exactly, even when exactness made it no easier to read.

*They know where I am. They always knew where I was. What's changed is that where I am now has walls they think they own.*

He closed the binder. Below them, in the hill town, the lamps came on one by one along the single street worth walking, and somewhere beyond the town a courier's horse was carrying three certified signatures and two sittings' worth of records down a road Cael would never see the end of, toward a desk he would never sit at, to be read by someone who had asked.

Karis climbed the standings the way water climbs a stair, without any visible effort and without stopping.

She had full standing as a transfer, and a scheduled bout every few days like anyone else, and in her first fortnight on the board she fought three of them. She won all three, none in more than three exchanges, and after each one the clerk who kept the board climbed his little ladder with his chalk and his rag and moved her name up a section. The schedule paired the top two of each cohort at the turn of the half-term. Everybody at Greyvane could count, and everybody knew what the next pairing would be some days before it went up on the board.

Cael did not spend those days watching her. He spent them, mostly, on the floor of Brom's room after the eighth bell, with his left forearm braced across his chest.

"Again," said Brom.

The road was a long way behind them now, and the work had moved indoors with them. Brom knelt with his left palm on Cael's forearm, Iron Skin coming up under the skin like frost forming on a pane, and leaned, slow and steady, the weight arriving the way a tide arrives. Cael waited for the force to settle into the bone, chose its road before it could choose its own, out, back along the line it came in, and reached.

He had been catching a quarter of the push for a month. It had become almost reliable; he caught it four times in ten, sometimes five, and the price had stayed exactly the same through all of it, a hot bar laid across the collarbones and two breaths he could not take. He had begun to think of the quarter as a floor he stood on. Tonight he was trying to find out whether it was also a ceiling.

The first six tries went as they usually went. Two caught at a quarter. Four did nothing. On the seventh he reached for more than he had chosen, greedily, a fraction too late, and the gathered force slid sideways off the road he had laid for it and went down into his left wrist. It did not hurt at once. Then it hurt a great deal, a deep, hot throb in the joint, and he sat back on his heels and held the wrist against his chest while Brom waited.

"Joint," said Brom.

"Wrist." Cael flexed the fingers, carefully. They moved. "I tried to take more than I'd decided to take. It went somewhere I hadn't decided."

"So decide more."

"That's the whole problem."

"No," said Brom, who had said this before, on a hillside in the pine needles, and seemed to enjoy saying it again. "That's the whole answer. Decide first, and decide bigger. Again."

The eighth try did nothing at all.

On the ninth, Cael did something he had not tried before. He did not reach harder. He chose a wider road. Before the force arrived he laid the line back out along Brom's arm and imagined it twice as broad, a channel cut for twice the water, and only then, when the push had settled fully into the bone, did he reach. The fragment gathered. It gathered further than it had ever gathered for him, a long, tightening pull that ran from his forearm up into his chest, and for a moment he held half of Brom's push in him like a breath he had taken too deep. And then the bill came, and it was not the same bill.

The second page set out the record. It did that accurately too. It noted the dates of two closed sittings and the finding of each, *assessment satisfied*, which the public calendar had recorded. It noted that the candidate had been seen at the rail of every floor in the building, filling a binder, and had been present at the standings bouts of the cohort's leading students. It said, without any heat at all, that whatever the candidate had demonstrated to the panel in closed session, it could not have been the practice of a child without a Path, because the panel had found it consistent and observable and the provision did not reward nothing. And then it drew its conclusion, in a sentence Cael read three times.

*The petitioner therefore submits that the candidate is evidently a practitioner of some classification, which the faculty has elected not to disclose, and that his enrollment under a provision designed for those without one places him outside the framework by which every other student's standing is measured.*

The third page made the request. It asked that the candidate's supervised floor access under the provision be suspended pending a review of his enrollment; or, in the alternative, that the candidate be assessed under the same framework as students of full standing, in public, so that the academy's standings might continue to mean what every enrolled student had been told they meant. And then, beneath it, in a last paragraph that Cael could hear in Edran's voice as clearly as if he had been standing at the rail, the real grievance, where petitions always put it.

*The petitioner has earned his standing at this academy in public, over three years, under its framework, and does not dispute any result that framework has produced. He asks only whether the framework is still the measure. If a student may stand outside it and be found satisfactory, then every student who has met it honestly is entitled to know by what measure they now stand.*

It was signed at the bottom of the third page, *Edran*, in the same hand that did not shake, with the date.

Somebody small pushed in at his elbow. He looked down, and it was Oona, with her slate under her arm and her tag on its string, reading the first page with her lips moving.

"Is that about you?"

"Yes."

She read on. She was a slow and thorough reader, and he waited for her, and the crowd behind them waited too, though it pretended not to. When she reached the bottom of the third page she frowned at it with her whole face.

"Is it true?"

He thought about it. He had told her he would not lie to her, and he found, standing there with half the academy at his back, that the promise was easier to keep than he had feared, because the honest answer was the simplest one.

"Nearly all of it."

"Which part isn't?"

He put one finger beside the sentence on the second page, not on it, as Prynn had put her finger beside the routing code.

"That part. Where he thinks the faculty is hiding what my Path is."

Oona read the sentence again. "Aren't they?"

"No," said Cael. "There isn't one to hide."

Oona looked at the sentence for a long moment, and then up at him, and he watched her work it through, the way she had worked through *shattered* in the taxonomy lecture: where it went, why it wasn't on the chart.

"Oh," she said at last. "Then he's asking the wrong question."

"No. He's asking a fair question." Cael looked at the three pages, at the careful hand and the brass tacks and the signature at the bottom. "He's only got one fact wrong in the middle of it. Everything else is right."

"Will you tell him?"

The first bell began to ring, slow and unhurried, from somewhere behind the lecture wing. The crowd began, reluctantly, to come apart round them.

Cael did not answer her straight away. He read the conclusion on the second page one more time, the way he had once read a category line three times over a cup of tea going cold, and found that he did not resent a word of it. Edran had asked in the open, in the yard, in front of a dozen people. Now he had asked again, in ink, in front of everyone, with his own name signed at the bottom, by the book and in daylight. It was the most honest thing anyone at Greyvane had done about him, apart from a girl who had walked into an archive to ask permission. It was right about the provision, and right about the children, and right about the board, and right about him in every particular but one.

The one was the only one that mattered, and nobody had ever written it down.

"I think I'll have to," he said.

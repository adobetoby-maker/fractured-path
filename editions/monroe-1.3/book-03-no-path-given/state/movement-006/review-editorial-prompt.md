# Chapter 40 — Contact Only

Lira laid down the terms at breakfast on the Tuesday, between the porridge and the bread, counting them off on her fingers as she counted the rumor market.

"One," she said. "Contact only. Your hand on the thing, not near it, not pointed at it, not waved at it from across a room because you've decided you're clever. Two. One point. Not two, not a row, not a pattern. One, and then you stop and we look at it." She held up a third finger. "Three. On something that isn't you and isn't any of us. Wood. Stone if there's no wood. Not a floor anybody has to walk on afterward and not a wall anybody has to sleep against."

"Four," said Brom, without looking up from his barley. "Buckets."

"Four, buckets. One of water and one of sand, and Brom holds the sand." Lira looked down the table at Karis, who had stopped eating to listen and had both hands flat on either side of her bowl. "And five is yours, if you want it. You don't have to come."

"I'd like to come," said Karis. "If the terms allow it."

"I've just made the terms. They allow it."

"Then I'd like to add one." Karis turned her spoon over and laid it down square to the edge of the table. "The second terms are for sessions where I'm the source. This isn't that; it's his, and I'll only be watching. So I'd like it understood that I write nothing down. Not conditions, not results, not the hour. Whatever happens goes in the binder and nowhere else." She looked at Cael. "My ledger already has one line in it that I'd rather it didn't have. I don't want to give it company."

Cael thought of the supplementary entry she had shown him on the Sunday, set under the barley clause in her small upright hand, and of the words *Source: K.* sitting on the same page as *acquisition, directed*. He had not asked her to change it, and she had not offered. It was the truth, and she kept the truth even when it was inconvenient.

"Nothing written," he said. "Agreed."

"And I'll count," said Karis. "Out loud. Counting isn't writing."

"It's barely different," said Lira.

"It's entirely different. One of them stays in the room." Karis picked her spoon back up. "Where will you find something that isn't any of us?"

Brom swallowed his barley.

"The kitchen woodpile," he said. "There's a stack behind the bakehouse door the cook keeps for the ovens. Split oak, mostly, and a few old fence posts somebody brought up from the town." He considered his spoon. "She calls it kindling."

Lira put her face in her hands.

"I didn't name it," said Brom, with great dignity. "I only know where it is."

Cael laughed, and found that he could not stop for a moment, and that it was the first time since the formal yard he had laughed at anything without first checking whether he was allowed to. Lira looked at him through her fingers, and something in her shoulders let go a little, and she took his bread.

He went to his supervised session in section four at the fifth bell with the warmth sitting low in his chest. He ran the framework for Quenna at the chalk and read the post through felt with Brom leaning on the far side, and nothing in him moved toward the new one. It sat where it had sat since Thursday, faintly and steadily warm, like a stone in a wall that has been in the sun all afternoon, and it did nothing at all, and he did not ask it to. Quenna signed the strip and handed it to him and did not mention the match, and he did not mention it either. Her question lay between them on the chalk like a coin neither of them would pick up.

---

The quiet room had not changed. It was the smaller of the two rooms off the training hall, whitewashed to the ceiling, with a scrubbed board floor and one narrow window high on the end wall, and the slate on the nail beside the door still said *K. — declaration practice* in Karis's careful hand, because the sixth bell was still hers. Twenty-two times he had stood at the far end of it and reached for something that would not come. He stood in the doorway now and found that his heart was beating as if he had run up the stair, and he made himself notice that, and name it, and go in anyway.

Brom had found the post. It was a length of old oak about as long as a man's forearm, grey with weather on one side and split along the grain on the other, and he had stood it upright in an iron bucket of sand from the yard, so that it rose out of the sand like a stump out of snow. The water bucket stood beside it. Lira had set both in the middle of the floor, well away from every wall, and had then moved them a pace further from the window without explaining why.

"Stand where you can reach it," she said. "Right hand. Not the arm."

He had not meant to use the arm. The burn under the loose linen had stopped beating and begun, over the last two days, to itch, which Wray had said was the good sign and Lira had said was the worst part. He kept it close against his side and knelt by the bucket so that the top of the post was level with his chest.

Karis stood three paces off, by the end wall, with her hands at her sides and no notebook anywhere on her. Brom stood at the sand bucket with a scoop. Lira stood by the water. Nobody spoke, and the room was very quiet, the noise of the training hall coming through the narrow door like weather through a shut window.

"Tell us what you mean to do," said Karis. "Before you do it."

He had thought about this for three days, in between the misfires and the columns and the clean mornings, and he had decided that it ought to be said out loud, the whole of it, in order, so that afterward there would be something to measure what happened against.

"I'm going to put my palm flat on the top of the post," he said. "I'm going to turn toward the new one, the way I turn toward the others, without reaching. Then I'm going to ask it for one point, the size of my thumbnail, at the middle of my palm, and let it go. And then I'm going to take my hand away and look."

"The size of a thumbnail," said Lira.

"That's what I mean. I don't know what it'll do."

"Nobody does," said Karis. "That's why we're here."

He put his right palm flat on the top of the post.

The wood was cold and dry and rough, the grain running under his hand in long low ridges, and a splinter caught at the heel of his thumb and he ignored it. He turned inward toward the warmth. It was there at once, low and patient and faintly familiar, and there was something in it that he had begun to think of, since the Sunday, as the feel of her: small commitments kept exactly, and no fuss made about any of them. He did not reach for it. He only attended, as you look at a coin in your palm before you decide to spend it, and then he asked it, as plainly as he knew how to ask anything inside himself, for one small point under the middle of his hand.

Nothing glowed.

He had expected a coin of light under his palm, a warmth climbing into his skin as Karis's points warmed the air above her hand, something he could feel gathering and hold for a count before he let it go. There was none of that. There was no gathering at all, and no holding, and no light that he could see through the cracks between his fingers. There was only a single instant, so short he nearly missed it, when something in him that had been warm was suddenly not warm, as a candle is not lit and then is, with nothing in between. Then it was over, and his hand was lying on a post, and he did not know whether anything had happened.

He took his hand away.

Under it, in the middle of the grey oak, there was a ring of char about the size of a copper coin, black at the centre and brown at its edges, and from the centre of the ring a thread of smoke was going up, thin and pale, straight as a plumb line in the still air of the room. As he watched, the black centre showed a bead of yellow, then a small true flame no bigger than a barleycorn. The flame was not dim, not faint, nothing like Karis's patient coins; it was an ordinary small fire, the colour of any candle, and it was eating along the grain of the wood toward the split edge as if it had somewhere to be.

"That's bigger than a thumbnail," said Lira.

"Put it out," said Cael.

Brom put it out. He did it with one scoop of sand, without hurry, as he did everything, and then pressed the sand down with the flat of the scoop and held it there for a count of five, and when he lifted the scoop there was a grey crust over the char and no smoke at all.

Then the cost came, and it came in a way he had not prepared for, because none of the others had ever sent their bill like this.

It began under his breastbone, a sudden cold drop, the feeling of putting a foot down in the dark for a stair that is not there. He caught his breath on it before he could stop himself. Then the cold ran out from his chest and down his right arm, into the hand that had been on the post, so that by the time he looked at the hand the fingertips had gone pale and faintly grey and his palm felt as if he had held it in the pump trough. It was not pain. It was more like being emptied of something he had not known he was full of, all at once and without any warning. And the warmth that had sat in him since Thursday, so faintly and steadily that he had stopped noticing it, was gone. Where it had been there was the old space, the shape he had learned in the quiet room by failing to fill it, and it was cold.

"Cael," said Karis.

He looked up. She had not moved from the end wall, but she was watching his face with all of her attention, as she watched a point in its last third of a breath.

"Your breath caught," she said. "When the smoke started. Not before."

"It costs," he said. His voice was steadier than he felt. "Not like the others. It comes after. Cold, here," and he touched his breastbone with the grey fingers, "and down the arm into the hand. And it's gone. The warm. I can't find it."

Lira had come round the bucket and taken his right hand between both of hers without asking, and was chafing it hard, as you chafe the hands of somebody who has come in from snow. She was looking at him and not at the post.

"I didn't see anything," she said. "You know that? I was watching your face the whole time, and I didn't see anything at all. You looked like you were thinking about your dinner."

"I know."

"I'd never have known." She kept chafing. "Somebody on a bench would never know. Somebody at a panel would never know. That's the thing I don't like."

Karis came forward then, slowly, as if the room had a chalk line in it nobody had drawn. She stopped at the post and looked at it, and then at Brom.

"May I touch it?"

Brom lifted the scoop. Karis brushed the grey crust of sand away with two fingers and bent over the ring of char, close, with the absolute attention she gave a timing that did not agree with anything she had written before. She did not touch the black. She held her hand flat above it, a finger's width off, and then laid one fingertip on the oak beside the ring, and then on the ring's brown edge, and took it away.

"That isn't my point," she said.

Nobody answered her. She was not talking to any of them.

"My point is a held heat. I lay it and it's mine, and it stays as long as I hold it, and it dies when I let go. That's the lag you timed, the dying." She straightened. "There's no point here. There was never a point here. You didn't lay anything. There's a fire in the wood, and the wood was keeping it, and it would have gone on keeping it until it ran out of wood." She looked at him, and her face was doing the thing it had done in the formal yard, the researcher arriving first and something else coming up behind her. "It doesn't behave like fire at all. Not like mine. Mine never catches anything unless I make it."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know yet," said Karis. "Ask me again when I've seen it twice."

---

He could not do it twice, not for some time.

When the cold had gone out of his hand, which took longer than he liked, he put the palm back on the post a finger's width from the crusted ring and turned inward, and there was nothing to turn toward. The space was there, but it was empty and cold, the way a hearth is cold the morning after, and asking it for anything was like asking an empty cup to pour.

"It won't," he said.

"Then wait," said Karis. "I'll count."

She counted aloud, slowly, in her even voice, the numbers going up through the quiet room like a clock with nothing else to do. Lira sat down on the scrubbed boards with her back against the whitewash. Brom leaned on the scoop. Cael knelt with his hand on the cold oak and attended, and at sixty there was nothing, and at eighty nothing, and somewhere after ninety he felt it come back. It did not arrive all at once. It crept in from the edges of the empty space, as warmth comes back into a room after a door has been opened and shut, until it was faintly and steadily there again, low in his chest, a stone in a wall in the evening.

"Now," he said.

"A hundred and four," said Karis. "Write that in yours. A hundred and four, the first time. It might change."

This time he did what he had learned to do with Compression on Brom's floor, which was the only thing he had learned in his whole life about doing something dangerous small. He chose the size first. Before he turned inward at all he fixed it, a thumbnail and not a hair more, as you might decide how much of a loaf to cut before you lift the knife, and he held the size in his mind as if it were a mark scored in wood. Then he turned, and asked, and let go.

The same instant came, the same candle that was not lit and then was. He took his hand away.

The ring of char was smaller. It was still larger than his thumbnail, nearer the size of a fingertip pressed flat, but it was smaller, and the flame that came up in the middle of it was a bead and not a barleycorn, and Brom sanded it before it could travel. The cold came after, under the breastbone and down the arm, exactly as before, but he had braced for it this time and it did not catch his breath.

"Smaller," said Lira, from the floor.

"I chose it smaller," said Cael. "It went most of the way."

"Choose the size first," said Lira. "You write that every week. I thought it was only for the other one."

"So did I."

He waited out the count again, a hundred and nine by Karis's tally, and then they tried the thing Karis had asked for while they waited. He held his palm a finger's width above the oak, not touching, and asked, and nothing happened at all; the warmth did not even stir, as if the question had been put in a language it did not speak. He lowered his hand until there was a hair's gap between skin and grain, so little that Brom had to kneel and squint along the post to be sure of it, and asked again. The candle lit, and the cold came, and when he took his hand away there was a ring on the wood a hair's breadth below where his palm had been.

"Contact," said Karis softly. "Or close enough to it that the difference wouldn't show on any instrument I know of. Not a finger's width. A hair."

"Contact-adjacent," said Cael. "That's what the notice said."

"Then the notice was right." She was quiet a moment, looking at the three crusted rings on the grey oak. "May I say what I think it is? It isn't a finding. It's only a better word than the one I had this morning."

"Say it."

"You haven't got fire," said Karis. "You've got a spark. A spark isn't a fire; it's what you strike to start one. And everything after the spark belongs to whatever you struck it on." She touched the oak beside the nearest ring. "That's why it isn't held. That's why there's no lag and nothing to let go of. You aren't keeping anything. You start it, and in the same moment you're finished with it, and the wood does the rest because that's what wood does."

Brom looked at the post.

"A spark without a flint," he said.

"Without a flint, or a steel, or a tinderbox. Only a hand." Karis looked at Cael's right hand, which was grey at the fingertips again. "And it costs you, the way striking costs. All at once, and after. Mine costs me at the end of a long hold, when I'm tired. Yours costs you every single time, and nobody looking would ever know."

He put the binder on his knee on the scrubbed floor and wrote it, and because Karis had asked to write nothing, he read it aloud as he wrote, so that she would hear what had gone into the only record there was.

*Tuesday, week seventeen. Quiet room, sixth bell. L., B., K. present, K. writing nothing. The new one, first deliberate use. Contact only, right palm, an oak post in sand. It doesn't gather and it doesn't glow. One instant, and then the wood is burning, an ordinary small fire, which keeps going until it's put out. Size: the first came bigger than I asked. Choose the size first; the second came most of the way. A finger's width off: nothing. A hair off: yes. Cost, every time, after: a cold drop under the breastbone, cold down the arm into the hand, fingertips grey, and the warmth gone until it comes back, about a hundred count. Watchers can't see it. L. watched my face and saw nothing.*

He stopped there, and then went on, because it was hers and she ought to hear him write it.

*K.: "You haven't got fire. You've got a spark. Everything after the spark belongs to whatever you struck it on." First word for it that fits. Not the last, she says.*

"Not the last," said Karis. "I'll want another one before I'm satisfied. I generally do."

---

On the Wednesday and the Thursday they went back to the quiet room at the sixth bell, and on both days he did nothing but choose sizes.

He came to understand, over those two afternoons, that this was going to be the whole of the work for a long while, and that it was the same work he had done with every fragment he had ever been handed: not finding out what it could do, which was the easy part and could be done in an hour, but finding out what he could make it do on purpose, every time, with nobody holding his hand. The Wind framework had taken him the better part of a year in Ardenmere before it would stay at the speed he asked for. Compression had taken him the whole of the autumn to get from a quarter to a slow push. He did not expect this one to be quicker because it was newer, and it was not.

On the Wednesday he made five marks. The first two came larger than he chose and the third came smaller, so small that the bead of flame went out on its own before Brom could reach it, and he sat looking at the little ring with real pleasure until Karis pointed out that a mark too small was exactly as wrong as a mark too large. The fourth and fifth came near enough to a thumbnail that Lira, measuring them with the end of her own thumb, allowed them. Between each the warmth went away and came back, a hundred and six, a hundred and twelve, ninety-eight, and the cold came after every one of them, and by the fifth his right hand had stopped warming up between turns and stayed grey and stiff until he had chafed it against his thigh for a quarter of an hour.

"Five's too many," said Lira, on the stair afterward. "Look at you."

"I'm all right."

"You're grey round the mouth. You went grey round the mouth on the fourth." She stopped on the landing and looked at him. "You'll do three tomorrow, and that's my rule, and it's for the same reason as the other one. Because the bill comes after and you can't see it coming."

He did three on the Thursday. All three came a thumbnail across, near enough that Karis, kneeling by the post with her face a hand's width from the oak, could not choose between them. They sat in a row on the grey wood, three small black rings with brown edges, each one crusted with sand.

"The same," said Karis. "Three of three, at the size you named, with the count between." She sat back on her heels. "Consistent. Observable, if anybody were observing. That's the provision's language, isn't it?"

"That's the provision's language."

"I don't know why I said that." She looked at the three rings for a while. "Yes, I do. Because a thing that does the same thing three times at a size you've chosen isn't a mystery any more. It's a capability. I'd have written that sentence about anybody's Path." She stood up and brushed the sand off her knees. "I'm not writing it about yours. I'm only saying it out loud, once, so it stays in the room."

He had been at the Thursday session in section four an hour before, as on every Thursday, with Quenna at the chalk and Brom leaning on the post, and the warmth had sat in him through all of it like a coin in a pocket, and he had not spent it. Quenna had watched him read the post and had asked him nothing. But on the way out of the section she had stopped him at the door, with her slate under her arm, and said only, "You're pale. Sleep tonight," and looked at his hands, and then at him, and gone. She had not asked why. She had been not asking why since the first week of the term, and it had never once sounded like not caring.

He thought of her question that night at the desk by the window, the one filed nowhere. *Whether you can not do it.* He had stood with his palm on the post three times that afternoon and chosen not to, between the marks, while the warmth was there and ready, and it had stayed where it was each time, obedient as a held breath. But that was a quiet room, with four buckets of caution round him and three people he trusted. He had learned at Ardenmere, and again in the formal yard, that nothing he did in a quiet room told him anything certain about what he would do in a ring. *Not doing it with nobody pushing proves nothing,* he wrote, and boxed the line, small, his mark for *not yet*.

Oona's mother came up on the Friday coach.

Cael saw her before he knew who she was. He was crossing the yard from the archive at the end of the afternoon, with the observation notebook under his good arm, and a small woman in a dark travelling cloak was standing at the gate with a bag at her feet while the clerk ran a finger down her list. She stood very straight, as if somebody had once told her to and she had decided it was good advice. Then Oona came across the yard from the residence wing faster than he had ever seen her move, her slate held against her chest in both arms, and stopped a pace short of the woman, and the woman held out both hands, and Oona put the slate down on the cobbles to take them.

He did not go over. He went on to the main hall and stood at the board for a while, looking at the line he had looked at every morning for a month.

*Kindling assessment, observer track. Oona. Registered Arbiter Station, town. Second bell.*

She found him there an hour later, alone this time, with the slate back under her arm and her face washed and very serious.

"That was my mother," she said.

"I thought it might be."

"She came a day early. She said the coach might be late and she wasn't going to risk it." Oona looked at the line on the board, as she had looked at it for seven weeks. "She's staying at the draper's. They've a room over the shop for people's mothers. Lira told me." She tucked the slate tighter. "I told her about you. She wants to meet you. Not now. Tomorrow, at the bottom. She says she'd like to see the person I've been writing about."

"What have you been writing?"

"That's private," said Oona. She considered him. "Mostly true things. That you don't lie to me. That you have a bad arm. That you're the only other one on the track, and that it's a strange kind, and that I'm not allowed to say what kind." She frowned. "She asked me what kind, and I said I wasn't allowed, and she said good."

He found that he could not say anything for a moment.

"Are you still coming down?" said Oona. "You don't have to. You said the top of the hill. That's what you said."

"I'll come down."

"My mother on the left," said Oona, as if reading him a list. "You on the right, because of your arm. When they say my name. And then you take your hand away when I stand up, not before." She looked up at him. "That's how Hesk did it. You said."

"That's how Hesk did it."

"Then that's how it's done." She nodded once, briskly, and wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice, and did not show him. "Second bell. Don't be late. The coach was nearly late."

She went off toward the guest stair by the gate, where her mother would be waiting to walk down the hill with her before the light went. Cael stood in front of the board in the emptying hall with the observation notebook under his arm. He found that the warmth in his chest and the old cold under it were both there at once, the new thing and the oldest thing, the spark and the bench in Denvash, and that he could not tell, for a moment, which of them his hands were shaking for.

That night he wrote in the binder, in the plain hand he kept for the evenings that came out right.

*Thursday: three of three at a thumbnail, with the count between. Consistent. K. said it out loud once and wrote nothing. Q. said I was pale and didn't ask why.*

*Tomorrow, the second bell, at the grey building. Her mother on the left. Me on the right. Take the hand away when she stands up, not before.*

He looked at that for a long time. Then he added one more line, smaller.

*I haven't been inside one of those buildings since. I've been past the door of this one every week since the autumn, and I've never once looked at the bench.*

# Chapter 41 — The Bench

Lira walked with him as far as the top of the hill and then stopped.

It was a hard bright morning, the frost still white in the shadow of the gate and the town below them sharp in the cold air, every roof and chimney drawn clean against the slope as if somebody had inked it. The road went down from Greyvane's gate in long turns between stone walls, past the draper's and the bakehouse and the street worth walking, to the corner at the bottom where the grey building stood with its board over the door. From up here it looked very small. He had been looking at it from the wall every evening since the autumn and had never once let himself look at it for long.

"I'm stopping here," said Lira.

"You can come down."

"I know I can. Somebody ought to be at the top of the hill." She sat down on the low wall beside the gate with her coat round her and her knees drawn up, as she sat on the other wall at dusk. "You told her you'd be at the top of the hill. Then she asked you to come down. So now you're both, and I'm the top." She looked at him. "Go on. You'll be late, and she'll have it written on her slate that you were."

He went down.

He took the road at the pace he would have taken it to any errand, and he noticed partway down that his feet had slowed without being asked, and he made them go on at the same pace, and they did. The town was waking round him, shutters coming open, a cart backing into the bakehouse yard, the draper's boy sweeping the step under the post-counter sign. Somebody was frying onions. It was an ordinary Saturday in an ordinary town, and the closer he came to the corner the more ordinary it was, and none of it helped at all.

The grey building was two storeys of plain dressed stone with a slate roof and a single door, painted dark green and recently. The board over it said *Registered Arbiter Station. Certification and Evaluation.* in square black letters that somebody had also recently painted. The door stood open. Inside was a short flagged passage with a door at the far end, shut, and along the left-hand wall of the passage, under a window of thick old glass, a wooden bench.

He smelled it from the doorway. Soap, and the wet stone of a floor scrubbed that morning, and under it a smell of lamp oil and paper.

He stood on the step with his good hand on the door frame, and the bench in Denvash came up out of him all at once and stood in the passage in front of the real one, the way a reflection stands in a dark window with the room behind it.

He had sat on the end nearest the far door. His feet had not quite reached the floor flat, so he had rested them on their edges, and he had looked at the grain of the boards between his shoes for the whole of a morning because looking anywhere else had been worse. Hesk had sat beside him with his big careful hands on his knees and said nothing, and every so often he had shifted his weight so that the bench creaked, which Cael had understood even then was on purpose, a sound to say *I'm still here*. There had been other people on the bench, a girl with a ribbon and a boy with a cough and their families, and one after another they had been called and gone in and come out with their faces changed, and the families had stood up and made the small glad noises families make, and gone. And Cael had sat, and been called last, and Hesk's hand had come down on his shoulder, here, and stayed there until he stood.

He stood on the step of the grey building in a strange town with the soap in his nose and his heart going hard, and he could feel the exact weight of that hand.

Then the far door of the passage opened and a clerk looked out, and the morning was this morning again, and Oona and her mother were sitting on the bench.

"Yes, ma'am."

"She's written about you every week since the autumn." The woman's voice was low and level, and it did not seem to care whether the clerk heard. "She says you've never once told her anything that wasn't so."

"I try not to."

"That's rarer than people think." She looked at him a moment longer. "Thank you for coming down."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and she seemed to think that was the right answer, and turned back to the far door.

He looked at Oona instead. She was sitting perfectly upright with her hands pressed on the slate, staring at the door, and she was so frightened that her lips had gone pale, and she was not letting any of it out.

"Can I ask you something?" she said, very low, without moving her eyes.

"You can ask."

"Was it quiet? Inside you. You said it goes very quiet."

"Very quiet," said Cael. "Like a held breath. And then it isn't, and you'll know. You won't have to be told."

"And it doesn't hurt."

"It doesn't hurt. Your mother's right."

"My uncle says it's like being hit by a door."

"Your uncle's wrong," said Cael, and Oona's mother made a small sound on the far side of her that might, in another room, have been a laugh.

"Oona," he said.

He only let the hand be heavy, so that she could feel it was there.

She sat for a breath, two breaths, with both their hands on her.

Then she stood up, and they took their hands away as she rose, not before. She picked her slate up off the bench and held it against her chest in both arms, and walked down the flagged passage to the far door, and did not look back, and went in, and the door shut behind her.

Her mother let out a long breath that she seemed to have been holding since the coach.

He did not know, afterward, how long they sat there. Long enough for the pale light to move a hand's width along the flags. Long enough for the bakehouse cart to come back up the street outside with a clatter of empty trays. Not as long as he had sat in Denvash. Nowhere near.

But Denvash sat with him the whole time. It did not come as a memory of the kind you can choose to look at and then put away; it came the way weather comes, into his body, without asking. His knees remembered the bench. His hands, pressed flat on them, remembered the cold. His shoulder remembered the hand. His stomach remembered the long quiet, the quiet that had gone on past where it should have stopped, the clerk's face when he came back to the door to call Hesk in, and the low voices behind the door afterward, and the word they had for him that was not a Path. All of it went through him on a bench in a strange town while a girl he had known for one term was behind a green door finding out what she was. He let it. He had learned in the formal yard that some things are better let through than fought. He sat with his hands on his knees and his shoulder remembering a hand, and let Denvash go through him all the way, from the bench to the road, and come out the other side.

"You've done this before," she said. "Sat here."

"Not here. Somewhere like it."

"It was bad." It was not a question, and she did not make it one. "She said it was the strange kind, and that it was rare. She didn't say what came after." The woman looked at the far door. "I could see on your face what came after. You don't have to tell me."

"It's a long time ago."

"A year and some," said Oona's mother. "She told me how old you are." She was quiet a moment. "That isn't a long time ago. That's last winter, near enough, to somebody my age. You came down anyway."

He did not have anything to say to that either, and this time she did not seem to need him to.

The far door opened.

"Anchor," she said. "Anchor Path. Copper."

Her mother stood up and put both arms round her, slate and all, and held on.

It came loose, a little at a time, from the careful calm she had walked out with, until underneath it there was a thirteen-year-old girl who had been afraid for seven weeks and two days and had just been told by the oldest instrument in the world that she was exactly who she had decided to be.

"It was quiet," she said, into her mother's cloak. "It was very quiet. And then it wasn't. And I knew." She pulled back a little. "It's on the chart. Anchor's on the chart. It's in the hardening family, between Shield and Wall. I've known where it was since the first week." She looked at Cael again. "It's ordinary."

"It's ordinary," said Cael.

"I said it would be."

"You said it would be in the first week," said Cael. "You've been right about everything."

She nodded, once, briskly, as she did when a sum came out. Then she turned the slate over in her arms and looked at it and did not write anything on it, which he had never seen her do before. She was holding a small card in the same hand as the slate, pressed against its frame, the way he had once held a card of his own that said nothing anybody could read, and she did not show it to him and he did not look at it. It was hers. It was the first thing she had ever owned that nobody else would ever be allowed to read, and she held it as if she already knew that.

The clerk was writing in his ledger at the far door. Cael watched him write, and knew without having to see the page what the line would be, because he had copied out the gloss of the category on his own form three times over cold tea in the first week of the term. *Has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment.* Somewhere in the clerk's ledger, and by the end of the week in a regional book at the bottom of a long road, and on Monday morning at the intake desk at the top of the hill, Oona was being moved out of a category she no longer fitted, by the plainest procedure there was. The observer track at Greyvane had held two names since the autumn. On Monday it would hold one.

He found, sitting on the bench, that he did not mind. He found that he was glad, in a large plain way that surprised him with its size. The category had never been built for him; he had borrowed it, and everybody at the intake table had known he was borrowing it, and here was the person it had actually been built for, walking out of it on the far side the way it was meant to be walked out of, with a card in her hand and her mother's arms round her. "Both inside the terms," she said. "Contact only. Brom's post. Lira's buckets. I write nothing." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the table. "I'd only like to know what *single channel* means. It's in the notice. I'd like to know whether it's true."

Nobody refused, and at the sixth bell they were in the quiet room again with the post in its sand.

The first thing she asked was whether he could light two at once. She did not mean two in a row with the count between; she meant two together, his palm and his fingertips, one ring under the heel of his hand and another under the pads of his fingers, at the same instant. He chose both sizes, and held both in his mind, and turned inward, and asked.

One ring came, under the heel of his hand. Under his fingertips the oak was clean.

He tried it the other way round, fingertips first in his mind, and one ring came under the fingertips and nothing under the heel. He tried it a third time, wanting both equally, holding the two sizes side by side like two coins in one palm, and this time he felt the thing in him hesitate for the smallest part of an instant, as if it had been asked a question in a language it did not have the grammar for. Then one ring came, under the heel, and the cold came after it exactly as it always did, and not one hair more.

"One," said Karis softly. "Whatever you ask for, it's one." She had knelt by the post to look at the clean wood under where his fingertips had been. "It doesn't divide. It isn't weaker when you ask for two. It simply picks."

"Single channel," said Cael.

"Single channel. The notice was right again." She sat back on her heels. "I can lay sixteen. You can lay one. And yet." She did not finish the sentence, and he did not ask her to.

The second thing she asked for was paper. She had brought a sheet from the back of one of her old notebooks, blank, torn along the fold, and she laid it flat over the top of the post and held it there by its corners with her fingertips well clear of the middle.

"Through it," she said. "If you can. Your palm on the paper, the paper on the wood. I'd like to know which one it belongs to."

He laid his palm on the paper and chose the size and asked.

The paper caught. Not the wood under it; the paper. A ring of brown went through the sheet under his palm and in the middle of it a bead of flame came up and began at once to eat outward across the page, fast, as paper burns, and Karis let go of the corners and Brom put it out with a scoop of sand before it had reached the edge. When they brushed the sand away the oak beneath was barely marked, a faint brown shadow where the heat of the burning paper had warmed it and nothing more.

Karis looked at it for a long time.

"It belongs to whatever you're touching," she said. "Not to whatever's under that. Not to what you meant." She picked up the scrap of charred paper and turned it over. "That's the spark again. You strike it on the first thing your hand is on, and after that it's the paper's business, or the wood's, or whatever's there."

"No." Karis folded the burnt scrap in half, and then in half again, and did not throw it away. "It's the same word with more evidence under it. I'll have a better one some day. You'd carry it about for years thinking it was true."

"You're keeping that," she said.

"I'm not writing anything," said Karis. "That's the term. It doesn't say I can't keep a piece of paper." She tucked it into her coat. "It's the first thing he's ever lit that I gave him. I'd like to have it."

*Tuesday, week eighteen. Asked for two at once, three ways. Always one. It doesn't split and it doesn't weaken; it picks. Single channel. At the regional registry office, eight days later, a clerk with a cold that had not gone away since the autumn entered one line in a book under the heading for Greyvane Academy, from a notation sheet sent up from the town station on the Monday post.

*Kindling assessment entered. Candidate formerly enrolled under demonstration provision. Classification: Anchor Path, Copper. Category of enrollment vacated; standard enrollment to follow.*

That night the line went into the long index with all the others and was run against every standing file, as every line always was, and it matched nothing. But it was copied, the next morning, onto the short daily sheet that went to the officer who had asked to see anything the registry received about that academy, because he had asked, a fortnight before, for exactly that. It reached Coss at his borrowed desk on the second floor at a little after the third bell, between a bound volume of enforcement digests and a sheaf of drafting records tied with faded tape, and he read it twice.

He had been at the regional office nineteen days.

The briefing had taken one afternoon. Everything after it had taken the nineteen days, and he had known that it would, and had not minded. A challenge to a chartered academy's enrollment was not a request for papers. It went before a magistrate in public and into the permanent registry, and every line of it would be read aloud by people who wanted it to be wrong. So every line had to stand on something that could be produced in a room and handed across a table. He had spent nineteen days finding those somethings, one at a time, from offices that kept their papers in the order that suited them and not in the order that suited him.

The plain-face argument had been sound from the first night, at the waystation table, with its two true lines. A category reserved, by its own gloss, for those who had *not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment*. A respondent who had undergone it, at the station in Denvash, on a date the registry held. That much he could have filed from his district office. But a tribunal did not like to be told that a provision meant what its plainest sentence said, if another sentence of the same provision said something wider; it liked to be shown. So he had gone looking for what the provision had been written to do.

The drafting records had been in three places, none of them where the index said. He had found them at last in a basement room under the regional chamber, in a box marked for a different provision entirely, and he had read them in an afternoon by a window that let in more cold than light. The demonstration provision had been written a long time ago, and it had been written as a kindness. The guilds in those days had sent their families travelling, carters and drovers and pedlars with a guild licence, and their children came to fourteen on the road, a month from any station, and arrived at the gates of the academies untested and unplaced. A chartered academy could not take an untested child. The provision let it, under watching, until the child could be brought to a station and found out. That was all it was for. It had been amended three times since, and every amendment had widened who it covered by a little and narrowed what it was for by not at all.

He had sat with that for a while in the cold basement room. He had thought it was a decent law. He thought so still.

Then he had found the precedents, which had been easier, because the enforcement digests were indexed by people who expected to be asked. Three times in other districts, across the long life of the provision, somebody had tried to stretch it over a person it had not been written for. Once it had been a youth past the age of Kindling who had refused to be tested. Once it had been a disputed assessment the family would not accept. Once it had been a clerk's honest mistake, never caught until it was challenged. Three academies, three enrollments, three challenges. Each enrollment had been voided. None had survived the hearing, and one had not survived to a hearing at all. He had read each digest twice and copied the citations onto a clean sheet in his small plain hand.

And now this line, on the short daily sheet, from the academy at the top of the hill.

*Candidate formerly enrolled under demonstration provision.* A child, then, a real one, of the kind the drafters had meant. A child who had come to Greyvane untested and sat under watching and gone down to the station at fourteen and been found to be something ordinary on every chart there was, and moved out of the category by the plainest procedure there was, exactly as the provision had been built to do. Coss read the line a second time and thought that if he had been asked to invent an exhibit to show a tribunal what the provision was *for*, he could not have invented a better one. The academy had sent it to him itself, on the third-day post, because the procedure said it must.

He did not take any satisfaction in it. He noted it. That was different.

The roll of the observer track at Greyvane, after this line, held one name.

"You're sure you want to go up yourself?"

The senior officer who had briefed him nineteen days ago asked it from the doorway of the borrowed room at the end of the afternoon, with his coat already on. He was a heavy, courteous man near the end of a long career, who signed whatever was put in front of him after reading every word of it, and Coss had come in nineteen days to like him a good deal.

"I'm sure," said Coss.

"The standard procedure is service by the regional channel. A sealed packet to the provost by courier, a copy to the magistrate's office, the date set from there." The senior officer looked at the packet on the desk, which was now as thick as a hand and tied in two directions. "It's a good packet. I've read it. It will stand up by itself. You don't need to walk it up a hill."

"No," said Coss. "I don't need to."

He did not try to explain the rest, because the rest did not fit on a form, and the senior officer had been in the service long enough to know that a man who said *I don't need to* in that voice was not finished. A packet sent by courier was read first by a provost alone at his desk, and then explained to its subject secondhand, by people who loved him and were frightened for him and would explain it in their own words. Secondhand bred errors. Errors bred appeals. And under that, where he did not need to look at it for long, there was a borrowed office in Ardenmere and a boy with his hands pressed white on his knees saying *Thank you*. Coss had stamped a summons closed-pending that afternoon with more force than a stamp required. He did not intend to do this one at the end of a courier's arm.

"The subject attends a proceeding on his own status by right," he said instead, which was true and fitted on a form. "I'd prefer he hear it from the officer who drafted it, in the room, with the provost present. It saves a step later."

The senior officer looked at him a while.

"I'll sign it," he said. "Put the request in tonight."

He put it in that night, in the flat language the form demanded, and it came back approved by the middle of the next morning. That was faster than the senior officer could have signed it and sent it up and had it back, and so it had not gone through the senior officer at all. It had gone to the same desk as before, somewhere above, where somebody had been waiting. On the cover sheet of the challenge packet, when the registry returned it to him stamped for service, the last field before the seal was the one he had stopped expecting to read: the short ruled bar, as neat as a line drawn under a sum, and the small type beneath it. *Field restricted. Recipient clearance insufficient.* He looked at it for a while, and then tied the packet up again in both directions, and did not untie it.

The credential came the same day, from a different window. It was a small card, stamped, plainly printed, his name and his grade and the seal of the Compact's field service, the sort of card that did not need to be large because the people it was shown to had been taught to recognize it. He had carried one for many years and almost never shown it. A Warden was a word a district would accept at its gate without thinking twice. A field agent of the Compact was a word that made a gate think. He had found, over a long career, that the first word got more honest work done than the second, and he had let it.

He would show the card at Greyvane, at the gate and again in the provost's room, because the procedure required that the respondent know exactly what kind of officer was standing in front of him. That, too, he did not mind. He had been honest with the boy every time they had sat across a desk, as honest as the job allowed, and the job now allowed a little more.

An aide was assigned him the next morning, a young man two years out of the regional school who could carry cases and copy fair and did not yet know when to stop talking. Coss liked him well enough. He told him they would leave before dawn on the Tuesday and reach the academy on the Monday following, by the coach to the hill town and the road up from there, and that he was to bring the bound digests and the drafting records and a great deal of clean paper, and that he would not need anything to read on the coach because there would be the file.

That evening he wrote to his daughter, as he did every week, a short letter about nothing: the regional town's bad bread, a chamber bell that rang the hours wrong, the senior officer's coat. He did not mention the case, and never had. At the bottom, after he had signed it, he sat for a moment with the pen over the page.

*I'm going up into the hills for a few weeks,* he added. *It's a long road. I'll write from the other end.*

He sealed it and put it in the evening bag. Then he went back to the borrowed desk and untied the challenge packet after all, and read it again from the first page, slowly, as if he had never seen the subject's name before.

# Chapter 42 — Correct Papers

The gate clerk had never seen the seal before, and she did not know what to do with her face.

It was the middle of the morning on the Monday of the nineteenth week, between the second bell and the third, and the yard was full of the ordinary traffic of a school between lectures. Students crossed with books under their arms and kit over their shoulders, a cart of flour sacks was being checked in at the kitchen gate, and a second-year was being shouted at by somebody for leaving a door open. Cael was sitting on the low step of the lecture wing with the observation notebook on his knee, halfway down a page about Edran's off arm. He had watched the third structure come up whole four times in five that morning at the high windows, and he was trying to write down why, when something at the gate pulled his eyes off the page as a wrong note pulls a listener's head round.

There were two travellers at the clerk's booth. One was young, with a satchel on each shoulder and a case in each hand, standing the half-step back that an aide stands from the person he carries for. The other was older, in plain travelling clothes the colour of the road, and he was holding out a small stamped seal on a card, and waiting.

The clerk had it in her inky fingers and was turning it over. She looked at it a long time, much longer than she looked at anything that came through her booth, and Cael watched her look and knew what the look was. It was the look of somebody checking a thing against her memory and finding nowhere in her memory to put it. She said something. The older man answered, patiently, and took a second paper from a document case under his arm, folded once, the sort of extra proof a careful traveller carries for exactly this moment. The clerk read it, and put her finger on her list, and found something on the list, and the gate opened as it opened for anybody the list expected.

He had sat across a borrowed desk from that patience once, a long time ago, in Ardenmere, with his hands pressed together on his knees and a paper of his own that he had found in a district archive in two days. The walk took four minutes, and he spent every one of them counting, because counting was easier than looking at the one thing he was not ready to look at. Fourteen students crossing the yard. Three crows along the kitchen ridge. His boots loud on the flags and then quiet on the trodden earth, and the wind cold on one cheek. He had rehearsed this day since Denvash in more versions than he could number, and in every one of them it had come as a knock: a courier on Hesk's front step on the second morning, one knock, a polite wait, a satchel. In none of them had it come as a patient man in road-coloured clothes holding up a seal at a gate, and the gate swinging open for him because nothing about him was wrong.

That was the lesson about the machine he seemed to need teaching over and over. It did not kick doors in. It never had to. Whoever had hung every door he had ever sheltered behind had hung it with the machine's leave.

The provost's door was open.

He saw it from the bottom of the last flight, the worn stair dipping at the centre of every step under his boots, and he slowed on the landing. Naveth shut his door on the conversations he meant to keep and left it wide on the ones he did not mind anybody hearing, and Cael had learned in the fourth week what a wide door meant in this building. A shut door meant something private. A wide one meant a procedure, with nothing in it the room was ashamed of. And Quenna had taught him, across a dozen sittings and without once putting it into words, that you fought a procedure differently from a secret.

He stopped on the threshold for the length of a breath. Over the visitor's shoulder Quenna looked straight at him, and there was no comfort in her face at all, only an order as plain as chalk: *say little, hear everything.* He had been taking that look across a panel table since the first sitting.

He went in.

---

Naveth was sitting behind his desk with his hands flat on the blotter and the stillness of a man who had arranged his own face in advance. Quenna stood by the tall window with her arms folded and her folder under one of them, watching the door and not the room. The aide was nowhere; he had been left on a bench on the landing below, which told Cael what register the meeting was going to be in. And in the chair angled toward the desk, with the document case open on his knee, sat the man from the gate.

He had aged in ways Cael's memory had not kept up with. There was more grey at the temples, and deeper lines at the corners of the eyes, the sort that come from reading by bad light. The patience under it was exactly the same.

"Cael," said Naveth. "This is Warden Coss. I understand the two of you have met."

"We have," said Coss, before Cael could say anything, and there was nothing unkind in his getting there first; it was only the plain efficiency of a man who preferred being exact to being polite. "In Ardenmere. A borrowed office, two days, and a good many questions, which I asked as gently as they could be asked."

"The Warden has come about your enrollment," said Naveth. "Your status is the subject of it, so you sit in on it as of right and not as a kindness, and I'd like that set down here first, ahead of everything." He glanced at Coss, and there was flint in the glance that Cael had never seen in him. "The Warden agreed."

"The Warden agreed at once," said Coss, unbothered. "I have no use for proceedings the subject hears about secondhand. Secondhand breeds mistakes, and mistakes breed appeals."

Quenna moved her head a fraction toward the empty chair, and Cael sat in it. To stay standing would have turned this into a quarrel, and it was not a quarrel; it was a procedure. Some time since Denvash he had learned to tell those two apart even when, from the inside, there was no difference at all in how they felt.

"You're a long way from your district," Cael said.

"I am." Coss opened the case on his knee and laid out what was in it, his hands moving with the ease of long habit, as if they had done this so often it no longer cost him a thought. "That's the first thing I owe you an account of. I'd like to be plain with you about what I am. I think you've spent a long while believing something smaller than the truth, and we'll get further this morning on the truth."

He held out a card.

It was small and stamped and plain, plain the way official things are once the people who made them have long stopped needing to impress anybody. Naveth's face did not move as Coss turned it to the room, and Cael knew from that alone that the provost had been shown it already, by himself, before anybody else was sent for.

"'Warden' was never a town title," said Coss. "It sounds like one, and it's meant to. A district will open its gate to a Warden without a second thought, where a field agent of the Compact would make it think twice, and the Compact has never troubled to put a district right while the mistake is useful to it. The grade is mine, though, and real, and I'd held it for years before your name ever crossed my desk." He let that settle without changing his voice. "I was never some local man who happened to notice you, Cael. Your file has been mine since the week of your Kindling. I kept it at arm's length, because arm's length was all the assignment wanted." He laid the card on his knee. "Greyvane changed the assignment."

Cael sat with that.

It did not surprise him. It came the way true things sometimes come, as a shape at last coming clear out of something he had been watching from the side of his eye for a long time without ever turning to face it. Somewhere below the binder, in the place where he kept what he would not write down, he had always known the gentleness was only part of it. The reports had been read by somebody. The questions across the borrowed desk had been written for somebody. There had been the junior assessor with his careful notebook in the market square, and the watchers he had taught himself to count on the circuit, and the long hush of a file nobody ever shut. Every bit of it had pointed at a hand somewhere at the far end. And now the hand sat in a provost's office with its credentials on its knee, honest at last because honesty had become the correct procedure. He had never had anything to tie that knowledge to. Now he had a road-worn face to tie it to, and the face remembered him at fourteen.

"You're here to file something," he said.

"I am." He did not pause over it. From the case he took a second document, far thicker than the card, tied both ways, with the armoured look of a thing written to be read by people hoping it would fail. "Your enrollment here is invalid as categorized. The category of unclassified observer is kept, by its own written gloss, for a candidate who *has not yet undergone formal Kindling assessment*. You were assessed. It happened at the station in Denvash, on a date the registry holds, and I have the entry. The provision's own drafting records say it was made for children who come to an academy's gate untested, and you were never such a child." He untied the packet and laid it on Naveth's desk with its corners squared to the blotter's. "And a chartered academy that keeps an enrollment it knows to be invalid puts its charter up for review. That isn't a threat, Provost. It's the fact that turns what happens next from a choice into a necessity."

Naveth's hands did not move on the blotter.

Cael watched him take it the way institutions take blows, with nothing showing on the surface and everything going into the beams underneath. He had watched this man walk a records request round his building on requisition slips at the exact pace the code permitted and not an hour faster. He understood, watching him now, that Naveth had already run every slow and lawful manoeuvre he owned against this morning in his head, before Cael ever came up the stair, and had already found that none of them reached it. Slowness worked on a request. This was not a request.

"You're filing a challenge," said Naveth.

"I'm filing it here and now, on the record of this room." Coss drew out a third paper, written already and signed at the foot in a small hand that had plainly taught itself the same thrift as the rest of him. "Under the charter, a challenge of this kind compels a public adjudication: a magistrate of the Compact in the chair, the sitting held on the academy's own ground, and the record kept in the permanent registry. Please understand that none of this gives me any pleasure. And please understand, too, that whether it pleased me was never the point."

"The provision has sheltered students at this academy for forty years," said Quenna from the window.

It was the first thing she had said. Her voice was level in the deliberate way of somebody who has looked over every voice available to her and chosen that one.

"Late Kindlers. Disputed assessments. Children your own registry filed wrongly and this academy caught. If this hearing voids the category, every one of those enrollments is precedent at risk."

"I've read your alumni ledger, Assessor. It's a fine record." He said it with no heat at all, and somehow that was harder to hear than heat would have been. "It isn't an answer. The provision sheltered those students while they were the students it was written for. Nobody has ever asked it to shelter a registered classification, because no academy before this one was ever," and he stopped, precisely, "*inventive* enough to try. This hearing won't be about forty years of anybody. It will be about one boy."

The room held still round the papers on the desk the way rooms hold still round a thing that has already happened, whether or not everybody in them has finished taking it in. Cael looked at Quenna and found nothing on her face but the careful blankness of somebody choosing, while it went on, which of several things she might do would actually help.

"How long," said Naveth at last, "before the hearing?"

"Three weeks. The interval is the magistrate's office's to set, not mine, and I wouldn't want it otherwise." He shut the case, and the little clasp caught with a click far too quiet for how final it sounded. "One more thing, for what it's worth to any of you. This isn't the first time I've watched somebody lean on this provision. It's been tried in other districts, for other people, for other reasons. I have never seen it survive."

Nobody asked him to say more. Nobody needed him to.

---

It ended as such meetings do, in arrangements. Dates were agreed, a copy of the challenge stayed on the blotter for the academy's counsel to start pulling at, and the room was named: the assembly room on the lecture wing's ground floor, the long plain hall with the lectern nobody used. Quenna had already turned half toward the window, and Cael could see the next three weeks beginning to eat into her. He kept still through all of it, with the kind of stillness he had learned from people who must keep a room level while something inside them goes on at another speed altogether. Some cool clerkly part of him took notes regardless, from habit. Public record. The academy's counsel to see the documents on a fixed timetable. Witnesses of fact, on either side, entered by name. Each point dropped into place in him with a small click, like furniture being carried into a house nobody had moved into yet.

Coss stood to go before Cael did.

At the door he paused and turned round, and spoke, for the first time since Cael had come in, to him and not to the business going on round him.

"You were fourteen," he said. "In a borrowed room in Ardenmere, with a paper you'd dug out of an archive in two days, and it was right, and I had to give way to it. I did that afternoon's work as gently as the work would let me. Gently enough, I hoped, that the gentleness would be what you kept, ahead of the rest." He held Cael's eyes and did not let the look go any softer than it was. "This is also the job. I'd like you to take that exactly as plainly as I say it. Knowing it doesn't make the thing any lighter. It doesn't make it less true either."

He went out, and they heard him go down the worn stair at an unhurried pace, and stop on the landing below to say something to his aide, and go on.

Quenna walked Cael out afterward. For most of the corridor she said nothing at all, and that was itself something to know, because Quenna without a prepared sentence was a rarer sight than Quenna blunt. At the head of the stair she stopped.

"I'll tell you now how the next three weeks will go," she said, "so that none of us wastes a day of them pretending. The academy's counsel will fight on procedure: the timetable, the scope, which documents and when. That wins us days. It doesn't win us anything else. What sheltered you was the provision's wording, and the wording is precisely where he's chosen to put his weight, which tells me he read our file as carefully as we wrote it." She drew a breath. "I put my name to the idea that you'd be a settled fact before anybody looked hard. And you are. Five sittings, one an exhibition in public and one a match, every record signed three times over. He looked hard anyway. He's betting the fact loses to the category." She looked at him, and under the flatness was something he had never seen in her. It was not fear. It was nearer to a debt being admitted. "I brought you into this, Cael. Whatever the next three weeks want from me, they can have."

He thought of the first sitting, and the empty hall afterward, and the voice she had used for the things she did not want written down. *Today proves you're real.*

"You told me the first job was to be on the record before anybody hostile read it," he said. "You never said what the job was after somebody did."

"After that," said Quenna, "the job is to be right."

She went back along the corridor toward whatever was already beginning to eat her afternoon, and left him at the top of the stair with the sentence. He put it carefully with the others, the small and growing collection of sentences he suspected he would be spending the rest of his life inside.

---

Coss had meant every word. That was the thing Cael kept coming back to, walking slowly down the worn stair with one hand on the cold wall, and it was the whole of the problem.

The machine was not run on cruelty. He could have hated cruelty; he had hated it all his life, and hating it had a clean edge and knew where to go. This was a man who did the work as kindly as it could be done and meant every word, who carried a card in his pocket and a packet tied both ways and a borrowed room in Ardenmere in his memory, and who had climbed the hill himself so that a boy would hear it from the hand that wrote it. And the work, the whole long patient work from Denvash to this morning, had never once needed anybody in it to be cruel. It had only needed men like Coss to be good at it.

Skill, used kindly, for a thing that had no room anywhere in it for what he was: he could not hate it cleanly, and he could not see how to fight it cleanly either, and the two troubles came together. He did not yet know what to do with that. He stood at the foot of the stair holding it for a while, and then went to find the others, because the rule was the day you knew it, and he had known since the gate.

---

He told them himself, before the notice could tell them for him.

They were at the scarred table at the end of the afternoon, under the bricked-up hay door, Brom with a cup and Lira with her boots on the bench and Karis with her notebook squared in front of her, and he sat down at the end and laid it out as he had laid out every fragment he had ever told them about. He gave it plainly, in order, with nothing softened. The card, and the grade. The file held from the week of his Kindling, at a distance. The challenge, and its argument. The magistrate. The three weeks. *I have never seen it survive.*

Lira went still halfway through. At *field agent* an old look went over her face, the look of a girl who had found out at fourteen how high above any local office the machinery reached, and had found it out from nineteen words on a sheet nobody showed her. Karis did not open her notebook, for once. She kept both hands flat on the scarred wood and pressed down a little, as though the table might lift if she let go.

Brom spoke first when it was over.

"Three weeks," he said. "Public. On the record." He said the facts slowly, one at a time, as a man puts his weight on a floorboard before trusting it with the rest of him. "Who speaks for Greyvane?"

"The academy's counsel, I suppose," said Cael. "That's tomorrow's question. Tonight's was making sure you heard it from me."

"And Coss," said Lira. Her voice had gone low and even, the voice she saved for the things she meant to get exactly right. "The man who sat across a desk from you when you were fourteen. Who wrote you down, and came after you, and sat and waited." She looked up. "And he calls it the job."

"He meant it."

"I know he meant it. I could hear it from here." She held his eyes down the length of the table. "So here's what I'd like you to stay angry at. Not him; he isn't worth burning anything on. The machine. It took a decent man on, and look what it's had out of him. Don't let his kindness use up your anger before the hearing gets its turn. You'll want all of it, and you'll want it aimed."

Karis moved at last. She took one hand off the table and picked up her notebook after all, and opened it at a clean page with the deliberate air of somebody choosing a weapon from a rack.

"Three weeks," she said. "What do you need?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then I'll ask it another way, since that answer will do for today and won't do for long." Her pen was already running, laying ruled columns down the page. "A public adjudication is a war fought with paper. Paper wars go to whoever has read the most, the soonest, with the best index. That's the one thing my whole life has trained me for, and very nearly the only thing." She raised her eyes. "By the time you know what you need, I'll be some way into it."

Brom stood up and gathered the empty cups along the table, his own and Lira's and the one Karis had forgotten, and stacked them one inside the next. He did that sometimes when he was thinking, the way other people paced. At the end of the table, with the stack in his hands, he stopped.

"Not three weeks ago," he said, "three hundred people and a panel of three watched you do something nobody had a name for, and the record held. Tonight a man with a card turns up to tell us the paperwork isn't strong enough to hold you." He considered the cups. "Personally, I think the paperwork ought to be more worried than you are."

Lira made a sound that was nearly a laugh. Karis wrote something down. Cael sat at the end of the scarred table with the three of them and did not answer any of them properly, because the honest answer was still putting itself together somewhere in him, and he would not hand them a piece of it before he knew what shape it was.

---

Lira was on the wall afterward, and he went out and sat beside her, because there was nowhere else in the building he wanted to be.

The night was clear and very cold. Below them the hill town's lamps were scattered down the slope as they always were, along the street worth walking and the street worth avoiding, and going out one by one as the town went to bed. One of them did not go out. It burned in an upper window above the draper's shop, at the back, a steady small square of yellow, long after the windows round it had gone dark.

"That's him," said Lira.

"How do you know?"

"The draper told me on Saturday. She's a room over the shop she lets to people's mothers. Oona's mother had it." Lira drew her knees up. "She told me this morning she'd let it again. To a gentleman from the Compact and his clerk, for three weeks, paid in advance." She looked at the lit window. "He's in the mothers' room."

Cael laughed before he could help it, and it came out of him in a way that hurt his chest, and Lira did not laugh, but her shoulder knocked against his, once, the old knock.

"He's reading," said Cael, when he could. "He reads everything from the first page. Every time. He'll have the file open on the bed."

"You know that about him."

"I've sat across a desk from it." He watched the small yellow square. "He read my paper three times, in Ardenmere, before he conceded it. I watched him do it. He didn't skip a line."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"Then you'd better read his," she said. "Three times. Without skipping a line." She stood up off the wall and shook out her coat. "And then you'd better read the thing he never read at all, because if there's anything in that whole building he hasn't read from the first page, that's where you go."

She went in. He stayed on the wall until the lamp above the draper's went out, which was a long time, and then he went in too, and lay awake for a while thinking about a case at the back of the archive, floor to ceiling, as deep as an old woman's arm.

# Chapter 43 — The Case at the Back

The notice went up before the seventh bell, pinned beside the assessment calendar in the clerk's small even hand, with the same plain efficiency Greyvane gave to every piece of its own paperwork.

*Public adjudication. In the matter of the enrollment of the candidate on the unclassified observer track, on challenge filed by the Compact's field service. A magistrate of the Compact presiding. Assembly room, lecture wing. The first day of the twenty-second week, first bell. The record will be public.*

He stood at the board and read it as he had read the assessment calendar on his first morning at Greyvane, his own name set out on a public schedule for anybody to see. But the calendar had been a door somebody had built for him. This was a date, fixed in ink, by a system that had spent every day since Denvash deciding he belonged on the far side of all its doors.

He stood there long enough that a second-year going past with an armful of practice staves gave him a second look before going on. Board, calendar, the fifth sitting's fair copy still in his binder, and now this: the whole of his standing in the world, four sheets deep, three of them pinned to cork. He looked at the line about the first bell, and the line about the magistrate, and the last line most of all, *the record will be public*, and somewhere in the looking, without his noticing the moment it happened, the fear finished turning into the other thing.

He knew that other thing. It was what fear became in him when it had been given long enough and something to push against. It had happened in Ardenmere with Reydan's name on the circuit board, and it had happened at the first sitting with Hobb across the chalk. A hearing was a formal proceeding, and formal proceedings ran on rules. Rules were written down somewhere. And anything that was written down could be read, closely and patiently, the way he had read every fighter who had ever tried to beat him, until he could see how it moved.

He walked to the archive.

Prynn was where he had left her that morning, at the high desk on her stool with her ledger open and her spectacles on their cord. She looked up as he came through the iron-banded door with the expression of somebody who had guessed from the runner's face hours ago that whatever it was would not be small, and had been waiting since then to find out how large.

"They've filed," said Cael.

It did not need more than that, not here, not to her.

"I heard. Three weeks." She set down her pen. "This academy has thin walls. Thinner discretion."

"Three weeks."

Prynn looked at him for a long moment with the same territorial care she gave to everything, the look of a keeper deciding whether a request would be worth the trouble of whatever shelves it disturbed. Then she nodded, once, as if he had already asked the question rather than only arrived carrying it.

"Where do you keep the Compact's registry code?" said Cael. "The text itself. Not a digest of it, and not what people say it says. The code, as it was written."

Something moved in her face. It was not surprise. It was closer to recognition, the look of a woman who has been keeping room on a shelf for years without knowing who would eventually come and ask for what was on it.

"Come with me," she said, and came down off the stool.

She took the lamp from the reading table and led him past it, down the long room, past the aisles he had walked a dozen times and the founding-era stock and the shelves of the academy's own records, to the very back of the archive, where the old waystation wall came up out of the floor in great blocks of grey stone. There was a section there he had never had a reason to look at closely, behind a pillar, in a corner the lamp had to be carried into. The thing in it was not a shelf.

It was a case. It went from the floor to the ceiling and was as deep as Prynn's arm was long. In it the bound volumes of the Compact's registry code stood in their dark identical spines, row on row, with the amendments filed beside them in thin paper fascicles tied with tape. Decades on decades of the same official language were laid down there like silt in a river bed, each layer pressed flat by the weight of everything that had been enacted since. The spines said what they were in faded gilt. *Charter. Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings. Obligations of Practitioners. Enforcement.* He read the words and knew them for the inside of every room he had ever lived in, and knew too that he had never once seen them in ink.

He looked up at it as he had once looked down from the rail of the training hall in the second week, at eleven Paths he did not know moving on the floor below: outmatched, outnumbered, and under the sensible fear feeling the old shameful stir of appetite. Here was the whole of Coss's authority, the machine's actual bones, standing on a shelf in an old waystation's back room within reach of his hand.

"It's long," said Prynn beside him, watching him take its measure. "It's dry as anything on these shelves, and drier than most. It contradicts itself in four places I've found, and I've never had anybody to tell." She lifted the lamp a little. "And if you want my opinion, which you haven't asked, I don't believe a single person who enforces it has read it whole in my lifetime. They read the part that lets them do the thing they came to do."

Cael looked at the case for a long moment.

"That last part," he said slowly, "might be the most useful thing anybody's said to me all day."

"It generally is." She set the lamp on the floor, reached up into the case without looking, as she reached for everything on her shelves, and drew down the first volume and put it in his hands. It weighed exactly what it looked as if it would weigh. "Reading table's where it's always been. I lock up at the eighth bell." She picked up the lamp again and looked at him over her spectacles. "You know what that's worth."

---

For two days afterward Greyvane ran a low fever.

He felt it everywhere he went. In the stable at meals the talk came in two volumes now, the ordinary one and a lower one that followed him from the hatch to the scarred table and stopped when he sat down. In the lecture wing an instructor lost the end of his own sentence when Cael came in late, and the whole room performed not turning round. In the main hall the notice had been read so many times by the second afternoon that its corners had gone soft as cloth. There were something over two hundred students at Greyvane, and by his own careful count every one of them knew that in three weeks a magistrate of the Compact would sit in their assembly room and decide whether the observer was allowed to exist there. Some of them looked at him with pity. Some looked with the interest people give to a house that has had its roof blown off in the night. And some, more of them than he would have expected, and he counted them honestly, had started giving him a small firm nod as they passed, the nod people give to somebody whose side they have privately chosen.

He spent both nights in the archive.

Prynn did not lock the door either night. He did not mention it, and neither did she. On the first night she came down off her stool at the eighth bell and put a cup of tea at his elbow and went back up, and on the second she did not come down at all, and the cup was there anyway when he looked up from the page, though he had not heard her bring it.

He read the enforcement provisions first, because that was where the teeth were, and the teeth were what Coss would use. He read them the way he had learned to read a fighter's declaration timing, not for what any single clause did but for how all of them moved, and he copied their shapes into the observation notebook as he went, one structure under another, as if the code were an opponent and each provision one of its declarations. They were dense and old and cross-referenced to death, and they were full of words like *voidance* and *sanction* and *removal* that he had never seen written down before, though he had lived inside every one of them. By the middle of the first night his eyes ached and he had nine pages of nothing.

Somewhere after midnight on the second night the pattern showed him its joint.

It did not arrive as an idea. It never had, for him, with a fighter or with anything else. It came by piling up: hour after hour of reading with the lamp hissing low and the cold rising out of the flagstones under the table, until the same small thing had happened in front of him so many times that he could no longer pretend it was not there. Every power the enforcement chapters granted, every single one of them, and he went back and checked them until the letters swam, was granted in the same shape. Authority *over classified practitioners*. Sanctions *proportionate to classification and standing*. Procedures that came into force *upon classification*. Powers exercised *according to classification*. He had read the word four hundred times in two nights. It carried weight in every sentence it was in. The whole of the Compact's reach rested on it like a roof on a single beam.

And nowhere in the enforcement chapters did the code say what the word meant.

He sat back in the cold and looked at that for a long time.

It was not proof of anything. It might be nothing at all; it might only be that the word was defined somewhere else, in some chapter he had not reached, in a clause so plain and so obvious that no drafter of enforcement had ever needed to look at it again. Codes did that, he supposed. Fighters did it. A fighter who had built a whole style on one strong leg never thought about the leg. But a fighter who had built a whole style on one strong leg had built it on one leg, and if somebody ever looked at the leg properly, the whole style was standing on whatever they found.

He told nobody that night. He wanted to have tried hard to prove himself wrong before he allowed himself to be right. He spent what was left of the second night doing exactly that, going back through the enforcement chapters looking for a single power granted in any other shape, one sanction that did not lean on the word, one procedure that came into force on anything else. He did not find one.

At the bottom of the ninth page he wrote: *Every power they have attaches to the classified. Every one. The word carries all of it. Where does the code say what the word is?*

He drew the small box round the last line.

---

Oona was waiting for him outside the stable on the Tuesday evening, between the two nights, with her slate under her arm and a new tag on the string round her neck.

He saw it before he saw her face. The hollow circle was gone, and in its place hung the academy's ordinary crest, a tower above a road, the same tag Brom wore, cut from the same tin by the same clerk. She had polished it. It caught the light from the stable door.

"They changed it on Monday," she said, seeing him look. "In the morning. Before the man came." She held it up a little on its string, to show him the back, where there was something stamped now that there had never been on the old one. "It says Anchor. And Copper. And the year."

"It suits you."

"It's ordinary." She let it fall back against her coat. She did not look as pleased as he had expected her to look; she looked as she looked when a sum would not come out. "I sit with the hardening first-years now in taxonomy. On the left bench. You're on your own on the right one."

"I noticed."

"Everybody noticed." She shifted the slate in her arms and looked at the stable door and not at him. "Can I ask you something rude?"

"You can ask."

"Is it because of me?" She said it very fast, as if she had been holding it all day and needed it out before it could be held any longer. "The man with the papers. The hearing. Is it because I'm not on the track any more, and now it's only you, so it's easier to say the track shouldn't have you on it?"

He looked at her for a moment in the light from the door, and thought about it properly, because she had asked properly and she would know if he did not.

"No," he said. "It isn't because of you. The man who filed it has been carrying my file since before I ever came to Greyvane. Since before I'd ever heard your name. He had walked the running road first. There was always running. "I'm not going anywhere," he said. "And Greyvane isn't going down on my account either. There's another way. Nobody's said it because it sounds mad, and I'd like to get it out before I lose my nerve."

"Say it," said Lira.

"I'm not going to defend the enrollment. You're right, sir. There's nothing there to defend, and trying would only spend the magistrate's patience on a lie." He had read the next sentence so many times in his head in two nights that it came out steadier than he had expected. "I want to fight the hearing on different ground. The whole challenge assumes the Compact has authority over me in the first place. Every power the code gives it is written in the same shape: over *classified* practitioners, according to *classification*, upon *classification*. I've read every enforcement chapter twice. The word carries all of it." He looked round the desk. "I haven't found yet where the code says what the word means. I want three weeks to find out whether it means what everybody has always assumed it means. Or whether everybody's assumed it for centuries because nobody with a reason to check ever checked."

The room took it in stages.

Brom's eyebrows went up slowly, as they always did, a step at a time. Karis's pen, which had been lying still beside her notebook, was in her hand before she seemed to know she had picked it up. Naveth's counsel-trained caution began, visibly, to draft objections behind his eyes. But it was Quenna that Cael watched, because Quenna was the one who had staked her name on him in front of a panel of three. Nothing on her face moved. What moved was in her shoulders, a sudden tightening, the thing he had seen fighters do in the instant a bout turned into one they had not trained for.

"You're going to argue registry law," she said, "against a field agent of the Compact. In three weeks."

"Yes."

"At fifteen."

"I'll study it the way I'd study anybody I had to fight," said Cael. "Systems have architecture. Architecture has seams. I've spent two years finding the joins in things people had drilled half their lives to keep shut. I can't see why this one should be any different, except that I'll be doing it with my eyes on a page instead of on a body."

"It is different," said Quenna, "because if you're wrong, what you lose isn't a bout. It's everything the bouts were ever for."

"I know what it costs if I'm wrong." He held her look across the desk and did not let it go. "I've been counting that cost since the afternoon I signed the form. If I'm going to lose, I'd sooner lose showing they never had the right, than lose by walking out quietly because showing it looked too hard."

Brom, who had not said anything yet, made a small sound against the bookshelf that in another room might have been a laugh.

"That's the most Cael thing I've ever heard you say," he said. "And I've heard you say a great many of them."

Naveth looked at Cael for a long moment. It was the measuring look, the look of a man recalculating a wager he had already placed once and was now being asked, under fire, to place again. Whatever the sum came to, it came to the same answer as the first time. That was the whole of who Naveth was, Cael thought: a man who had built an academy out of doubling the bets other people folded.

"If this is the road," said the provost at last, "the academy won't stand in it. I can't endorse an argument that goes at the Compact's jurisdiction head-on. My charter limits what an institution under it may say against it, and I'd be handing the Warden a second challenge with my own signature on it. But a student may make his own case, in his own hours, and use what this academy has to offer, exactly as he might while getting ready for a sitting." He paused. "And nothing at all prevents me from being remarkably slow to notice how many hours a particular student is spending in the archive."

"That's the second useful thing you've done for me this week," said Cael. He meant it as thanks, though it came out flatter than he had meant it to.

"Don't thank me yet," said Naveth. "Thank me if it works."

Prynn laid down the rules of the room on the first full morning, before she laid down a single volume.

He took the rules as he took a new instructor's floor discipline, without arguing, because a floor's discipline told you about the floor. This floor had kept centuries of paper alive through damp winters and dry ones, and through the waystation's roof, which Prynn described with real bitterness as a quarrel she had been having with the weather since before he was born.

"Most students who come in here want an answer," she said, and set the first volume of the charter in front of him with both hands, as other people carry babies, or eggs. "I'm not angry at it."

Over the top of the volume her eyes stayed on him, and he could see her taking his measure afresh.

He thought about how to say it exactly, because Prynn was a person who heard the difference between exact and nearly.

He read the code the way he read an opponent he had not fought yet. He took in everything there was before he let himself chase any single question, built the shape of the whole before he went after any one part of it, and trusted the pattern to show itself to somebody patient enough to sit still while it did.

The first two days gave him almost nothing but the shape. The shape, he came slowly to understand, was the first real finding.

The language changed down the generations as a fighter's style changes across a long career. In the oldest chapters it was cautious, in the years when the Compact had still been a treaty among guilds that did not entirely trust one another, and every clause was hedged with *by consent* and *where the parties agree*. In the middle centuries it was confident, almost careless; nobody was arguing with the Compact's authority any more, and its drafters wrote as people write who do not expect to be questioned. And in the newest amendments it was defensive again: hedged and cross-referenced and qualified, every power wrapped in three conditions. Somewhere along the way, he thought, the drafters had been burned, and had learned that one careless clause could still be making trouble for them a century after the ink was dry.

He filled eleven pages of the observation notebook with nothing but the places the code had flinched. A defensive amendment was a scar, and a scar meant there had been a wound. Every one of them meant that at some point somebody had found a weak place in the wording and pushed something through it hard enough to hurt the Compact. He listed the scars as he would have listed the places an opponent guarded. No single scar was a way in. But the pattern of them showed where the system had once been hurt and had learned fear. More usefully, it showed where it had never learned fear at all.

The enforcement chapters were thick with scars. The obligations had a good many. The charter's oldest pages had a few, healed over long ago.

The schedule had almost none.

He wrote that at the bottom of the second evening's page, under the scar count, and boxed it, and could not yet see what it meant. There seemed to be two ways a thing could go unscarred: nobody had ever gone for it, or nobody had ever had any reason to. He sat looking at the two possibilities with the lamp ticking and Prynn's pen scratching at the high desk, and it did not occur to him for two more days that there might be a third.

He did not stop the other work while he read. Lira would not have let him, and he would not have let himself.

On the Thursday, at the sixth bell, he went from the long table to the quiet room with the code still in his head, and Lira went with him, and Brom carried the bucket of sand. Karis was on the founding shelves and had sent word by Brom that she trusted the two of them to count without her, which Lira said was the nicest thing Karis had ever said about anybody and the most suspicious. The oak post stood in its bucket in the middle of the scrubbed floor with fourteen small rings already burned into its grey side from the weeks before. He knelt and laid his palm on a clean place and chose the size, and made three marks, one after another, with the count between. All three came the width of his thumbnail. The cold came after each, and went, and his fingertips went grey and came back, and the count between each was ninety-six, then a hundred and one, then ninety-nine.

"Steady," said Lira, from the wall. "Same as last week. Same as the week before."

"Steady."

"Then I want to try something." She came off the wall. "Put your hand back on it. Clean place. Get it ready, the way you do, so it's sitting there wanting to go. Don't light it."

He looked at her.

"Quenna's question," said Lira. "I'm not answering it for you. I can't. But I'd like to know one small thing, and so would you." She went round behind him, out of his sight. "Hand on the wood. Ready. And hold it."

He laid his right palm on a clean place on the oak and turned toward the warmth until it was there, low and patient and ready, the size chosen, the asking a breath away from being asked. He held it. He could feel it waiting under his hand like a horse that has seen the gate open.

Behind him, without any warning at all, Lira clapped her hands together hard, once, a crack like a dropped plate in the small whitewashed room.

He flinched. His whole body flinched, his shoulders came up and his breath went in and his heart jumped into his throat. His palm stayed flat on the oak.

Nothing lit.

He knelt there with his heart going hard and the warmth still sitting under his hand, ready, unspent, exactly where he had held it. After a long moment he took his hand away. The wood under it was clean grey oak.

"Well," said Lira quietly.

Brom had stopped with the scoop half lifted. He put it down.

"That isn't an answer," said Cael. His voice was not quite steady. "A clap in a quiet room isn't somebody who wants me gone. It isn't a fight."

"No," said Lira. "It's a clap in a quiet room." She came back round where he could see her and crouched on her heels in front of him. "But a week ago you didn't know whether a fright would spend it for you. Now you know that one doesn't. That's one thing. You write it down as one thing and not as two."

He wrote it kneeling on the scrubbed boards, with the binder on his knee.

*Thursday, week nineteen. Three of three, a thumbnail, count about a hundred. Then L. clapped behind me with my hand on the post and the new one ready. I flinched all over. It didn't light. One fright in a quiet room. Not a fight, not anybody who wishes me harm. One thing, not two.*

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he drew the small box round it, his own private mark for *not yet*, and closed the binder, and went back to the code.

# Chapter 45 — The Wrong Road

The third day went entirely on a wrong turn, and he took it before he took the right one.

The obvious road was the one anybody would have taken, and he took it. It was the demonstration provision itself: its drafting, its amendments, every enrollment that had ever leaned on its words. If the challenge was that he had been fitted into a category not built for him, then surely the place to fight was the category, and the place to start was the moment it was made. He asked Prynn for the provision's history at the first bell, and she looked at him over her spectacles for a moment longer than she needed to and then went and got it without a word, which he understood only later was the most she would ever do to warn a reader off a shelf.

It was all there, in the order it had grown. The provision had been revised three times, and each revision sat in its fascicle with its date and its reasons attached in the flat language of the drafting clerks. He read the newest first, out of habit, and then made himself go back and read them in order, as Prynn had told him to. When he reached the bottom of the oldest box he found the first draft, written in a hand so old that the letters leaned against each other like tired men.

It had been written as a kindness.

He sat with that for a long time in the cold. In those days the guilds had sent their families travelling, carters and drovers and pedlars on a guild licence, and their children came to fourteen on the road, weeks from any station. They arrived at the gates of the academies untested and unplaced and turned away, because a chartered academy could not take a child nobody had tested. Somebody had minded. Somebody had sat at a desk with a pen and written a few lines so that such a child could come in under watching, and be taught, and be kept warm, until the family could get the child to a station and find out what the child was. That was the whole of what it was for. Every revision after it had widened who it covered by a little, a late Kindler here, a disputed assessment there. Not one of them had changed what it was for.

He thought of Oona on the bench in the grey building, with her mother's hand on one shoulder and his on the other, trembling very finely under the clean grey cloth. He thought of her coming out through the far door with the card held flat against her slate, and her face coming loose over her mother's shoulder. She had come up the hill to Greyvane untested from a guild family, and been taught, and been kept warm, and gone down to the station when the time came and been found to be exactly who she had decided to be. Whoever had written the first draft in that leaning hand had written it for her. They had never imagined him at all.

He walked the road for eleven hours. He traced every revision and every enrollment the digests recorded under it, every late Kindler and disputed child and clerical error the provision had ever sheltered. He was looking for one single case, anywhere, of somebody already Kindled and classified who had been carried by it and allowed to stay. There was none. The road went nowhere. However generously anybody read it, the provision had been written for the untested, and he had been tested, and no amount of its history could change what its words said or what its first drafter had meant by them. Coss had walked this same road weeks ago, he was certain of it. Coss had found the box in the basement, or wherever the regional office kept its copy, and had read the leaning hand, and had walked all the way to the end. Coss would be standing there waiting for him, in the assembly room, with the first draft in his document case.

At the eighth bell he drew one long diagonal line through eleven hours of notes and wrote under it, in the square hand:

*Stop studying my own position. Study his. What is his authority standing on?*

He sat back and looked at the line. It was the same correction Lira had driven into him in a cold yard in Ardenmere, a lifetime ago, in a different language: *stop watching your own feet.* He had spent a whole day watching his own feet.

Prynn came down off her stool and took the provision's boxes away without being asked, and put them back on their shelf, and came back with a cup of tea, and set it down at his elbow.

"That road," she said, "goes nowhere."

"You knew."

"I've known for forty years. I've certified forty copies of it, and read every one of them aloud." She looked at the crossed-out pages. "I'd have told you if you'd asked. You didn't ask. You wanted to walk it." She went back to her stool. "Most people have to. It's a good road. It's only the wrong one."

---

The shape of it, once he could see the shape at all, was very nearly elegant.

He drew it again on the fourth morning, on a clean page, from the top, the way he had drawn it for Prynn as a declaration tree, but this time asking a different question of every part of it. Not *what does this say?* but *what does this stand on?*

The charter was at the top. It was the founding document, the thing the Compact existed because of, and it was wide and high-minded as founding documents are, full of phrases like *the stewardship of practice* and *the ordered development of the Paths*, which bound nobody to anything you could measure. Under the charter was the schedule, the working list, amended and amended again down the centuries, naming each Path, each tier, and each provisional or disputed category the Compact had ever put its formal name to. Under the schedule came the obligations, all the things a classified person owed once the system had given them a name: to register, to be assessed, to behave, the whole quiet harness that came with being part of a thing, asked or not. And under everything, at the very bottom, where the teeth were, was enforcement. Voidance, sanction, removal: the machinery that had walked a fourteen-year-old out of Hesk's workshop in Denvash in the course of one courier's morning.

Four layers, each one resting its whole weight on the one above it. Enforcement rested on the obligations. The obligations fell on the classified. Classification was made by the schedule. And the schedule drew its life from the charter.

A structure like that, like any structure, stood on whatever held up its top. If the thing that held up the top would not bear the weight, nothing under it stood either. It did not matter how solid it looked from across a field, or for how many lifetimes it had stood there with nobody ever leaning on it to find out.

He went back into the charter, looking for the place where it said what classification was.

The fourth morning found it for him, in the definitions clause of the charter, the first article after the preamble. The words were so plain that his eyes slid over them twice before he felt what they were carrying. He had been reading for something clever, something hidden, and it was neither.

*Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.*

He read it a third time, aloud, very quietly, because Prynn had taught him that ears were more honest.

*Shall mean.* It did not say *shall include*, or *shall be taken broadly as*, or *shall be understood in the light of*, or any of the soft wide phrases the middle centuries had been so fond of. The charter did not describe what classification was. It defined it. It shut the word like a door and sealed it, and left the schedule as the one opening in the whole wall: no category could reach the Compact's authority except by passing through it, and the Compact's authority could reach nobody who had not. *For all purposes.* Every power in every enforcement chapter he had read in two nights by lamplight, every *over classified practitioners* and *according to classification*, reached back through that sentence to the schedule. They could reach nowhere else.

That was why the schedule had no scars.

That was why the schedule carried no scars. Nobody had ever gone at it. It was not a wall at all; it was the ground the wall stood on. Nobody hits the ground.

---

He spent the next two days and most of two nights on the schedule itself.

He read it as he had never read anything, and he had spent the better part of two years learning to read things that did not want to be read. He took every Path family and every tier designation. He took every provisional category the Compact had ever tried out before making up its mind: Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, Dual-Registered, and half a dozen more that had come and gone down the generations, as the Compact's notion of what a Path even was kept revising itself, slowly and clumsily. He read the amendments to the schedule in order, as Prynn had taught him, and watched categories born in one generation's confidence and struck out in another's embarrassment. Even the struck ones were still there on the page, ruled through and still readable, because the code kept its own mistakes as carefully as it kept its rules. Every category had a name. Every one was written down, in the schedule itself, in the flat clerical language that makes a thing real in law and not only real in the world.

He looked for the word they had written next to his name in Denvash.

It was not there.

Then he went through it again, more slowly, in case some subsection or appendix had got past him, or some cross-reference hidden in the margin of an amendment. After that he made a list of every place in the code where a category could lawfully be kept. There was the main enumeration, and the provisional annex, and the appendix of disputed standings. There were the amendment schedules, one for every revision, and the definitions cross-index at the back of the charter. He went through them one at a time, as he would go through every answer an opponent might have, and crossed each off, until nothing was left to guess at, only what remained. It had not got past him.

On the sixth night he read the whole schedule a third time, by lamplight, from the first page to the last, with Prynn at the high desk silently refilling his cup at an hour when no duty on earth required her to still be in the building. The third reading said what the second had. Nowhere in the schedule, in all its generations of piled-up text, did the word appear. Not once. Not as a footnote, not as an entry struck out and kept in the record of its own repeal, and not as something once proposed and voted down. The schedule kept its refusals as well. He had come across three: categories somebody had argued for and lost, each refusal written up with the same clerical care as everything else.

His word was not even among the rejected ones.

It existed nowhere in the code. It existed only in what an Arbiter's instrument wrote out at the moment of a Kindling, a flag the instrument itself produced. Generation after generation of enforcement had simply treated that flag as if it were a classification, because it looked like one and sat where one sat on the form, and because nobody in all that time, so far as he could tell, had ever thought to go and look.

For a long while he sat still with it and did not write anything.

He did not trust it. It was too big to believe the first time it was seen, and the worst bouts of his life had all been bouts where he had thrown himself at a seam before he had made certain it was there. Somewhere there had to be the page he had missed: in this building, or in some records hall of the Compact three districts away. There had to be an amendment, or an annex, or an enabling order that made the flag lawful. A system did not run for centuries on nothing.

Except that it did. He knew that it did. On the Ardenmere circuit he had seen whole generations of fighters live by rules that no charter had ever set down. He had sat through a market interview conducted on an authority that not one person in the square could have named. At fourteen he had seen his own life turned off its road by a single morning's procedure, which every person present had called law, because every person in every room had always called it law. Custom in law's coat was nothing unusual where he came from. So far as he could tell, it was what most of the world had on.

He wrote it down.

He began the outline on the tenth day, the Friday, and spent most of the eleventh making himself admit what it actually said.

His first try, that morning, was the argument that the enrollment was sound after all. It was a fallback, a second line to fall back to, the reflex of a fighter who never walks onto a floor without knowing a second way off it. He got most of a page into it. Then he stopped, and drew one long diagonal line through the whole page, and sat a long while with the notebook shut in front of him while Prynn's pen scratched at the high desk.

The enrollment could not be defended. He had known it since his first afternoon at Greyvane, over cold tea, when Quenna had shown him the category and said without any softening that the form's words and the truth beneath them were not the same. To stand up and say otherwise would be to defend a falsehood in front of somebody whose whole trade was hearing falsehoods defended. He would lose, and deserve to. Worse than losing, it would spend the magistrate's patience at the start and poison everything he said after it. A fallback that weakened the main position was not a way off the floor. It was a hole in his own guard, cut with his own hand.

So there could be no second argument. The first would have to stand as Brom stood: nothing in it for show, nothing he could not hold with the whole weight on it, and every joint either carrying load or cut away.

He opened the notebook again and began the outline over, and this time he wrote first the sentence he had been walking round since the night he circled the word.

*I'm not going to argue that my enrollment is valid. I'm going to agree that it isn't, and then ask on what authority the Compact proposes to void it. Their authority runs only to the classified. Classification means the schedule, for all purposes. And the word they wrote next to my name in Denvash is nowhere in the schedule, and never has been.*

An argument was worth exactly as much as the record it would be laid against. So he went to the fourth shelf of the second case and took down the enforcement digests, and spent two days learning what the Compact had in fact done, every time, to anybody like him.

There were four.

That was the whole of the recorded history. Four practitioners, scattered down the centuries, who had drawn the same flag from the same instrument and been written into the digests under it, each with the enforcement chapters' usual care. He read all four files through, slowly, from the first sheet to the last. He did not hurry. In the whole written record they were the only four who had ever stood in his place, and reading them carefully was the smallest thing he could give them.

Nobody had spoken for any of them. None had been given a hearing of any kind. In every case the removal had run on an administrative order, unopposed and unanswered, complete within a matter of days: a notice, a courier, a door, a road. He knew the shape of it. He had walked it.

Three of the four files simply stopped, the way files stop once the system has decided their subjects are not worth more ink. The last of the four closed on one line of notation. He read it six times. Then he copied it out whole into the back of the observation notebook in his own hand, every word of it. It was the entire life of a person, folded down by procedure into eleven words, and he did not mean to let himself forget it.

None of the four had ever been given the chance to answer. He would be the first who was.

He wrote that down too, and found that his hand was not entirely steady while he did it, and let the unsteadiness go into the letters instead of waiting for it to pass. Vell had taught him at the Ironyard that if the person keeping a record hid what they felt, the record was only half kept, and he had kept her rule ever since.

Alone at the long table, with the lamp low and the founding shelves dark behind him, he understood at last the full size of what he meant to do.

A win would not only keep one borrowed enrollment at one minor academy. It would crack the machine, in public, on a record with his own name across it. Every office of the Compact that had ever expelled or watched or flagged somebody under that word would have to face, in the end, that a boy of fifteen had read their code with more care than they ever had. And every official made to face it would learn his name, since the record that held the argument would hold the one who had made it.

It was the kind of argument that made enemies instead of losing politely. It was also, as far as anything he had read could tell him, the one argument that happened to be true.

He sat with the outline until the lamp had burned down to the hour Prynn would ordinarily have closed her ledger. She did not close it. She sat on at the high desk with her pen still, and did not look at him, and did not go.

Then he picked the pen up and went on writing.

*K. is reading the schedule from the beginning, in the evenings, as if she'd never heard of me.*

*The code is architecture. Architecture has seams. I've spent the better part of two years learning where the joins are in things that have them: a fighter's stance, a Path's timing, and now a system's rules about what it may do to people. It's the same work. The difference is that nobody limps away from it.*

He read the last line, and then, for the sake of accuracy, he put the pen back to the page.

*It leaves them later. On whoever loses.*

He closed the binder. Outside the narrow window the grounds had gone fully dark, the settled winter dark of the twentieth week, and down the hill the town's lamps were going out one at a time. The hearing was the week after next. He had his seam. He had four files belonging to four people who had never been allowed to stand where he would stand. What remained was to make the argument strong enough to bear all of him in front of a panel of the Compact, and he had a week of evenings and a second reader at his shoulder to make it with.

He put out the lamp. In the morning there would be four bowls at the end of the scarred table, and Lira would already have taken his bread.

Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

|---|---|

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

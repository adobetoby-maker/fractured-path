# Chapter 46 — At His Own Election

Brom did his witnesses the way he did his fetch drill, one at a time, slowly, and writing down the numbers afterward.

He started with Edran, because Edran was the obvious one and Brom distrusted obvious things until he had walked round them. He found him at the high windows at the second bell, building the third structure on his off arm and letting it go, building it and letting it go, and he waited at the rail until Edran had finished his count. Cael was not there. He heard it from Brom that night, at the scarred table, in Brom's careful way of reporting a conversation, which left out nothing that had been said and added nothing that had not.

"I asked him if he'd say what he saw," said Brom. "At the hearing, if they call for witnesses of fact. The exhibition, the match, any sitting he watched." He turned his cup round on the table. "He said he'd say what he saw. He said he wouldn't say what he thought it was, because he doesn't know what it was, and he's not going to stand up in front of a magistrate and guess." Brom considered the cup. "I told him that was exactly what I wanted. He looked at me as if I'd paid him a compliment he hadn't expected and didn't know what to do with."

"You had," said Lira.

"I had," Brom agreed.

Hobb took less time. Brom found him on the defensive floor and asked him how many sittings he had stood in as the partner, and Hobb said four, and Brom asked him whether anything had been different in any of them, and Hobb thought about it for a long while and said, "Same as last time." Then, after a pause, "Four times." Brom wrote that down and thanked him, and Hobb nodded once and went back to his chalk, and Brom said afterward that it was the best testimony he had ever heard and that the magistrate would be lucky to get it.

Gerda came to find Brom herself, which surprised him. She stopped at the end of the scarred table on the Monday evening with her coat buttoned and her hands folded, and told him in her procedure voice exactly how a witness of fact was entered. It went by name, in writing, with the hour each witness's evidence was expected, delivered to the magistrate's office through the academy's counsel no later than the day the delegation came. Every word of it was set down separately, as if she were reading a clause.

"How do you know that?" said Brom.

"I filed one once," said Gerda. "At the other place. I entered two witnesses. Neither of them came." She looked at him levelly. "Yours will come. Make sure the names are spelled the way the people spell them. They'll strike a witness for a wrong letter."

Oona came on the Tuesday.

She stood in front of Brom at the end of the stable with her slate in both arms and her new tag on its string, and said that she had been on the front bench at the fifth sitting and had seen the light under his hand, closer than the panel, and that she would say so to any magistrate who asked her.

Brom looked at her for a long time.

"No," he said, as gently as Cael had ever heard him say anything.

Oona's chin came up. "Why not? It's true. Nobody else will say it, because nobody else believes it."

"That's one reason," said Brom. "You'd be telling the truth, and it's a truth that would start everybody in that room asking the wrong questions about the right person." He leaned his forearms on the table so that his face was nearer to hers. "And here's the other. I've made myself one rule for this, and I'm not breaking it for anybody, not even you. I won't call anybody who loves him."

"I don't love him," said Oona at once. "I study him."

"That's how it starts," said Brom.

Oona looked at him with her whole serious face, and then, slowly, the corner of her mouth moved, and she looked away.

"A magistrate can't tell from the back of a room," said Brom, "whether somebody's saying a thing because it's so or because they'd like it to be. Not when the witness cares. So I'm only calling people who'd rather he lost and saw him anyway, or didn't care either way and saw him anyway. Edran. Hobb. People who'll stand up and be dull." He straightened. "You're not dull. You'd be the least dull thing in the room. That's why you're in the gallery."

Oona considered this.

"I'll be in the gallery," she said. "On the front bench."

"I'd expect nothing less."

She went off with her slate held tight, and Brom watched her go, and then looked across the table at Cael.

"There's something else," he said, lower. "The Warden's aide was in here at supper yesterday. The young one with the satchels. He sat with the second-years by the fire and bought the tout a heel of bread, and asked him what people said about the fifth sitting." Brom turned his cup. "He wasn't asking about the match. He was asking about the story. The one Lira's been squashing. *The observer made fire.*"

Cael did not say anything.

"I'm not telling you what to do about it," said Brom. "I'm telling you it's being asked."

---

Lira came for him on the eighth day, the Wednesday, as she had promised and without any appointment, at an hour Prynn's card on the door marked *quiet study*. She stood in the doorway of the archive with the look of somebody who had read the card and made her decision about it anyway.

"Yard," she said. "Now. You've got ink on your face, and your shoulders are up round your ears again."

"I'm halfway through the enforcement digests."

"You're halfway to being a chair." She held the iron-banded door open with one hand. "A week ago I told a room full of people I'd keep you a fighter. It wasn't a remark about some day. Yard."

The drills at dusk were brief and old: patterns from his first year, footwork his feet could have found asleep, the evasion frames they had run in Ardenmere long before Greyvane was a word either of them knew. It was not training for anything, and neither of them pretended it was. It was upkeep on something they did not name. By the third sequence he could feel how much the week had quietly taken out of him while he was looking at pages instead. There was a stiffness all down his back, and a lag of a tenth of a beat in his pivot, and his eyes kept wanting to fix on one point instead of softening to take in the whole floor.

"There you are," she said, when the lag closed at last, somewhere in the fourth sequence. "You went missing round about Saturday. I thought I'd better come and see whether the archive had kept you."

"The argument needs the archive."

"The argument needs the arguer." She stepped back, the drill done, breathing easily. "You keep calling this a fight, and you're studying it as a fight, and you're half right. But nobody gets ready for an opponent by giving him every hour of their body for three weeks. You study him. And then you eat, and you sleep, and you go on being yourself, so that what steps out onto the floor on the day is the fighter who put in the year. Not a boy who spent three weeks hunched at a table rehearsing which face to wear."

She tossed him the cloth from the pump for the ink.

He wiped his face, and stood in the cold with the cloth in his hand, and told her what he had been carrying since Brom's report at the scarred table.

"The sixth sitting's tomorrow," he said. "Quenna posted it on Monday. And she posted a note under it." He had read it a dozen times; he did not need to look. *Under the charter's procedure for a challenge filed and pending, the Compact's officer of record may attend the respondent's assessment as an observer, without voice. The Warden has given notice that he will attend.* "He'll be on a bench by the wall, writing."

"I said he would be," said Lira. "In the provost's office. I'd have wagered barley on it, if Brom would ever let anybody wager barley."

"I'm going to show the new one."

Lira went very still.

He had known she would. He held the cloth and waited, and she looked at him across the frozen ring for a long moment with her breath going up white, and he saw every argument she had arrive in her face one after another, and saw her set each one down in front of him to look at before she said any of them.

"Show enough to pass," she said at last. "Bank everything that matters. That's been the rule since the first sitting. That's the rule that's kept you on that track for twenty weeks."

"I know."

"And you want to show him the one thing that's newest and least understood and most yours, while he sits there writing."

"Not him," said Cael. "The record." He folded the cloth. "Three hundred people saw something in the formal yard. Most of them saw a lattice fall down. A few saw a light under my hand. There's a story going round the stable that the observer made fire, and you've spent a month squashing it, and you can't squash it any more, because now a young man with satchels is buying bread for the tout to hear it told." He looked at her. "In three weeks, that story is going to be in the assembly room. If it's still only a story, then it's whatever Coss says it is. A mystery. Something the academy hid. Something nobody's ever written down because nobody dared to. A mystery can be made into anything."

Lira said nothing.

"If it's on the record, it's small," he said. "It's a thing with a size, that does the same thing three times when I say it will, at contact, with a rest between. Consistent, observable practice. That's all the provision has ever asked of me. It asks what I can do. It doesn't ask where any of it came from. It never has." He turned the cloth over in his hands. "Wray told me before the match. Nothing should be spent that doesn't have to be. Spend it on purpose. I think this is on purpose."

Lira looked at the ring for a long time, at the chalk circles from the last night's lattice still faintly showing under the frost.

"You've thought about the cost," she said. "Not the bill. The other cost."

"Once it's on the record, it's on the record. Somebody reads it one day who wants me gone." He thought of Quenna in the empty hall after the first sitting. *The assessments that matter come later.* "That's what she told me after the very first one. That this was the one that would be read. I think she's been waiting for it the whole term."

"And Quenna's question." Lira did not look away from the ring. "Whether you can not do it. In front of people who'd like you gone."

"This isn't an answer to that."

"No," said Lira. "It isn't." She was quiet for a while. "It's the other half of it, maybe. Doing it exactly as much as you said, and not one hair more, with him watching." She bent and picked up the coil of rope from where she had dropped it by the pump, and began to wind it. "Tell Quenna before. Tonight. Not at the chalk."

"Before."

"And Cael." She hung the rope on its nail. "Three times. Not four. I don't care if the panel asks for four."

He went up the narrow stair at the back of the main hall at the seventh bell, and knocked, and told Quenna in her small tidy room with the cold grate what he meant to do at the first bell, and why. She heard all of it without moving, her hands flat on her desk.

"I'll write what I see," she said, when he had finished. "No more. You know how I write."

"I know how you write."

"You'll name the size before you begin. You'll do it the same each time. You'll say nothing about where it came from, because nobody will ask you, because the provision doesn't." She looked at him for a long moment. "It isn't an answer to my question."

"No," said Cael. "It's the other half of it. Lira said so."

Something moved at the corner of Quenna's mouth, and was gone.

"Lira," said Quenna, "is wasted on a re-certification track." She opened a folder and took out a clean sheet and laid it in front of her. "The first bell. Come rested. You never are."

---

The sixth sitting was in the training hall, as the first two had been, with the six sections swept back to their pale ghosts and a single ring laid fresh at the centre, and three chairs at the far end on their chalk ticks.

The benches along the wall were full, which they had not been for a closed session since the autumn. The notice on the main board had brought the whole school to wonder what a sitting looked like with a man from the Compact at the back of it, and the clerk had let in as many as the benches would hold and then shut the door. Quenna sat in the middle chair with her slate. Wray was on her left in the brown coat. The rota had given the right-hand chair to the broad-faced woman from the movement floor who had sat the second sitting, pleasant and unhurried, with her pointer laid across her knees, and Cael found that he was glad of her, and could not have said why.

Coss sat on the end of the last bench by the wall, with his aide beside him and a document case open on his knee, a pen in his hand and a clean sheet on the case. He looked exactly as he had looked in the provost's office: patient, tidy, in no hurry, wholly attentive. He did not look round at the benches or up at the high windows. He looked at the ring.

Hobb was at the north mark with his feet a shoulder-width apart and his hands loose. When Cael came to the south mark Hobb looked at him once, and it was the workman's look again, the one that said they had done this job before and would do it properly.

"For the record," said Quenna, and the hall went flat and slow. "Demonstration assessment, unclassified observer track. Sixth sitting. Present: the presiding assessor, the instructor's seat, the rotating faculty seat. Present also, under the charter's procedure, the Compact's officer of record, as an observer without voice." She did not look up from her slate. "The candidate demonstrates at his own election. Begin when you choose."

He showed the framework, four passes at panel speed, and Wray broke the count on the third as she always did, and he read Hobb's hips through the broken rhythm as easily as through the steady one. He showed the read, four calls at two paces, the last with his eyes shut, and Hobb's weight went where Cael said it would go every time, and on the fourth Hobb did not try to change his mind halfway, which Cael understood was a courtesy. It was exactly what he had shown five times before. He had built it to be.

The panel did not confer. Wray wrote her line, and Cael could have recited it with her.

"Does the candidate elect to demonstrate anything further?" said Quenna.

The procedure allowed the question at any sitting. In five sittings Quenna had never once asked it, because she had never needed to, and the hall knew the shape of a sitting well enough to know it was over. Somebody on the near bench had already started to stand up.

"Yes," said Cael.

The person who had stood up sat down again.

"The candidate will state what he elects to show," said Quenna, in exactly the voice she used for everything else on the record, "and the panel will take note before he begins."

"An ignition at contact," said Cael. "On the training post. A point the width of my thumbnail, where my palm rests. Three times, the same size each time, with a rest between of about a hundred count. It needs the rest." He heard his own voice in the high hall, level, ordinary, the voice of a candidate describing a footwork pattern. "The post should stand in sand. There should be a bucket."

Wray had already risen. She nodded to Hobb, and Hobb went to the corner of section four without a word and came back carrying the oak post in its iron foot as easily as a man carries a chair. Wray herself fetched a bucket of sand from the store by the door and set it down beside the post, and a scoop beside the bucket, and stood back.

The hall had gone so quiet that he could hear the rotating seat's pointer creak as she shifted it on her knees.

He did not look at the bench by the wall. *Don't look at anything that isn't her*, Gerda had told him before the match, and there was no her this morning, only a post. He looked at the post. He knelt by it on his right knee, the bad arm in its loose sleeve held against his side, and laid his right palm flat on the top of the oak, and chose the size first, a thumbnail and not a hair more, fixed in his mind like a mark scored in wood.

Then he turned inward toward the warmth, and asked it, and let go.

The candle that was not lit and then was. He lifted his hand.

A ring of char the width of his thumbnail, black at the centre and brown at its edge, and a bead of ordinary yellow flame coming up in the middle of it. Hobb leaned over with the scoop and put it out, and pressed the sand down, and held it for a count of five.

The cold came after, under his breastbone and down his arm into his hand, a stair that was not there in the dark, and he did not let it reach his face. He knelt with his grey fingertips on his knee and waited, and counted, silently, as Karis had counted for him in the quiet room. Behind the panel's chairs he could feel the whole hall not breathing. At the back, by the wall, very faintly, he heard a pen moving.

At a hundred and two the warmth came back. He laid his palm on the post a hand's width from the first ring, chose the size, turned, asked, let go.

The second ring was the same size as the first.

Hobb sanded it. The cold came. Cael counted. At the end of the count he laid his palm a hand's width further along the oak and did it a third time, and lifted his hand, and the third ring sat beside the other two on the grey wood, all three the width of a thumbnail, all three black at the centre and brown at the edge, as alike as three coins from the same mould.

He stood up. His right hand was grey to the second knuckle. He put it in his pocket.

The rotating seat leaned forward on her chair, with her pointer across her knees, and looked at the post for a long moment.

"The panel may ask questions of the candidate at its discretion," said Quenna.

"Range?" said the broad-faced woman.

"Contact. A hair off it at most. A finger's width off, nothing."

"Duration?"

"Once," said Cael. "Then it's the wood's. It isn't held. Once it's lit it isn't mine any more."

She looked at the post again.

"One at a time?"

"One at a time. Then the rest. Then one more."

The woman nodded slowly, as people nod who have taught a Path for a long time and have just been given a fact about one they had not met before, and sat back, and wrote.

The panel conferred at the chairs, low and brief. Wray spoke first, without any change in her voice.

"Ignition at contact, three of three, at the stated size. No safety concerns."

Then Quenna wrote, for longer than she usually wrote, and read it out in the flat even voice of the record, and he understood as she read it that she had drafted every word of it the night before in her tidy room with the cold grate, and had then sat and taken words out until there was nothing left that anybody could take hold of.

"The candidate demonstrated, at his own election, an ignition-point capability, contact-adjacent, single channel. Three times, at a size stated in advance, with a rest between. Consistent, observable practice. No safety violation noted. Assessment satisfied."

That was all. There was not a false word in it, and not one that told anybody anything about where it had come from, because nobody had asked, because the provision did not.

At the back of the hall, by the wall, Coss had stopped writing. He sat with his pen lifted and his eyes on the post, on the three small black rings in a row on the grey oak, and his face did not change at all. Then he wrote one more line, and capped the pen, and closed the document case, and the aide beside him closed his own. As the benches began to empty Coss stood, and came along the wall to the end of the panel's table, and stopped, and spoke to Quenna, and not to Cael.

"Entered as of today's date, Assessor?"

"As of today's date, Warden."

Coss nodded, once. Then he looked across the swept boards at Cael, for the length of perhaps a breath, with the same patience he had brought to a borrowed desk in Ardenmere. He did not say anything. He went out by the north door with his aide a half-step behind him and his document case under his arm.

Cael stood in the ring with his grey hand in his pocket and the cold still sitting under his breastbone, and watched him go.

Lira was at the rail when he came out, as she always was. She looked at his face, and then at his pocket, and then at his face again.

"I didn't see anything," she said. "You looked like you were thinking about your dinner."

"I know."

"I'll never get used to that," said Lira, and took his arm, the good one, and walked him to the stable for breakfast he did not want and ate.

---

The stable was louder at the midday meal than it had been since the match, and the noise went round their corner as it always did, without coming into it.

"It's dead," said Lira, sitting down at the scarred table with her bowl and his bread. "The story. *The observer made fire.* You killed it this morning. I watched it die on the benches." She tore the bread. "Nobody can tell it any more, because now it's on the record, and the record's the dullest place a story can go. Three little black marks on a post. A size. A count. The second-years were bored by the time Hobb fetched the sand."

"I wasn't bored," said Brom.

"You're never bored. You're a different case."

Karis had her notebook shut beside her bowl and her hands folded on top of it, and she had been quiet since the hall.

"It's on the record as a thing with a size," she said. "That's the best place for a thing to be, if it has to be anywhere." She turned her cup round. "I said in my consent that if it worked, the documentation mattered. I meant my documentation. I didn't think about yours." She looked at him. "Quenna's sentence is better than anything I'd have written. There's nothing in it to pull on."

"Coss stopped writing," said Cael. "At the end. Before the last line."

"I saw," said Karis. "He was looking at the post. Not at you." She was quiet a moment. "He'll read the record more times than anybody. He'll find exactly what's in it, and nothing else, because there's nothing else there to find."

Edran came past the end of the table on his way to the hatch with his bowl, and slowed, as Hobb slowed at rails, and did not quite stop.

"Three of three," he said, without looking at anybody. "Same size. If they ask me, that's what I saw." He went on to the hatch.

Oona was waiting by the stable door when they went out, with her slate under her arm and her tag polished. She had been on the front bench of the near wall; he had seen her there out of the corner of his eye without letting himself look.

"It's written down now," she said to him. "In the record. Three times." Her chin came up. "Nobody on my landing can say I didn't know where to look."

"Nobody can."

"Good," said Oona. She wrote something on her slate and underlined it twice, and for once she turned the slate round and showed him. It said *THREE OF THREE. I SAW THE FIRST ONE.* Then she tucked it under her arm and went off toward the lecture wing, walking very straight.

---

He began the outline on the tenth day, the Friday, and spent most of the eleventh making himself admit what it actually said.

His first try, that morning, was the argument that the enrollment was sound after all. It was a fallback, a second line to fall back to, the reflex of a fighter who never walks onto a floor without knowing a second way off it. He got most of a page into it. Then he stopped, and drew one long diagonal line through the whole page, and sat a long while with the notebook shut in front of him while Prynn's pen scratched at the high desk.

The enrollment could not be defended. He had known it since his first afternoon at Greyvane, over cold tea, when Quenna had shown him the category and said without any softening that the form's words and the truth beneath them were not the same. To stand up and say otherwise would be to defend a falsehood in front of somebody whose whole trade was hearing falsehoods defended. He would lose, and deserve to. Worse than losing, it would spend the magistrate's patience at the start and poison everything he said after it. A fallback that weakened the main position was not a way off the floor. It was a hole in his own guard, cut with his own hand.

So there could be no second argument. The first would have to stand as Brom stood: nothing in it for show, nothing he could not hold with the whole weight on it, and every joint either carrying load or cut away.

He opened the notebook again and began the outline over, and this time he wrote first the sentence he had been walking round since the night he circled the word.

*I'm not going to argue that my enrollment is valid. I'm going to agree that it isn't, and then ask on what authority the Compact proposes to void it. Their authority runs only to the classified. Classification means the schedule, for all purposes. And the word they wrote next to my name in Denvash is nowhere in the schedule, and never has been.*

An argument was worth exactly as much as the record it would be laid against. So he went to the fourth shelf of the second case and took down the enforcement digests, and spent two days learning what the Compact had in fact done, every time, to anybody like him.

There were four.

That was the whole of the recorded history. Four practitioners, scattered down the centuries, who had drawn the same flag from the same instrument and been written into the digests under it, each with the enforcement chapters' usual care. He read all four files through, slowly, from the first sheet to the last. He did not hurry. In the whole written record they were the only four who had ever stood in his place, and reading them carefully was the smallest thing he could give them.

Nobody had spoken for any of them. None had been given a hearing of any kind. In every case the removal had run on an administrative order, unopposed and unanswered, complete within a matter of days: a notice, a courier, a door, a road. He knew the shape of it. He had walked it.

Three of the four files simply stopped, the way files stop once the system has decided their subjects are not worth more ink. The last of the four closed on one line of notation. He read it six times. Then he copied it out whole into the back of the observation notebook in his own hand, every word of it. It was the entire life of a person, folded down by procedure into eleven words, and he did not mean to let himself forget it.

None of the four had ever been given the chance to answer. He would be the first who was.

He wrote that down too, and found that his hand was not entirely steady while he did it, and let the unsteadiness go into the letters instead of waiting for it to pass. Vell had taught him at the Ironyard that if the person keeping a record hid what they felt, the record was only half kept, and he had kept her rule ever since.

Alone at the long table, with the lamp low and the founding shelves dark behind him, he understood at last the full size of what he meant to do.

A win would not only keep one borrowed enrollment at one minor academy. It would crack the machine, in public, on a record with his own name across it. Every office of the Compact that had ever expelled or watched or flagged somebody under that word would have to face, in the end, that a boy of fifteen had read their code with more care than they ever had. And every official made to face it would learn his name, since the record that held the argument would hold the one who had made it.

It was the kind of argument that made enemies instead of losing politely. It was also, as far as anything he had read could tell him, the one argument that happened to be true.

He sat with the outline until the lamp had burned down to the hour Prynn would ordinarily have closed her ledger. She did not close it. She sat on at the high desk with her pen still, and did not look at him, and did not go.

Then he picked the pen up and went on writing.

---

Karis came to the archive on the Saturday evening, after her own day on the founding shelves, with dust on her cuffs and a lamp in one hand and nothing at all in the other. She stood at his shoulder for a while without speaking, reading what he had open on the table, which was the schedule.

"May I?" she said at last.

He moved the volume a little toward her.

"Not that," she said. "The whole of it. From the beginning. On my own." She pulled out the chair beside his and sat down in it, and set the lamp between them. "You've read it three times and found nothing there. I believe you. But you've read it looking for one word, and anybody who reads looking for one thing will see the shape of it in places it isn't, or miss it in a place it is." She opened the first volume of the schedule at its first page. "I'll read it as if I'd never heard of you. If I find it, you're wrong, and we'll know in time. If I don't, there are two of us who didn't."

"That could take you a week."

"Then it'll take me a week." She drew her lamp nearer. "The founding shelves are mine in the day. This is mine in the evenings. I don't need much sleep. I never have." She did not look up from the page. "It isn't the argument, before you say it. It's a second reading. That's a different thing. Any finding worth having has two."

He sat and watched her read for a while, her pen lying capped beside the book, her lips moving very slightly on the clause numbers, her whole attention gone into the page as it went into a point in its last third of a breath. Then he went back to his outline. They worked side by side at the long table without speaking until Prynn's lamp at the high desk had burned down to its last finger of oil. She did not put it out.

---

He wrote the log that night at the desk by the window, with the observation notebook open beside the binder and the outline folded into its back pocket, next to Karis's consent and her soft-cornered paragraph, and the fair copy of the sixth sitting's record, which Quenna had given him on the stair without a word.

*Twentieth week. The sixth sitting: the new one shown, at my own election, three of three at a thumbnail, with the count between. Q.'s record: an ignition-point capability, contact-adjacent, single channel. Consistent, observable. C. watched and wrote, and stopped, and wrote one line more. He asked the date. He didn't ask anything else. Nobody did.*

*The outline concedes everything and keeps one thing. No fallback. Built like B.'s stance.*

*Four files. Three end. The fourth has eleven words, copied in the back of the other notebook. Nobody answered for any of them.*

*K. is reading the schedule from the beginning, in the evenings, as if she'd never heard of me.*

He looked at that for a while, and then turned to a clean page, and wrote in the hand he kept for the things that mattered.

*The code is architecture. Architecture has seams. I've spent the better part of two years learning where the joins are in things that have them: a fighter's stance, a Path's timing, and now a system's rules about what it may do to people. It's the same work. The difference is that nobody limps away from it.*

He read the last line, and then, for the sake of accuracy, he put the pen back to the page.

*It leaves them later. On whoever loses.*

He closed the binder. Outside the narrow window the grounds had gone fully dark, the settled winter dark of the twentieth week, and down the hill the town's lamps were going out one at a time. The hearing was the week after next. He had his seam. He had four files belonging to four people who had never been allowed to stand where he would stand. What remained was to make the argument strong enough to bear all of him in front of a panel of the Compact, and he had a week of evenings and a second reader at his shoulder to make it with.

He put out the lamp. In the morning there would be four bowls at the end of the scarred table, and Lira would already have taken his bread.

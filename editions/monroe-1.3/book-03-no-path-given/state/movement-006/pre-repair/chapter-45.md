# Chapter 45 — The Wrong Road

The third day went entirely on a wrong turn, and he took it before he took the right one.

The obvious road was the one anybody would have taken, and he took it. It was the demonstration provision itself: its drafting, its amendments, every enrollment that had ever leaned on its words. If the challenge was that he had been fitted into a category not built for him, then surely the place to fight was the category, and the place to start was the moment it was made. He asked Prynn for the provision's history at the first bell, and she looked at him over her spectacles for a moment longer than she needed to and then went and got it without a word, which he understood only later was the most she would ever do to warn a reader off a shelf.

It was all there, in the order it had grown. The provision had been revised three times, and each revision sat in its fascicle with its date and its reasons attached in the flat language of the drafting clerks. He read the newest first, out of habit, and then made himself go back and read them in order, as Prynn had told him to. When he reached the bottom of the oldest box he found the first draft, written in a hand so old that the letters leaned against each other like tired men.

It had been written as a kindness.

He sat with that for a long time in the cold. In those days the guilds had sent their families travelling, carters and drovers and pedlars on a guild licence, and their children came to fourteen on the road, weeks from any station. They arrived at the gates of the academies untested and unplaced and turned away, because a chartered academy could not take a child nobody had tested. Somebody had minded. Somebody had sat at a desk with a pen and written a few lines so that such a child could come in under watching, and be taught, and be kept warm, until the family could get the child to a station and find out what the child was. That was the whole of what it was for. Every revision after it had widened who it covered by a little, a late Kindler here, a disputed assessment there. Not one of them had changed what it was for.

He thought of Oona on the bench in the grey building, with her mother's hand on one shoulder and his on the other, trembling very finely under the clean grey cloth. He thought of her coming out through the far door with the card held flat against her slate, and her face coming loose over her mother's shoulder. She had come up the hill to Greyvane untested from a guild family, and been taught, and been kept warm, and gone down to the station when the time came and been found to be exactly who she had decided to be. Whoever had written the first draft in that leaning hand had written it for her. They had never imagined him at all.

He walked the road for eleven hours. He traced every revision and every enrollment the digests recorded under it, every late Kindler and disputed child and clerical error the provision had ever sheltered. He was looking for one single case, anywhere, of somebody already Kindled and classified who had been carried by it and allowed to stay. There was none. The road went nowhere. However generously anybody read it, the provision had been written for the untested, and he had been tested, and no amount of its history could change what its words said or what its first drafter had meant by them. Coss had walked this same road weeks ago, he was certain of it. Coss had found the box in the basement, or wherever the regional office kept its copy, and had read the leaning hand, and had walked all the way to the end. Coss would be standing there waiting for him, in the assembly room, with the first draft in his document case.

At the eighth bell he drew one long diagonal line through eleven hours of notes and wrote under it, in the square hand:

*Stop studying my own position. Study his. What is his authority standing on?*

He sat back and looked at the line. It was the same correction Lira had driven into him in a cold yard in Ardenmere, a lifetime ago, in a different language: *stop watching your own feet.* He had spent a whole day watching his own feet.

Prynn came down off her stool and took the provision's boxes away without being asked, and put them back on their shelf, and came back with a cup of tea, and set it down at his elbow.

"That road," she said, "goes nowhere."

"You knew."

"I've known for forty years. I've certified forty copies of it, and read every one of them aloud." She looked at the crossed-out pages. "I'd have told you if you'd asked. You didn't ask. You wanted to walk it." She went back to her stool. "Most people have to. It's a good road. It's only the wrong one."

---

The shape of it, once he could see the shape at all, was very nearly elegant.

He drew it again on the fourth morning, on a clean page, from the top, the way he had drawn it for Prynn as a declaration tree, but this time asking a different question of every part of it. Not *what does this say?* but *what does this stand on?*

The charter was at the top. It was the founding document, the thing the Compact existed because of, and it was wide and high-minded as founding documents are, full of phrases like *the stewardship of practice* and *the ordered development of the Paths*, which bound nobody to anything you could measure. Under the charter was the schedule, the working list, amended and amended again down the centuries, naming each Path, each tier, and each provisional or disputed category the Compact had ever put its formal name to. Under the schedule came the obligations, all the things a classified person owed once the system had given them a name: to register, to be assessed, to behave, the whole quiet harness that came with being part of a thing, asked or not. And under everything, at the very bottom, where the teeth were, was enforcement. Voidance, sanction, removal: the machinery that had walked a fourteen-year-old out of Hesk's workshop in Denvash in the course of one courier's morning.

Four layers, each one resting its whole weight on the one above it. Enforcement rested on the obligations. The obligations fell on the classified. Classification was made by the schedule. And the schedule drew its life from the charter.

A structure like that, like any structure, stood on whatever held up its top. If the thing that held up the top would not bear the weight, nothing under it stood either. It did not matter how solid it looked from across a field, or for how many lifetimes it had stood there with nobody ever leaning on it to find out.

He went back into the charter, looking for the place where it said what classification was.

The fourth morning found it for him, in the definitions clause of the charter, the first article after the preamble. The words were so plain that his eyes slid over them twice before he felt what they were carrying. He had been reading for something clever, something hidden, and it was neither.

*Classification, for all purposes under this charter and its subordinate provisions, shall mean designation under the Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings, as amended.*

He read it a third time, aloud, very quietly, because Prynn had taught him that ears were more honest.

*Shall mean.* It did not say *shall include*, or *shall be taken broadly as*, or *shall be understood in the light of*, or any of the soft wide phrases the middle centuries had been so fond of. The charter did not describe what classification was. It defined it. It shut the word like a door and sealed it, and left the schedule as the one opening in the whole wall: no category could reach the Compact's authority except by passing through it, and the Compact's authority could reach nobody who had not. *For all purposes.* Every power in every enforcement chapter he had read in two nights by lamplight, every *over classified practitioners* and *according to classification*, reached back through that sentence to the schedule. They could reach nowhere else.

That was why the schedule had no scars.

That was why the schedule carried no scars. Nobody had ever gone at it. It was not a wall at all; it was the ground the wall stood on. Nobody hits the ground.

---

He spent the next two days and most of two nights on the schedule itself.

He read it as he had never read anything, and he had spent the better part of two years learning to read things that did not want to be read. He took every Path family and every tier designation. He took every provisional category the Compact had ever tried out before making up its mind: Contested-Standing, Interim-Assessed, Dual-Registered, and half a dozen more that had come and gone down the generations, as the Compact's notion of what a Path even was kept revising itself, slowly and clumsily. He read the amendments to the schedule in order, as Prynn had taught him, and watched categories born in one generation's confidence and struck out in another's embarrassment. Even the struck ones were still there on the page, ruled through and still readable, because the code kept its own mistakes as carefully as it kept its rules. Every category had a name. Every one was written down, in the schedule itself, in the flat clerical language that makes a thing real in law and not only real in the world.

He looked for the word they had written next to his name in Denvash.

It was not there.

Then he went through it again, more slowly, in case some subsection or appendix had got past him, or some cross-reference hidden in the margin of an amendment. After that he made a list of every place in the code where a category could lawfully be kept. There was the main enumeration, and the provisional annex, and the appendix of disputed standings. There were the amendment schedules, one for every revision, and the definitions cross-index at the back of the charter. He went through them one at a time, as he would go through every answer an opponent might have, and crossed each off, until nothing was left to guess at, only what remained. It had not got past him.

On the sixth night he read the whole schedule a third time, by lamplight, from the first page to the last, with Prynn at the high desk silently refilling his cup at an hour when no duty on earth required her to still be in the building. The third reading said what the second had. Nowhere in the schedule, in all its generations of piled-up text, did the word appear. Not once. Not as a footnote, not as an entry struck out and kept in the record of its own repeal, and not as something once proposed and voted down. The schedule kept its refusals as well. He had come across three: categories somebody had argued for and lost, each refusal written up with the same clerical care as everything else.

His word was not even among the rejected ones.

It existed nowhere in the code. It existed only in what an Arbiter's instrument wrote out at the moment of a Kindling, a flag the instrument itself produced. Generation after generation of enforcement had simply treated that flag as if it were a classification, because it looked like one and sat where one sat on the form, and because nobody in all that time, so far as he could tell, had ever thought to go and look.

For a long while he sat still with it and did not write anything.

He did not trust it. It was too big to believe the first time it was seen, and the worst bouts of his life had all been bouts where he had thrown himself at a seam before he had made certain it was there. Somewhere there had to be the page he had missed: in this building, or in some records hall of the Compact three districts away. There had to be an amendment, or an annex, or an enabling order that made the flag lawful. A system did not run for centuries on nothing.

Except that it did. He knew that it did. On the Ardenmere circuit he had seen whole generations of fighters live by rules that no charter had ever set down. He had sat through a market interview conducted on an authority that not one person in the square could have named. At fourteen he had seen his own life turned off its road by a single morning's procedure, which every person present had called law, because every person in every room had always called it law. Custom in law's coat was nothing unusual where he came from. So far as he could tell, it was what most of the world had on.

He wrote it down.

---

"You've stopped moving," said Prynn, from the high desk, in the voice of a woman who had been quietly watching his stillness for considerably longer than she was going to admit.

"I've found something."

"When people tell me that in here, they've usually found a word spelt two ways and decided it means something." She did not look up from her ledger. "Which is it?"

He turned the notebook round on the table so that she could see the page. It held the four layers, the definitions clause copied out beside them word for word, and the list of every place a category could live, with every one of them ticked. At the bottom, on its own, circled, with nothing joining it to anything above it, was the word from Denvash.

Prynn came down off her stool and came round the long table and looked at it.

"The schedule's closed," said Cael. "Enumerated. Every category the Compact has ever reached for is in it, by name, because putting it in the schedule is the only way the code lets the Compact reach for anything. The charter says *classification shall mean designation under the schedule*. Not resembles. Not implies. Means. If the schedule doesn't list a category, there's nothing there to enforce, because a category only exists to be enforced once the schedule says it does." He put one finger beside the circled word. "And this isn't there. It never was. It's what the instrument writes, and a few hundred years of everybody treating it as if it were in the schedule. And custom isn't code."

Prynn looked at the page for much longer than the argument, on its face, should have needed. She read the list of places, and the ticks beside them. She read the definitions clause twice, her lips moving a little on the second reading, as they moved when she was matching a citation to the shelf where she remembered it living. When she looked up, something in her face had changed. It was not surprise. It was the quiet stillness of somebody deciding afresh how seriously to take a fifteen-year-old.

"Sixty years I've kept these shelves," she said at last, as dry as the dust she put up with and never complained of. "You're the first person to read the code the way it's written instead of the way it's enforced."

He looked at her.

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation. It turns into a compliment or it doesn't, depending on what you do next." She set his cup down, square, in the small clear space he had left for it at the corner of the table, as she had been setting it down every night for a week without either of them mentioning it. "When people dig up a seam that deep, they generally run straight off to hire somebody to argue it. It's worth asking yourself why you haven't."

"There isn't a lawyer within three districts who'd take it," said Cael. "And if there were, it would turn into the lawyer's argument. The magistrate would weigh it as a professional's trick, something bought and paid for. It has to come from me, or it sounds like a trick instead of a reading."

"That's the clever answer." Prynn folded her hands in front of her. "I asked for the true one."

For a moment he did not answer. Inside its brass the lamp was cooling, and it ticked.

"Everything that's ever been decided about me was decided without asking me," he said. "Assessors and clerks and a station in Denvash and a courier with a satchel, and a man in a borrowed office in Ardenmere, and somebody above him I've never met signing things I've never seen. Every argument anybody's ever made about what I am was made over my head." He looked at the circled word. "If somebody else stands up in that room and makes this one for me, then the one argument that says nobody should have spoken for me will have been spoken for me as well. I've read it all now. So I get to say it myself."

Prynn was silent for so long that he thought the conversation had ended, and that she had merely decided not to go back to her stool yet. Then she picked up the empty tea tin from the end of the table and turned toward her desk.

"Fourth shelf, second case," she said, without looking round. "The enforcement digests. The full run, every case they ever brought under the enforcement chapters." She stopped by the high desk. "If it's to be your own voice, then you'd better have read every case they'll throw back at it."

---

Karis came to the scarred table late at supper on the sixth day, before he went back down to the archive for the night, with dust to the elbows of her coat and a smear of something grey along one cheekbone that she had plainly not noticed. She sat down at the fourth bowl and ate her barley in silence for a while, the way she ate when her head was somewhere else, and Lira slid the larger half of a heel of bread down the table to her without a word.

"I've been on the founding shelves for five days," Karis said at last, to her bowl. "I'll tell you what I've found, because it's fair you should know, and then I'll tell you what I haven't."

"Go on," said Lira.

"I've found how they wrote." She set her spoon down. "Before the code was put in the order it's in now, the registers weren't boxes. They were sentences. An assessor at a waystation would write a whole paragraph about a person: what they'd seen, what they thought, what they couldn't decide. Some of them argued with themselves on the page. Some of them crossed things out and wrote *I was wrong about this* in the margin a year later." She looked at Cael. "Nobody does that now. Now there's a box, and you're either in it or you're a flag."

"And what haven't you found?"

"Anybody like you." Karis picked up the bread. "Not yet. I've read four hundred entries and every one of them is somebody the old words could hold. Ash and Stone and Current. People nobody had any trouble naming." She tore the bread in half. "That isn't a finding. It's the shape of a shelf I haven't finished. There are a great many shelves."

"The hearing can't use any of it," said Cael, and heard himself say it, and stopped.

Karis looked at him across the table with something very like amusement.

"You've said that four times this week," she said. "I've counted. I think you say it to remind yourself that your search is the one with a date on it." She ate a piece of the bread. "Mine hasn't got a date. That's why I can afford to be thorough. Eat your barley. Brom's watching it."

"I'm always watching it," said Brom. "It's never once let me down."

---

On the Tuesday of the twentieth week, the seventh day, Brom came to the archive door at the end of the fifth bell and stood there without coming in, because Prynn did not like people who came in only to fetch other people out.

"Wray's building something," he said, when Cael came to the door. "This afternoon. The sixth bell, on the defensive floor." He looked at the floor of the passage. "I'd like you to watch it. You read my wall before anybody but her did. I'd like somebody at the rail who can see where it is."

Cael went.

The defensive floor was the long hall beyond the training hall where the hardening students worked, with its own pairings board outside the door and its own rail down one side. Wray stood at the edge of the chalk in her brown coat, buttoned to the throat, with her slate on her hip. Four people stood round the ring at four marks, and Cael looked at them and understood before anybody said a word that this was not the three-striker drill. Hobb was at the north mark, broad and planted, with his hands loose. Edran was at the west with his ready pair already built on his forearms, the shell pale on the left and the edge on the right. Karis was at the south with her off hand still at her side. Lira was at the east, up on the balls of her feet, the only one of the four who was smiling.

Brom stood in the middle.

"A month ago," said Wray, to Brom and to nobody else, "I sent three people at you one at a time, each faster than the last, and you saw your wait every time, and you said the second half needed somebody faster than anybody here. You were right. There's nobody on this hill who can come at you at Silver speed. I can't fetch you a Silver." She tapped the slate. "So I'm going to build one. Out of four people who aren't."

Brom said nothing. He settled his feet.

"It isn't a queue," said Wray. "That's the difference. Last time each of them came at you, and you turned it, and then the next one came. Today the next one starts before you've finished with the last. Hobb comes first, with his weight, from the front. You take it and you redirect it, and your redirect has to go somewhere. It goes into Edran's line, because Edran's already coming from the west by the time you're turning Hobb, and he's bursting a shell on your guard as he arrives. Then Karis's channel is under your feet where you have to step, because you'll have to step to turn Edran, and it'll shove up at your shins as you do. And Lira finishes it, from whatever side you've left open." She looked at the four of them and then at him. "No one of them is faster than you can read. All four of them together arrive faster than you can turn. That's what Silver is, from the inside. Too much arriving, too close together, from too many places to finish one before the next."

"How fast?" said Brom.

"I'll start slow. I'll call the intervals with my hand." She raised one finger. "Each round, I take a little out of them. When I've taken out enough, we'll see where you stop."

She lowered the finger.

Hobb came. It was his honest walking weight, both forearms up, the push of a Stone fourth-year who could not be hurried and did not mean to be, and Brom met it with the Iron Skin coming up along his forearms like frost forming on a pane and took it and gave and turned. Hobb's weight went past him, slow and sure, along the line Brom chose for it, and out toward the west, where Edran was already arriving. Edran's shell burst on Brom's guard in a spray of pale glitter. Brom took that too, turning, his feet moving to follow the turn. As his right foot came down a thin line of heat lit across the boards in exactly that place, from one of Karis's points to another. It shoved up at his shin like a stove door thrown open, and he rode it, smoking faintly at the boot, and set his feet. And Lira came in from the open east, light and quick, and her palm was a hand's width from his ribs when he turned her away.

"One," said Wray, and wrote, and raised her finger again.

They went round four times.

On the first round the intervals were long enough that Brom finished every turn before the next force touched him, and he was beautiful to watch, and Cael at the rail could see that he knew it. On the second round Wray took out perhaps a quarter of the time, and Brom finished Hobb and finished Edran, and was still finishing the channel's shove when Lira's palm landed flat on his right side below the ribs. It landed gently, at Wray's half force, but it landed.

"Touch," said Wray.

The third round was where Cael began to see it, and he saw it because he had been watching Brom's wait since the night of the null sessions. It was the small held instant between the read and the turn, the place Wray had shown with her own hand on the seventh strike. At the long intervals it did not matter, because the wait finished before anything else arrived. Now there was no room for it. Hobb's weight came, and Brom read it, and waited his instant, and turned it. In the instant while he waited, Edran was already a pace nearer than he had been the time before, and so the turn into Edran's line came a shade too late, and the burst came a shade too close, and the step onto Karis's channel came a shade too soon. Every lateness piled on the last, until by the time Lira came in, the whole of Brom's body was still busy finishing something else.

She landed. Brom took her palm on the ribs again, and stood, breathing hard, and did not move for a moment.

"There," he said. He did not say it to Wray. He said it to the floor. "I'm waiting there. Right there. After I've got it and before I send it. Every time."

"Yes," said Wray.

"I can see it." Brom looked at his own forearms, at the frost-sheen of the Iron Skin going dull. "I can see it coming at me before it comes. I know exactly where I'll be late." He looked up. "And I'm late anyway."

"Yes," said Wray. "Again."

The others had seen it too, and they were learning. Cael watched them learn, because watching people learn was the oldest thing he knew how to do. On the fourth round Karis laid her channel half a step further along the line Brom would have to take, so that its shove came up at the very moment Hobb's weight was still on his guard, two forces from two directions at once. Edran built his shell and burst it a beat early, before Brom's redirect had finished arriving, so that the burst met the turn halfway. And Lira did not wait for Brom to finish anything at all. She had found his instant on the third round, and on the fourth she came in at the start of his wait instead of at its end, so that her palm arrived in the exact space he left open while he held what he had read and before he sent it anywhere.

She landed before Brom had turned Edran.

"Four," said Wray, and wrote it, and lowered her slate. "That's enough."

Brom stood in the middle of the ring with his hands on his knees. There was a smear of pale Glass glitter along one cheekbone where Edran's burst had caught him, stinging but not cut. His boots were scorched along both toes. His right side would be one long bruise by morning where Lira had landed three times in the same place, at half force each time, which was still Lira. He breathed for a while, and nobody spoke, and the floor was very quiet except for the hiss of the last of Karis's channel going out on the boards.

Then he straightened up.

"Seen," he said. "Every time. At the speed that matters." He looked at Wray. "Not closed."

"No," said Wray. "Not closed. I can make you see it at the speed it matters. You can see it now better than anybody I've taught in twenty years. I can't make you fast enough to close it." She did not soften the next part, because she never softened anything, and Cael understood that this was its own kind of respect. "Nobody on this hill can. I can build you an imitation of Silver for four rounds out of four students who'd rather be eating their supper. To close it you'd need somebody who's Silver all day long, every day, for a year. That isn't here." She tucked the slate under her arm. "I'll keep looking. I said I would. I don't say that twice."

She went back to the chalk at the far end of the floor. The four who had made the imitation came off their marks one at a time, Hobb first, nodding once at Brom as he passed, as one workman nods to another, and then Edran, who stopped.

"You turned me cleaner than anybody's ever turned me," said Edran. "Round one. I wanted you to know."

"Round one was slow," said Brom.

"Round one was slow," Edran agreed, "and it was still the cleanest." He went out.

Karis came over to the rail where Cael was standing, and Brom came with her, slowly, wiping the glitter off his face with the back of his wrist.

"I laid the fourth one where I'd want to be stopped," said Karis. "If I'd been you. I'm sorry about your boots."

"They're Velmere boots," said Brom. "They've had worse."

Lira came last. She stood in front of Brom with her hands on her hips and looked at the side of him where she had landed, and her face was not pleased with itself.

"I found it on the third," she said. "The wait. I could hit it every time after that, the instant it opened." She was quiet a moment. "I didn't like hitting it. It's like hitting somebody who's holding a door for you."

"Then you'd better keep hitting it," said Brom, "or I'll never learn where the door is." He took his number sheet out of his coat and unfolded it against the rail, under the old column of fetch numbers and the second column he had begun on Wray's floor a month before, and wrote in his square capitals while they watched.

*GAUNTLET. FOUR STRIKERS, NO QUEUE. SEEN EVERY TIME AT THE SPEED THAT MATTERS. NOT CLOSED. LIRA IN AT THE LAST, THREE OF FOUR. — B.*

He looked at it, folded the sheet, and put it back in his coat.

Cael had been thinking, all through the fourth round, about Lira's chalk ring in the frozen yard and the night his number had stopped at four. "On the lattice," he said slowly, "Lira told me to pick faster. I was deciding in the space where I should have been moving. Is it that?"

Brom thought about it, honestly, as he thought about everything.

"No," he said. "Yours was a choosing. You had two ways and you couldn't pick, and picking faster fixed it, as near as anything does." He flexed his right hand, slowly, and watched the dull sheen go out of the skin. "Mine isn't a choosing. I know which way. I know where it's going before it's arrived. Mine's a waiting. Something in me has to finish holding it before it'll let me send it on." He looked at the floor. "You can't hurry a wait by deciding to. That's the difference. That's why it's a wall and not a habit."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"It's an honest wall," said Brom at last. "I'll say that for it. I can put my hand on it now. That's more than most people get." He picked up his coat off the rail. "Come on. You're due on a floor at dusk, and I'm due to throw peas at you, and I'd like my supper before either."

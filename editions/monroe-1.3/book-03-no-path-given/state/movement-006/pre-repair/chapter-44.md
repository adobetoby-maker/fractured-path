# Chapter 44 — Enactment Order

They worked out who would do what over the next hour, in the provost's office with the challenge squared on the blotter, and the plan came together as every good plan in his life had come together. Nobody handed it down. Everybody who held a piece of the problem laid that piece on the desk, until the whole thing had a shape that none of them could have made alone.

"I'll take the code," Cael said. "With Prynn, if she'll have me. She knows those shelves better than anybody alive, and I've found a first thread already. I need somebody beside me who'll say so when I've found a true seam, and say so louder when I've only found the one I was hoping for."

"She'll have you," said Quenna from the window. "She's been waiting most of her life for a student who reads her shelves instead of asking her what's on them."

"I'd like the old registers," said Karis.

She said it at once, with no pause at all before it, and Cael understood from the speed that she had been thinking about exactly this since the stable on Monday night, long before anybody asked her. "The founding-era shelves. Everything from before the code was put in the order it's in now. If the schedule really is silent about what you are, then there's a reason it's silent, and the reason is written down somewhere, even if nobody's opened the room it's in for a generation. People wrote about other people before anybody had decided on today's words. I want to know what they wrote."

"That isn't the argument," said Cael, carefully. "Whatever we say in that room has to stand inside the code as it reads now, or it doesn't stand. The hearing won't care what anybody wrote before."

"I know." Karis met his eyes and did not look away. "I'm not looking for the argument. You are. I'm looking for what's true. Those are two different searches, and I think you know that better than anybody I've ever met."

He did know it. He gave her the point and did not argue it further, and watched her rule a clean page into the columns of a search she would plainly begin within the hour. Under the weight of the morning he felt an odd comfort in it. Let the hearing rule what it liked about him; somewhere on the continent now there was one person whose entire method was pointed at what he really was, and who did not care whether any tribunal ever asked. The two searches would run side by side for three weeks. He suspected, without being able to say why, that hers was the one still going in three years.

"I'll take the witnesses," said Brom. He was still standing against the alumni books with his hands behind his back. "The hearing's public. Witnesses of fact can be entered by either side; you said so. Edran withdrew a petition on the record, in his own hand. Whatever he says about what he's seen you do, nobody can call it friendship. There'll be others who watched the sittings. I'll go round and find which of them will stand up and tell it straight, what they saw and nothing on top of it."

"And you'll keep them honest," said Quenna. "Not coached. A magistrate can smell a rehearsed witness from the back of the room."

"I know the difference between helping somebody remember a thing and telling them what they remember," said Brom. He said it a little drily. "I grew up on an estate where people were told what they'd seen. I'll not be doing that to anybody, not for any cause."

"Quenna and I will hold the procedure," said Naveth. "The schedule for the documents. Disputes of scope. The magistrate's office and its letters. Every day of lawful delay my counsel can produce is another day in the archive for somebody." For the first time that morning something like humour moved across his long tired face. "I'm reliably told this academy has a gift for lawful delay. I'd like to think it's an institutional gift and not merely a personal failing."

Lira had been quiet through all of it, with her arms folded and her boots planted, watching the plan put itself together round a version of Cael she plainly had opinions about. When the room's attention came round to her at last, she did not wait to be asked.

"I'll take the part nobody's said," she said. "You're still a fighter, and you've still got a monthly sitting coming whether there's a hearing or not, and a man with a card sitting at the back of it this time, I'd bet. You don't get to go down into the dust for three weeks and climb back out having forgotten what your feet are for." She unfolded one arm and pointed at him. "So I keep you sharp. The yard, same as always, at dusk, and no excuses because you're busy being clever." Something under the flat practical words was fiercer than the words alone could hold. "Whoever walks into that room at the end had better be you. Not a lawyer wearing your face. Lose yourself somewhere in all that reading and it won't matter to me how fine the argument is, because the part that counted will have gone first."

He held her eyes across the crowded desk.

It came to him then as a few things in his life had come, whole and at once and not built up piece by piece: of everything the room had put on the desk for him that morning, this was what he needed most, and the one thing he would never have thought to ask for. Naveth was defending the academy. Karis was going after the truth. Brom was gathering the witnesses. Only Lira had put herself between him and all of it: the boy himself, under the case, guarded against the argument, and against three weeks of it, and against the sort of surviving where the thing that survives is somebody else.

"Dusk, then," he said. "Same as ever. With whatever the archive hasn't eaten."

"Whatever's left of you," said Lira, "will still be better on a floor than most people are at their best. Don't forget that either."

---

The council broke up without a resolution, as such meetings do. Each of them had a corner of a thing too big for any one pair of hands, and each turned away on their own to make a start on it. In the corridor at the foot of the worn stair the six of them went off in five directions. The provost went toward his counsel and Quenna toward her folders. Karis went toward the residence wing at the stride she kept for beginning things. Brom went out to the yard, where Edran would be at the high windows at this hour, and Lira toward the floor desk to book their slot at dusk. For a moment Cael stood alone at the junction of the passages and watched them go, and had the strange double experience of seeing it from outside. Five people were turning their whole lives round to fit a fifteen-year-old's mad sentence, because he had said it aloud and meant it.

He went to the archive, because that was where the work started.

Prynn was waiting at the long table. She had three volumes open on it already, and a folded card beside them, and a fresh pot on her desk, as if she had known before anybody told her which way the meeting would go.

"I heard," she said, without looking up. "Sit down. We have three weeks and a very great many years of code between us, and I mean to use every hour of both."

He sat.

She already knew how she meant him to do it. Of course she did.

"You'll read it in enactment order," she said, and turned the first volume round so that it faced him. "Not the order it sits on the shelf in. The shelf is only where it was put away. The order it was enacted in is the order it was made, piece by piece, and whatever is weak in a thing is weak because of how it was made, not because of how somebody shelved it later." She laid her finger on the folded card. It was a concordance of the amendments, ruled by hand in faded ink, the columns narrow and dense and perfectly straight, and it was plainly older than his parents would have been. "The first amendment, then the second, then the third, all the way up. You want to know what generations of amenders thought they were fixing, and you want it in the order they took fright."

"In the order they took fright," Cael repeated.

"Every amendment is a confession," said Prynn. "Each patch went on because somebody had found a hole, and it sits exactly where the hole was. Read them in the order they went on, enough of them, and you'll have drawn yourself every weak place that wall has ever had."

He looked at her.

"The taxonomy master said that," he said. "In the first week. He said when I was older I should read the amendments. He said every one of them was a confession."

Prynn did not look surprised. She looked, very briefly, pleased, and then she put it away again as she put everything away.

"He sat at this table when he was about your age," she said. "A long time ago. He had it from me." She pushed the card an inch nearer to him. "I'm glad somebody's finally taken him up on it. He's been saying it to first-years for thirty years, and not one of them has ever come down here afterward to see whether he was right."

Cael looked at the card, and then at her, and felt a door come open onto a discipline he recognized.

He was quiet a moment. "I read fighters like that," he said. "By what they've had to fix. Nobody practises a guard against a blow that's never landed on them. If somebody's drilled their left side until it shines, somebody once hurt them on the left."

"Then you already know how to read law," said Prynn, dry as the shelves. "Most people start by believing the text is finished. It's never finished. It's only stopped for now." She took off her spectacles and polished them on her cuff and put them back. "You'll save us a week."

They worked until the eighth bell and past it, because she had no intention of locking anything.

She took the concordance and he took the text, and the first evening's work was nothing but an inventory, the whole code laid out in the order it had grown. He blocked out its shape in the observation notebook in the same pattern he used for any Path he was learning. *Charter* went at the root, granting everything. Beneath it went the *Schedule of Recognized Paths and Standings*, and he wrote that one large, because it was the first commitment, the one everything after it depended on, as every move in a fight depends on the declaration that made it possible. Beneath that went the *Obligations of Practitioners* and then the *Enforcement* chapters, as the follow-through: each of them drawing its force from the commitment above it, each of them helpless without it.

He drew it as a declaration tree, because that was the shape his thinking had been trained into, the way some people think in sums. When he turned the notebook round to show her, Prynn looked at the diagram for a long time. Then a noise came out of her that he had not known she could make, short and dry and dusty, and it was only afterward, walking up the stair to bed, that he understood it had been a laugh.

"All my years at that desk," she said, "and the first honest drawing of the code anybody's ever shown me is a fight chart."

---

Karis was on the archive steps when he came out, going in as he came up. She had a lamp in her hand and a satchel of empty notebooks slung across her, and she looked like somebody who had just drawn her rations for a long march.

"Prynn's given me the founding shelves," she said. "The waystation registers. Everything from before the last time anybody put the code in order." There was a light in her face he had seen only twice before: once in this archive on the day she had first asked his permission for anything, and once on the floor of the formal yard with a wrecked lattice smoking round her feet. "People wrote things down about people the schedule couldn't hold, before anybody had decided on today's words for them. I want to know what words they had."

"The hearing can't use any of it."

"You keep telling me that as if you think it'll put me off." She hitched the satchel higher. "The hearing can have your search. I'll have mine. Go and train. Lira's been walking up and down the yard for a quarter of an hour, and the look on her face isn't a patient one."

---

Lira collected him at dusk, exactly as she had threatened.

The session was short and had no mercy anywhere in it. She ran him through footwork first, then the old Ardenmere patterns from the yard behind the grain exchange, then three rounds of the chalk-and-pea lattice she had apparently decided was now a permanent part of his life whether or not any lattice was ever going to be scheduled again. Brom threw the bags from the edge of the ring and said nothing. Four touched him on the first round and three on the second, and on the third he came out of the second sequence late and stiff, and Lira stopped.

"You've been reading all day," she said, circling him. "Look at you. You stand like a clerk at a high desk. Shoulders up round your ears, chin forward, as if you're leaning in to read the small print." She came in at half speed and tapped him on the breastbone with two fingers, lightly, and he was too slow to slip it. "Fix it before it sets. Otherwise by hearing day you'll lean at the magistrate like a man reaching for a book on a high shelf."

"The magistrate won't care about my shoulders."

"Everybody cares about shoulders. Nobody knows they care." She swept his lead foot with an economy that would have shamed him a year ago and now only made him laugh, and he went down on the frozen earth, and she stood over him with her hand held out. "That's the real secret of every ranked fighter and every courtroom in the country, and you've had it from me for nothing."

He took the hand.

Somewhere in the second half of the hour, moving through the old patterns while the day's reading drained out of his head, he noticed that this was the only hour since the gate in which he had thought about nothing at all. Not the word. Not the case at the back of the archive. Not the man with the card. Only Lira's feet and Brom's bags and the cold, and the old turns coming up out of his body one after another because his body knew them. He understood all over again exactly what she had taken on in the provost's office that morning, and why nobody alive would have been better at it.

"There," said Lira, at the end, when the third sequence finally came clean. "That's you. I'd lost you somewhere around the second bell this morning."

"I was in a meeting."

"You were in a meeting with your shoulders." She coiled the rope off the pump's nail and hung it back. "Same time tomorrow. And the day after. I don't care what you find in there. You come out at dusk and stand on a floor."

---

That night, back in his room with the observation notebook shut for once and the code's first volume closed on the desk, he opened the binder at the back. Since the moment in Naveth's office when he had said the mad thing out loud and been startled to find he meant every word, one line had been waiting all day to be written, and he wrote it.

*New opponent. Four hundred pages, no tells, no third exchange.*

He looked at the line a while. Then, because it was true, and writing down true things was the only method he had ever entirely trusted, he wrote what came after it.

*Three weeks. Watch it the way I'd watch anybody, until I can see how it moves. Prynn says read it in the order it was frightened. L. says come out at dusk and stand on a floor. Both of those are the same rule.*

He wrote to Hesk after that, at the same desk, because Hesk had told him to write when something happened, and this was the first thing since Denvash that had happened in quite this way.

*Hesk —*

*Something is happening, and I'd like you to hear it from me before you hear it from anybody else.*

*The man from the borrowed office in Ardenmere came up the hill on Monday with correct papers. He was let in because the papers were correct. He has filed a challenge to my enrollment, and there will be a hearing in three weeks, in public, with a magistrate of the Compact. He was honest with me about what he is. He was gentle about it. It's the job, he says. I believe him.*

*I'm going to answer it myself. Not the enrollment. That can't be answered, and I won't pretend it can. Something underneath the enrollment. I can't put it in a letter yet, because I'm not sure of it yet, and I don't write down things I'm not sure of. You taught me that before Vell did.*

*Please don't come. It isn't that I don't want you. It's that the road is long, and you'd only end up sitting on a bench outside a room, and I'd know you were there, and I'd want to come out and sit beside you instead of doing the work.*

*The arm is mending. Lira takes my bread. There are four of us now at the end of the table.*

*Cael*

He read it back. There was nothing in it that a stranger could have used, and nothing in it that was not so. He wrote the Denvash direction on the outside and set it on the corner of the desk for the third-day post, and sat a while looking at it, thinking of a stove that smoked when the wind was east and a bench somebody had mended so that it would be ready whenever.

---

Prynn laid down the rules of the room on the first full morning, before she laid down a single volume.

Nothing open overnight. Never more than three volumes in a pile. The ink kept to the far end of the table, well away from any binding, and the pen laid down on its side, not on its point. A slip of blank paper put into the gap of every volume he drew from a shelf, so that no shelf in her keeping need spend even an hour uncertain about what it was missing. He took the rules as he took a new instructor's floor discipline, without arguing, because a floor's discipline told you about the floor. This floor had kept centuries of paper alive through damp winters and dry ones, and through the waystation's roof, which Prynn described with real bitterness as a quarrel she had been having with the weather since before he was born.

"Most students who come in here want an answer," she said, and set the first volume of the charter in front of him with both hands, as other people carry babies, or eggs. "They want the one page that settles it, so they can go. You won't find one page. If a single page could have settled it, somebody in the Compact would have found that page generations ago and settled it. I can promise you you're not the first person who's ever been angry at this code."

"I'm not angry at it."

Over the top of the volume her eyes stayed on him, and he could see her taking his measure afresh.

"No," she said at last. "You aren't. So what are you?"

He thought about how to say it exactly, because Prynn was a person who heard the difference between exact and nearly.

"I'm studying it," he said. "The way I'd study anybody I was going to fight."

Prynn considered that for longer than it seemed to need. Then she went back to her desk and came back with a second volume, and a third, and kept coming back, and did not stop until the stack at his elbow stood eleven high, eight past her own rule. She did not mention the rule, and he did not point it out.

"Then you'll want the concordance open beside you all day," she said. "A fighter watches how a man moves before he bothers about where he's standing."

He read the code the way he read an opponent he had not fought yet. He took in everything there was before he let himself chase any single question, built the shape of the whole before he went after any one part of it, and trusted the pattern to show itself to somebody patient enough to sit still while it did.

The first two days gave him almost nothing but the shape. The shape, he came slowly to understand, was the first real finding.

The language changed down the generations as a fighter's style changes across a long career. In the oldest chapters it was cautious, in the years when the Compact had still been a treaty among guilds that did not entirely trust one another, and every clause was hedged with *by consent* and *where the parties agree*. In the middle centuries it was confident, almost careless; nobody was arguing with the Compact's authority any more, and its drafters wrote as people write who do not expect to be questioned. And in the newest amendments it was defensive again: hedged and cross-referenced and qualified, every power wrapped in three conditions. Somewhere along the way, he thought, the drafters had been burned, and had learned that one careless clause could still be making trouble for them a century after the ink was dry.

He filled eleven pages of the observation notebook with nothing but the places the code had flinched. A defensive amendment was a scar, and a scar meant there had been a wound. Every one of them meant that at some point somebody had found a weak place in the wording and pushed something through it hard enough to hurt the Compact. He listed the scars as he would have listed the places an opponent guarded. No single scar was a way in. But the pattern of them showed where the system had once been hurt and had learned fear. More usefully, it showed where it had never learned fear at all.

The enforcement chapters were thick with scars. The obligations had a good many. The charter's oldest pages had a few, healed over long ago.

The schedule had almost none.

He wrote that at the bottom of the second evening's page, under the scar count, and boxed it, and could not yet see what it meant. There seemed to be two ways a thing could go unscarred: nobody had ever gone for it, or nobody had ever had any reason to. He sat looking at the two possibilities with the lamp ticking and Prynn's pen scratching at the high desk, and it did not occur to him for two more days that there might be a third.

---

He did not stop the other work while he read. Lira would not have let him, and he would not have let himself.

On the Thursday, at the sixth bell, he went from the long table to the quiet room with the code still in his head, and Lira went with him, and Brom carried the bucket of sand. Karis was on the founding shelves and had sent word by Brom that she trusted the two of them to count without her, which Lira said was the nicest thing Karis had ever said about anybody and the most suspicious. The oak post stood in its bucket in the middle of the scrubbed floor with fourteen small rings already burned into its grey side from the weeks before. He knelt and laid his palm on a clean place and chose the size, and made three marks, one after another, with the count between. All three came the width of his thumbnail. The cold came after each, and went, and his fingertips went grey and came back, and the count between each was ninety-six, then a hundred and one, then ninety-nine.

"Steady," said Lira, from the wall. "Same as last week. Same as the week before."

"Steady."

"Then I want to try something." She came off the wall. "Put your hand back on it. Clean place. Get it ready, the way you do, so it's sitting there wanting to go. Don't light it."

He looked at her.

"Quenna's question," said Lira. "I'm not answering it for you. I can't. But I'd like to know one small thing, and so would you." She went round behind him, out of his sight. "Hand on the wood. Ready. And hold it."

He laid his right palm on a clean place on the oak and turned toward the warmth until it was there, low and patient and ready, the size chosen, the asking a breath away from being asked. He held it. He could feel it waiting under his hand like a horse that has seen the gate open.

Behind him, without any warning at all, Lira clapped her hands together hard, once, a crack like a dropped plate in the small whitewashed room.

He flinched. His whole body flinched, his shoulders came up and his breath went in and his heart jumped into his throat. His palm stayed flat on the oak.

Nothing lit.

He knelt there with his heart going hard and the warmth still sitting under his hand, ready, unspent, exactly where he had held it. After a long moment he took his hand away. The wood under it was clean grey oak.

"Well," said Lira quietly.

Brom had stopped with the scoop half lifted. He put it down.

"That isn't an answer," said Cael. His voice was not quite steady. "A clap in a quiet room isn't somebody who wants me gone. It isn't a fight."

"No," said Lira. "It's a clap in a quiet room." She came back round where he could see her and crouched on her heels in front of him. "But a week ago you didn't know whether a fright would spend it for you. Now you know that one doesn't. That's one thing. You write it down as one thing and not as two."

He wrote it kneeling on the scrubbed boards, with the binder on his knee.

*Thursday, week nineteen. Three of three, a thumbnail, count about a hundred. Then L. clapped behind me with my hand on the post and the new one ready. I flinched all over. It didn't light. One fright in a quiet room. Not a fight, not anybody who wishes me harm. One thing, not two.*

He looked at the last line for a while. Then he drew the small box round it, his own private mark for *not yet*, and closed the binder, and went back to the code.

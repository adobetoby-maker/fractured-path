whether this is self-review, a fresh-context simulated cold read, or a real human
read. Do not claim independent testing when you have the drafting history.

First give an unscored reader response: where interest caught, where it slipped,
which person matters, which event you expect next, and whether you would continue.
Then use two perspectives: a fluent thirteen-year-old reader and an adult genre
reader. These are lenses, not demographic research. Do not infer a sales forecast.

For each applicable dimension give a 1–10 score, an exact passage location, a reason
and confidence. Mark an absent feature N/A instead of giving invented evidence:

| Keep reading | Unresolved pressure and satisfying local payoff |
| Interest / freshness | Specific people, choices and problems |

| Read-aloud quality | Punctuation, referents, dialogue attribution and cadence |

Anchor interpretation: 5 = understandable but inconsistent; 7 = engaging with
located weaknesses; 9 = compelling with few substantial distractions. These are
editorial judgments, not calibrated measurements. Do not average the two readers
into a release gate. Do not award points for matching another book's word counts.

Separately check canon, physical state, power limits, knowledge boundaries and
reserved disclosures. Cite the project evidence for a contradiction. If only the
plan supports a claim, identify it as plan adherence rather than a canon error.

Return a concise repair brief with at most three priorities. Each needs location,
observed issue, effect on the reader, proposed scope and a strength to preserve.
Retain the developed action the owner requested. Punctuation can repair an unclear
sentence; do not shorten a successful fight merely to reduce its word count.

If comparing runs, keep chapter coverage and scoring rubric equal. Hide model and
seat labels from a fresh reader where feasible. Report tradeoffs and uncertainty;
one movement does not prove which seat is better across a series.

# Chapter 17 — Three Audiences

He was the last one into assembly, and everybody in the room knew why.

The assembly room sat at the end of the lecture wing's ground floor, a long plain hall with benches in rows and a lectern nobody used at the front. By the time Cael came through the door the benches were full and the instructor reading the day's notices had reached the second of them. He did not stop reading. Nobody said anything. But a ripple went along the back rows as Cael found a space at the end of the last bench, the small, collective adjustment of two hundred people who had just been reading about him and now had to pretend they had not.

He sat down and folded his hands on the binder and looked at the lectern.

The notices were ordinary. A change to the lamp oil allowance in the residence wing. A lecture on Current Path footwork moved from the third bell to the second. A reminder that the kitchen garden was not a shortcut. The instructor read each one in the same patient voice, and Cael listened to every word as if it mattered, because the alternative was to feel the backs of two hundred heads deciding not to turn around.

Lira was three rows ahead. She did not turn either. But halfway through the notice about the garden she lifted one hand, as if to scratch her neck, and held up two fingers where only he could see them, and then put the hand down.

Two fingers was a circuit sign. In Ardenmere it had meant *I see you, stay where you are, I'll come round after*. He had not known until that moment how badly he had wanted somebody to make it.

Assembly ended on the fourth notice. The room came apart into noise at once, and he stayed where he was on the end of the bench while it emptied, because getting up in the middle of it would have meant walking through it.

Brom found him first. He did not say anything about the petition. He sat down on the bench beside Cael with his elbows on his knees and looked at the empty lectern.

"Lira's gone to find out who saw Edran go in to the clerk," he said. "She thinks there was a witness to the filing. She's furious she didn't know yesterday."

"There's nothing to find out. He signed it."

"I know. I told her." Brom considered the lectern. "She said finding out was for her, not for you."

Cael almost laughed. It came out as a breath.

"You read it?" Brom asked.

"All of it."

"And?"

"It's right," said Cael. "Nearly all of it. He's got the provision right and the record right. He's got the children right." He turned the binder over on his knees. "He's only got one thing wrong, and it's the thing in the middle that the rest stands on."

Brom nodded slowly, the way he nodded when a fight went the way he had thought it would go and he had not wanted it to.

"Then it's a good petition," he said.

"It's a very good petition."

"That's worse."

"I know."

They sat for a while longer in the empty room, and then the second bell rang for lectures, and Cael went to the registry history block because it was on his schedule and because walking into a lecture hall on time was one of the few things he was sure he could still do correctly that morning.

He did not take a single note. He sat in the back row with the binder open to a clean page and the pen in his hand, while the thin woman at the front talked about the third revision of the station fees, and the page stayed clean. Twice he caught himself reading the conclusion on Edran's second page from memory. *Evidently a practitioner of some classification, which the faculty has elected not to disclose.* It was a good sentence. It was careful in the right places. He would have been proud to write it, if it had been true.

At the end of the block a junior clerk was waiting outside the lecture hall door with her hands folded.

"The provost would like a word," she said. "Now, if you can."

She said it in the tone of somebody who had been told to make it sound less important than it was, and she did not quite manage it.

---

Naveth's door was shut.

Cael had climbed the worn stair to that office three times now, and every time before, the door had stood open. He stopped on the top step and looked at it, a plain dark panel with an iron latch, and understood that this conversation had been decided to be private before he had been sent for.

He knocked.

"Come."

There were four chairs this time. Somebody had carried two more up the narrow stair and set them against the wall, and two of the four were already taken. Quenna sat by the window with her folder on her knee. Wray sat beside her in the brown coat buttoned to the throat, her hands resting flat on her thighs, as still as she had been on the panel at both sittings.

Naveth sat behind his desk. The three pages of the petition lay squared on the blotter in front of him, and Cael saw at once that they were not the originals. The originals were downstairs on the board, held up by brass tacks, where the rule said they must stay. These were copies in a clerk's hand. Naveth had had the petition copied before he read it, so that he could read it as often as he liked without being seen to stand in front of the board.

"Sit," said Naveth. "This concerns you more than anyone, and I'd rather you heard all of it than the useful parts."

Cael sat in the fourth chair, the one facing the desk.

"You've read it."

"Yes."

"Then I won't read it to you." Naveth laid one long hand flat on the pages. "I'll tell you what I can and can't do with it, and then Quenna will tell you what she thinks I should do instead, and then Wray will tell us both whether it's possible. That's the order we agreed on the stair."

He raised one finger, as he had on the first morning with the alumni ledger.

"I can't dismiss it. It's clean. Every clause is in the right place and every date is correct, and the young man who wrote it has standing to file it, three years of standing. If I set it aside because it's inconvenient, I tell every student in this building that a petition is a thing the provost answers when he likes the question." He paused. "A good many of the students in this building came here because some other place answered their questions that way. A petition that gets posted and then answered is the only protection some of them have ever had. I won't spend it on you. You wouldn't thank me if I did."

"No," said Cael.

A second finger.

"I can't grant it either. Suspending a student's floor access pending review of his enrollment isn't a thing a provost can do alone. It needs a formal review of the enrollment itself." He looked at Cael over the copied pages. "And a formal review of your enrollment, a week after a sealed bundle of your records went down the hill to the Compact, is the one thing in the world I least want to sign. Whatever that review wrote would be the first thing anyone read."

"And doing nothing," said Quenna, from the window.

"Doing nothing is a decision." Naveth did not look round at her; it had the sound of a line they had already said to each other once that morning. "It's the worst one I have. Silence from this office reads as weakness to one half of the building and favour to the other, and both halves would be partly right. I need an answer that's public, lawful and true, and I need it today."

He took his hand off the pages and leaned back. The chair gave its worn creak.

"That's all I've got," he said. "Quenna has something better."

---

Quenna did not stand up. She turned a little on her chair so that she faced Cael directly, and she put her folder on the floor beside her, which he had never seen her do.

"The petition asks two things," she said. "Suspend him, or assess him in public under the same framework as students of full standing. Naveth can't do the first. So read the second one again."

Cael did not need to read it again. He had it by heart. *That the candidate be assessed under the same framework as students of full standing, in public, so that the academy's standings might continue to mean what every enrolled student had been told they meant.*

"He's asked for a public assessment," he said slowly.

"He has. In writing, signed and posted." Quenna's voice had the flat, careful cadence it took on at the sittings, each word set down on its own. "Your sittings have been closed. Formal, recorded, and closed. That was right for the first two. It isn't right now, because now the closed door is the thing being argued about. So we open it."

She held up one hand, palm out, and laid the plan on it one piece at a time.

"An exhibition bout, held in the formal yard, under standings rules, with the full faculty seats present and the school free to watch. Logged as this month's demonstration assessment under the provision, in the provision's own language. Consistent, observable practice. We don't grant his petition and we don't refuse it." She closed the hand. "We answer the question it asked, in front of everybody who's asking."

"Against Edran," said Naveth. It was not really a question. He had heard this on the stair.

"Against Edran. He filed it. He's earned the right to be the one who finds out."

Cael sat with it.

He did what he always did with a plan that was not his own, which was to walk around it looking for the place where it would break, the way he walked around an opponent in the first exchange. He found it at once, because it was not hidden.

"If I lose," he said, "his petition is answered the way he wanted. I'll have shown the whole school that he was right."

"Yes."

"Not partly. In front of everybody. In the record."

"Yes." Quenna did not soften it, and she did not hurry past it. "If you lose, the record will say that the candidate under the demonstration provision was assessed in public against a student of full standing and was beaten, and the petition will stand on that page for as long as the page exists. I would not be proposing this if I thought you would lose. But I want you to walk into it with the stakes named, not with the stakes I'd like them to be."

She held his eyes.

"I've watched Edran train since he came here at fifteen. He has a seam, as everyone does. The question isn't whether it's there. It's whether you find it before he finishes the conversation."

Cael looked down at his hands.

He thought of the board in the main hall and the three pages pinned in a row. He thought of Oona's face working through *Then he's asking the wrong question*, and his own answer, that it was a fair question with one fact wrong in the middle of it. A fair question deserved to be answered where it had been asked. Edran had asked in the yard, and then in ink. Answering him in a closed hall with three people and a slate would only be one more door shut in his face.

"Schedule it," he said.

---

Wray had not spoken. She spoke now, without moving her hands from her thighs, in the voice she used to read a measurement off a gauge.

"He's the best I have on the Edge floor," she said. "Better than his rank. He will press early, because there will be a crowd, and he always presses with a crowd. And he learns inside a bout. Most people learn between bouts. He changes his timing mid-exchange when something fails." She looked at Cael. "Whatever you find on him, find it twice. He won't leave it lying where it was."

"I'll find it twice."

"Then I'll assess it." She turned her head toward Quenna. "Standings rules. Exchanges called on my count. A touch is a touch, a yield is a yield, the ring is the ring. No allowance either way for the category."

"No allowance," Quenna agreed.

Naveth reached for a blank posting form from the drawer of his desk, and then stopped with it half out and held up his other hand.

"One more thing first," he said. "And I want it said in this room, with the door shut."

He looked at Cael as he had on the first morning, with the joiner's look, the one that decided whether a beam would carry a floor.

"You understand what Quenna has turned this into. In eight days most of this school will stand around that ring and watch you fight, and everything you show them will go into the assessment record with two hundred witnesses beside it. If Edran beats you, we have a problem." He paused. "If you beat him by too much, I'm not sure the problem we'd have instead is any smaller."

Cael said nothing. He had been thinking the same thing since the word *public*, and he had been waiting to see whether anyone else would say it aloud.

"I'm not telling you how to fight," said Naveth. "I wouldn't know how. I'm telling you that this fight has three audiences. The students on the benches, who want to know whether the board still means anything. The panel, who will write down what they see. And a file, which will be read some day by people who were never in the yard and don't care how the morning felt." He folded the posting form flat on the blotter. "Only one of the three will be standing across the chalk from you. Don't fight for that one alone."

"I know," said Cael.

Then, because the man had shut his door to say it and had earned more than two words, he said the rest.

"It's what I've been doing since Ardenmere. Every bout on the circuit, I had to win in front of people without showing them more than they'd already seen. Winning at a chosen size." He looked at the copied petition on the blotter. "It's the only way I know how to win anymore."

Naveth looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in the old weathered face that Cael could not read, and then Naveth uncapped his pen.

"Winning at a chosen size," he repeated, writing nothing, as if testing the words. "That tells me more about you than your intake file did." He turned to Quenna. "The first bell, eight days from today, in the formal yard. Write me the wording and I'll sign it before the fifth bell."

Quenna was already writing.

---

The answer went up beside the petition before the fifth bell, as Naveth had promised, on a single sheet in Quenna's hand and his signature, pinned with the same brass tacks.

*Answer to the Petition of Standing filed by Edran, Glass Path, Iron Rank Two. The petition's alternative request is granted in the following form. The candidate enrolled under the demonstration provision will be assessed in public, in the formal yard, at the first bell on the eighth day from this posting, in an exhibition bout under standings rules against the petitioner. The bout will be logged as the candidate's demonstration assessment for the month, under the standard of the provision. The faculty seats will sit. The academy may attend.*

That was all of it. It was shorter than any one page of the petition.

Cael saw the crowd in front of the board from the far end of the main hall, at the change of bells, thicker than the morning's and noisier. He did not go to it. He stood by the archive passage with the binder against his chest and watched people read.

He watched the shape of the reading change as it moved through the crowd. At the front, where people had finished, it had already turned into something else. He could see it in their shoulders and hear it in the pitch of the talk. In the morning the hall had been full of the low, careful noise of a verdict being handed round in pieces. This was louder and quicker, with laughter in it here and there. Two second-years at the back of the crowd were arguing, and one of them was holding up fingers.

Gerda came out of the crowd on its near side, tall and narrow and straight, with her patched coat buttoned and her slip for the floor already in her hand. She saw him by the passage and changed course, and stopped two paces off.

"Answered," she said.

"Yes."

"The same day it was posted." She looked back at the board, at the single sheet beside the three. "That's the good kind. They didn't put it in a drawer and let it go cold. They didn't refuse it in a sentence. They gave him what he asked for, in a shape they could live with." She looked at him again. "Now it's yours to answer properly."

"You've seen one answered badly."

Gerda considered him for a moment, as if deciding how much of a true answer could be spared.

"I've seen one answered correctly," she said. "That isn't always the same thing."

She went on toward the floor without another word, checking the clock above the door as she passed it.

Edran was not at the board. Cael had not expected him to be. He had gone to the board once already, in the morning, early, before anybody else, to read his own petition pinned up in his own hand, and Cael was sure he would not go and stand in front of it now with the crowd watching him read the answer.

But at the change of the sixth bell, crossing the formal yard, Cael saw him.

He was on the far side of the yard by the standings board, where his name now sat second, in the same clerk's hand at the same size, one line below Karis's. He was not looking at the standings. He was looking across the yard at the main hall door, where a few students were still coming out from the board in twos and threes, talking with their hands. Then his eyes moved, and found Cael.

He did not come over. He lifted his chin very slightly, a single small movement that meant, as plainly as if he had said it across the chalk, *I've read it. Accepted.*

Cael lifted his own in return.

Edran turned away and went through the north gate toward the open floor, and Cael stood in the yard and realized that the conversation he had told Oona he would have to have was now a great deal harder. He had meant to go to Edran and tell him the one wrong thing on the second page. Now the yard stood between them, and the answer on the board, and eight days, and anything he said before the first bell on the eighth would look like what it was not.

He thought about that all the way to supper.

---

Lira had read the answer by the time he reached the stable. He could tell from the far end of the long room, from the way she sat, very upright at the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, with her bread untouched in front of her.

"They've put you in a ring," she said, before he had sat down. "For the whole school to stand round and look at."

"Yes."

"Two hundred people. On a calendar. With a date." She pushed the bread away. "Ever since Ardenmere I've watched people look at you as if you were something on a table to be weighed, and now they've sold tickets."

"Nobody's selling tickets."

"Somebody will." She looked at him with her jaw set, the small muscle working at the hinge of it. "Wasn't there anything else? Anything at all, that didn't mean standing you up in front of everybody?"

Brom set down his spoon.

"They've put him in a ring with rules," he said. "Wray's count. A touch is a touch. That isn't a market square, and it isn't a back room with a man asking careful questions. It's the floor." He looked at Lira steadily. "You walked a Force Path off the edge of that same ring in the second week because you wanted twenty people to see it. You said so on the wall."

Lira opened her mouth, and shut it.

"That was different."

"It was the same," said Brom. "Except he's got two hundred, and you only got twenty."

For a moment Cael thought she was going to throw the bread at him. Then something in her shoulders gave, very slightly, and she pulled the bread back toward her and tore it in half.

"I hate it when you do that," she said.

"I know."

"I hate it more when you're right." She looked at Cael across the table, and the anger had gone out of her face, and what was left under it was the thing the anger had been standing in front of. "All right. It's a ring. I know rings." She lowered her voice, though the two second-years playing tiles by the fire were nowhere near. "What are you going to show them?"

"The framework."

"And?"

"Nothing else."

"Not the read?"

"Not even the read." He turned his bowl a quarter-turn on the table. "It's on the record twice already. It's safe. But a fight won with one thing is smaller on paper than a fight won with two."

Lira looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the nod she gave an opponent who had done something she would have done herself.

"Good," she said, and pushed the larger half of the bread across to him. "Eat. You've got eight days of being looked at, and you can't do it hungry."

---

He went anyway, the next morning, because he had told Oona he would, and because a thing being harder had never once in his life been the same as a thing being wrong.

He found Edran where Brom had said he would be, on the open floor before the first bell, alone, drilling in the far corner by the high windows. Nobody else was in the hall. The light came in grey and level through the glass and lay along the boards, and Edran moved through it in short, exact sequences, shell after shell forming along his forearms and bursting outward in a glitter that hung for a heartbeat in the grey air before it was gone.

Cael stopped at the rail and waited.

Edran finished the sequence he was in, all the way to the end, without hurrying it. Then he let his arms drop and turned.

"You're early for the rail."

"I didn't come for the rail."

Edran looked at him, and then at the empty hall, and then back. He did not come any closer. He stood in the middle of the floor with chalk on his hands and his breathing settling, and waited.

"There's one thing in your petition that's wrong," said Cael. "On the second page. I wanted you to hear it from me, before the eighth day, so that you'd know I wasn't hiding behind the bout."

Edran did not move.

"Don't," he said.

It was quiet and quite firm, and it stopped Cael where he stood.

"I mean it," said Edran. "Don't tell me." He wiped his palms down the front of his training shirt, slowly, one and then the other. "If you tell me now, I'll have your word for it. And I'll probably believe you. You don't seem to lie much, and I've watched you a while." He looked at the floor between them, at the chalk and the grey light. "Then I'll go into that yard knowing something nobody earned in front of anybody. One more thing on the board that didn't come from the board."

"It isn't about the board."

"Everything I've asked has been about the board." Edran's voice did not rise. "I didn't file it to find out what you are. I filed it to find out whether the framework can still tell. If it can't, I'd rather know that from the floor than from you."

Cael stood at the rail with the thing he had come to say still in his mouth, and found that he could not argue with one word of it.

"All right," he said.

Edran nodded once.

"For what it's worth," Cael said, "it was a fair question. Both times."

Something passed over Edran's face. It was not quite surprise, and not quite pleasure, and it was gone before Cael could name it.

"I know it was," said Edran. "I'm still not sorry I asked it in ink." He turned back toward the high windows and set his feet. "Eight days. Go and watch somebody else. I'll be here every morning, and you're allowed the rail, and I won't change anything for you." He paused with his hands half raised. "I'd think less of you if you didn't come."

He began the next sequence.

Cael watched the first shell form and burst. Then he did what he had been told, and went to watch somebody else, because the first bell was ringing and because he understood that he had just been given permission to study the man for six days, by the man himself, in daylight, in the open.

Oona was at the board when he came back through the main hall, in front of the answer, with her slate under her arm. She had been waiting for him; she did not pretend otherwise.

"Did you tell him?"

"I tried."

She turned round. "What happened?"

"He told me not to." Cael looked at the four sheets on the board, the three in Edran's hand and the one in Quenna's. "He said if I told him, he'd have my word for it, and he didn't want my word. He wanted the floor to tell him. In front of everybody. The way he asked."

Oona thought about that for a long time, with the whole of her face, while students went past behind them toward the second bell.

"Then he's not asking the wrong question anymore," she said at last.

"No?"

"No. Before, he was asking what you are. That's the one he had wrong." She looked at the answer on the board, Quenna's short square paragraph. "Now he's asking if the board can tell. He's allowed to ask that. That's his." She frowned, working it through. "And you can't answer that one with words anyway. It's not a words one."

He looked down at her. She was thirteen and had been at Greyvane for six weeks, and she had just said in four sentences the thing it had taken him a whole night to understand.

"No," he said. "It's not a words one."

"So you have to win," said Oona, as if that settled it. "Or lose. Either one answers it." She tucked her slate under her arm and considered him. "I'd rather you won."

"So would I."

"Then you should," she said, quite seriously, and went off toward the lecture wing.

That night he wrote the whole of it down under the four headings, because the four headings were where a fight began.

*Exhibition. Eighth day from posting. Formal yard. Standings rules, Wray's count. Edran, Glass, Iron Rank Two. Function: answer the petition in public. Benefit: if it goes well, the question gets answered where it was asked. Cost, if I lose: the petition stands, in the record, for as long as the record does. Cost, if I win too well: whatever the record says I did, somebody will read it who wasn't there.*

He looked at that last line for some time.

*Three audiences. Only one across the chalk.*

Then, beneath it, on its own line, because he wanted it where he would see it every night for a week:

*He told me not to tell him. He wants the floor to say it. I owe him the floor.*

# Chapter 18 — Odds

By the next evening the whole academy had decided the exhibition was the best thing that had happened to it all term.

Cael had braced himself for something else. He had spent the night after the posting lying awake in the narrow room, building the week ahead the way he had once built the reader under the lamp, carefully and from every unkind thing he remembered. He had expected the low, sidelong noise of the first week, the conversations that stopped on the stair as he passed. He had expected people to look at the hollow circle on his tag and then very carefully not look at his face.

None of it came. What came instead took him most of three days to recognize, because he had not been on the receiving end of it since Ardenmere.

People asked him things. Plainly, to his face, in corridors and at the meal hatch.

A Storm Path fourth-year he had never once spoken to stopped him on the stair outside the lecture wing and asked whether he meant to fight Edran close or at range. A pair of Copper first-years from the movement floor asked, very politely, whether observers were allowed to wear gloves. A girl from the hardening family asked if it was true that he had fought on the Ardenmere circuit, and when he said it was, she said, "Then you've done this before, with people watching," and went away looking reassured on his behalf.

On the second day, in the line for barley at the seventh bell, a second-year from the movement floor fell in beside him and began, without any introduction at all, to talk odds.

He was a thin, quick boy with ink on his thumb, and he talked the way circuit touts had talked on the stairs of the Ironyard, fast and pleasant and entirely without shame. He had grown up, he said, in a river town with a fighting pit behind the tannery, and he had been taking wagers on things since he could count. Not money, he added quickly. Greyvane didn't hold with money. Crusts and kitchen duty and the good seat by the stable fire.

"Edran's the favourite," he said, as if this were news. "Three to one, on the stairs. Five to two in the hardening hall, because they like an underdog in there. The Edge floor won't take a wager against him at all." He lowered his voice. "I'd be very grateful for anything you could tell me. Not much. A word."

"What sort of word?"

"Whether you've got something you haven't shown."

Cael looked at him. The boy looked back, bright and hopeful, with his bowl held out for barley he had forgotten about.

"No," said Cael.

"No, you haven't?"

"No, I won't tell you."

The boy considered this. Then, to Cael's surprise, his whole face split into a delighted grin.

"That's the right answer," he said. "That's exactly what a real fighter says. I'll put a crust on you myself." He took his barley and went off down the long room toward the fire, and Cael heard him a moment later announcing to a table of second-years that the observer had refused to give inside information, and that this was the best sign yet.

Lira, at the end of the bench, had watched the whole thing with her chin on her fist.

"You've made his week," she said.

"He wanted me to tell him what I can do."

"He wanted you to refuse. Touts never want the answer." She pushed half her bread across the table to him. "Three to one on the stairs. I've been asking. It was four to one this morning, and then somebody found out you fought on the Ardenmere circuit and it came in."

"You've been asking?"

"What is?"

"This." She waved her bread at the room. "Nobody's whispering. They're betting crusts. A week ago they were making up stories about rivers and sealed courts." She ate. "It's a fight now. Everybody knows how to talk about a fight."

He wrote it down that night, because it had taken him three days to find the shape and he did not want to lose it.

*Sport. That's how they're taking it, not as a verdict. I keep bracing for the dread, and every time it's a boy wanting to know whether I'll fight close or at range instead. I think this is what a question looks like when a school decides to ask it out loud, instead of letting it go round behind my back.*

He read it over and added one more line.

*Three to one. Lira says that's fair.*

He spent the six days on Edran.

He had done this before, many times, in a city where the cost of getting it wrong had been a split lip and a long walk home. He had spent six days on Feryn once, and lost to him anyway, and the losing had taught him more than the six days had. He had spent longer than six days on Reydan. He knew how to do it. The only new thing was the size of the price, and he wrote that down on the first night so that he would not pretend otherwise.

*Ardenmere, losing cost me a bruise and some pride. This costs the provision. Study accordingly.*

Edran trained on the open floor every morning before the first bell, in the far corner by the high windows, exactly as he had said he would. He did not change anything for the rail. He nodded to Cael on the first morning when Cael came in, and after that he did not look at the rail at all, and he did not need to, because Cael was always there.

The first three days taught him the Path before they taught him anything about the man.

Glass ran on discrete commitments. Cael had known that in outline since his second week, when he crossed out a page of notes about brittleness and started again. Now he watched it close enough to see the grain of it.

A Glass structure formed whole. Edran called a shell along his forearm and it came up all at once, bright and hard and finished, like ice that had decided to be ice everywhere in the same instant. Once it was there, it could not be changed. He could not thicken it on one side or thin it on another, or turn its edge a little further round as a strike came in. If he wanted a different shape, he had to let the first one go and call a new one.

Letting go meant bursting. The shell broke outward in that glittering spray that carried force away with it, and only after the glitter cleared could the next shell begin.

It was the exact opposite of Lira's Path. Wind could be feathered. Lira could start moving one way and change her mind halfway through the motion, and the wind in her went with her, adjusting as she went. It was the opposite of Brom's, too. Iron Skin could be laid on late and pressed harder as the blow arrived. Glass did neither. A Glass fighter chose before he moved, committed everything to the choice, and lived with it until it broke.

*Choose first,* Cael wrote in the margin, and then stopped and looked at the words, because they were his own rule, the one the Compression fragment had beaten into him on the road and Lira had beaten into him again before the first sitting. Glass was a whole Path built on the lesson he was still learning.

He watched Edran's offense on the second morning.

Glass was in the Edge family with Blade, and he saw why. When Edran struck, the shell along the outside of his forearm came up with an edge to it, a thin hard ridge along the bone, so that a forearm strike was also a cut. The taxonomy instructor had refused to finish his story about a broken bottle. Cael thought he understood it now. A thing that broke well still cut.

Edran ran his sequences in sets. Shell, edge, burst. Edge, shell, burst. He ran them in twos and threes and fours, against the empty air, without a partner, and every one was clean. Cael began to understand the shape of the training, and then the shape of the person who had chosen it.

A Glass fighter could not waste a motion, because every motion was a commitment he could not take back. A Glass fighter who trained sloppily would fight sloppily. There was no feathering to save him. So Edran did not train sloppily, ever. He had come to Greyvane at fifteen and drilled this way, Cael was willing to bet, every single morning for three years, until there was nothing left in him that had not been chosen.

*Glass doesn't forgive,* he wrote on the second night. *So neither does he. Not himself, anyway.*

On the third day, in the afternoon, he went to the archive and asked Prynn for the standings record.

"Which year?"

"All of his."

He found the seam on the fourth morning.

He found it the way he had found every seam that mattered, not by watching for a mistake, because Edran almost never made one, but by watching for the place where the precision itself cost something. It took him a long time to see, because it lived in a gap so small that only the shape of the whole sequence showed it.

Edran ran a set of four. Shell, edge, edge, burst. The first shell was already up when the sequence began, called before he moved, waiting on his forearm like a held breath. The second came up out of the glitter of the first, so fast that the two seemed to be one long motion. Cael had watched that a hundred times now and it never stopped being beautiful.

The third did not come out of the glitter of the second.

It came a sliver later. Not much. A tenth of a breath, perhaps less. But in that sliver, between the second structure bursting and the third one forming, Edran's forearm was bare, and his weight sat on the commitment that had just ended, with nothing new yet to carry it.

Cael watched him run the set again. The sliver was there again, in the same place, between the second and the third. He watched the set of three, and the set of five, and the sliver sat in every one of them, always after the second, always before the third.

He understood it on the walk back across the yard, with the binder held flat against his chest in the cold.

The first shell was ready before anything started. The second could form out of the burst of the first, because the first was a structure Edran had built at leisure, with his full attention, before the fight asked anything of him. But the third had nothing behind it. The ready one was gone and the second had been called in a hurry. The third had to be made from nothing, in the middle of everything, and making a structure from nothing took a sliver longer than making one from the glitter of another.

It was not Edran's mistake. Any Glass fighter alive would carry that sliver in a sequence of three or more, and the better trained they were, the smaller it would be, but it would always be there. It came out of the Path itself, out of the rule that a structure could not change once made. Edran had narrowed it as far as three years of perfect mornings could narrow it. He could not close it, because it was not his to close.

The record had said it too, in Wray's square hand. *Tires in the sequence, not the body.*

He wrote it in the binder that night in the plainest words he had.

*The gap sits between the second and the third. He didn't put it there; the Path did. There's nothing in him to fix. Patience, not correction.*

He did not sleep much.

On the fifth and sixth days he confirmed it, from three directions, because one direction was a guess and two was a coincidence.

He watched Edran drill alone in the mornings, and the gap held. He watched him spar in the afternoons against two partners Wray put across from him. One was a Blade fourth-year with very fast hands. The other was Hobb, who stood like a wall and made Edran come to him, so that every sequence had to run long. Against the Blade student the gap hardly mattered, because the bouts ended early. Against Hobb, who refused to be moved, Edran's sequences ran to four and five, and the gap opened after the second every single time, and Hobb, who was not looking for it, never used it.

Hobb nodded to Cael at the rail afterward, on his way out, and said nothing at all, and Cael was almost certain that Hobb knew exactly what he had been watching.

The third direction was the record. He went back to the archive on the sixth afternoon and read the four losses again, with Prynn's terrible tea at his elbow, and this time he knew what to look for. Every one of the four had been lost to a fighter who outlasted the first two structures in an exchange. Every one had been lost after the third exchange, when Edran was pressing to finish.

The gap held from every direction. It was structural, not incidental, and it was there whether Edran was fresh or tired, alone or watched.

He did not train against it.

He wanted to, very badly. He wanted to go into section four in his supervised hour and run the framework with an imagined Glass sequence in front of him, timing a step into the sliver over and over until his legs knew it. He did not. Edran had said he would not change anything for the rail, and Cael did not doubt it, but Wray would be in that hall for one of his hours, and Quenna for the other, and a seam that anyone could watch him rehearsing stopped being a seam the moment it was seen. He had learned that on the circuit, from the wrong end, more than once.

So he ran Lira's circuits in his supervised hour and the framework against nothing and the slow shoulder work for the Reydan joint, and Quenna wrote less than usual on her slate. Once she looked up at him across the section with an expression he could not read at all, and then looked back down.

The one thing he did train, those six nights, had nothing to do with Edran at all.

"Compression," he said on the fifth night, on Brom's floor after the eighth bell. "Quarter only. I'm not reaching for half this week."

Brom lowered himself onto the boards opposite with the care of a man whose left wrist still remembered the night it had been knocked back a hand's width. "Why not?"

"Because half costs four breaths and a locked shoulder, and I want both shoulders on the eighth." Cael set his left forearm across his chest and braced it. The wrist he had misrouted into a fortnight ago had gone from swollen to stiff to nothing, and he flexed it once to make sure it stayed nothing. "And because it gets worse when I leave it. Two days without practice and the timing slides. If I let it slide this week, it'll wake on the eighth with bad manners."

"It'll wake anyway," said Brom. "If he puts a shell on you."

"I know. I want it to wake the way it's used to waking. Quietly. So I can tell it no."

Brom looked at him for a moment, and then set his left palm on Cael's forearm without any further argument. Iron Skin came up under the skin like frost forming on a pane, and he leaned.

They did ten. Cael waited each time for the force to settle into the bone, and chose the road before the fragment could choose one, out and back along the line it came in, a quarter's width and no wider. He did not let himself imagine the channel any broader than that. Five of the ten caught. Each of the five went home into Brom's wrist with the small solid knock of a door shutting in another room, and each one cost exactly what the quarter had always cost, the hot bar across the collarbones and two breaths he could not take. None of them went into a joint.

On the sixth try he did something new, and he did it on purpose.

The force arrived. The fragment woke and gathered and reached for it, and the road was laid and open. He let it gather all the way, until he could feel the quarter held in him like water in cupped hands, ready to go home.

And then he let it go.

He did not send it anywhere. He opened his hands, as it were, and let it spill out harmlessly, as he had at the first sitting with three pens moving. His forearm took the rest of the push the ordinary way, with a dull flat ache.

Brom eased off and looked at him.

"You had that one."

"I had it."

"Then why—"

"Because on the eighth I'll have to have it and not use it," said Cael. "In front of everybody. I've done it twice in my life, both times by surprise. I wanted to do it once when I'd decided to."

Brom sat back against the leg of his desk and looked at him for a long while. Then he made the low sound in his chest that did him for a laugh.

"Most people practise catching," he said. "You're practising dropping."

"Lira says I've always done everything backward."

"Lira's right." Brom turned his left wrist over and looked at it, as if it might have something to report. "Again. Catch four. Drop the fifth. I'll count."

They did ten more. Four caught and went home. Two did nothing. Four times Cael gathered a quarter and held it and then let it go, and the fourth time it went so quietly that Brom, with his palm on the forearm, could not tell from his side whether anything had been caught at all.

"That last one," said Brom. "Did you have it?"

"Yes."

"I couldn't feel it go out." Brom took his hand away. "That's either very good or very strange."

Cael wrote it up that night under the four headings. *Compression-adjacent. Quarter only, by choice. Five of ten caught. Then four caught and dropped on purpose, after the gather, before the send. The last one so quiet Brom couldn't feel it leave. Cost of a drop: none that I could find. Integration: partial. Learning to put it down.*

Then she sat down across from him and wrapped both hands round her own mug.

"You've had it since the fourth day," she said.

It was not a question.

"I wanted to be sure."

"You're always sure by the fourth day." She blew across the top of the mug. "You've just stopped believing yourself that early. You used to. In Ardenmere you'd come home on the fourth night and say, *it's his left foot*, and you'd be right, and we'd go and eat."

"Being wrong on the fourth day used to be cheaper."

She did not argue with that. She drank, and looked at him over the rim, and he could see her decide not to say three different things before she said the fourth.

"It's a good seam," she said. "I read it. It's real."

"I know it's real."

"Then stop reading the page." She reached across and closed the binder for him, gently, with two fingers on the cover. "You've read it ten times. I counted from the door. There's nothing on it tonight that wasn't on it at supper."

He let her close it. He sat with his hands flat on the cover and the hot mug steaming beside them, and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

The fire at the far end of the room was down to its last coals. Two second-years were playing tiles beside it and had very carefully not looked up when Lira came in. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. It was the kind of quiet the two of them had got good at, on more nights like this than either of them could have counted, across a table in some building that did not belong to them.

"Brom's done his numbers," Lira said eventually. "Fetch. Eight today."

"Eight."

"He told me at the hatch as if he were reporting a death in the family. Then he went away looking pleased." She smiled into her mug. "He's started carrying the sheet in his coat. He takes it out when he thinks nobody's watching and looks at it."

"Like Edran with his shells."

"Like you with that." She nodded at the closed binder. "Everybody in this building has a thing they take out when they think nobody's watching. Mine's the dot." She drank. "Karis has two notebooks. Gerda checks the clock."

"What does Edran take out?"

Lira thought about it with the same care she gave an opponent's feet.

"The board," she said. "He goes and looks at the board."

They sat until the coals were nearly black and the tile players had packed up and gone, and the lamp at the end of the table had burned down so far that there was no reason left to sit on. Then Lira stood and took both mugs, and put her free hand on his shoulder for a moment as she passed, the way she always had, and said, "Sleep. Tomorrow's the seventh. You get one more day of being sure."

He slept better than he had all week.

# Chapter 19 — The Observer's Floor

The formal yard had never held so many people, and it had not been built to.

Its benches were meant for forty. By the time Cael came through the gate from the main hall, a quarter-hour before the first bell, the benches had stopped meaning anything. "He's slow," said somebody on the bench behind her. A third-year from the Edge floor, by the sound, all collar and opinion. "He's just running away."

"He's measuring," said Lira, without turning round.

"He's running away and calling it measuring."

He was standing the way he stood when he meant to stand all day, with his hands behind his back and his weight in his heels, and only his eyes were going.

"I saw."

She had been watching for that since the frost lifted, if she was honest. Not for the framework, because she had taught him the framework without knowing it and could read it in her sleep. Not for Edran, because she had watched Edran fight three times this term and knew roughly what he would do. She had been watching Cael's chest. A shell bursting against his guard was a push arriving in the bone, and the thing he had taken from Reydan on the last night in Ardenmere lived in exactly that place, and woke for exactly that. She had seen it wake on Brom's floor. She had seen what it cost him to catch half of a slow push. If it woke in front of two hundred people and three slates and he let it go home, the whole yard would hear the knock.

It had woken. He had let it go. She could breathe now, for one exchange.

Lira read it before Wray's hand came down, which was a thing she could do with Cael and nobody else in the world, and had been able to do since the second month in Ardenmere. She read it in the way he set his feet on the south mark: a fraction narrower than in the first exchange, his weight a little forward. He was not going to give ground this time. He was going to stand closer than was sensible, inside the reach of a sequence, and stay there.

Brom's eyes came round to her. "Where?"

Edran came in harder than the first time, because the first time had told him the observer would give ground, and a fighter who gives ground can be pressed to the chalk. He came in with a sequence already running, shell up on the left, edge rising on the right, and Lira saw Cael not give.

He stepped in.

Not far. Half a pace, when everything about the first exchange said half a pace back. It put him inside the arc of Edran's right arm, close enough that the first strike had to come short and cramped, and the framework took him round it by a finger's width. He slipped it by less than he could have, Lira saw. He could have gone out wide, out of the sequence altogether, and the third would have cut nothing but air.

He stayed.

He stayed for the third. She could see him wait for it, see the stillness come over his face that she had seen on him across a hundred circuit rings, when he had stopped trying to do anything and was only looking. The second structure burst on Edran's left arm in its spray of light. For a sliver of a moment, so short she would not have seen it if she had not been watching Cael's eyes instead of Edran's arms, there was nothing on Edran's left forearm at all.

A shell on the left first, already formed, the held breath. Then the edge on the right, up out of the glitter of the shell. Then the third. Then the fourth.

Cael let the first one land where it had to land.

It came at his chest, the same hard short push that had woken the fragment in the first exchange, and he did not try to slip it clean. He took it on the outside of his right forearm, angled, so that the shell burst along his arm instead of into it. The second came from above, the edge on the right, down at his collarbone. The framework read it off Edran's shoulder and moved him inside it, half a pace to the right, and the edge went down past his left shoulder close enough to feel the chill.

Then it burst. Edran had to let it go to call the third, and it went in its spray of light, and for the length of a sliver, between the second breaking and the third forming, Edran's right forearm was bare and his weight sat on a commitment that had just ended.

Cael walked back to the south mark. His left forearm stung where the edge had crossed it, a thin cold line of heat. His heart was going nearly as hard as Edran's had been under his palm. He did not look at the crowd. He looked at Edran.

Edran stood where Cael had left him in the middle of the ring, with the third structure still on his right forearm, bright and useless. He was looking at it. He looked at the shell he had called a sliver too late as if it were a page in his own handwriting with an error on it he had never seen before.

Then he let it go, and it burst into glitter and was gone.

At the table, Quenna had lifted her pen from the ledger. "One touch each," she said. "Three exchanges are concluded. The bout stands—"

"A fourth."

Edran had not raised his voice. He did not need to. The whole yard was listening to him.

Quenna looked at him across the frost-grey earth for a long moment. Then she looked at Wray, and Wray looked back at her and gave the very smallest nod, and Quenna looked at Cael.

Cael nodded too.

"A fourth exchange is allowed," said Quenna, "at the request of the petitioner, on the instructor's count." She wrote it down. "Take your marks."

Cael went back to the south mark and understood, as he went, that he had just learned more about Edran from those four words than from six mornings at the rail.

Most fighters, watching their best sequence fail against a gap they had not known they carried, would have needed a moment. They would have taken the draw Quenna was offering and walked away to think about it, and come back next term with the gap narrowed. Edran had not taken the moment. He had asked for another exchange at once, in front of everybody, with the gap still open in him and the whole school knowing it was there.

He wanted to fix it now. On the floor. While it was still costing him.

Cael set his feet.

Wray's finger fell, and Edran came across the chalk, and Cael saw at once that something had changed.

It was the timing of the shoulders. Six mornings of watching had taught him the rhythm of a sequence of four the way he knew the rhythm of the bells, and this one was wrong. The first shell went up on the left, ready, the held breath. The edge came up on the right out of its glitter. And before the second had burst, while it was still whole on Edran's right arm, Cael saw the left shoulder begin to load.

Edran was calling the third early. On the other arm. While the second still stood.

It was so simple that Cael almost laughed. The sliver lived in the gap between one structure breaking and the next forming, because Glass could not form on a forearm that still had glitter on it. So Edran had stopped forming on the same forearm. He was laying the third on the left while the second still held on the right, so that by the time the second burst, the third was already half made. In the space of four words and one walk back to the north mark, he had found a way to fold the gap in on itself.

It narrowed the sliver to almost nothing.

Almost.

The framework had already read the shoulder and moved him out of the line of the second edge, and he saw, as he went, the cost of what Edran had done. A third structure called while the second still stood was a structure called with half a mind. It was coming up on Edran's left forearm thinner than the others, paler, not quite finished. It would be there sooner. It would not be as strong.

The second burst.

The gap opened, and it was half the width it had been in the third exchange, and there was a thin pale edge already rising in it on the wrong arm.

A seam did not have to stay wide once you knew exactly where it lived.

Cael went in earlier than he had in the third exchange and lower, under the line of the half-made edge instead of beside it. He did not wait to see whether it would finish forming. He knew it would, a hair too late to stop him and a hair too soon to miss him entirely.

It finished forming as he passed it. The thin new edge came round with Edran's arm and caught him low on the right side, across the ribs, as he went by. It was a glancing thing, half formed and fast, and it did not cut so much as rake. It still hurt, a sudden hot line under the coat that drove the breath out of him.

His right palm was already on Edran's breastbone.

He held it there for a heartbeat, flat, firm, and felt Edran's heart under it, going as hard as ever. Then he stepped back.

Wray did not answer at once. She came to the edge of the ring and looked at Edran's half-formed edge, and at the line it had left in Cael's coat, and at Cael's hand, and her face did not change.

"Touch," she said. "Candidate. The petitioner's edge was unformed at contact. Glancing."

Quenna's hand came up from the ledger before Edran could open his mouth.

"That's the bout," she said. "Logged."

For a breath the yard was silent, with the silence of two hundred people who had come to see one particular thing and had not truly expected to be shown it.

Then the quiet broke, and it did not break into cheering, because Greyvane was not a place that cheered. It broke into the enormous, churning noise of two hundred people who all needed to say something to the person beside them at once. The Edge floor was on its feet and silent. The second-year tout was on his feet and not silent at all. The first-years on the tool shed roof were pounding it with their heels. Somebody on the wall was explaining the third exchange with both arms.

Cael stood at the centre of all that noise, ribs on fire, hands hanging loose, breath coming hard and white, and let it roll past him without trying to sort any of it.

He found, when he looked, that in the whole loud yard there were exactly three things that were still.

Wray, at the edge of the ring, writing on her slate as if the noise were weather.

Karis at the rail, with her pen stopped on the page, looking at him the way she looked at a timing that agreed with something she had written down before.

And Edran.

# Chapter 20 — Witness

Edran did not walk away.

*Edran withdrew. On the floor, in public, for the record, in his own words. Whatever you are, it's real. Not friendship. A witness. I don't know yet what that's worth.*

He stopped there, and remembered Brom, and turned back to the Glass page.

Under the line he had written in his second week, *Glass believes you can afford to break if you break first and on purpose*, he had left a question and a space beneath it. *And what does he believe?* The space had been empty for a month.

He wrote in it now.

*He believes the board ought to be able to tell. He would rather lose to it than win around it. When he found a gap in himself, in front of everybody, he asked for another exchange so he could close it while it still cost him. He narrowed it by half in the time it takes to walk to a mark.*

He looked at that. Then he added one more line, because Brom had told him to and because it was true.

*I beat a very good one today.*

He closed the binder and lay back on the bed with his right side held carefully off the mattress and breathed all the way down, slowly, three times, the way he had been told. It hurt every time.

Across the corridor Brom was snoring. Two doors down, Lira was quiet. Somewhere in the residence wing Karis was almost certainly still awake, writing in the second notebook by a low lamp.

Somewhere on the far side of the yard, Edran was almost certainly awake too, with his own sheet of paper and his own pen, working out how to make a third structure on the other arm come up a little less thin.

Cael found that he hoped he managed it. Then he fell asleep before he could decide what it meant that he hoped so.

# Chapter 21 — Read Back

He went to watch Edran the next morning, because Wray had told him he might and because he wanted to find out whether he would be welcome.

The ribs made the stair a misery. Wray had been right about that, as she was right about most things to do with bodies. Every step down from the residence wing pulled at the line across his right side, and by the bottom he was holding the rail with one hand and breathing through his teeth. He made himself breathe deep anyway, all the way down, the way she had said. It hurt as much as it had the night before. It did not hurt more, and he decided to count that.

The defensive hall at the first bell was cold and grey and nearly empty. Wray stood at the edge of the far section with her slate against her hip. Edran stood in the middle of it, alone, running sequences of three against nothing, and Cael saw at once what he was doing.

He was calling the third structure on the opposite arm while the second still stood, again and again, as he had in the fourth exchange. And again and again it came up thin and pale and late. It was not much. A Glass shell that should have been the thickness of a thumbnail came up like the skin of ice on a puddle at dawn. Wray watched each one and said nothing at all, and Edran burst it and began the sequence again.

Cael stood at the rail. He did not take out the binder.

After the ninth try Edran let his arms drop and stood breathing, looking at his left forearm. Then he looked up and saw Cael at the rail, and did not seem surprised.

"It's thin," Edran said. It was not addressed to Cael exactly. It was addressed to the hall in general, in the voice of a man reporting a measurement.

"It was thin in the yard."

"I know. I felt it go across your side." Edran turned his forearm over and looked at it again. "Wray says a term. To get it thick on the off arm while the other one's still up. Maybe more." He set his feet. "I said a term was fine."

"It is fine."

Edran glanced at him. Then he began the next sequence, and Cael watched the third structure come up a hair less thin than the one before, and understood that he had been told everything Edran meant to tell him. He stayed until the first bell had finished ringing. He did not write anything down. Some things he wanted to remember without his handwriting on them.

On the way out Wray caught his eye and gave him a single nod, and he went to his lectures holding his side.

The week turned, and the sessions turned with it, and by the second Tuesday after the exhibition they had a shape.

They had always had a shape of sorts. Since the first Thursday they had been held inside his supervised hours, in section four, at the fifth bell, with Quenna standing at the chalk and Karis on her borrowed stool. But the first sessions had been tentative in the way any new thing is, each of them feeling for where the edges were. Now the edges had been found. The work had settled into its own rhythm, the way his days at the Ironyard had once settled, until he could have walked through it blind.

Tuesdays and Thursdays. The fifth bell. Section four.

On the first Tuesday after the exhibition, before he began, Karis had done something she had not done before. She had taken a stub of chalk from her pocket, knelt at the edge of the section, and drawn a single straight line across the boards from one side to the other, about two paces in front of her stool. Then she had sat down behind it and opened her notebook.

Nobody mentioned it. Not Karis, not Quenna, not him. It simply cut the section in two: his side, for the work, and hers, for the watching. In every session since, nobody had crossed it, not even Quenna, who stood on Karis's side of it at the chalk with her slate as if she had always stood exactly there.

He did not ask about the line. He thought he understood it. Clause four said he could refuse a session, and clause seven said either of them could end it all with a word, but neither clause said anything about the plain physical space of a room. The line said it. It said *this much is yours*, in chalk, every Tuesday and every Thursday, and it said it without anybody having to make a speech.

The work itself was plainer than anyone watching from the rail would have guessed.

He ran the framework at half speed while Karis timed his recovery between passes against a mark on her page. He ran it at the working speed and she timed it again. He ran Lira's circuits, and the slow shoulder work for the Reydan joint, and the breathing Wray had given him for the ribs, and she wrote down all of it, the ribs included, as if a boy breathing carefully were as much a capability as anything else in the room.

Then there was the post.

Quenna had drafted it, in the end. She had come to the second Tuesday with a page in her hand and read it aloud in her flat sitting voice before anybody had moved. *The candidate may place his hand on the training post. The witness may lean on the post from the far side. The candidate and the witness may not touch.* Then she had folded the page into her folder and nodded at Brom, who had come to that session as witness under the second clause and had not known until that moment that he was about to be useful.

The post was a heavy upright of old oak that stood in the corner of section four, set in an iron foot on the boards, used for drilling strikes when there was nobody to drill them on. Karis had brought three pieces of cloth from the stores: a plain linen, a quilted padding of the kind the floor used under shields, and a thick felt. She wrapped the post in each in turn.

The surface read worked at contact. He had always known that. It worked out to a few paces too, thinner with every pace, but at contact it was strongest, a pressure along the skin that told him where a body's weight was about to go. What none of them knew was whether it worked through something. Whether he needed skin on skin, or only a path for the weight to travel along.

So Brom leaned on the far side of the post and shifted his weight wherever he liked, whenever he liked, and Cael stood on the near side with his palm flat on the wrapped oak and his eyes open, and called it.

Through the linen it was nearly the same as skin. The weight moved in the post the way it moved in a body, faintly, as if the wood had become a long stiff arm. He called nine shifts out of ten, and the one he missed he missed because Brom changed his mind halfway through, and even Brom admitted afterward that he had not known which way he was going.

Through the padding it thinned. The weight still came through, but late, and blurred, like a voice heard through a wall. He could tell forward from back. He could not tell left from right until it was nearly done.

Through the felt it was almost gone.

Almost. He stood with his hand on the thick grey cloth and his eyes shut, and Brom leaned and shifted on the far side, and for a long time there was nothing at all in his palm but wood. Then, just once, very faintly, something like a thickening came through the felt on the left side of the post, a sort of density in the cloth before anything moved. A breath later, Brom's weight went left.

"Left," said Cael, too late.

"Left," Brom agreed, from behind the post.

"I felt it before it went," said Cael. "Just once. Not the shift. Something before the shift. Heavier on the left, in the cloth."

When she had finished she looked up, across the chalk line, and did something nobody had ever done to him before in his whole life.

She read it. She read every word he had said through each of the three cloths, in his own order, with his own phrases. *Nearly the same as skin. A voice heard through a wall. Something before the shift. Heavier on the left, in the cloth.* She had written it down exactly as he had said it, even the places where he had said it badly, and she read it back in the flat careful voice she used for timings.

He stood by the post and stared at her.

The Compact had put him through its assessments. The circuit had given him a rating. A certification clerk had stamped a word on him. Karis had written him down and then asked him whether she had written him down right. It was. He had always thought so. Nobody holding a notebook had ever thought so too, until her.

Whatever the total was, she did not say it aloud. She nodded, once, and looked back down at the page she had not been reading.

Cael had watched Lira make up her mind about people for a long time. He was fairly sure he had just watched her do it again.

"Thank you," he said to Karis.

He meant it more than the two words could carry. She seemed to know it. She dipped her head, the small precise nod she gave a result when it came in, and asked for nothing else.

He did not say anything else that evening.

Karis went next, with her notebooks, and paused at the stable door and looked back once and said nothing. Lira went last. She put her book under her arm and stood up and stopped beside him, and laid her hand flat on his shoulder for a moment, the light pressure that meant *later, if you want*.

Then he was alone in the long room with the coals.

The hypothesis had not, he understood, done anything to him as a research question. As a question it was simple. Under a room he had always trusted, a floor had given way. He could feel all of himself shifting down onto the new hollow, timber after timber, and before he showed the damage to anybody he wanted to know how far it went.

Even them. Even her.

# Chapter 22 — Weather

He took the binder up to his room that night and read it from the back to the front.

He had not done that since the road. He read it often, but always forward, from the newest page he needed back to the first heading of the section it belonged to. Tonight he sat on the narrow bed with the lamp turned up higher than the oil allowance strictly permitted and opened it at the very last page, and turned the pages backward, one at a time, all the way to the beginning.

The Greyvane pages went first. The eleven Paths and the line at the bottom of each one, the board and the tags and the refusal, the ribs and the post and the cloth. Then the road. Then Ardenmere, a great thick block of it, the circuit and the Ironyard and Vell's ledger copied out in his own hand. And then, at last, the oldest pages of all, in the too-careful lettering of a boy who had been trying to make a notebook look official because something official had just spat him out.

He knew every page. He had written every one. He had never once read them in this direction, looking for this one thing.

He found it on every page he looked at.

Wind-adjacent was first, and Lira's. It had arrived after months of sparring with her every morning in Ardenmere, before either of them had money for anything better, months in which he had watched her move with the whole of his attention. He had watched that hard because his attention was the only thing he had that she did not. He had written at the time that he did not know where it had come from. Reading it now, from the back, he could see exactly where it had come from. It had come from a hundred mornings of watching one person turn out of a strike.

Pressure-adjacent was second, and Feryn's. He had lost to him. He had studied him for six days before the bout, mapped him, filled pages with him, gone to sleep thinking about the way his hands moved and woken up thinking about it. He had lost anyway, fairly, and afterward the redirect had been in him.

Iron-adjacent was third, and Brom's. It had come after weeks of sessions in which learning how Brom's body read force had been, in plain fact, the thing they were there to do. Watching Brom had been the assignment.

Compression-adjacent was fourth, and Reydan's. It had come at the top of the hardest fight of his life, against an opponent he had watched more closely than any opponent before, in the third exchange, when he had nearly lost and had reached for something with every scrap of attention he had left.

Behind the four sections sat the page for session nine. He turned past it without stopping, as he always did, and added nothing to it.

He sat with the binder open on his knees and the lamp hissing and the pattern lying on the pages in front of him. It was so plain that he could not understand how he had never once seen it. Then he understood exactly why he had never seen it. He had been living inside the entries instead of holding them at arm's length. He had written every one of them the day it happened and had never once stepped back far enough to see them as a row.

Karis had stepped back far enough. She had done it from the outside, without the binder, without any of it.

---

Fragments came. Everything else he believed about himself stood on that.

From the first nameless thing that had moved him the way Lira moved, in a sparring circle in Ardenmere, through every notice since, they had come on their own schedule, without being asked, like weather. He did not choose rain. He did not decide which storms crossed which stretch of sky. A sensible person learned the shape of the weather after it had come, and wrote it down, and made what use of it he could. He had written that, or something like it, more than once. He had said it to Karis's question in section four. *Does it feel borrowed? I don't know.*

He had never examined why it mattered so much to him that the fragments were weather.

He examined it now, sitting up on the bed with the lamp burning down, and found that the not-choosing had been holding up a great deal more of him than he had known.

It was what let him think of himself as somebody things happened to. Not somebody who did things. And that difference had mattered enormously to a boy who had been handed his classification at fourteen by people who never once asked what he thought he was.

*Shattered* was a thing done to him. The registry was a thing done to him. The four other cases, all gone within weeks, were things that had been done to four other people, and might one day be done to him. And the fragments, in the same grammar, were things that had happened to him. Everything in his file agreed on that grammar. Everything in the Compact's patient, faraway ledger of his life agreed on it. Cael, the object. The world, the subject. Things arrived, and he was where they arrived.

He had hated that grammar. He had leaned on it anyway, his whole life, because its other side had never once occurred to him.

If it could be aimed, the not-choosing had been a story he told himself.

And if it had never been weather at all, then what had it been?

He made himself think it, all the way to the end, because a thought stopped halfway was the most dangerous kind there was.

It had been something nearer appetite. Something in him that looked at people, and kept looking, and took. It had worked below the level where he could watch it work. It had taken from the people he cared about most in the world, without his knowing and without theirs. It had taken on a schedule he had told himself, for years, that nobody had any say in.

Lira, in the mornings in Ardenmere, with no idea that the boy across the sparring circle was doing anything but learning to fight. Brom, in his own sparring circle, offering his arm to be read because Cael had asked. Feryn, who had beaten him fairly and owed him nothing. Reydan, who had stood across from him in front of six hundred people and fought him honestly.

He sat very still on the bed.

So things had not been happening to him. He had been doing them. All along, it seemed, and never known. Not knowing did not soften it. It made it worse, the way a bad habit you never noticed is harder to forgive yourself for than an accident nobody could have stopped.

He did not sleep until the lamp had burned itself out. When he did, he dreamed of nothing he could remember, and woke with his right side aching and the binder still open on the blanket beside him, the last page he had looked at being the first one he had ever written.

*Couldn't look at Oona. Afraid I'd take something. Then glad she had nothing to take, which was worse. Made myself look. Nothing happened.*

He looked at the last line.

*Nothing I could feel happened. That isn't the same.*

*You supplied that part yourself.*

Cael took the binder out from under his arm and opened it on the rail and wrote the sentence down, exactly as Brom had said it, because it was true and he had not seen it. That was what the binder was for: the distance between a thing being true and his seeing it.

It did not close the question.

He knew that before he had finished writing. Brom had not closed it. He had made it as narrow as it honestly was, and honestly, it was still wide. It was wide enough to hold four fragments taken from four people who had never once been asked. Lira, Feryn, Brom, Reydan. Whatever he had meant by it, and he had meant nothing, because he had not known, the four of them were still on the four pages at the front of the binder with their names beside them.

Intent was the whole difference. Brom was right. But intent could only start from now. It could not reach backward. It could not go back into a sparring circle in Ardenmere and tell a boy watching a girl turn out of a strike that he ought to stop and ask her first.

He carried the narrowed thing through the fifth bell and the sixth. He carried it through supper, where he ate without tasting the barley and Brom did not look at him once, on purpose. He carried it up the stairs to the residence wing and then back down them again, and stood in the passage at the bottom with his hand on the cold wall.

Lira had not asked him anything in two days.

She had put her hand on his shoulder on Wednesday night in the stable, and had said *later, if you want*, and then she had left him alone with it. She had not knocked on his door. She had not caught him in the corridor. She had sat across from him at supper twice and talked to Brom about the honey thief and Wray's book and the rude baker, and had not once looked at him as if she were waiting.

She was waiting. He knew it the way he knew the bells.

There was one person in the world this belonged to before it belonged to anybody else. It belonged to her more than to Brom, because hers had been the first name at the front of the binder, and more than to Karis, who had only seen the outside of it. More than to him, perhaps.

He had learned, over a long time of turning toward hard things rather than away, that a weight carried alone only grew heavier for whoever was there when he finally put it down.

He went out through the residence wing door into the cold.

# Chapter 23 — Better Your Hand

She was already on the wall.

He saw her from the residence wing door, a dark shape against the darker yard, sitting where they always sat on the low stone wall with her heels against it and her coat pulled round her. The frost crept into his palms. Down the hill a dog barked twice and stopped.

"You told me what you knew," Lira said at last, "when you knew it."

Her voice was steady, with no give in it anywhere.

"In Ardenmere, you sat with it two weeks on your own, and then you told me. You didn't wait for me to catch you. You didn't wait until it was useful. You told me the day you were sure there was something to tell." She did not look at him. "You've told me every one since, the day it came. In your own handwriting. In a binder you put in my hands because you wanted me to read it. I never once made you."

"That's not—"

"I'm not finished." She turned her head then and looked at him properly, and in the starlight her face was very clear. "Say the thing in you was pointed at me, somehow, where you couldn't see it and I couldn't feel it. That doesn't touch a word of what I just said. Go on as you have. When you know something, tell me." She looked back at the yard. "I can't forgive you for something you didn't know you were doing, Cael. There's nothing there to forgive. There's only what you do with it now that you know."

"That's generous."

"It isn't generous. It's arithmetic." She bumped her shoulder against his, the old worn knock from more walls than either of them could count. "You want to know what would cost you with me? Really cost? Knowing, and keeping it from me. That's the day I'd stop believing the binder." She let that sit in the cold. "You've never done it. You aren't doing it tonight. You're sitting on a frozen wall telling me before you even know it's true."

He looked down at his hands.

"So stop apologizing for something you haven't done yet," said Lira, "and go and find out whether it's real."

---

They sat for a while without speaking.

Somewhere in the town below a door opened and shut, a tiny sound from far off, with somebody's whole life on the other side of it that he would never know. A lamp went out on the street worth walking. Another went out on the street worth avoiding.

"You're not afraid of it," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I didn't say that."

She was still looking at the yard.

"I'm afraid of most of what's coming for you, if you want the whole ledger. I'm afraid of the Compact the day it stops being patient. I'm afraid of that request, and whoever sent it, and whatever they make of three people's handwriting saying you're satisfactory." She drew her knees up tighter. "I'm afraid of the bill, every time the thing in you comes out bigger than anybody measured it last. I've watched you on Brom's floor. I know what half a push costs. I can count."

She breathed out, white, into the dark.

"But I'm not afraid of *you*. So a girl with lovely handwriting thinks there may be a handle on it. That isn't going to make me start being afraid of you." She turned her head and looked at him again. "If there's a handle, better your hand than nobody's. That's all I think about it."

"Better my hand than nobody's," he said after her, slowly, testing how much it would hold.

"Somebody's steering," said Lira.

She said it plainly, the way she said the score at the end of a bout.

"That's what her hypothesis means, if it's true. Somebody's been steering this whole time. In Ardenmere, in the mornings, with me. With Brom. With all of them." She did not soften it. "What's left to decide is whether the steering stays with the part of you that never asks."

He sat with that. The cold had gone right through his coat now and into his ribs, into the line across his right side, and he let it.

"I don't know how to steer it," he said. "I've never once tried. I don't even know what trying would be."

"No. You don't." Lira unfolded herself off the wall and stood on the frosted gravel in front of him, with her hands in her coat pockets, looking down. "So learn it, Cael. You were going to anyway. Not understanding something has never once made you leave it alone. It's the most reliable fact about you." The corner of her mouth moved. "And I've been keeping records on you longer than she has."

He looked up at her.

"In Ardenmere," she said, "the first time, when you didn't know what you were doing with me, I told you to tell me when you were sure. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"That's still the rule." She took one hand out of her pocket and put it on his shoulder for a moment, light and brief. "Tell me when you're sure. About any of it. And when you're not sure, tell me that." She took the hand back. "Same rule. Heavier load. It'll hold."

She went in.

He heard the residence wing door open and shut behind her. He did not turn to watch her go. He sat on the wall in the cold and understood that the thing she had just done had been done once before, a long time ago and much lighter. It was the covenant from Ardenmere, made over again under a load it had never been meant to carry, and it had held anyway. Things that had been built properly the first time tended to hold when the weight went up.

---

He stayed on the wall long after she had gone in. The cold came through his coat, and below him the lamps of the town went out, one and then another.

Each one going out was a household closing its accounts for the day. Behind him in the residence wing, Karis was probably still up, adding a line to a page called *Not asked*. Somewhere in a city three days to the west a woman kept a ledger of every bout ever fought on her floor, so that the truth about people would be stored somewhere against the winter. Somewhere further off, in Denvash, a man kept a workshop, and had once walked a boy out through a back door on the worst morning of his life.

The world was full of people keeping accounts. He had been one himself his whole life. He had never once audited the account that mattered most, the one written into his own architecture, in handwriting he was only now beginning to think might be his own.

He took the binder out of his coat. He could barely see the page. He wrote anyway, slowly, in the square hand he saved for lines he meant to keep believing after he had started to doubt them.

*If it can be directed, I have to learn to direct it. Not to have more. To stop not-knowing what I'm doing. Not-knowing is the version that eventually gets someone killed.*

He read it back once by the light from the residence wing's one lit window.

He understood, as he closed the binder, that the fear underneath the sentence was not really about power. It never had been. It was about whoever stood near enough to him to be used up by it, whether he had ever meant to use them or not. Lira on the frozen wall telling him the arithmetic came out clean. Brom with his column of numbers in his coat, defending barley. Karis with her chalk line and her page of half-questions. Edran, even, with his thin third structure, and Hobb, who had stood in front of him twice and hit exactly as hard as he was asked.

They were close. He had been watching all of them.

It was their account too, as much as his, and always had been. The only honest payment he could make on it was to learn to read it.

---

Brom was at the stable table at the first bell with porridge and his number sheet. He looked up once when Cael sat down, and looked at his face, and looked back at his porridge.

"You talked to her."

"Last night."

"Good." Brom ate. "What did she say?"

"Better my hand than nobody's."

Brom chewed slowly, considering it as if testing it with his teeth, the way he had considered Wray's three phrases after the first sitting.

"That's right," he said. "That's the right one." He turned the number sheet over on the table so the clean side was up and pushed it a little toward Cael. "Seven again yesterday. Didn't go down. Wray says that's the more important number. The one where it stays."

"Why?"

"Because the first number's luck and the second one's the floor." He took the sheet back and folded it into his coat. "You'll want to remember that. For whatever you're about to start."

He did not ask what Cael was about to start. He went back to his porridge with every sign of contentment, and Cael ate his own and found, for the first time in two days, that he could taste it.

Karis came through the stable on her way out with her notebook under her arm. She did not stop at the table. She slowed as she passed, enough to glance down at the binder lying open by his bowl, at the line he had written at the top of the morning's page. Then she looked at him.

"Your handwriting's back," she said.

"Is it?"

"To about the size it was before the request." She shifted the notebook under her arm. "I'll enter it. Cause unknown."

"Probable," said Cael.

She looked at him a moment longer, and he saw the flicker at the corner of her mouth that she usually corrected. She did not correct it.

"Tuesday," she said.

"Tuesday."

She went on out into the yard. Brom watched her go over the rim of his bowl and then looked at Cael, and did not say anything, and did not need to.

Quenna stopped him on the stair at the second bell, with her folder under her arm and the air of a woman already late for something else.

"The third sitting's record," she said. "Copied into the ledger last night." She took a folded sheet from the folder and held it out between two fingers. "A fair copy. Yours. You've read every other page of your own record before anybody else has, since Karis wrote that clause. I didn't see why the academy's should be the exception."

He took it. It was in her own hand, the slow, exact one she kept for the ledger. Wray's twelve words. The rotating seat's long paragraph, which ran to most of the page and which he read standing on the stair while students went past him on either side. It described the four exchanges with a heat instructor's eye for timing. It ended with the sentence the old man had spoken aloud in the yard, set down more formally: *The candidate fought the petitioner's sequence rather than the petitioner.* Below it was the withdrawal, entered in Quenna's hand with the hour. Below that, *Assessment satisfied*, and her initials.

He read it twice. There was nothing in it about a fragment. There was nothing in it about anything but a framework and a gap and a hand on a breastbone, and a petition withdrawn.

"It's dull," he said.

"It's very dull." Quenna took nothing back and did not smile, but something in her face eased. "Naveth told you."

"He said it was the dullest paragraph he'd ever wanted to read."

"He told me the same thing, at some length, at the sixth bell, standing in my doorway with his hands behind his back." She shifted the folder under her arm. "Keep it with the others. The day somebody reads the file who was never in the yard, I'd like you to have already read every line they'll read."

She went on up the stair. He stood where she had left him with the fair copy in his hand. Then he folded it and put it into the back pocket of the binder, behind the refusal and the stranger's note and Vell's sheaf and Karis's first four pages. He put it among the papers that proved things.

---

Lira took him down the hill at the fourth bell, because the post had come up from the valley the evening before and the man at the draper's counter had sent a boy to the gate to say that there was something for Greyvane.

She did not say much on the way down. She walked a half-pace ahead of him on the steep part of the road with her hands in her pockets and her eyes on the town below, and when he said something about the frost on the mill race she answered it, and when he did not say anything she did not either. It was not an unhappy quiet. It was not a quiet he had heard from her before.

The man at the draper's counter knew Lira by name and had the letter ready before she asked. He laid it on the counter between them, a single folded sheet, sealed with a plain blob of wax, with Cael's name and *Greyvane* on the outside in a square, careful hand that Cael would have known in the dark.

He did not open it at the counter. He did not open it in the street, or at the baker's, where the baker looked at his tag with fresh contempt and wrapped Lira's loaf as if it had personally offended him. He carried it unopened all the way back up the steep road, with Lira beside him, and at the top of the hill, at the corner where the road met the gate and you could see the whole valley, he stopped and broke the seal.

*Cael —*

*Both of yours came. The second a week behind the first, which is the post's fault and not yours.*

*You signed it with your eyes open. Good. A form is a form. It says what it says and you are what you are, and the two of them can sit in the same drawer without either one minding.*

*The shop is the same. The stove still smokes when the wind is east. I mended the bench you used to sit on, so it is ready for whenever.*

*You wrote that there is a woman who argued all afternoon to find a word for you. Mind her. People who will argue for a word are rarer than people who will argue for a wage.*

*I am glad of the two you came through the gate with. I am glad of the third.*

*Write when something happens. Write when nothing does. I read both kinds the same.*

*H.*

He read it three times, standing on the corner in the cold with the valley spread out below.

It was not long. Hesk's letters were never long. But he could hear every line of it in Hesk's voice, slow and dry, saying it across a workbench with his hands still busy. *I mended the bench you used to sit on.* He read that line a fourth time and had to look at the far hills for a while.

Lira waited beside him with the loaf under her arm. She did not ask what the letter said. When he folded it at last and put it inside his coat, against the binder, she only nodded, as if something had been set down in the right place.

"He mended the bench," Cael said.

"Good," said Lira.

"He says mind Quenna."

"He's right." She looked out at the valley. "He's always right, your Hesk. It's very annoying in a man you've never met."

They went in through the gate together.

---

He saw her stop at the board in the main hall on the way through.

Not the petition board. The petition and its answer and Edran's withdrawal had come down from their brass tacks two days ago, after their week on the board, and the space beside the calendar was bare again. She stopped in front of the calendar itself, the public assessment calendar in its weekly columns, and stood looking at one line in it.

He knew which line before he reached her. He had seen it go up, three weeks ago, in the clerk's small even hand, in the same ink as everything else.

*Re-certification Review, formal. Lira. Two recorded bouts before a panel of three. Formal yard, first bell.*

The date beside it was nine days off.

Two lines below it, on the next morning, in the same hand, sat Gerda's name, with the same words after it.

Lira did not say anything. She did not touch the line or point at it, or make a joke about it, or tell him what she thought the panel would make of her. She looked at it for about as long as it would have taken to read it four times. Then she shifted the loaf under her arm and went on through the hall toward the residence wing, with her chin level and her stride unbroken, as she had walked past the marker at the floor desk every morning since the second week.

He watched her shoulders as she went. A half-second after she passed the board, they came down by about an inch.

He did not follow her. He had been told something, on the wall, about telling and being told. It went both ways, or it did not hold.

Gerda came in from the yard a minute later, with her patched coat buttoned to the chin and her slip for the floor in her hand, and stopped at the calendar where Lira had stopped. She did not look at Lira's line. She looked at her own, two below it, and read it once, with her lips pressed together. Then she looked up at the clock above the door, as she always did, and checked it against something in her head.

She saw him by the archive passage, and did not come over, and then, after a moment, did.

"Nine days for her," she said. "Ten for me."

"Are you ready?"

Gerda considered the question as if it were a clause she had been handed to read aloud.

"I've read the procedure," she said. "All of it. Twice this week. Two bouts, a panel of three, the results entered with the assessors' initials, and a recommendation in the file." She folded her slip once along a crease that was already there. "The procedure is very clear. It doesn't care whether I'm ready. That's what's good about it."

"Is it?"

"It's what's good about it," said Gerda again, more quietly, "and it's the other thing as well." She looked at the calendar. "At the other place, I was ready. It didn't matter. The procedure was very clear there too."

He waited. She did not go on. She looked at the clock again instead, and he understood that whatever had happened at the other place was folded along a crease that was already there, and that she was not going to open it in a hall with people passing.

"Lira will be all right," said Gerda. "She fights like somebody who's already lost once and decided it didn't count." She tucked the slip into her coat. "That's worth a good deal on a floor. I used to have it."

"You could have it again."

Gerda looked at him then, properly, with her pale level eyes, and he could not tell what she saw.

"Perhaps," she said. "Ten days. We'll find out what the floor thinks." She nodded once and went on toward the training hall, and at the door she checked the clock one more time before she went through it.

---

At the sixth bell, on his way back from the archive, he looked in at the open floor.

It was nearly empty. The light was going, grey and cold through the high windows, and the last of the full-standing students were packing their bags at the far end. In the near corner, alone, Lira was working.

She was not sparring. There was nobody to spar with. She was running the old Ardenmere drills, the ones she had taught him in the mornings before either of them knew what he was learning. They were short sharp sequences of footwork against an imagined opponent, slip and turn and step and slip again, at full speed, over and over, until the boards rang faintly under her feet.

She did not see him at the rail. Or she saw him and did not look up, which with Lira was the same as a closed door.

He stood and watched her for a while. He knew every one of those sequences. He knew them because he had watched her run them a hundred mornings in a cold yard in Ardenmere with the whole of his attention, and because some part of him had taken them and kept them, without asking, without her knowing. They lived in him now, with her timing in them, so deep that Karis could count it from the far side of a chalk line.

He watched her now, and he knew he was watching, and he made himself know it.

She was very quiet, working. She did not talk to herself as she sometimes did, or mutter at a slip that came late, or laugh when one came right. She moved and turned and moved again in the grey light. There was something in it he had seen on her only a few times in all the time he had known her. It was the stillness of a drawn bow, the quiet of a thing pulled back as far as it would go and held, waiting to see where it would be sent.

He did not interrupt her. He went up the stairs to the residence wing and sat on his bed with the binder on his knees and Hesk's letter folded inside the front cover, and he did not write anything for a long time.

Then he took out a sheet of paper instead, because Hesk had said to write when something happened, and something had.

*Hesk —*

*Yours came today. Lira fetched it from the draper's and the baker was rude to us both, so it was a good day before I even opened it.*

*Thank you for the bench.*

*There was a petition about me. A student filed it, the best fighter in the school, and it was fair, and it was right about everything but one thing. They answered it with a bout in public. I fought him in front of nearly everyone here and won, not by much, and he stood up afterward and withdrew it, out loud, in his own words. He didn't have to. I think he's one of the people you'd call honest. I don't think he's a friend. I think he's something else, and I don't have the word for it yet.*

*I took a cut on the arm and one on the ribs. Both fine. The shoulder still complains.*

*There's a girl here from a bigger school who studies how people fight, and she asks before she looks. Nobody has ever done that. I've let her. I think you'd approve of the asking.*

*I'm minding the woman who argued for a word. You'd like her. She doesn't say anything she can't stand behind.*

*Something happened, so I've written. I'll write when nothing does.*

*Cael*

He read it over. It was not long, and he had left out more than he had put in. He had left out the fragment that woke for a shell and was given no road. He had left out the binder read backward, and appetite, and a girl he could not look at across a stable table. Some things were for the binder. But he had not written anything in it that was untrue, and he had told Hesk about the asking. That was nearer to the whole of it than any letter he had ever sent down a hill.

He folded it and wrote the Denvash direction on the outside, and set it on the desk for the next third-day post.

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the date at the top of it, and under the date, one line.

*Week eight. Something in me has always been looking, and taking, and I have never once told it where to look. That has to change. Not for more. So that the people next to me stop being the ones it lands on.*

He looked at it for a while.

Then he wrote one more line underneath, smaller, because it was the honest one, and because Lira had told him to say so when he was not sure.

*I don't know how. I've never aimed it once. I'm going to find out.*

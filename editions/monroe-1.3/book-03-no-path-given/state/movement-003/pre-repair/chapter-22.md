# Chapter 22 — Weather

He took the binder up to his room that night and read it from the back to the front.

He had not done that since the road. He read it often, but always forward, from the newest page he needed back to the first heading of the section it belonged to. Tonight he sat on the narrow bed with the lamp turned up higher than the oil allowance strictly permitted and opened it at the very last page, and turned the pages backward, one at a time, all the way to the beginning.

The Greyvane pages went first. The eleven Paths and the line at the bottom of each one, the board and the tags and the refusal, the ribs and the post and the cloth. Then the road. Then Ardenmere, a great thick block of it, the circuit and the Ironyard and Vell's ledger copied out in his own hand. And then, at last, the oldest pages of all, in the too-careful lettering of a boy who had been trying to make a notebook look official because something official had just spat him out.

He knew every page. He had written every one. He had never once read them in this direction, looking for this one thing.

He found it on every page he looked at.

Wind-adjacent was first, and Lira's. It had arrived after months of sparring with her every morning in Ardenmere, before either of them had money for anything better, months in which he had watched her move with the whole of his attention. He had watched that hard because his attention was the only thing he had that she did not. He had written at the time that he did not know where it had come from. Reading it now, from the back, he could see exactly where it had come from. It had come from a hundred mornings of watching one person turn out of a strike.

Pressure-adjacent was second, and Feryn's. He had lost to him. He had studied him for six days before the bout, mapped him, filled pages with him, gone to sleep thinking about the way his hands moved and woken up thinking about it. He had lost anyway, fairly, and afterward the redirect had been in him.

Iron-adjacent was third, and Brom's. It had come after weeks of sessions in which learning how Brom's body read force had been, in plain fact, the thing they were there to do. Watching Brom had been the assignment.

Compression-adjacent was fourth, and Reydan's. It had come at the top of the hardest fight of his life, against an opponent he had watched more closely than any opponent before, in the third exchange, when he had nearly lost and had reached for something with every scrap of attention he had left.

Behind the four sections sat the page for session nine. He turned past it without stopping, as he always did, and added nothing to it.

He sat with the binder open on his knees and the lamp hissing and the pattern lying on the pages in front of him. It was so plain that he could not understand how he had never once seen it. Then he understood exactly why he had never seen it. He had been living inside the entries instead of holding them at arm's length. He had written every one of them the day it happened and had never once stepped back far enough to see them as a row.

Karis had stepped back far enough. She had done it from the outside, without the binder, without any of it.

---

Fragments came. Everything else he believed about himself stood on that.

From the first nameless thing that had moved him the way Lira moved, in a sparring circle in Ardenmere, through every notice since, they had come on their own schedule, without being asked, like weather. He did not choose rain. He did not decide which storms crossed which stretch of sky. A sensible person learned the shape of the weather after it had come, and wrote it down, and made what use of it he could. He had written that, or something like it, more than once. He had said it to Karis's question in section four. *Does it feel borrowed? I don't know.*

He had never examined why it mattered so much to him that the fragments were weather.

He examined it now, sitting up on the bed with the lamp burning down, and found that the not-choosing had been holding up a great deal more of him than he had known.

It was what let him think of himself as somebody things happened to. Not somebody who did things. And that difference had mattered enormously to a boy who had been handed his classification at fourteen by people who never once asked what he thought he was.

*Shattered* was a thing done to him. The registry was a thing done to him. The four other cases, all gone within weeks, were things that had been done to four other people, and might one day be done to him. And the fragments, in the same grammar, were things that had happened to him. Everything in his file agreed on that grammar. Everything in the Compact's patient, faraway ledger of his life agreed on it. Cael, the object. The world, the subject. Things arrived, and he was where they arrived.

He had hated that grammar. He had leaned on it anyway, his whole life, because its other side had never once occurred to him.

If it could be aimed, the not-choosing had been a story he told himself.

And if it had never been weather at all, then what had it been?

He made himself think it, all the way to the end, because a thought stopped halfway was the most dangerous kind there was.

It had been something nearer appetite. Something in him that looked at people, and kept looking, and took. It had worked below the level where he could watch it work. It had taken from the people he cared about most in the world, without his knowing and without theirs. It had taken on a schedule he had told himself, for years, that nobody had any say in.

Lira, in the mornings in Ardenmere, with no idea that the boy across the sparring circle was doing anything but learning to fight. Brom, in his own sparring circle, offering his arm to be read because Cael had asked. Feryn, who had beaten him fairly and owed him nothing. Reydan, who had stood across from him in front of six hundred people and fought him honestly.

He sat very still on the bed.

So things had not been happening to him. He had been doing them. All along, it seemed, and never known. Not knowing did not soften it. It made it worse, the way a bad habit you never noticed is harder to forgive yourself for than an accident nobody could have stopped.

He did not sleep until the lamp had burned itself out. When he did, he dreamed of nothing he could remember, and woke with his right side aching and the binder still open on the blanket beside him, the last page he had looked at being the first one he had ever written.

---

The Thursday session was at the fifth bell. He went to section four at the fourth.

Karis was already there, setting out her stool and her two pens on the boards behind the chalk line. Quenna was not yet in. The section was empty, grey light from the high windows lying across the boards and across the oak post in its iron foot, still wrapped in the felt from the last session.

Karis looked up when he came in. She looked at his face once, and then at his hands, and then back at his face. He saw her take in whatever she saw there and set it down somewhere, the way she set down a timing.

"Clause four," he said.

He had not known until he heard himself say it that it was the clause he had come to say. He had come down early meaning to tell her he was well enough, and that they could work, and that nothing had changed. All three would have been lies. He had promised Oona he would not lie to her, and he had not promised Karis anything of the kind, and he found that it made no difference at all.

"Not today," he said.

Karis did not ask why. She did not ask whether it was the hypothesis, though it could not have been anything else, and she must have known it. She did not ask whether he was well, or whether he wanted to talk, or whether she had said something wrong on Wednesday. She nodded once, as she nodded at a result received.

"Noted," she said. "Tuesday?"

"Tuesday."

She bent and picked up her two pens and put them back in their case, and folded the borrowed stool, and went to tell Quenna, so that he would not have to. At the edge of the section she stopped, with the stool under her arm, and looked back.

"The clause works," she said. "Brom wrote it in three lines in capitals, so that nobody could misread it. I'm glad it's there." Then she went.

He stood alone in section four with the light lying across the boards.

*He can say no to any one session without saying no to all of them. No reasons.* He had watched Brom write it, in letters three times the size of anybody else's, and had thought at the time it was a small clause and a kind one. He saw now that it was neither small nor only kind. It was a door Brom had built into the arrangement on purpose, at the bottom, where a person who had fallen through the floor of himself could still find it in the dark.

He went to the stable and sat at the scarred table and did not open the binder, and when the fifth bell rang he was still sitting there.

Oona found him there at the sixth.

She came down the long room with her slate under her arm and a rolled sheet of paper in her other hand, and stopped at the end of the table, and did not sit down until he looked up. That was new. In the first week she would have sat and started talking.

"I'm learning the chart," she said. "For winter. The whole column. My mother says if I know all the Paths before my Arbiter comes, I won't be surprised whatever it says." She unrolled the paper on the table. It was a careful copy of the taxonomy chart from the lecture room, in her own hand, the families in neat capitals and the tiers along the top in coloured chalk that had smudged. "Will you ask me them? Brom said you know them all. He said you've got a page for eleven."

He looked at the chart. Then he looked at her, and stopped.

She was standing very close, with her hands flat on the paper the way Karis put her hands flat on a table, and her face turned up to his, waiting. Before the winter was out she would walk down the hill to the grey building on the corner. An Arbiter would come for her, and she would have a Path, and her first declaration, and everything that came after. She would stand on a floor somewhere and use it, with her whole attention, while people watched.

He found that he could not look at her.

It took him a moment to understand why. When he did, it went through him cold. He was frightened of looking at a thirteen-year-old girl. He was frightened that some part of him behind his eyes would look at her the way it had looked at Lira in a sparring circle, and wait, and remember.

And then, a breath behind the fear, came something worse. Relief. She had no Path yet. She had nothing yet to take.

He sat very still at the scarred table with that, and hated it, and could not make it go away.

"You're not looking at me," said Oona.

"I'm looking at the chart."

"You're looking at the table." She frowned at him with her whole face. "You always look at people. You looked at Hobb for a whole sitting. Lira says you look at people like they're a book you're going to have to give back." She considered him. "Did I do something?"

"No." He made himself raise his eyes. He made himself look at her, at the small serious face and the slate and the chalk on her fingers, and it was only Oona, and nothing in him moved at all. "No. You didn't do anything. I'm having a bad day. It's mine, not yours."

She weighed that, as she weighed everything.

"Is it a true one?" she asked. "Or a grown-up one?"

"A true one."

Oona nodded, satisfied, and pushed the chart a little toward him across the table. "Then ask me the Hardening family. I always forget Wall."

He asked her the Hardening family. She forgot Wall, and then remembered it, and was cross with herself. He asked her the Movement family and the Edge family, and Ember and Ash, and Mire and Tide, and she got them all, and then she asked him to ask her the tiers in order and the fraction of people who reached each one. She knew those too, down to *Platinum, historical* and *Void, a word in stories, you won't be tested on it*, which she said in a fair imitation of the soft-voiced instructor.

He looked at her the whole time. He made himself. Nothing happened. Nothing at all, except a girl reciting a chart and getting it right.

When she had finished she rolled the paper up and tucked it under her arm with the slate.

"You're good at asking," she said. "Better than my mother. She gives me hints."

"Hints are kind."

"Hints are cheating," said Oona firmly, and went off up the long room. At the door she turned back. "I hope your bad day stops," she said, and went out.

He sat at the table and looked at the place where the chart had been. It had been the most frightening quarter of an hour he had spent at Greyvane, worse than the yard, worse than the sittings. And nothing had happened in it at all.

He did not know yet whether that was evidence of anything. He wrote it down anyway, in the back pages, because a thing that frightened him that much deserved to be written down even if it turned out to mean nothing.

*Couldn't look at Oona. Afraid I'd take something. Then glad she had nothing to take, which was worse. Made myself look. Nothing happened.*

He looked at the last line.

*Nothing I could feel happened. That isn't the same.*

---

The second day was worse than the first, because by the second day he knew what he was doing.

He sat through a lecture at the third bell, the declaration ethics block for the senior years, which he had chosen weeks ago because an observer could attend any lecture he liked. He did not see, until he was sitting on the back bench with the binder on his knee, how the subject would land on him that morning. The instructor was a quiet woman from the movement floor. She talked for most of an hour about the ethics of sharing a declaration. When it was right to show one. When it was right to ask. What a teacher might and might not ask of a student.

Cael filled half a page with notes he could not remember taking. When he read it back afterward it was in his handwriting and he did not recognize a word.

At the end of the block the instructor asked the room a question, as she seemed to at the end of every block.

"If you learn something about another person's Path by watching them," she said, "closely, for a long time, without their knowing you're doing it. Have you read their declaration?"

Most of the room said no. A few said yes. One boy near the front said it depended what you did with it afterward, and the instructor said that was the right answer, and the bell rang.

Cael sat on the back bench with his pen in his hand for some time after the room had emptied.

At the fourth bell he went to the rail of the open floor, because that was where he went at the fourth bell. He realized, standing at it with his forearms on the cold wood and the binder unopened under his arm, that he did not know what he was doing there.

Below him the floor was busy. It was the hour for open practice among students of full standing, and most of the eleven Paths he had filled pages on were in motion at once. A Storm pair at the far end traded pressure across a chalk ring, and the air between them smelled of rain. Gerda was in the near corner with a Copper first-year, laying her low grey haze across the boards and walking him through it at half speed. Two Blade students drilled in their absolute silence. And in the section nearest the rail, under the high windows, a pair of third-years from the Edge floor ran Glass sequences against each other, shell and edge and burst, the glitter hanging in the cold air between them.

He watched the Glass-work.

He had watched Glass for a month. He knew its grain. He knew where the sliver lived between the second structure and the third, and he found the sliver in the nearer of the two third-years almost at once, wider than Edran's by half. He watched it open and close and open again.

And then, for the first time in his life, he caught himself watching himself watch.

*Am I looking at that because it's interesting?*

He kept his eyes on the Glass pair.

*Or is something behind my eyes looking at it like a hand looks at bread?*

He could not tell.

That was the whole of it. He stood at the rail and looked at the Glass pair, and he could not tell. Watching was the oldest thing he did. He had done it before he had ever heard of Paths or fragments or the registry. He had watched Hesk's hands at the workbench and the street outside Hesk's door and every person who had ever stood across a circle from him. Watching was how he had learned everything he knew. It was the one thing he had always been sure belonged to him.

Now he could not look at a pair of third-years running sequences without wondering whether some part of him behind his eyes was sitting down to eat.

He made himself look away. He looked at Gerda instead, at the Ash haze and the first-year moving slowly through it. Then he understood that he was looking at Ash now, with the same eyes. He could not tell whether that was better. He looked down at the rail, at his own hands on the cold wood, and found that his own hands were the only safe thing in the hall to look at. He kept looking at them.

That was where Brom found him.

---

Brom found people the way weather finds valleys. He seemed to have no intention about it, and he was entirely reliable, and once he had arrived he settled.

He came off the defensive floor still in his training things, with chalk to the elbows and his number sheet sticking out of his coat pocket, and took the stretch of rail beside Cael without asking. He did not say anything. He leaned his forearms on the wood and watched the floor below in what anybody passing would have taken for comfortable silence, for the length of two whole bouts.

That was Brom's method, so far as Brom would admit to having one. He let the quiet make the opening argument.

"You've been looking at your hands," he said at last, "for about a quarter of an hour. Before that, the Glass pair. Before that, Gerda." He did not look round. "Two days after Karis says it might be aimable. I can count too."

"I can't tell anymore what I'm doing when I watch." Cael said it to his hands. "That's the true answer. Maybe I never could."

Brom considered that with the thoroughness he gave everything. Cael could feel him turning it over, the way he would turn an opponent's stance to find where the weight really sat.

"First month we trained," Brom said, "in Ardenmere, you watched me like a ledger. I knew it. Everybody who's ever stood across a circle from you knows. You hide that worse than anything." He shifted his forearms on the rail. "You think the watching changed what it was because she gave it a name on Wednesday?"

"I think it might always have been something I didn't know about."

"Mm."

Brom was quiet for another bout's worth of the floor below. The Storm pair finished and bowed and stepped out of their ring, and the smell of rain went with them.

"When Wray corrects my footwork," Brom said, "she isn't stealing my balance."

Cael turned his head.

"She stands two paces off and watches me for an hour," said Brom, "closer than anybody's watched me in my life. Closer than you did, the first month. She finds where my weight goes before I've finished putting it there. Then she says *there*, and I fall over, and I get up, and I'm better." He was looking at the floor, not at Cael. "From the outside, that's the same as what you do. The same behaviour. Somebody watching somebody else so hard they learn the other one's body better than he knows it himself."

"It isn't the same."

"No. It isn't." Brom straightened up off the rail, slowly, rolling a shoulder that had taken something on the defensive floor that morning. "The difference is what she means by it. That's all the difference there is. And what you mean by it is the one part of the whole business that's yours to decide."

He caught Cael's face, and shrugged, a small careful movement.

"I'm not saying it's simple. I'm saying you've been sitting with the worst possible version since Wednesday night. And the worst version isn't the only one that fits." He turned to go. "She said *aimable*. She didn't say *hungry*. You supplied that part yourself."

He went off along the rail toward the residence wing, with his number sheet sticking out of his pocket and his chalked hands swinging. Cael watched him go.

It was, he thought, entirely Brom. He had arrived, said a handful of sentences that rearranged a problem from the bottom up, and left before anybody could argue with him.

*You supplied that part yourself.*

Cael took the binder out from under his arm and opened it on the rail and wrote the sentence down, exactly as Brom had said it, because it was true and he had not seen it. That was what the binder was for: the distance between a thing being true and his seeing it.

---

It did not close the question.

He knew that before he had finished writing. Brom had not closed it. He had made it as narrow as it honestly was, and honestly, it was still wide. It was wide enough to hold four fragments taken from four people who had never once been asked. Lira, Feryn, Brom, Reydan. Whatever he had meant by it, and he had meant nothing, because he had not known, the four of them were still on the four pages at the front of the binder with their names beside them.

Intent was the whole difference. Brom was right. But intent could only start from now. It could not reach backward. It could not go back into a sparring circle in Ardenmere and tell a boy watching a girl turn out of a strike that he ought to stop and ask her first.

He carried the narrowed thing through the fifth bell and the sixth. He carried it through supper, where he ate without tasting the barley and Brom did not look at him once, on purpose. He carried it up the stairs to the residence wing and then back down them again, and stood in the passage at the bottom with his hand on the cold wall.

Lira had not asked him anything in two days.

She had put her hand on his shoulder on Wednesday night in the stable, and had said *later, if you want*, and then she had left him alone with it. She had not knocked on his door. She had not caught him in the corridor. She had sat across from him at supper twice and talked to Brom about the honey thief and Wray's book and the rude baker, and had not once looked at him as if she were waiting.

She was waiting. He knew it the way he knew the bells.

There was one person in the world this belonged to before it belonged to anybody else. It belonged to her more than to Brom, because hers had been the first name at the front of the binder, and more than to Karis, who had only seen the outside of it. More than to him, perhaps.

He had learned, over a long time of turning toward hard things rather than away, that a weight carried alone only grew heavier for whoever was there when he finally put it down.

He went out through the residence wing door into the cold.

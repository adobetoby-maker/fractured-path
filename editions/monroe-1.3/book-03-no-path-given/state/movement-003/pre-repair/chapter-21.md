# Chapter 21 — Read Back

He went to watch Edran the next morning, because Wray had told him he might and because he wanted to find out whether he would be welcome.

The ribs made the stair a misery. Wray had been right about that, as she was right about most things to do with bodies. Every step down from the residence wing pulled at the line across his right side, and by the bottom he was holding the rail with one hand and breathing through his teeth. He made himself breathe deep anyway, all the way down, the way she had said. It hurt as much as it had the night before. It did not hurt more, and he decided to count that.

The defensive hall at the first bell was cold and grey and nearly empty. Wray stood at the edge of the far section with her slate against her hip. Edran stood in the middle of it, alone, running sequences of three against nothing, and Cael saw at once what he was doing.

He was calling the third structure on the opposite arm while the second still stood, again and again, as he had in the fourth exchange. And again and again it came up thin and pale and late. It was not much. A Glass shell that should have been the thickness of a thumbnail came up like the skin of ice on a puddle at dawn. Wray watched each one and said nothing at all, and Edran burst it and began the sequence again.

Cael stood at the rail. He did not take out the binder.

After the ninth try Edran let his arms drop and stood breathing, looking at his left forearm. Then he looked up and saw Cael at the rail, and did not seem surprised.

"It's thin," Edran said. It was not addressed to Cael exactly. It was addressed to the hall in general, in the voice of a man reporting a measurement.

"It was thin in the yard."

"I know. I felt it go across your side." Edran turned his forearm over and looked at it again. "Wray says a term. To get it thick on the off arm while the other one's still up. Maybe more." He set his feet. "I said a term was fine."

"It is fine."

Edran glanced at him. Then he began the next sequence, and Cael watched the third structure come up a hair less thin than the one before, and understood that he had been told everything Edran meant to tell him. He stayed until the first bell had finished ringing. He did not write anything down. Some things he wanted to remember without his handwriting on them.

On the way out Wray caught his eye and gave him a single nod, and he went to his lectures holding his side.

---

The week turned, and the sessions turned with it, and by the second Tuesday after the exhibition they had a shape.

They had always had a shape of sorts. Since the first Thursday they had been held inside his supervised hours, in section four, at the fifth bell, with Quenna standing at the chalk and Karis on her borrowed stool. But the first sessions had been tentative in the way any new thing is, each of them feeling for where the edges were. Now the edges had been found. The work had settled into its own rhythm, the way his days at the Ironyard had once settled, until he could have walked through it blind.

Tuesdays and Thursdays. The fifth bell. Section four.

On the first Tuesday after the exhibition, before he began, Karis had done something she had not done before. She had taken a stub of chalk from her pocket, knelt at the edge of the section, and drawn a single straight line across the boards from one side to the other, about two paces in front of her stool. Then she had sat down behind it and opened her notebook.

Nobody mentioned it. Not Karis, not Quenna, not him. It simply cut the section in two: his side, for the work, and hers, for the watching. In every session since, nobody had crossed it, not even Quenna, who stood on Karis's side of it at the chalk with her slate as if she had always stood exactly there.

He did not ask about the line. He thought he understood it. Clause four said he could refuse a session, and clause seven said either of them could end it all with a word, but neither clause said anything about the plain physical space of a room. The line said it. It said *this much is yours*, in chalk, every Tuesday and every Thursday, and it said it without anybody having to make a speech.

The work itself was plainer than anyone watching from the rail would have guessed.

He ran the framework at half speed while Karis timed his recovery between passes against a mark on her page. He ran it at the working speed and she timed it again. He ran Lira's circuits, and the slow shoulder work for the Reydan joint, and the breathing Wray had given him for the ribs, and she wrote down all of it, the ribs included, as if a boy breathing carefully were as much a capability as anything else in the room.

Then there was the post.

Quenna had drafted it, in the end. She had come to the second Tuesday with a page in her hand and read it aloud in her flat sitting voice before anybody had moved. *The candidate may place his hand on the training post. The witness may lean on the post from the far side. The candidate and the witness may not touch.* Then she had folded the page into her folder and nodded at Brom, who had come to that session as witness under the second clause and had not known until that moment that he was about to be useful.

The post was a heavy upright of old oak that stood in the corner of section four, set in an iron foot on the boards, used for drilling strikes when there was nobody to drill them on. Karis had brought three pieces of cloth from the stores: a plain linen, a quilted padding of the kind the floor used under shields, and a thick felt. She wrapped the post in each in turn.

The surface read worked at contact. He had always known that. It worked out to a few paces too, thinner with every pace, but at contact it was strongest, a pressure along the skin that told him where a body's weight was about to go. What none of them knew was whether it worked through something. Whether he needed skin on skin, or only a path for the weight to travel along.

So Brom leaned on the far side of the post and shifted his weight wherever he liked, whenever he liked, and Cael stood on the near side with his palm flat on the wrapped oak and his eyes open, and called it.

Through the linen it was nearly the same as skin. The weight moved in the post the way it moved in a body, faintly, as if the wood had become a long stiff arm. He called nine shifts out of ten, and the one he missed he missed because Brom changed his mind halfway through, and even Brom admitted afterward that he had not known which way he was going.

Through the padding it thinned. The weight still came through, but late, and blurred, like a voice heard through a wall. He could tell forward from back. He could not tell left from right until it was nearly done.

Through the felt it was almost gone.

Almost. He stood with his hand on the thick grey cloth and his eyes shut, and Brom leaned and shifted on the far side, and for a long time there was nothing at all in his palm but wood. Then, just once, very faintly, something like a thickening came through the felt on the left side of the post, a sort of density in the cloth before anything moved. A breath later, Brom's weight went left.

"Left," said Cael, too late.

"Left," Brom agreed, from behind the post.

"I felt it before it went," said Cael. "Just once. Not the shift. Something before the shift. Heavier on the left, in the cloth."

He opened his eyes. Karis was writing.

When she had finished she looked up, across the chalk line, and did something nobody had ever done to him before in his whole life.

"I'm going to read back what I've written," she said. "Tell me if it's what you said, or if I've smoothed anything."

She read it. She read every word he had said through each of the three cloths, in his own order, with his own phrases. *Nearly the same as skin. A voice heard through a wall. Something before the shift. Heavier on the left, in the cloth.* She had written it down exactly as he had said it, even the places where he had said it badly, and she read it back in the flat careful voice she used for timings.

He stood by the post and stared at her.

The Compact had put him through its assessments. The circuit had given him a rating. A certification clerk had stamped a word on him. A panel of three had written him down twice in closed halls and once in an open yard, and he had never once seen what they wrote until afterward, and never once been asked whether it was right. Karis had written him down and then asked him whether she had written him down right. She was treating the gap between what she had heard and what he had meant as a thing worth chasing down to the last word.

It was. He had always thought so. Nobody holding a notebook had ever thought so too, until her.

"That's what I said," he managed.

"All of it?"

He thought. "*Heavier on the left.* I think I said *thicker.*"

She looked at her page. Then she drew a single stroke through *heavier*, so that it could still be read, and wrote *thicker* above it, and the date in the margin.

"Thicker," she said. "Corrected on the subject's reading."

At the rail, Brom had come out from behind the post and was looking at Karis with an expression Cael had last seen on him when Wray told twenty second-years that he might be right.

---

She almost asked him something on the Thursday.

It was near the end of the hour. He had run the framework four times and the post twice, through the padding and the felt. Through the felt, twice more, he had caught that strange thickening before Brom's weight went, not every time and not even often, but twice out of perhaps twenty. Each time Karis had written it down and read it back.

He was standing by the post getting his breath when she looked up from her page.

"When you first—"

She stopped.

She stopped in the middle of the word, with her mouth still a little open, and he watched her close it. She looked down at her notebook. She uncapped her pen and wrote something in the margin, small, quick, three or four words, and capped it again. Then she looked up at him as if nothing had happened.

"Thank you," she said. "That's the hour."

Quenna signed the strip. Cael took it and stood by the post a moment longer, looking across the chalk line at Karis, who was packing her pens.

He knew what that had been. He had a page in the back of the binder that had been growing for a fortnight, a small side tally he had started without quite deciding to. In it he wrote down every time Karis's pen stopped while he was still working, which she had told him meant something she was seeing agreed with something she had written before. Under it, on a new line, he had begun to write down every time she began a question and did not finish it.

There were six of those now. This was the seventh.

He understood that the note in the margin was the question, filed instead of asked. He understood, too, that the sixth clause gave him the right to read everything she wrote about him before it existed anywhere else. The margin was something she had written. It was about him.

"Clause six," he said.

Karis stopped with a pen halfway into its case. She looked at him for a moment, and he saw her understand what he was asking and decide, very quickly, that the clause was the clause.

She opened the notebook and turned back through it. Then she turned it toward him across the chalk line, without crossing it, and held it out at arm's length so that he had to lean to see.

It was not a page from the session. It was a page near the back of the notebook, ruled into a single column, headed in her small upright hand *Not asked*. Under the heading ran a list of beginnings, each one dated, each one stopping where she had stopped it.

*When you first —*

*Does it hurt when —*

*Is the lead the same for —*

*Did you know before —*

*Who taught you to —*

*When you first —*

*When you first —*

The last one was today's, and the ink was still wet.

He looked at the column for a long time. Seven questions, three of them the same, none of them finished. She had built a whole page of the things she had caught herself almost asking. She had kept them as carefully as she kept her timings, and she had not once said any of them aloud.

"You could ask me," he said. "Any of them. Clause four lets me refuse."

"I know." She did not take the notebook back. "But clause four lets you refuse a session, not a question. And clause five says I don't write how. Most of those are how." She looked at the column. "If I ask, you'd have to decide whether to answer, in front of Quenna and the chalk and whoever's at the rail. If I write the half of it I've already thought and stop, nobody has to decide anything. It stays a question. Questions keep."

"They keep."

"Better than answers do," said Karis. "Answers have to be right."

She closed the notebook. He found that he minded the page much less than he would have expected. Perhaps he did not mind it at all. Someone who filed her questions rather than asking them knew that some doors could only be opened by whoever stood on the other side.

He wrote it up that night as its own line, in the back pages, under the side tally.

*K. keeps a page called Not asked. Seven half-questions. Three of them are "When you first". She showed me because clause six says she has to. She didn't seem to mind.*

He looked at it, and then added the honest part.

*I don't mind either. I thought I would.*

---

On Wednesday evening Brom gave back volume six.

He did it in the stable after supper, at the scarred table under the bricked-up hay door, where the four of them had begun to sit without anybody arranging it. The fire was down to coals. The kitchen had produced barley for the third night running, and Brom, who had called it honest food on the second night, was still defending it against everybody, Lira most of all.

Lira was in the corner of the bench with her boots off and one foot tucked under her, reading a thick brown book on defensive theory that Wray had lent her. She was reading it with the expression of somebody auditing an opponent rather than studying a subject. Cael had the binder open in front of him and was not writing in it. Karis sat at the end of the table with her own notebook, and her pen was moving in short runs and stopping.

Brom set the notebook down in front of her, squared to the table's edge.

"Six," he said. "Pages forty to fifty-two. You said bring it back at the end of the month. It's the end of the month."

Karis looked at it, and then up at him.

"With marks in it," said Brom. "You said you'd like to see them. I hope you meant it."

She opened it. Cael watched her turn to page forty and then fifty, and stop. In the margin beside the struck paragraph, below the younger hand's *Can't test this* and Wray's square *There is one now. Prove it. W.*, a third hand had added something. It was Brom's large square capitals, unmistakable even upside down, three lines of them, and below them a short column of numbers.

Karis read them twice. Then she turned the notebook so that Cael could see.

*FETCH DRILL. HOBB. DAYS 1–24.*

*11. 11. 10. 9. 9. 9. 8. 8. 8. 7.*

*NOT PROOF. ONE COLUMN. GOING DOWN. — B.*

"Seven," said Cael.

"Seven today." Brom sat down at the end of the bench, a little red along the jaw. "Wray says it's still not proof of anything. She says one column going down for a month is a column going down for a month, and the ceiling's a thing you prove across years, not weeks." He picked up his spoon. "Then she wrote the numbers on her own slate. I saw her."

Karis was still looking at the margin. Cael had seen her pleased before, in the small room with Quenna, a flicker at the corner of the mouth that she corrected almost at once. This was different. She did not correct it.

"It's the first number anyone's ever written on that page," she said. "Three people have written on it and only one of them brought a number." She looked up at Brom. "May I keep it like this?"

"It's your notebook."

"It's yours as well now," said Karis. "Twenty-four days of it." She closed volume six carefully and laid it on top of her own. "Keep counting. I'd like it back with more numbers next month, if Wray hasn't redrawn the curve by then."

Brom went back to his barley, looking as if he had been handed something heavier than a notebook.

Cael watched Karis sit with her hands on volume six for a while. Then he watched her pick up her own notebook and close it.

It was not the absent snap of someone done for the night. It was a flat, settled, deliberate closing, as if she had been carrying something for a while, looking for the right room to set it down in, and had just found it.

---

"I want to say something," Karis said. "It isn't a finding. It's a hypothesis. I want the three of you to hear it together. If I'm right, it changes what I think the sessions are for, and you ought to answer that together, not one by one."

Brom put down his spoon.

Lira put a finger in Wray's book to keep her place and did not look up from it. Cael knew that by now; it was the most attention Lira ever paid anybody. Keeping her eyes on the page was a kindness. It gave a speaker room to finish a difficult sentence before any face in the room answered it.

"Go on," said Cael.

"First I have to ask something." Karis turned on the bench, not to him but to the corner. "Lira. Clause five is yours. *No written speculation on how any capability arose.* I haven't written any, and I won't. What I want to say is speculation about how, and I can't write it down." She paused. "I'd like to say it aloud. Once. To the three of you. If you'd rather I didn't, I won't, and I'll never mention it again."

Lira lifted her eyes from the book at last and looked at Karis down the length of the bench.

"You promised him he'd read it first," she said slowly. "The thing your pen stopped for. At the second session. Brom told me."

"I did."

"And you can't write it."

"No."

"So the nearest you can get to *read it first* is that he hears it first." Lira considered that. "With us in the room, so he doesn't have to carry it across to us on his own."

"Yes," said Karis. "That's exactly why."

Lira looked at her a moment longer. Then she looked at Cael, and he did not know what she saw, but she saw it.

"Say it," said Lira, and went back to her book, and did not turn a single page of it for the rest of the evening.

Karis set it out as she set out every result: in order, plainly, hedged exactly as far as the evidence needed and not a word further.

"I've been documenting what I can see from outside," she said. "I don't know where any of it came from. You haven't told me, and I haven't asked, and I won't. But for a month three things have been sitting next to each other in my notebook, and I can't stop seeing them side by side.

"The first is the framework. I have a page on Lira's footwork, from two of her standings bouts. I took it from the rail, for her, not for you." She did not look at Lira. "When you turn out of a strike, there's a small drop of the hip before the step. A quarter turn. Most Wind fighters don't do it. Lira does. You do it too, on the same count, to the beat. Not something like it. The same."

In the corner, Lira's finger did not move on the page.

"The second is the read. Brom told me, the week I came, that he waits on the second half of his turn longer than anybody teaches. When you read a body, through the cloth or without it, you have a wait in it of the same shape. You name one shift, and then you wait before you're sure of the next, the way he waits before he turns."

Brom had gone very still on the end of the bench.

"The third is the lead," said Karis. "I told you the framework and the read lead by the same quarter of a breath. Two different things you do, and they both come early by the same amount. And at your very first session I timed the gap between your eyes settling on where a strike would come from and your feet going. That's a quarter of a breath as well." She put one hand flat on her closed notebook. "So in both of them, the first thing that happens is that you look. Whatever comes after, the looking comes first. By the same margin, every time."

She drew a breath.

"So here's the hypothesis. Two capabilities, each carrying the signature of a person you've watched very closely, for a long time, at close range. Both of them started by the same act, which is watching. Whatever it is you do, the watching seems to come first. It's upstream of everything else." She paused, and he knew from the pause that she had arrived at the part that mattered most. "And if watching is what goes in, then it's a thing you choose. You choose where to look. You choose how long. So in principle, whatever comes out of the other end ought to be directable. You ought to be able to aim it. Not only receive it."

The room went still.

It had nothing to do with the barley going cold or the fire needing tending. It was the stillness of a floor in a house when something heavy has been set down on it, and everybody standing on it waits to hear whether it will hold.

---

Cael held the sentence still and let it sit, as he did with anything that moved a thing he had believed was nailed down.

He was aware, from a long way off, of the coals ticking in the grate. He was aware of Brom's spoon resting against the rim of his bowl at an angle that would have let it slide in and drown, if Brom had been paying attention to anything but Karis. He was aware of Lira's finger still holding a page she was no longer reading.

Most of all he felt how large the thing was that she had just said, and how strange it was to hear it from somebody standing outside him.

Karis had never seen the binder. That was the thing he kept coming back to in the long seconds while nobody spoke. She did not know about Feryn, or the Pressure redirect he had banked for a year. She did not know about Reydan, or the thing in his chest that had woken twice that month and been given no road. She did not know the four names written beside the four sections at the front of the binder, or the notices copied out underneath them word for word.

She had only what she could see from outside. Timings and recovery intervals and the read through three grades of cloth. A drop of the hip, and a wait, and a quarter of a breath. The traces his capabilities left in the world where anyone could watch them. Out of nothing but that, inside terms that shut her away from every fact that would have helped, she had drawn the shape of a question the three of them had been walking round for a long time and never once said plainly.

It should have frightened him, and some of it did. If a clever enough person could read him from the outside, then one day somebody he had never agreed to would read him the same way.

But that was the second thing he felt. It was not the first.

The first thing he felt, before the fear had time to arrive, was relief.

Somebody else could see it. It was solid enough to be seen from outside. For so long he had been the only one able to measure himself that he had forgotten how lonely it was, the way a person stops noticing a weight he has never once set down. Now somebody had looked at the outside of him for a month and said, plainly, at a scarred table in a stable, *this is the shape of it*. And the shape she had said was the shape he had half known.

Then the dread came. It came in behind the relief and sat down next to it, and he let them both stay.

Brom spoke first, softly. It was not quite a joke.

"Told you she had a method."

"It isn't proof." Karis said it at once, before anybody could make it bigger than it was. "It's a hypothesis. Three pieces of support, all from outside. I'd want controlled conditions to test it properly. You might never want it tested. That's yours to decide, all of it, not mine." She looked at Cael. "I didn't want you to work out the shape of it from what I do and don't ask. I wanted you to hear it from me, straight, in a room with the people you'd tell anyway."

Lira looked up from her book then, at last. She studied Karis across the bench for a long moment with an expression Cael could not wholly read, some column being added up behind her eyes. Whatever the total was, she did not say it aloud. She nodded, once, and looked back down at the page she had not been reading.

Cael had watched Lira make up her mind about people for a long time. He was fairly sure he had just watched her do it again.

"Thank you," he said to Karis.

He meant it more than the two words could carry. She seemed to know it. She dipped her head, the small precise nod she gave a result when it came in, and asked for nothing else.

He did not say anything else that evening.

He sat at the scarred table with the binder open in front of him and the pen beside it, and wrote nothing. Brom finished his barley. The fire burned down to nothing. One by one the others went up to the residence wing in their usual order. Brom went first, with his number sheet folded in his coat and a hand on Cael's shoulder in passing. Karis went next, with her notebooks, and paused at the stable door and looked back once and said nothing. Lira went last. She put her book under her arm and stood up and stopped beside him, and laid her hand flat on his shoulder for a moment, the light pressure that meant *later, if you want*.

Then he was alone in the long room with the coals.

The hypothesis had not, he understood, done anything to him as a research question. As a question it was simple. Under a room he had always trusted, a floor had given way. He could feel all of himself shifting down onto the new hollow, timber after timber, and before he showed the damage to anybody he wanted to know how far it went.

Even them. Even her.

Chapter 50 — A Talk Owing

Lira was at the rail at the top of the fish steps where she had said she would be, with her arms folded on it and her face turned to the river, and she turned round when the door of the post stuck and opened and let him out.

She did not ask. She looked at him, the whole of him at once, and he watched her read it: that he was walking, and alone, and had no paper in his hand, and that there was something else in his face under the walking that she had not seen there before.

"It held," he said. "Legal review. Months."

She let her breath out. He saw her shoulders come down a finger's width, and then go up again, because she had seen the something else.

"And?"

"Walk with me."

He did not know, when he said it, where he was going. He knew only that he could not stand still at the top of the fish steps with the post at his back and say the rest of it, not yet, not with the door that stuck still swinging behind him. His legs knew where they were going before he did. They took him up away from the river and across the corner of the food market, past the bread woman's high cracked lantern and the cheese stall's dark one, and up the narrowing street toward the yards. It was not until he saw the black square of the Cinder House sign at the corner two streets below the lane that he understood.

It was Thursday, and there was a card.

Lira walked beside him the whole way and did not say a word. Once, at the corner of the market, she looked at him sideways as if she were about to, and he saw her pick the question up and look at it and put it down again beside her, as she had learned to do. He was grateful to her for that. He did not have the words in the right order yet, and he did not want to give them to her in the wrong one.

The yard was full of the Thursday noise, which was smaller than the Sunday noise and more knowing. Two men from the river workshops were in the circle, a Blade and a Force, going at each other in the thin spring light with the rope only two deep, and the pie boy was going along the rope with his tray, and the crows were on the eaves. Vell was at her table in the shade of the back wall with her tea and her ledger and her pen laid across the page.

Cael went round the outside of the rope to the table, and stood in front of it, and waited for the exchange in the circle to finish, because you did not talk to Vell while there was an exchange in the circle.

The Force man put the Blade on his back. Vell said "End of the exchange," and then turned her head.

She looked at him, and at his left arm, which he was still holding a little away from his side, and at the bruise showing under his cuff, and at his face.

"They say," said Vell, "that there's a woman come from regional to sit in the post."

"There was."

"And you've come up from the fish steps with a face like that, and you're standing in front of my table." She turned her tea a quarter turn by its handle. "Go on, then."

"I want a bout. Next week."

"Your arm."

"Sunday week it'll be an arm."

Vell considered the arm with the flat look, the thorough one. Then she looked past him, over his shoulder, at something in the crowd along the rope, and he saw the corner of her mouth do a thing it very seldom did.

"I've had somebody asking," she said. "For a month. Since before the Bronze. Whenever you came to this table wanting a bout, she said, I was to tell her before I told anybody else." She picked up her pen. "So I'm telling her."

He turned round.

Dessa had come away from the rope. He had not seen her in the crowd, which was like her; he never saw her arrive anywhere. She walked across the dirt to the table with her hands in her pockets and her hair tied tight, and stopped beside him, and looked at Vell's ledger, and not at him.

"You said not this month," said Cael.

"It isn't this month any more." Dessa looked at the ledger a moment longer, at the page where the last bout was written in Vell's small hand. "I said it was owing. You've been to see a Bronze since. I watched from the gate." She turned her head then and looked at him, at the arm and the cuff and the colour of the bruise, as she might have looked at a joint somebody else had made. "Sunday week, you said."

"Yes."

"Sunday week, then." She took her hands out of her pockets. "Say the terms. To me. I like to hear them."

He had said them to Vell on a frozen Saturday three weeks ago, before Feryn, and to Dessa herself in the autumn, before the first time. He said them again now, standing at the table with Lira somewhere behind him and the Blade from the river workshops getting up off the dirt in the circle. He came in unrated. He fought whoever Vell put across from him, and it was Dessa. It ended when Vell called it. Once he was over the rope nobody stopped it but her. What happened inside was his to carry, and nobody was making him do it.

Dessa listened to all of it with her face doing nothing at all.

"Good," she said, and nodded once, and went back to the rope.

Vell had written while he talked. She blotted the line and turned the ledger a little toward him, which she did not usually do, so that he could see it upside down: Sunday week — Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against unrated (vouched). Asked by both.

"Third bout," said Vell. "After the lamps are lit, before they're hot." She turned the ledger back. "Go and put that arm in cold water. And come to the grey half on Saturday and let the girl look at it, not before."

"Lira looks at it every morning."

"Then she can look at it on Saturday as well." Vell picked up her pen and looked at the circle, where the next pair were coming over the rope. "You're standing in my light."

He stepped out of it.

Lira took him up on the cooper's roof, because she said there were some things the yard should not hear, and she could see from his face that this was one of them.

They went up the ladder at the back of the shed and sat on the flat of the roof with their backs against the warm chimney. The spring had not got very far yet. The river was high and brown with the melt from somewhere up the valley, and the barges were going down it faster than usual, and over the water the Ranked core stood in its pale stone with the afternoon going yellow on its windows.

He told her all of it, in order.

He told her about the desk that did not rock, and the folded paper under its leg. He told her about Ilsev's three square things on the desk, and your objection was correct, and the way she had given him section four from memory, word for word, and then stopped and waited like somebody listening in a hall for doors. He told her section one says, and that he had stopped at the end of the fourth step and not hoped at her, and Lira made a small satisfied sound and did not interrupt. He told her about the silence, and the counting, and the book soft at the corners, and you're right, said twice. He told her about the slot of a room behind the partition, and Coss at the window saying resolved, and Ilsev telling him he could carry his closing form back two days in a cart, because it was light.

Lira laughed at that, and put her hand over her mouth, and then took it away again because he had not laughed.

Then he told her about the face-down sheet, and the flag.

She did not laugh at that. She sat with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and listened, with her eyes on the Ranked core, and when he had finished she was quiet for long enough that the light on the windows over the river went from yellow to the colour of an old penny.

"A mark on your record," she said. "That went on the morning she opened you up like an ordinary case. That she didn't ask for. That she can't find the end of, and she can see further up than Coss can."

"Yes."

Lira took one hand off her knees and held it up between them, and put a finger down.

"The sweep in the autumn. The man with the pin, walking the market for you five weeks after you came through the gate, when Torvin's seen four pins in eleven years." She put a second finger down. "Coss, who didn't know who sent it, and said so across his own desk, and wouldn't be asked twice. A careful man not knowing a thing like that is a thing on its own." A third. "And this." She looked at the three fingers. "Three things nobody can find the end of."

"Or one."

"Or one." She folded her hand shut. "The old man in the yard said the pin was only the end of a string. Do you remember? The day after the sweep, at his gate. Don't wonder whether it was you he was walking for. Wonder what changed." She turned her head and looked at him. "I think you've found a bit more of the string."

He thought about that. He had thought it himself on the stool behind the partition, in other words. It was different to hear it said out loud on a roof by somebody who was not inside it.

"I want to go back down to the reading room," he said. "Tomorrow. Ask Halden for whatever's after Part Six."

"And find what?" Lira did not say it unkindly. "There's no shelf for this. Your assessor went through every book she's got, and she's got more books than Halden. You've got a mark you can't see on a paper you'll never be shown, and you can't read it, and you can't fight it, and you can't cite it." She put her chin back on her knees. "Put it in the back of your book. Put it with the washers. You'd keep a broken washer if somebody told you it was evidence. Then leave it there, and look at it every so often, and see if it's moved."

"That isn't very much."

"No. It's what there is." She was quiet a moment. "She told you, though. Your assessor. She didn't have to. She could have written it in her report and gone home on the cart and you'd never have known there was anything to keep." She looked at the core across the river, which was going grey now, window by window. "That's something. I don't know what it is. But it's something."

They sat until the lamps began to come out along the lower bridge.

"You put your name down for Dessa," Lira said, without looking at him. "On the way here. Before you told me any of this."

"Yes."

"You walked out of the post with a flag on your record, and the first thing you did was go and get yourself knocked down by a Stone Copper Five."

"I didn't think about it. My legs went there."

"I know. I watched them go." She bumped his shoulder, gently, on the right side, the side that did not hurt. "I'm not cross. It's what I'd have done. I just want it written down somewhere that I noticed."

He wrote to Hesk that night at the kitchen table, after the lamps.

He wrote it all. He had promised to, and he wanted to, which was not always the same thing. He wrote about Part Six and the bone knife and the uncut pages, because he thought Hesk would like the uncut pages more than anything else in the letter. He wrote section four out, and section one, and the four steps between them, plainly, the way he would have written out a load figure. He wrote you're right, twice, and legal review, and months. He wrote Coss at the window. He wrote the flag, exactly as Ilsev had given it to him, with nothing added and nothing taken away.

He wrote that he had a bout with Dessa on Sunday week, and that she had asked for it herself, and that his arm would be an arm by then.

Then he stopped, with the pen over the page.

There was a question he had been carrying since the roof. It had been sitting in him, under everything else, and it had not got any smaller. He did not know how to put it, and he had nobody else to put it to who would know more than he did, and he thought that if anybody in the world did, it might be a man who had worked in the Compact's own offices at twenty-two and left them.

He wrote it as plainly as he could.

I want to ask you something, and I don't need the answer quickly. People here know me now. Not what I am; most of them don't know that, and the ones who do haven't made much of it. But they know my face. The cook. Vell. The yard. The boys with the bets. Forty-one people stood in the sleet to see me lose. If whoever made that mark wanted me gone, would any of that matter? Or does it only feel like a wall, because I'm standing inside it, and from the other side it isn't one at all?

I don't know which. I think you might.

— Cael

He read it over twice by the candle end. Then he folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning, and sat at the scrubbed table with the range ticking, and counted, as he did now, what he had not told Hesk.

There was nothing to count; he had told him everything there was. It was only that the everything had got larger.

At noon on Friday the carriers' clerk weighed it on the brass balance and looked at Cael over it.

"Thick one again," he said.

"Yes."

"Torvin's," said the clerk, and hung it on the third hook.

He went to the Sunday card that week to watch, because there was nothing else he knew how to do with a week, and Dessa was not on it.

He had known she would not be. She fought when Vell put her down, and Vell had put her down for him, and Dessa had never in her life wasted a bout on a week she meant to spend getting ready. But he went to the rope on the shaded side anyway with his hand on the iron stake. He watched the first bout, a pair of Coppers from the dyers' sheds, and the second, the kiln boy against a girl from Fenrow who let his heat go by over her shoulder twice, and did not let it go by a third time. He watched them the old way, for the Log, with the pencil going.

Halfway through the second bout he looked up across the circle and found Dessa on the third bench.

She was where she had sat for Corbin, at the end of the row against the cold wall, beside an old man in a scarf, with her hands folded in her lap. She was not watching the kiln boy, or the girl from Fenrow. She was looking straight across the circle and the rope at Cael, at his hand on the stake and the pencil in his other hand, and when he looked up and met her eyes she did not look away, as most people would have. She went on looking. Then she looked down at his hand on the stake, deliberately, and back up at his face.

He took his hand off the stake.

He did not know why he did it. He stood at the rope with both hands at his sides like a boy who has been caught reading somebody else's letter, and across the circle Dessa watched him do that too, and then turned her head at last and watched the kiln boy lose.

Lira had seen it from the barrel.

"She's still at it," she said at the gate afterward, falling in beside him. "She's been at it since the autumn. Every bout you've had, she's been on that bench." She glanced at him. "You went into her the first time with four pages on her. How many do you suppose she's got on you?"

"More than four."

"A great many more than four. And she hasn't had to write any of them down." Lira put her hands in her jacket pockets. "You'll go in looking for her foot."

"She'll have fixed the foot. She'll know I found it."

"So what will you look for?"

He did not have an answer. He walked a whole street without one, and Lira let him.

"Ask it the other way," she said at the well with the iron cover. "Not what's she got. What have you got, that she's had all winter to watch?" She stopped at the turning for her lane. "Think about it tonight. Tell me in the grey half. Same time tomorrow."

He thought about it that night on the cot, and in the morning, coming up the hill in the dark with the bread, he gave her the list.

"I put my hand on the ground at my mark. Everybody knows that now. I go in on a tell, when I've found one; I always have. I count my breath, four and four, the way she does. I stand off for the first exchange and watch. I don't strike much." He gave her the bread. "That's all I can think of."

Lira tore the loaf and gave him half. She did not say anything. She went to her mark, and stood there in the grey half with her hands loose, and looked at him.

Then she nodded.

It was a small nod, very short, a dip of the chin no bigger than a sparrow taking a drink, and it was his. He knew it at once, the way you know your own handwriting on a page you do not remember writing. It was the nod he gave himself on his mark when the plan had come right and he was about to go. She had found it in the autumn, and done it at him then, and he had spent most of the winter trying to wear it away.

"That," said Lira.

"It's nearly gone."

"In the grey half, it's nearly gone. When you're fresh and I'm telling you to watch for it." She dropped her hands. "Against Corbin, in the fifth, it was there. Against the Fenrow man it was there. In the fourth against Feryn it was there, a heartbeat before you went to slip, and if Feryn had been watching your chin instead of putting his hand where you'd be, he'd have had it." She looked at him steadily. "When you're tired it comes back. When you're hit, it comes back. And she's watched you be hit and tired in every bout you've had since the autumn, from that bench, with her hands folded."

He stood with the bread in his hand and did not eat it.

"Doss said—"

"Whatever you do twice, you've done once too often." Lira nodded. "You've done that one about fifty times."

They worked on it all week.

It was not like the step, or the build, or anything he had learned in the grey half before. There was nothing to learn. There was only something to stop doing, and stopping was harder than doing, because the nod did not come from anywhere he could reach. It came out of the very place where he made up his mind; it was the outside of the inside of deciding. Every time he got ready to go in the box drill, his chin went down, a hair, and Lira said there and he had to begin again. By Wednesday he could keep it in when he was fresh, nine times in ten. By Wednesday afternoon, after the mender's and two hours of the box drill at full speed, he could keep it in three times in ten, and the other seven Lira said there, more and more quietly, until she stopped saying it and only touched her own chin.

"Eight days isn't a season," she said on Thursday, sitting on the low wall. "It didn't go in a season and it won't come out in eight days."

"Then what do I do?"

"I don't know." She gave him his half of the bread. "Go in knowing it's there. That's more than you had yesterday."

Hesk's letter came on Friday, against the salt crock.

It was the answer to the thin one, the one with the second notice in it, and it was thin too, which he took as a good sign before he had opened it. Hesk wrote long when he was frightened and could not do anything with it. He wrote short when he had decided.

He took it out to the low wall by the pump to read.

Cael,

Two, then. I read your letter on the step and at the bench and at the kitchen table, which makes three, and I'll stop there, because there's a level for the survey office on the bench that won't true itself.

I'll tell you what I felt first, since you'd ask. I was frightened for your arm before I was frightened for the other thing. That should tell you what I am before I'm anything else. Work it in cold water. Don't lift with it till it tells you you can.

The other thing I can't help you with, and I won't pretend to. I don't know what a hand can carry. I don't know what anything can carry, in this. I know you wrote "one data point" in your own book where you'd have to look at it, and I was prouder of those three words than of anything else in the letter, the notice included. Keep writing them. A man who writes "one" when he'd like to write "two" will be all right.

A man who beats you and then buys your supper is a man worth knowing. Keep his account open.

Tell the girl she's still first. I find I like being second to her. It means there's somebody near you who's first.

— H.

He read it three times on the wall, as he always did.

Then he folded it along its own folds and put it in the leather book with the others, and went in, and the stew had real vegetables in it, though nobody in the house had fought since Monday week.

"For Sunday," said Torvin's wife, to the range. "In advance. The boots woman says you'll lose. I've a bun on you."

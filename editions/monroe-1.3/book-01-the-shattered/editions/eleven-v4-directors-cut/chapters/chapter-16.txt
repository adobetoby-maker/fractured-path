Lira caught him trying the skip on the second morning after Brenna.

He had been practising it at night, and he had not meant to tell anybody or to be caught. He was caught anyway, because she came round the corner of the public house earlier than usual and found him alone at the post in the grey half of the yard, going from catch to send with no middle, over and over, with his face screwed up.

She stood by the gate and watched him do it four times before she said, "Stop."

He stopped.

"Do it again. Slowly. I'll come in."

She came in at the cup-reaching speed. He caught her strike on the outside of his forearm and went straight on to the send without resetting, the whole thing in one movement, as he had seen her do it on the second day at the board, and her strike went away past his shoulder. Then her other hand came round from nowhere and rested, very gently, on the side of his neck.

"That's where you are. You caught it, and it moved you, and you didn't come back, and now your weight's on your left foot with nothing behind it. You sent my right hand away, and my left hand's come in through the door you left open." She took the hand away. "That's not a skip. That's the hole I told you about."

"It worked for you."

"It works for me because I know where my weight goes when I catch, without looking, and I've known it for years." She stepped back. "You've known it a week. Again. The long way."

He did it the long way, catch, reset, send, and she came in again and again. The third time he let his arm go round in the send without coming properly back to centre first, and her left hand was on his neck again before he knew he had done it.

"Again."

He did not do it a fourth time. He felt the moment in the catch when his weight went across, and he let the reset bring it home before he sent, and when her left hand came in he had a forearm in its way.

They went on until the shadow left the post. He made the hole twice more, early on, and then never again, because he had found the place where it happened. It was not in the send at all but at the very end of the catch, when the strike had pushed his arm across and he wanted to be finished with it.

Once he knew the place, it was a matter of not wanting to be finished.

She noticed, and he saw her notice, but she said nothing until they had stopped and she had sat down on the low wall at the side of the yard with the bread, and he had sat beside her, and she had eaten half her half.

"You're not a natural."

He waited.

"I'm saying it plainly, because if I said you were one you'd believe me, and it would be a lie, and I won't tell lies to anybody I train." She brushed a crumb off her knee. "A natural gets it right and couldn't tell you how. You get it wrong. You get it wrong the first time, every time, and then you see exactly why it was wrong, and then you don't do it again." She looked at him sideways. "Ever. I've been watching for it. I've made the same mistake a hundred times in my life. You make a mistake once and then it's gone, as though you've crossed it out."

"Is that worse than being a natural?"

"It's better," she said, after a moment. "Naturals coast. They go a long way on whatever they were born with, and then one day it runs out, and they're stuck there, because nobody ever made them learn how anything is put together." She looked at the post, where the sun had just reached the canvas. "You're the most efficient learner I've ever stood across from. Whatever you end up being, you'll have built it yourself, and you'll know where every piece of it came from. I'd take that over a natural every time."

He did not answer at once, because he was trying to find somewhere to put it.

"Nobody's ever said that. About the classification. That it might be a thing you build, and not a thing that happens to you."

"I wasn't talking about the classification." She stood up and held a hand out to pull him off the wall. "I was talking about you. They're not the same." She hauled him up, carefully, on his good side. "Up. We're not finished."

He wrote it in the back of the Log that night, after the front was finished, and it was the first line in the back that was not a worry.

Lira says I build. She says it's better than being born with it. I think she might be right, and I think I want her to be right more than I can afford to, so I'm writing it here and not in the columns.

The mender paid him on the third afternoon.

It was not much, a handful of small coins pushed across the bench without comment. It paid for two afternoons of sorting a tea chest full of hinges, hasps and screws into trays by size and kind, and for sweeping, and truing the spoke of a child's wheel and the arm of a pair of spectacles.

Cael counted it on the walk back along the river and found that it would not pay a week at Torvin's, though it would pay three days of one.

Slows the pouch, he wrote, in the back of the old notebook, under the figure. Doesn't stop it.

In the market, on the way home, a man at the fish stall looked up from his board as Cael went past.

He was the bearded cousin, the one who knew about fish. Cael had never bought anything from him, and had spoken to him only once, to ask what a long silver fish was called, after which the man had told him and gone back to his knife.

Now he looked up from the board with the knife in his hand, caught Cael's eye and nodded, a small downward tip of the chin of the kind men in Denvash gave each other across the depot yard, and then went back to his fish.

Cael walked on down the row with the coins in his pocket and his forearm aching, and for half a street he did not understand what had happened. Then he did.

The fish man had been at the rope, or somebody who bought from the fish man had been at the rope and had told him, and now a man Cael had never spoken to knew who he was and had decided that this was worth a nod.

He did not know what to do with that, and told himself it was only a nod. He put it in the Log that night anyway, in the front, because it was a fact, though he could not think what column it went in; in the end he wrote it in the margin with no column at all.

Fish man, bearded. Nodded. I've never spoken to him.

The letter came on the nineteenth day.

He knew it was the nineteenth because he had been counting. The letter to Hesk had gone in the west bag on the fifth day, and the clerk had said about a week, and Hesk would have needed a day or two or three to write back, and then it would be another week coming.

He had worked out on the road that the earliest a letter could come was about the nineteenth, and the latest was about whenever Hesk decided, and he had tried very hard not to think about the second figure.

Yeni had it. She was sitting on her cot with her sewing kit open beside her when he came up the stairs, and she held it out without looking up from her needle.

It was folded and sealed with a dab of plain wax, and on the outside it said Cael, care of Torvin's, ROOMS (the O leans), Unranked District, Ardenmere, in Hesk's square hand. Below that, in another hand, somebody had written Torvin's — runner.

"Came at noon. West bag."

"Thank you."

"Mm." The needle went in and out. "People who get letters back stay longer still."

He sat on his cot by the window and turned the letter over twice before he opened it, because he could not quite believe it was not going to say something terrible. Then he broke the wax.

It was two sheets, written on both sides, and the first lines were older than the rest; he could tell from the ink, which was a shade paler, and from the hand, which had been steadier and slower when it began.

Cael,

Ressa had two loaves wrapped the morning after. She put one back. She wouldn't take money for the one she put in your bag; she says it was yours. I told her you'd have eaten it on a wall somewhere about the first milestone. Tell me if I was right.

He had eaten it on a wall. It had been the second wall, not the first milestone, but it had been a wall, and he sat on the cot and laughed once, quietly, and Yeni did not look up.

Joren came to the workshop door with your note. He's read it standing up, flat-footed, the whole way through, and he wanted you told. He says he's working on it. He wasn't angry with you. He wanted you to know that most of all.

I went on Tuesday. Garrik's kept your washer in the arm and he says he isn't going to take it out. He wanted you told that too. The woman in the knitted hat says they won't know what they've got in Ardenmere. I'm inclined to agree with her.

He read that paragraph twice. He could see the back room, the folding chairs and the sheet on its frame, and Garrik threading with his back to the room, though he could not see Garrik's face, and he thought that was probably how Garrik would have wanted it.

The next part was in the same pale ink, and it was about the guild.

The guild has opened a review of my standing, because of the notice. They want a written account of who lives here, and I've sent it. It's routine, they say, and slow, and it will most likely come to nothing. I'll tell you how it goes, as it goes. I'm telling you now because you asked me to tell you things as they come, and I said I would, and I'm finding out that's harder to keep than I expected. Don't worry about it. I know you will. Worry about it a little, then, and then stop.

He stopped reading.

He sat with the sheet on his knee and looked at the window, at the grey square of it with the rooftops in it and a line of somebody's washing, and did exactly what Hesk had told him he would do: he worried.

He worried about the guild letter with its polite paragraphs, and the workshop, and the drawer marked Springs, do not use with a bent bracket's space in it, and the canal gauges and the survey levels and the clock inside the walking stick. He worried that the review would not come to nothing. He worried that it would, slowly, over a year, while Hesk sat in the workshop waiting for it.

Then he made himself look at the other thing, which was harder.

Hesk had told him, and had not had to. He could have written about bread and washers and Joren's heels and left the guild out, and Cael would never have known until it was over, one way or the other.

Instead Hesk had sat at the kitchen table, Cael could see it, with the pen over the paper and the lamp lit, and had decided to put it in, because Cael had stood in a workshop and asked him to.

As they come, Cael had said. Not managed.

And in the letter he had written at the waystation, sitting at the end of a table with a stew he had not eaten yet, he had left out a turnip cart.

He read on. The ink went darker and the hand quicker, because this part had been written later, after his own letter had come.

Your letter came on the twelfth day, so the clerk was right about the week and I'm writing back the same evening. I read it at the table. I read the part about the fifth entry three times, which seems to be the family number.

I'm glad you rode the last miles. The carter sounds a decent man, and a Stone Path with turnips is a fine thing to have met on your first day. I'm glad of the waystation woman's coins and of the bread you didn't eat. I'm glad you're tired and your legs hurt and you're all right.

Write and tell me what Ardenmere is like. Not what the reference room said. What it's like. Who you've met. What you're doing with your days. Whether you've found anything you're good at that nobody there knew about. I'll be here.

— H.

He folded the letter along its creases and held it.

Whether you've found anything you're good at that nobody there knew about.

He thought about the circuit, about Vell's book with no name in it and Marrow's slate and Brenna's shield coming round like a gate, and about the third exchange against Renn, lying in its corner with the cart.

He wrote back that night, at the kitchen table, after the others had gone up.

He told Hesk about Torvin's and the O, and Yeni, and the nut boy and the sister who flicked his ear, and the board at the crossroads, and Lira, and how she had stood behind him for eleven minutes. He told him about the circuit without dressing it up or making it smaller.

He wrote that it was a ladder that did not ask to see a card, and that he had put himself on the bottom rung of it. He had lost twice now, he wrote, to a Blade Path man and to a Shield Path woman, and each time he had lasted longer than the time before.

I'm not trying to win yet, he wrote. I'm trying to learn how people move. You taught me where to look. I'm looking.

He told him about the shoulder and the ribs, because Hesk would want the truth about those and because they were healing, and about the mender and the spice balance and the two afternoons, and the fish man's nod.

He did not tell him about the third exchange.

He did not decide that quickly, and he made himself say the reasons one at a time in his head, the way you call a load figure.

It would be a week getting there, and Hesk would read it at the kitchen table with nothing he could do about it except sit with it for another week, waiting for the next letter. And there was nothing to tell, because he did not know what had happened; he had a claim and no evidence and no ruling.

If he wrote it, he would be writing a guess in the same hand as the facts, and Hesk would read it as a fact, because it came from Cael.

All of that was true. He knew, while he was saying it, that it was also exactly what Hesk had told himself for years.

Don't make a thing real before it is.

He wrote one more line instead, at the bottom, and he meant it.

Thank you for telling me about the guild. I know it was harder to put in than to leave out. I'll worry about it a little, and then stop.

Then he signed it, and folded it, and put it inside the leather book to keep it flat, and went up to bed with the feeling of a man who has paid a debt in one currency and taken on another in a different one.

Amrit Sole was the next night's third bout. Vell had told him so on the morning after Brenna, over the ledger. Copper Rank 4, Ember Path. He won't chase you round the circle. He won't need to.

He was older than Brenna, older than Renn, a heavy-shouldered man somewhere near forty with a shaved head and a short grey beard, and forearms that were shiny and hairless in patches, like a baker's after years of reaching into ovens.

He sat on an upturned crate by the wall through the first two bouts and drank water and spoke to nobody. When a boy tripped over his outstretched boots on the way to the pie tray, he caught the boy by the collar before he fell, set him on his feet, and gave him back the coin he had dropped.

"He cooks," said Lira, beside him on the barrel. "At the eating house by the lower bridge. Has done for years. He fights twice a month because he says it's restful."

"Restful."

"He says nobody shouts at him in the circle."

On Marrow's slate, the line had moved again. Over-under: three exchanges.

"He's an honest fighter. He doesn't feint much. He doesn't need to. But I'll tell you one thing, because you'll find it out in about four seconds anyway." She did not look at him. "He doesn't miss with the heat by much. And at Copper, Ember isn't fire, mostly. It's the feeling of standing too near an open oven. Until it's not."

"Third bout," said Vell.

Amrit stood up from his crate as if he had been called to a table, and stepped over the rope, and nodded to Cael across the circle with grave courtesy, and set his feet.

"Amrit Sole. Copper Rank 4, Ember Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Amrit raised his hands. He held them loose and open in front of his chest, the palms turned a little toward Cael, as if he were warming them at a fire.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the air in front of his hands began to move.

It was very slight, like the air above a road at midday or above the top of a stove when the iron has just begun to tick. The lamplight behind his hands wavered and the rope behind him seemed to ripple, though the rope was perfectly still. Cael saw it, and wondered what it was, and was still wondering when Amrit stepped in and opened his hands.

Heat came out of them.

It was not flame; Lira had been right. It was the breath of an oven with its door flung open, a wall of dry, hard heat that struck his face and his chest and his raised forearm all at once. It drove the air out of his mouth and made his eyes water so hard that for a moment he could not see.

He went back three steps without deciding to, and when his eyes cleared there was a smell of singed wool, and he looked down and saw that the cuff of his right sleeve had gone brown at the edge.

Amrit had not followed him. He was standing in the middle of the circle with his hands lowered, waiting, with the patience of a man who knew how long a loaf took.

The first exchange was over. It had taught him one thing, which he had nearly missed and then had not: the air had moved before the heat came. Not much before. Half a beat.

He went back to his mark and watched Amrit's hands.

In the second exchange he moved on the shimmer.

Amrit came in slowly, with his hands up and open, and Cael watched the air in front of them, and the moment it began to waver he went sideways, left, along the groove. The heat came out of Amrit's palms into the place where he had been standing. It brushed his right cheek and his ear in passing, hot and dry, and did no more than that.

Amrit turned to face him. The shimmer came again. Cael went again, right this time, and again the heat went past him.

Without the heat, Amrit's hands were only hands. They were big, and they were quick enough, but they were a cook's hands and not a fighter's, and when he swung a plain fist at Cael's shoulder it came round so wide that Cael could have walked under it. He did.

He came out on the far side of Amrit with his heart going hard and a grin trying to get onto his face that he did not let onto it.

At the table, Vell had stopped writing. She sat with the pen lifted above the page and simply watched, which he had not seen her do before.

In the third exchange he read the shimmer every time, and he began to feel something he had not felt in the circle before, which was that he knew what was going to happen.

The air moved. He went. The heat came where he had been. The air moved. He went. He was not trying to touch Amrit, any more than he had tried to touch Brenna. He was only trying to be somewhere else when the heat came, and he was managing it, exchange after exchange, and every time he managed it the knowing got a little surer and a little warmer.

That should have warned him. That was the line he put in the back of the Log, later. It did not warn him at the time.

He was watching Amrit's hands. He was watching nothing else. And across the circle Amrit had begun to watch him watching.

Cael saw it, at the very end of the third exchange, and did not understand what he was seeing. Amrit's eyes went to Cael's eyes, and followed them down to his own hands, and came back up. It was quick, and grave, and almost kind. It was the look of a cook who has noticed that the boy at the back door is not watching the pot but the hand that lifts the lid.

In the fourth exchange the air in front of Amrit's hands began to waver, and Cael went sideways, left, along the groove, and nothing came out of Amrit's hands at all.

The heat was not there. The shimmer was there, wobbling the lamplight, as clear as it had been every time before, but Amrit was holding it, keeping it in his palms like a breath held in the chest, and Cael had already gone. He was committed to the step, his weight going left, his eyes on the shimmer still.

And Amrit, who had not moved his feet at all, simply stepped after him and swung a plain closed fist into the meat of his right arm above the elbow.

It was not a heat strike, only a fist, a cook's fist with twenty years of lifting stockpots behind it, and it turned Cael half round and nearly put him down.

He stayed up. He stood on the edge of the groove with his right arm gone dead from the shoulder to the fingers, and looked at Amrit, and Amrit looked back at him, and there was nothing in Amrit's face but the grave courtesy he had had from the start.

He showed me the shimmer, Cael thought. He showed it to me on purpose. Like Baro's left side. He's put it there for me to see.

Vell called the end of the exchange. He went back to his mark with his right arm hanging and the feeling coming back into it in hot pins.

In the fifth exchange he tried to tell them apart.

He watched the shimmer and he watched Amrit's breath, because Brenna had taught him that the breath could talk. When the shimmer came the first time, Amrit's chest stopped moving. His shoulders went still, as a man's do when he braces to lift something. Cael went, and the heat came, and passed him.

The second time the shimmer came, Amrit was breathing. His chest went up and down under his shirt. Cael held. He held his ground for the length of a heartbeat, against everything his feet wanted, and the heat did not come, and Amrit's plain fist came instead, and he slipped it.

The third time, he could not tell.

The shimmer came and he looked at Amrit's chest and he could not see it moving or not moving. The lamp was behind Amrit now, and his shirt was dark, and Cael had a half-heartbeat to choose. He chose wrong. He held, and the heat came, full, from a pace away, and he got his left forearm up in front of his face, the scraped one, and the heat struck it like the side of a kettle.

He did not cry out. He went back four steps with his teeth shut and his forearm held away from his body as if it belonged to somebody else, and the sixth exchange came, and he knew before it began that it was going to be the last one.

He knew it with great clearness. He knew it the way he knew the bracket would fail when the needle swung.

Amrit came in, unhurried. He had seen the forearm. He was not going to chase it, and he was not going to spare it either; that was not how this yard worked.

He was simply going to walk Cael to the edge of the circle, where there was nowhere left to step, and open his hands from a pace away. Cael had been watching him do it for five exchanges. He could see the whole of it before it happened: the groove at his heel, the shimmer, no room left to the left or to the right, the full open heat at a distance from which there was no getting the arm up in time.

He would be burned. Not badly, perhaps. Badly enough. He would be burned in front of sixty people on Lira's vouch, and he would go down into the groove, and he would learn nothing from it at all that he did not already know.

The groove was at his heel.

The shimmer began.

He raised his hand.

He held it up, open, the palm toward Vell, the way the grey-templed Stone woman had held hers up in the second bout on his first afternoon in this yard. He held it there, breathing hard, with the heel of his boot on the groove.

Amrit stopped. The shimmer in front of his hands wavered once and went out, like a stove door closing.

"Called," said Vell. "Conceded. Ember Path."

The yard did not applaud him as it had applauded the Stone woman, and he had not expected it to. But there was some clapping here and there along the rope, not loud and mostly from the shaded side. One of the people clapping was a girl on a barrel by the drain who did not stop until he had walked all the way to the table.

Vell wrote a good deal, then blotted it and looked up at him, and her eyes went to his forearm, which had come up red and shiny from the wrist halfway to the elbow, over the old scrape, and then back to his face.

"Six," she said. Then, after a moment, in exactly the same voice: "That was the right breath. A breath later and you'd have been at a healer's in the morning."

"I know."

"I know you know. That's why it was the right one." She closed the ledger. "Go and put that arm in cold water. Not butter. People will tell you butter. They're wrong."

Amrit was waiting at the water bucket. He had a clean cloth in his hand, wet, and he held it out without a word. Cael took it and laid it over his forearm, and the cold went into the burn with a feeling so close to pain that for a moment he could not tell which it was.

"Keep it wet." His voice was low and unhurried and surprisingly gentle. "A long time. Longer than you'd think. Don't break the blister if one comes up; let it go down by itself." He looked at the arm. "It'll be red for a week and it'll itch for two. It won't mark you." He picked up his own cup and drank and set it down. "You were watching my breath, in the fifth."

"Yes."

"It was a good thing to watch." Amrit nodded, as if he had tasted something and found it nearly done. "Not good enough yet. But good." Then he went off through the gate into the dark, toward the lower bridge and the eating house, and did not look back.

Lira did not walk him home that night. She had a bout of her own to see across the district. She only came down off the barrel long enough to wet the cloth again at the pump by the gate and lay it back over his arm with more care than she would have admitted to.

"The right people clapped," she said. "And Vell said the right breath. I've never heard her say that to anybody." Then she was gone, and he walked back to Torvin's alone, with his arm held out from his side and something in his chest that was not a bruise gone warm and tight in a way he did not trust.

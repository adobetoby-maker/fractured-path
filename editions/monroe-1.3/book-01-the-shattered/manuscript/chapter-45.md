# Chapter 45 — Word Ahead

Lira could not build, but she could hold, and for three weeks she held.

She stood at her mark in the grey half every morning with the frost on the dirt and the post at her back, and got ready to step, and did not step. She kept the readiness in her for a breath, or two, or three, while she did everything she could think of on top of it to hide it: she walked, and rolled her shoulders, and talked to him about the bread, and pushed her hair off her face with her wrist, and once, to make the girls on the wall laugh, sang the first two lines of a song about a miller's daughter. Then, when she had held it as long as she meant to, she went, with the two empty touches and the ground folding, and her palm came across his ribs or his shoulder or the back of his neck, and she said, "When?"

"Your second breath."

"I started on the first."

"I didn't see it on the first."

"Then that's when you saw it." She went back to her mark. "Again."

It was not Pressure, and they both knew it. A Wind fighter getting ready to step did not hold anything heavy in the middle of her body the way Corvane had; she held a lightness instead, a sort of tilt, as if the whole of her had decided to fall forward and was only waiting to be allowed. It was a different thing to look for, in a different place. But it was a thing that did not move under things that did, and it had to be found the same way, by watching what was not happening, and that was the part he had to learn.

He learned it slowly, the way he had learned the moving.

On the first morning he saw her readiness twice in thirty. By the fourth morning he was seeing it nine times in thirty, and by the end of the first week he was seeing it more often than not, and she had begun to change how she hid it. She stopped walking and stood still instead, and talked, and he found that a person standing still and talking was much harder to read than a person walking, because there was so little to tell the holding from. Then he learned that too, a little, and she changed again.

"You're getting better," she said on the eighth morning, breathing hard. "And I'm getting better at hiding it. So it looks the same. That's what it'll be like in there. Remember that. He'll be getting better at hiding it while you're getting better at seeing it, and it'll feel like you're standing still."

In the afternoons, when she went to her sparring hour across the district, he drew Corvane's figure.

He drew it in the dirt of Torvin's yard with a stick, by the pump, and in the margins of the Log, and on the back of a bill from the mender. Then he drew it with his eyes shut, on the inside of his eyelids, the stick figure and the spiral wound tight and the row of four strokes, until he could see it standing up and walking about on top of the stone it was carrying. Yeni watched him draw it once by the pump, from the kitchen door, and said nothing, and the next day there was a stub of red chalk on the low wall where he sat, which he had not put there.

He did not tell anybody else about the step.

He practised it at the post when Lira was late, and by the cot at night, on purpose, as he had done since the thirty-ninth night. It was still clumsy. It was a little less clumsy than it had been, or he thought it was; the boards went short under him by the width of a hand, and once, on a night near the end of the first week, by perhaps a hand and a half. It still cost his breath and his hip, a little less each time, or a little more, depending on how tired he was before he began. It still never came clean when he called it.

Once, in the second week, it came without his calling it at all, in the box drill at full speed. Lira came at him from the corner he had not called, and the ground went short under him and he was out of her way before he had decided anything. She stopped dead, with her palm still out, and looked at him.

"That."

"I know."

"Do it again."

He could not. That night he wrote it in the back of the Log as the fifth instance, the first since the notice, and the first in the grey half since the drop. But in the yard he tried for the rest of the morning and it did not come once, not when she came at him from the corner he had not called and not when she came at him from the one he had. By the end he was so tired from trying that his hips had gone square again. She stopped at last and sat on the low wall with her elbows on her knees, and he sat down beside her and they ate the bread.

"It's not going to be reliable. Not yet. You've had it a month. It'll want a season before it comes when it's called. My step didn't come when I called it for a year after I was declared, and it was mine from the day it was declared."

"I haven't got a season. I've got eleven days."

"Then on the twelfth day you'll have what you've got, and not a crumb more. Worrying through the eleven won't add a crumb, and not worrying won't take one away." She tore her bread and gave him the larger half. "So don't spend them worrying. Spend them on the build. The build's yours. The step's—" She stopped, and looked at the bread in her hand. "The step's whatever it is. Don't stand in a circle in front of a Bronze waiting for it to come. If it comes, it comes. Read the build."

---

On the second Thursday Vell gave him a Copper 3 Shield from the dyers' sheds, a boy of about seventeen with blue hands, whom Cael had watched twice in the autumn and written down as *careful*. He was careful still. He held his shield up the way Brenna had held hers, a pane wider than his shoulders, a hand's breadth off the forearm, like the side of a bucket of water stood on end. He walked it at people as if it were a door he meant to shut on them.

He also dipped it on the step-through, as Brenna had before she trained it out, a finger's width at the bottom edge, every time.

Cael saw the dip in the first exchange and let it go by, and in the second he stepped in under the dipped edge as it came and put the one strike he owned on the boy's jaw, and stopped it there, a finger short.

"Called," said Vell. "Unrated."

That was all of it. It had taken less than two exchanges, and the yard made the ordinary pleased noise, and the boy from the dyers' let his shield go out like a lamp and stood looking at the fist by his jaw.

Then he did something Cael had not expected. He did not go to the water bucket. He stayed where he was, in the middle of the circle, and looked at Cael with his blue hands hanging at his sides.

"Is it true?" he said. "You've got a Bronze coming?"

Cael looked at him.

"Monday week," said the boy. "That's what they're saying on the dye vats. A Bronze Rank 2, from away, and you. Is it true?"

"Yes."

The boy whistled through his teeth, softly.

"Rather you than me," he said, without any spite in it at all, and went to the water bucket.

Cael stood in front of Vell's table while she wrote. He was thinking about the boy's question and not about the bout, and he found that it was the first time he had ever stood at the table without thinking about the bout he had just fought. He did not know whether that was a good thing.

"Result stands as called," said Vell. "Unrated, over a Copper Rank 3, Shield Path. Second exchange. Called. Witnessed and entered." She did not look up. "They're talking about Monday on the vats, then."

"He said so."

"They've been talking about it on the fish steps since the day after I told you." She blotted the line. "I didn't tell anybody else. You didn't. The girl didn't." She laid the pen across the book. "It's a funny thing about a yard. You can keep a thing in a book for thirty years and nobody knows it's there. You can keep a Bronze off the board for three days and the whole district knows he's coming and who he's coming for."

---

On the second Saturday there was no card, and Vell made the card.

She did it at her table in the middle of the empty yard with the frost still white along the east wall, writing on the backs of old bills in her upright pencil, one bout to a bill. The pie boy stood by to run them to the board. Cael had come to the post for the grey half and found her already there. She had not looked up, but she had said "Here," in the voice she used for it, and he had come across the dirt and stood in front of the table.

She finished a card and gave it to the pie boy, and he ran.

"Monday," said Vell. "Noon. I've set the gate. You'll not pay it." She turned her tea a quarter turn by its handle. "Say the terms back to me."

He looked at her.

"You said them to me on the first morning," said Vell. "Before Renn. You've not had to since, because you've not been in front of anything that made me want to hear them again." She did not look up. "Say them."

So he said them, standing in front of her table in the cold, as he had said them on the first morning, slowly, checking each one before he set it down. He came in unrated. He fought whoever she put across from him. The bout ended when she called it. Once he was over the rope, nobody stopped it but her, and what happened inside it was his to carry out. Nobody was making him do it.

He found, saying it, that it meant more than it had meant the first time, and not because the words had changed.

Vell listened to all of it without moving.

"Good. He may not like the look of you when he sees you. That's his to say, and yours to answer. Not mine." She picked up a fresh bill and began to write on it, and he understood that he had been sent away.

Lira was at the post. She had watched the whole of it from the grey half and had not come over, and when he came back she did not ask what Vell had wanted. She gave him the bread, and then she stood in front of him with her hands at her sides and her weight a little forward.

"I've decided," she said.

He waited.

"Survivable." She said it carefully, as if she were reading it off the side of a crate. "If you fall properly. If you don't stand up into anything he's built at his full weight. If he goes heavy and you're still on your feet and there's nothing left to find out, you go backward, and you keep going backward, and you lose long. I don't care how it looks." She looked at him hard. "That's the ruling. I said I'd make it and I've made it. You'll listen."

"I'll listen."

"You'll listen, and then in there you'll do whatever you do." She almost smiled, and did not let it get very far. "I know. I've watched you. But I've said it, and you've heard it, and that's what I promised on the first morning of the six weeks." She turned to the post and struck it, low, twice. "Box drill. And this time, when I hold, I'm going to talk to you about the weather the whole time, and you're going to find it anyway."

---

The district knew.

He found that out a piece at a time, the way you find out a roof is leaking, by noticing a wet place and then another. Feryn's name was not on the board, because Vell never put a bout with a gate on the board; the gate was all the notice it got. But by the second Saturday somebody had chalked a small square in the corner of the board, under Sunday's card, with a *B* in it and an arrow pointing at nothing. Then somebody else had chalked a *?* beside the arrow, and somebody else again had rubbed the *?* out and written *Monday*.

The errand boys had a bet on it. Cael heard it from them before he heard anything else, on the fish steps, where the one in the red cap was explaining it to two girls with baskets. It was a mark each, which was more than either of them had ever had at once in their lives. Red Cap had the boy lasting three exchanges and the other one had him lasting one, and they had each spent several days trying to get the other to raise the stakes.

"He'll say *gone*," said Red Cap to the girls, with great confidence, "and then he'll do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing he does," said Red Cap, who did not know.

Renn was taking full credit. Cael heard that on the long quay from a woman with a fish basket, and then from Renn himself, who came down off a barge with a sack on each shoulder to tell him about it.

"I've told everybody," said Renn. "I've told them all. *I taught that boy everything he knows, and I let him win, and now he's going to go and lose to a Bronze and make me look bad.*" He set the sacks down and grinned. "That's the story. It's a good story. Don't spoil it."

"I'll try."

"Don't try. Lose properly." Renn looked him over with the craftsman's look, the one that checked a joint, and the grin went for a moment. "Pressure," he said, more quietly. "I fought one once, at Fenrow, years ago. A Copper. I couldn't see anything. It was like fighting the weather." He picked up his sacks again. "Watch his middle, not his hands. That's all I've got, and I got it by lying on my back."

"Somebody else told me that."

"Then two people have told you, and you'd better listen." Renn started up the plank. "And I'll be at the rope. On the sunny side. Shouting."

Marrow had a slate already, though there was nothing on the board. Cael saw it from the gate of the Cinder House on the second Sunday, when Marrow was standing by the barrels with his very clean hands folded and the slate on its string round his neck. On the bottom of it, under the Sunday prices, he had written in small careful chalk *Mon: F. (Br 2) — unr.*, with no price beside either. He saw Cael reading it and smiled the good smile and, for the first time since Cael had come to Ardenmere, said something to him directly.

"I can't price it," said Marrow. "I've tried for a week. Every number I write is either too long or too short, and I can't tell which." He turned the slate over to show him the other side, which was covered with rubbed-out figures. "You've ruined my week."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not," said Marrow, quite pleasantly, and turned the slate back.

And on the scaffolds round the new houses on the east road, when he went that way to the mender's with a basket of mended hinges, Sarel called down to him from three storeys up.

She was sitting on a plank with her legs hanging over the edge and her lunch on her knee, among the scaffold people, with her short dark hair full of lime dust, and she leaned down to look at him as easily as if she were sitting on a bench.

"Pressure, I hear," she called.

He stopped in the road and looked up.

"Yes."

She looked down at him, chewing. The scaffold people round her had stopped eating to listen.

"Fall properly," she called. Then she went back to her lunch.

Dessa found him at the board on the Monday evening, the last Monday but one, in the dusk, when he had stopped to look at the chalk square with the *B* in it.

He did not hear her come. She was simply there beside him with her hands in her pockets and her hair tied tight, looking at the board as he was looking at it, as though they had arranged to meet there and read it together. She had a way of arriving that was like her way of fighting: you did not see her come, and then she had been there for some time.

"Monday," she said.

"Yes."

She read the board a little longer. A cart went by behind them toward the triangle with its lamp swinging.

"I'll be at the gate," said Dessa. "Not the benches. There won't be benches, not on a Monday." She did not look at him. "I watched you from the benches for Corbin. I'd like to watch this one standing up."

"Why?"

She thought about that seriously, as she thought about everything.

"Because he won't be trying to find out what you are," she said. "Everybody you've fought here was. Renn was. I was. Corbin, all the way to the fifth." She turned her head and looked at him at last, with the small white scar through her eyebrow pale in the dusk. "A Bronze doesn't bother. He already knows what he is, and he'll find out what you are by standing on it. I want to see what you do when somebody isn't curious."

He did not have an answer to that, and she did not seem to want one.

"Afterward," she said, "if you can still stand. You and I have a talk owing." She took her hands out of her pockets. "Not this month. But it's owing."

Then she went off up the street toward the yards, and he stood at the board for some time in the cold, looking at the *B* and the arrow, and at the word *Monday*.

---

Hesk's letter came on the last Tuesday, propped against the salt crock on the kitchen table.

He knew what it was before he touched it. It was the answer to the five, written at the bench or the kitchen table in Fen Street a week ago, on the night the five arrived; it had come as fast as a letter could. He took it out into the yard and sat on the low wall by the pump with it, in the cold, and turned it over twice in his hands.

Then he opened it.

*Cael,*

*I read your letter on the workshop step, which seems to be where I read your letters now. I read it twice. Then I went inside and put the kettle on and did not drink any of it, and then I sat down and read it again.*

*I'm not going to tell you I wasn't frightened. I was. I am. I'm frightened in the way I was frightened on the step about the summons, a week late and all at once, and I've decided that this is simply what it is to have you somewhere else. You told me as it came. I'll tell you the same: I was frightened, and then I was proud of you, and I'm both still, and I expect I'll go on being both.*

*You asked what I think of your not telling me. I think you had reasons, and I think one of them was mine, and I think you've paid me back for the kitchen table on the second morning more handsomely than I deserved. I'm not angry. I've no right to be, and I'm not.*

*I don't know what it is either. The archive tells you what became of four people, as far as anybody wrote it down. It doesn't tell you what any of them ever did. Don't go looking in it for an answer it hasn't got.*

*Keep the record. You'd keep it anyway, but keep it the way you'd keep a load sheet somebody's life might hang on, because it might. Show the back of it to nobody but her.*

*And remember what I said to you in the side room at Weaver's Row. Don't let anyone see you move. Not until you know what you're doing. When I said it, I knew none of this. I find I'd say it again.*

*Tell the girl thank you, from me, for not flinching. I've met a great many grown men who would have.*

*— H.*

He read it three times on the wall, in the cold, with the pump dripping beside him into its iced trough. Then he folded it carefully along its own folds and put it in the leather book with the others.

He sat there a while longer. He thought about Hesk on the step with the kettle boiling inside and not drinking any of it. He thought about *I've no right to be*, and what it had cost a man like Hesk to write that down. He thought about the side room at Weaver's Row, which seemed a very long way off now: a tree outside the window growing at an angle, and Hesk's prepared stillness, and that sentence said for the first time.

*Don't let anyone see you move.*

On Monday, at noon, he was going to stand in front of forty people or fifty or a hundred and a Bronze Rank 2, and he did not know what he was going to do there, or what might come up out of him without asking. He did not know whether he would know what he was doing. He thought he would not, mostly. He thought that the only honest answer to Hesk's sentence was the one Lira had given him on the low wall: read the build, and don't stand in the circle waiting for anything else.

He went in to supper. Torvin's wife looked at his face and gave him a bun she said she had been keeping for the boots woman, who had not deserved it.

---

On the last Wednesday the mender gave him back a key.

It was one of the small ones from the tray, from the desk that had gone for firewood, a little brass box key that Cael had put on the pile of keys that had lived in somebody's pocket every day for years. The mender laid it on the bench in front of him without a word, and beside it a small tin cash box with a dented lid, of the kind people kept under a floorboard.

"Came out of the same house," said the mender. "Out of the wall, when they took the chimney down. Locked." He did not look up from his clock. "I tried the ones you'd said were used. Third one."

Cael picked up the key and turned it over. The bright worn places on its wards caught the light from the window, where the lock it had lived in had shaped it, a little every day, for longer than he had been alive.

"What was in the box?"

"Letters." The mender considered his clock. "A woman's. To a man who went downriver and didn't come back. Thirty years." He moved a wheel a hair with the end of a pin. "The family have them now. They didn't know they were there."

Cael put the key down very carefully beside the box.

"You've a good eye for what's been used," said the mender. Then, after a long time, while Cael was sorting washers one-handed into their tins: "Monday."

"Monday. The Bronze."

"I know what Monday is." The mender did not look up. "I meant Monday here. After." He moved another wheel. "And after?"

It was the third time he had asked it. Cael had said *I don't know yet* twice, and both times it had been true.

"I'll be here. Monday and Wednesday. As long as you'll have me."

The mender nodded slowly over his clock, as though a figure had come right that he had been working on for some time, and said nothing else at all for the rest of the afternoon.

---

On the last night before the bout he could not sleep, and did not try very hard.

He lay on his right side on the cot with Yeni's lamp down to a bead and the wheezing man whistling by the door, and he did the thing he had done on the night before Kindling and the night before Renn and every night before a bout since: he went through it. Not the fight, which he did not know, but everything round it, the things he could count.

He counted three weeks of mornings in the grey half, twenty-one of them, and one morning in the box drill when the step had come without asking and never again. He counted Corvane's figure, drawn perhaps two hundred times. He counted two bouts won, a Fenrow Stone and a dyers' Shield, neither of which anybody would remember, both of which had been easy because he had done the work. He counted the money for Corvane, and the money for the gate, which Vell had said he would not have to pay because he was fighting in it, and Lira had said that was the only sensible thing anybody had said about the whole affair.

He counted what he knew about Feryn, which was what Vell had said, and what Renn had said, and what Corvane had said and shown him in a brick yard by the river. It came to a page and a half. He had gone into his first bout with Renn knowing nothing at all. He had gone into Dessa knowing everything there was to know. This was the first time he had gone in knowing a page and a half about a man he had never seen, and the page and a half was mostly about what he would not be able to do.

He found that he was not frightened. He looked for it, honestly, the way he would have looked for a tell, and it was not where he expected it. There was something else there instead, in the same place, and it was a great deal more like wanting.

He wanted to see it. He wanted to stand in front of the thing the eel woman had laughed about and Renn had lain on his back under, and find out what it was, and what he was, standing in front of it. It was not a sensible thing to want, and he knew it. He wanted it anyway, the way he had wanted to know what the word meant in Pellin's office, before he knew enough to be afraid of it.

He thought that probably said something about him, and did not write it down.

He woke before the light, as he had woken on the morning of Kindling, and lay still and took stock, as he did on days that mattered: ceiling, the beam across it with its old nail holes, the wheezing man asleep, Yeni's cot empty already, the grey of an Ardenmere morning in the window, which he had stopped seeing months ago and saw again now on purpose. He got up and dressed and went down, and Torvin's wife put a bowl of oats in front of him without a word, and stood over him with her spoon until he had eaten all of it.

Lira was waiting outside, on the step under the leaning O, with her canvas bag over her shoulder.

He knew the bag. It was the one she carried things in that she thought he might need and would not have thought to bring: a cloth, a flask of water, the end of a loaf, a strip of linen rolled tight. She had carried it to every bout since Dessa. She stood up when he came out, and read his face the way she read the board.

"You didn't sleep."

"Some."

"That's not an answer." She fell in beside him. "I'll let you off today."

They walked up through the district in the cold, toward the Cinder House, not hurrying. A sleet had begun somewhere over the river and was coming in slantwise, small and grey, not settling. At the corner by the board somebody had rubbed out the *Monday* in the chalk square and written *TODAY* instead, very large.

"Whatever happens in there," said Lira, not looking at him, "you've already done what you could with the time there was. I want you to believe that before you go over the rope. Not just hear it. Believe it."

"I believe it. Mostly."

"Mostly will do." She bumped his shoulder with hers, the left one. "Today, mostly will do."

# Chapter 58 — As It Happened

In the third exchange Darrow Innes leaned on a gate.

That was what it looked like, from across the circle. He came off his mark when Vell said *begin* and did not walk Cael anywhere. He stood in the middle with his right foot forward and his left set, and brought his left arm down and rested the elbow on his own hip, easily, the way a man leans on a gate at the end of the day to talk to his neighbour. And he turned, not much, a quarter of a turn, so that his left side went a little away and his right came a little forward.

It was nothing. Anybody on the benches would have said he was resting. But the low left side was under the elbow now, and behind the turn, and every road Cael could think of to it went past both of Darrow's hands.

*He's found the door,* thought Cael. *And he's shut it.*

He tried anyway, because he had to know how shut. He went in low from the left, under the right hand, and the right hand came down and met his forearm and turned him away like a man turning a dog off a path. He went round and tried from outside, where the elbow was, and before his strike had properly left his hip Darrow's left forearm had come off the gate and was lying flat in front of the place, waiting. His knuckles hit it and stopped as if he had punched a doorpost.

It had been there first.

He went back toward his side of the circle with his knuckles stinging and looked at the barrel. Lira was looking at him. She lifted one hand and touched her chin, once, and folded her arm again.

*The nod.*

It went into him cold. He had been doing it all afternoon. Every time he had gone for the crossing, something in his neck had said *now* a sliver before the rest of him, as it had said it for Dessa all winter, and he had not once felt himself do it. And a man who read feet first and hands second and faces hardly at all had not needed to watch his face. Two exchanges had been enough, and a dozen small nods, and now the forearm got there before the fist.

He could not stop it. He had stopped it once, against Dessa, by deciding the whole of the exchange on his mark and letting every other plan go, and Dessa had told him on the dirt afterward that it was a thing you got to do once. It was done. If he went for the crossing now, he would nod, and the forearm would be there.

So he would stop going for it.

He thought of Feryn in the sleet. Feryn had built his air and held it and not let it go, and put his hand where the exchange was going to finish, where Cael was always going to arrive, and simply waited there for him. Cael had slipped round a thing that was not there, straight into the hand.

Darrow had to hit him sometime. Nobody won a bout leaning on a gate. And to hit, Darrow had to take the elbow off his hip; whatever else a strike of his did, it would finish with that arm out and away for a moment before it came back. That was where every one of Darrow's own exchanges ended. The gate swung open. Then it swung shut.

So Cael kept his strike in his hand and did not throw it.

He held it as Feryn had held his air, short and ready, and he did not let his neck say *now*, because there was no *now* to say. He went in close and stayed close, closer than any sense would have let him, inside a Bronze's reach where the Bronze could hit him whenever he liked. And he waited to be hit, so that he would be standing where the hitting ended.

He spent most of the exchange being hurt.

Darrow had felt the hum in his own side. Cael had seen him feel it, with his hand on it like a purse. Somewhere behind his mild face he had decided that this afternoon had gone on long enough, and he set about ending it without any hurry. He hit Cael short in the right side, in the place he had been adding to all through the second exchange, and the old place went white. He hit him again in it. Cael kept the strike in his hand and did not throw, and the arm went back to the hip, and the gate shut. He hit him a third time, and Cael rode half of it and could not ride the rest, and it bent him over, so that for a moment he was looking at the dirt and the front of Darrow's boot on it.

He saw the boot move.

It came in low behind his own right heel, the hook of the sweep, and in the same instant, above him, he felt a straight blow begin, short, down the middle. It was the kind that would put a boy with his heel taken flat on his back and keep him there. There was no ground to give. His heel was gone, and there was nowhere behind him or beside him that a foot could reach.

And the ground went short.

He had not asked for it. There was no time to ask for anything, and he had not asked for it since the trough. It came up out of the dark behind his breastbone as it had come in the sleet, already done before he knew it was happening, and the yard was a hand's width narrower than it had been. His weight was not where his heel had been. It was round the edge of the blow, on Darrow's left, at an angle no step of his own could have got to from where he stood. The straight went through the space where his chest had been, close enough that he felt it go by along his shirt.

The yard went silent.

Three hundred people, the crows on the eaves, the man with the hot nuts in the lane: all of them stopped at once, the way a room stops when somebody drops a plate. In the middle of it he heard one of the Fenrow men at the nails say something short to the other. Then he heard chalk.

So that was spent. He had kept it in the dark for two weeks like money sewn into a coat, and now three hundred people had seen it, and some of them would be telling it tonight in rooms he would never stand in, and telling it wrong, and telling it again. He felt it go out of him into the yard. It did not come back.

But he was on Darrow's left side, and Darrow's right arm was out at the far end of a blow that had found nothing, and his left was coming off the gate to turn.

The gate was open.

He let go the strike he had been holding all exchange. Short, low, on the left side, with nothing else in it at all. The eighth. It went in, and the place was still busy.

Darrow turned on him fast, for the first time all afternoon, and put a short one into his middle that took all his air out and doubled him. And as he went down over it, with nothing left in his chest, Cael put the ninth into the same place. It was the ugliest thing he had thrown in his life. Lira would have made him do it fifty times over.

Under his knuckles, this time, it was not like the floor over the river. It was like the gate of the market on a Saturday morning, when the carts have all come at once, and the first is still wedged in the gateway when the second runs up against its tail, and the third against the second. Nothing about it was weak. It was only full. Everything he had put into that place was still in it, trying to get down to a foot, and the ninth had nowhere to go but on top.

He came up out of the bend with his breath returning in small pieces and his right side ringing like a dropped pan.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

At the nails, the Fenrow men had finished their sums while the yard was still silent. They had wiped off fifteen and written thirteen, and a man had gone very quickly from slate to slate with coins in his fist. Marrow watched it happen. He looked at his own slate, and at the circle, and at Cael with one hand on his knee getting his air back. Then he lifted the slate off its nail and turned it round and hung it with its face to the wall. One after another the Fenrow men did the same.

The book had shut. It shut at thirteen.

---

He stood on his mark and breathed, and every breath was a price.

The inside of his lip had split, and he could taste it. His left forearm was a bar from wrist to elbow. His right side, low, under the arm, where Darrow had been adding all afternoon, had gone past hurting into a pulse with its own opinion. He paid none of it any attention. His eyes were on Darrow, and Darrow was doing the one thing Lira had warned him of on the bench.

Darrow had come to the bottom of his sums.

It was not anger. There was nothing in his face like anger or like wanting. He had run the afternoon as a man runs a long job, steadily, knowing what each part would cost him, and somewhere in the space where a boy had been and then had not, he had added up the column and seen what it came to. He took his elbow off his hip. He brought his left side round square, slowly, not hiding it. He set his left foot and tapped it, and tapped it, and then a third time, which he had not done all afternoon.

He was going to put the whole of himself into one, and finish it, and be kind afterward.

The yard saw it. Most of them could not have said what they were seeing, but they saw a man stop resting, and the sound they made had a fright in it. Cael looked at the barrel.

Lira looked back. Her arms were folded on the rope. She did not touch her chin or her side or anything at all. Her face was as still as the old man's in his gate.

*After the third is his.*

Behind her table, Vell had got to her feet.

"Begin," she said.

---

Darrow came across the circle like a door coming off its hinges.

Cael read it from the floor up, because that was where Darrow had taught him to read all afternoon. The left foot, set three times. Then the strike being made, down there, in the foot, and climbing: up the left leg, through the low left side, through the shoulder and down the arm, out along the very road every one of Cael's nine had gone in by, only the other way. It was everything Darrow had, coming up out of the ground through the crossing on its way to his fist. The front foot came across to shut the right side off. The long left came from the back.

And Cael knew where it would land, and how wide it was, because he had watched this man for three exchanges, and Darrow had watched him back.

Darrow had seen the third exchange. He had seen a boy be a hand's width round the edge of a blow, where there was no hand's width to be. So he had not thrown this one at the boy. He had thrown it at where the boy would go. It was Feryn's trick, the hand put down at the end of the exchange, and Cael could see the edge of it out past the edge of anywhere the step had ever taken him. It was three hands out. The step would give him one.

The Wind was already moving in him. He could not have stopped it now. It would take him round, a hand's width, into the place Darrow had already put his fist.

He did not decide anything after that.

He would go back over it a great many times, on the cot, and in the grey half, and on the wall by the pump, and it never once came out with a choice in it. His weight was going round on the Wind, and it went further than the Wind went. And in the dark behind his breastbone, beside the leaning thing, the heavy one was there.

It was not gathered. It had taken no breath. It had not shown in his middle like a stone in a sack; there had been no time for it to show anywhere. It was simply all there at once, the whole weight of it, the way it had never once been in front of the trough. At the trough, reaching for one had made him drop the other. Now nothing was dropped. They were in his hands together, both handles of one basket, and his body picked the basket up as if it had been carrying it for years.

He went into the blow.

Not round it. Not away. Into it and along it, down the line of the long left arm, the way the sleet had run down his slanted forearm in the winter and off. And the heavy thing in him opened like a door and took the blow in. More came into him than he had ever held; it was not the water in the trough or the pear buds leaning or a bucket going a finger along the flags. It was a weir in flood where there had been a cup.

And it went out again.

He had seen it once, by moonlight. When the trough's water went flat, the rings had run out to the stone edge and met it and come back. This was that. Some of what had come in did not stay in him. It ran out to some edge he could not see and met it and came back, back down his arm, out of him, into Darrow's wrist and elbow and shoulder. It went up the road it had just come out of, and down into the low left side, into the crossing, where nine strikes were still trying to get to the floor.

Nobody had ever sent Darrow Innes anything back.

What had not turned hit him.

It came through and landed low on his right side, in the place Darrow had been building on since the second exchange, and the whole side of him went off like a lamp. Something in his ribs went *crack*, flat and sharp, the sound a frozen board makes in the timber yard when the first warm morning gets into it, and he heard it from inside. The yard tilted and did not come all the way back. Its edges went loose and moving, as the moon in the trough had gone when the water broke, and stayed that way. The pain did not arrive at first. It was coming. He could feel it on the way, a long way off, like weather down the river.

He did not go down. He did not know why, for a moment. Then he did. His knees had not locked. His bones were on his bones. Nothing in him was holding up anything that did not have to be held, because there was nothing left in him to hold with, and so he simply stood, the way the old man stood, and the standing cost nothing.

In front of him, at the end of his own long left arm, Darrow had stopped.

He had stopped like a man on a ladder who reaches for the next rung in the dark and closes his hand on air. All of him had stopped at once, halfway through finishing, square, and the left arm had come away from Cael and fallen to his side and hung there. His face, for the first time that afternoon, had nothing on it at all. No account. No sum. It was the face of a man who has done a thing a thousand times and has had it, this once, not work.

And Cael's arms were filling.

He felt it start in both shoulders together, the wet sand, pouring down. Every other time it had waited until after. It was not waiting now. It was coming into his arms, a hand's breadth at a time, from the shoulder toward the wrist, while in front of him a Bronze stood with his left side square and bare and nothing over it.

*Spend nothing you don't have to,* said the old man, in his yard, by his post. *Then when you do have to spend, you'll have it.*

He had it. Not much. A few heartbeats of arm, with the sand coming down.

That part he chose. He knew he was choosing it while he chose. He did not wait to understand what had happened, and he did not go looking for it, or try to get away from it. He took one long step in, inside the hanging arm, with his ribs beginning to shout and the yard sliding at its edges, and the crossing was there in front of him, low on the left side, with nothing over it for the first time since the second exchange.

He put the tenth into it.

It would not have moved the straw post. His arm was half full of sand by the time it got there, and there was nothing behind it at all. Nobody at the nails would have given a quarter-mark for it. It had only its place, and its moment, and nine strikes and a man's own blow already standing in that place in front of it, waiting to get down.

It made a noise like a latch.

Darrow knelt. He went down onto one knee slowly, as a man kneels in a field to look at something in the grass. Then the other knee. Then he leaned over, very gently, and put his right hand on the dirt, and lay down on his side in the raked groove with his dead arm across him.

---

Vell had been on her feet since the fourth began. She did not remember standing.

She was across the groove before she had decided to go, and that had not happened to her in twenty years. She stood over the man on the dirt and began to count, aloud, at the pace she had counted at all her life, and the yard, which had started to roar, heard her and stopped.

"One."

He was breathing. She looked for that first; she had always looked for that first. He was breathing deep and slow, like a man asleep after harvest, with his cheek on her raked dirt and his eyes open.

"Two. Three."

He spread the fingers of his good hand on the ground and pressed down. She watched him do it. She watched for the push to come up through the arm and lift the shoulder, and it did not come. It was like watching a man work a pump handle at a dry well: the handle went down, and down again, and nothing came up it. There was nothing hurt that she could see. Nothing was out of its place, and nothing was bleeding. The man had simply asked his own body for the thing it had never once in eight years refused him, and his body had nothing in it to give.

"Four. Five. Six."

He stopped pressing. He rolled onto his back, slowly, and looked straight up at the sky over her yard, where the crows were going round.

"Seven."

She looked at the boy.

He was standing a few paces off, where the tenth strike had left him. His arms hung at his sides as if his sleeves had been filled with sand, and his right side was held stiffly, and his eyes were not quite going where he put them. He was looking down at the man in the groove with an expression she had seen on people at the side of a river after a bridge has gone.

"Eight. Nine."

She had seen something in that exchange she had no word for. She had seen him go into the blow instead of out of it, and the Bronze stop all at once, and she had kept this book thirty years and seen every Path there was used every way it could be, and she had never seen that. She knew already that she would sit at her table tonight and try to write it, and not be able to.

"Ten."

She straightened. It was so quiet that she could hear the pie boy's breathing behind her.

"The match is called," said Vell. Her voice went to the back wall and out into the lane without being raised. "Cael Hesk-ward. By incapacity. Fourth exchange." She let it stand a moment. "So entered."

Then the yard let go of whatever it had been holding, and the noise of it hit her in the back like a wave as she walked to her table. She sat down and opened the ledger at the page and picked up her pen, and found that her hand was not quite steady, which it always was, and waited until it was.

There were two lines under the date that she had written that morning. The Bronze's, with his rank and Path. And under it the other one, the way she had written it for seven months: *unrated (vouched)*. The assessment lived in her margin, not on the line.

The custom was hers. She had made it up herself, thirty years ago, at a table very like this one. A book that wrote down every name that came over its rope was a book nobody needed. A name went in when the yard came to see it.

The yard had come.

She wrote his name. She wrote it at the beginning of a fresh line, the first time it had ever begun a line in her book, and after it the rest, in the order it had happened, plain. The rating she had given him. The man he had beaten, and the man's rank and Path. The exchange, and how.

"It goes in the book," said Vell, low, to no one, to the page, with the pen still moving and the whole yard going round her table like weather, "as it happened."

The pie boy, standing at her elbow with the coin bag, heard her and did not understand, and remembered it all his life.

She had a word she kept for herself inside the back cover, with a tally beside it. *Atypical.* She had written it eleven times in thirty years before the boy, and since the autumn she had written it beside him more often than she liked to count. She did not write it now. It looked, from where she sat, too small, and too much like a reason.

Instead she turned to the back pages, where she wrote what nobody needed to read but whoever kept the book after her, and wrote a single line.

*To whoever has this next: I saw this. I can't tell you what it was. He couldn't either. Write what you see, and leave what it was until somebody knows.*

She blotted it and looked up.

The Bronze was getting up. Hands had gone out to him from the rope, a dozen of them, and he had turned every one away, politely, as a man turns away a second helping. He came up the slow way, on one hand and then a knee and then the back straightening on top of the legs, one piece set on the next. Nobody in the yard made a sound while he did it. Every one of them had stood up off the dirt at least once in front of people.

She watched him all the way to his feet. Then she wrote that down as well.

---

Somebody was under his left arm. He knew it was Lira before he looked, by the way her shoulder fitted and by the fact that it was the side that did not hurt. She had come through the rope at some point that he had missed. She was saying that he should sit down, and he was saying that he would stand, and neither of them was listening.

"You've no colour," she said. "None at all. Sit."

"Not yet." It seemed to matter more than anything. Three hundred people had paid to see it. They could see it with him on his feet. "In a minute."

Lira said nothing to that, which meant she was keeping it for later, and kept her shoulder where it was.

His arms would not come up. He tried to put his right hand to his side, where the lamp was still lit, and the hand rose a little way, much later than he had sent it, and stopped, and sank. They were full to the fingertips. In all the nights at the trough it had never once come during. It had always waited for him to finish.

Darrow Innes was on his feet across the circle.

He stood a while, finding out how much of him there was. He lifted his left arm with his right hand under the elbow, like a man lifting a parcel to see what it weighs, and let it down. Then he walked across the circle, at his own pace, on his own legs, and the people at the rope opened for him without being asked, and he stopped in front of Cael.

He had looked broad at the mark. He looked smaller now, and more tired, and somehow more like a man who had come up a river on a barge.

"I know every Path there is by how it lands," said Darrow. His voice was level, and keeping it level was costing him, and he did not hide what it cost. "Eight years. Shield and Stone and Force and Blade. Wind, when I can catch it. Pressure, which is like being sat on." He lifted his left hand an inch with his right and let it fall. "That wasn't any of them. That was mine. That was my own hand, come back up my own arm, and it came home to the one place on me I didn't know was there till you showed me."

He looked at Cael's face. Nothing was filed in his own now. There was only the question, plain, with nothing round it.

"What are you?"

The yard had gone quiet enough to hear him.

Cael felt the quiet all round him, and three hundred people inside it waiting to carry something home. He thought of the Log in his coat on the bench, open at last night's page. He thought of the line under *Evidence* with nothing after it but *Ruling*. He had filled that line in this afternoon, for the crossing. There was another line, under all of it, that had never had anything written beside it at all, since a word on a brass plate in Denvash, and there was nothing beside it now. Something in him had done a thing he could not describe. He had watched it happen from the inside, like a tenant watching the house he rents get up and walk.

"I don't fully know yet."

Darrow looked at him a long moment.

Then he laughed, once, short, as if somebody had surprised it out of him, and winced, and put out his right hand. Cael looked at the hand. His own would not come up to it. He tried, and it lifted perhaps a finger's width and stayed.

Darrow looked at the hand that would not come, and then at Cael, and understood something.

"Did that do that?" he said quietly. "Whatever it was?"

"Yes."

"Good." He took Cael's right wrist in his own hand and lifted it, and shook the hand himself, carefully, and set it back down at Cael's side. "I'd hate it to be free."

He went back across the circle for his jacket and his bag. At the gate the pie boy ran after him holding out the coin he had paid to come in, and Darrow folded the boy's fingers back over it, as he had at the start, and went out into the lane. The lane moved out of his way the way people step back for a heavy cart they have seen come safely down a hill.

Lira put him on the bench before his legs could give their own opinion, and stood in front of him, and held up one finger, and moved it, and watched his eyes try to follow; she moved it the other way, and frowned at what his eyes did. She pressed the heel of her hand very gently against the low right side, rib by rib, and stopped where he made the sound, and felt round it, and frowned harder.

Then she sat down on the bench beside him, on the side that did not hurt, and did not do anything at all for a while.

He felt it come before he looked at her. He felt her go still against his shoulder, and then not still. When he turned his head she was not hiding any of it. It had come up into her face, the afternoon and the two weeks and a great deal from before that, and she was letting it sit there in the open with three hundred people round them. She did not wipe it away or turn her head.

"I measure your step every morning," she said. "On the straw. I know how far it goes to the width of a finger." Her voice went thin, and she let it, and it came back. "In the fourth I saw where he'd thrown, and I knew. Before you did. I knew it was short. I had a leg over the rope." She looked at the rope. "My whole leg. Nobody saw it. Vell didn't even see it. I hadn't got a plan. I'd got a leg. And then there was nothing to go through for, because he'd stopped, and you were standing there, and I—"

She stopped. When she went on it was quieter.

"I made myself a rule. In my room. The night you came up the stairs." She looked down at her own hands. "During, I coach," said Lira, and her mouth shook on it. "After, I get to be a person."

"Then be one."

She laughed, which came out as something else, and put her hand over her eyes for a moment. Then she took it away and turned, and put her arms round him, round the shoulders and nowhere near the ribs, and put her forehead against his good shoulder, and held on.

He could not lift his arms. He sat on the bench in the noise of the yard with his arms full of sand at his sides and Lira holding on to him, and there was nothing he could do about any of it but sit still and let her. So he sat still and let her. It turned out to be enough.

After a while she let go and sat up and was a coach again, though her face was still wet and she did nothing about it.

"Ribs," she said. "A week. Two, if you argue. You'll breathe like a man of ninety and you'll hate it." She took his chin in her hand and looked at his eyes again, close. "And if this doesn't go by the morning, you're going to a healer, and I'll carry you there myself." She let go. Then something came into her face that was not fear and not relief either; it was the barrel look, the one she wore when somebody in the circle did a thing she had never trained against and she began, without meaning to, to add. "And when you can lift a cup, you and me and the trough are going to have a very long talk."

"I don't know what it was."

"I know you don't. I saw your face." She stood. "That's why it's a long talk."

Feryn found them there. He had been at the nails, where Marrow was paying out with a face like a cellar, and he came across with his frayed coat open and his paper slip held up in one hand like a flag.

"Eleven," he said. "He tried to tell me it shut at thirteen. I told him my slip was dated breakfast." He looked down at Cael on the bench, and the noise went out of him, and what was left had nothing in it but hunger. "Do you know yet?"

"No."

"Then it's still open." Feryn nodded slowly, as if he had written something down. "Good. I'd hate it closed."

---

They went back to Torvin's the long way, in the last of the light, with Lira on his left.

The lanes were ordinary. That was the strange thing. The district was doing what it did at the end of a Sunday: shutters going up, a cart backed into a yard, a child being called in. Then at the well a man he had never seen in his life took off his cap as Cael went past and said *Hesk-ward*, the way you say good evening to a neighbour, and put the cap back on. At the triangle the cooper and the dyer were both in their doorways, across from each other, and as he came by they both stopped looking at each other and looked at him instead, for the whole time it took him to cross, and neither of them said anything at all, which in seven months he had never once seen happen.

At the top of the lane of yards the old man was standing in his gate.

He was always there. He had been there on the evening after Feryn and the evening after Dessa, with his sleeves rolled down and the yard behind him going blue, and Cael saw him from a long way down the lane and knew what was coming. The old man would look at the way he was walking. Then he would say a number, the number of the exchange.

The old man watched him come. He looked for a long time. He looked at the arms, which hung, and at the side, which was held, and at Lira's shoulder ready under the left arm. Then he looked at Cael's face.

He did not say a number.

He tipped his head. It went down by perhaps one degree and came back up. It was the largest thing Cael had ever seen him spend.

Then the stillness came down over him again, and he went back to watching the empty lane.

Torvin's yard door was propped open with a kitchen chair. Torvin was at the head of the table with his bowl, and when they came in by the yard door, walking, he looked up. He looked at Cael's arms, and at Lira, and then, for a longer time, at Cael's legs, which were carrying him.

"You walked," said Torvin.

"Yes."

Torvin thought about it.

"Good," he said, and went back to his stew. After a moment he got up, and took the chair from the yard door, and stood holding it, and then set it back at the table exactly where it lived, as if somebody might ask him later where it had been.

---

His arms came back to him a little after the hour, enough to get the shirt off with Yeni holding the other sleeve and looking firmly at the ceiling. When she had gone down, he sat on the end of his cot in his undershirt and looked at his side by the light of her lamp, which she had turned toward him on the shelf before she went.

It had come up dark from the rib under the arm to the top of the hip, one long stain under the skin, deepest at the fourth rib up, where something had cracked like a board in the timber yard. Breathing in hurt, and breathing out hurt differently. The edges of the room were steadier than the edges of the yard had been, but when he turned his head quickly the shelf came after him a moment late. He watched it do that, and did it again, and found it a little less late than the first time.

Yeni's lamp put its bead of light on the boards. He looked at it, and the quiet came.

It came the way the others had come. There was the quiet first, the quiet before a thought arrives whole; then the turning over; then, in the dark place behind his breastbone where nothing had lit since the word, something written.

He sat with it a long time.

*Recorded.* Whatever kept that book had seen what the yard saw, and that was all it had to say. Vell had at least said *so entered*, and said his name, and written down how; this one wrote less than Vell, and did not even say who had won. He found, sitting there, that this made him want to laugh, and his ribs were very clear that he was not to.

He took the Log out of his coat and opened it on his knee.

The front went first, as always, and went down easily, because it was his and he had built every piece of it on the straw. *Seventeenth. Darrow Innes, Bronze Rank 1, Iron Path. Won, fourth exchange, called, by incapacity.* Each exchange in a line. The crossing, with the empty line filled in at last: *Ruling: it held. Low left, rib to hip. One man; one afternoon.* Doss's thing, and what he had done about it: believed his hands. The nod, read in two exchanges, and Lira's chin. Feryn's hand, put where the exchange would end, borrowed and used. *What won it: Lira's way. Say it plainly. Ten in one place, and not one of them meant to count.* The costs: the forearm, the right side, the ribs, a week, the edges, going. The arms full of sand in the middle of a bout, which had never happened, and which Lira was owed a long talk about.

Then he turned the book over to the back.

*Seventh instance. Darrow, third exchange. The step. Not asked. Everybody saw.*

*Eighth instance. Darrow, fourth exchange.*

He sat with the pencil over the page for a long time.

He tried to draw it first, the way Corvane had taught him to draw a thing he could not say, with a stick figure and a long arm coming at it and two marks in its chest; but the two marks were wrong, because they were two and it had not been two, and he struck it through. Then he tried words. *The Wind took me round and then* — and struck that, because there had been no *then* in it anywhere. *I picked up both* — and struck that, because he had not picked up anything. He had reached for one and found himself holding a basket with two handles, and the reaching and the finding were in the one room in himself that he had never been able to see into, so that he could no more write down how to do it again than he could have written down how he had grown an inch since the autumn.

So in the end he wrote the only sentence that would stand up on the page. It did not explain anything; he wrote it the way you drive a stake in at the edge of a marsh, not to say what is out there in the reeds, but to say where the dry ground stops.

*They're not separate things I switch between. They're one thing with parts I haven't found all of yet.*

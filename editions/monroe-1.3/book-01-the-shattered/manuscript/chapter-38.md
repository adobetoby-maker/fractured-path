# Chapter 38 — A Door

Lira got as far as the board before she had to stop walking.

She had meant to go straight down the lane and through the triangle and on, anywhere, at the pace she used for markets, so that people got out of her way without knowing why. But at the board her feet simply stopped, as if somebody had put a hand on her chest, and she stood in front of it in the cold with her hands in her jacket pockets and pretended to read it. Near the bottom, nearly gone into the grain under the new card and last Sunday's *Sarel… sixth exchange*, was her own result against Brenna, with the three charcoal strokes beside it rubbed half away by a sleeve.

She was angry, and she let it be big, because she had learned a long time ago that if you tried to make anger smaller while you were still standing in it, it only went somewhere you could not see it and waited. So she stood in front of the board and was angry all the way through, at a boy with a notebook who had stood at the rope three weeks ago, seen a thing about her, and decided she could not have it yet.

*Once isn't a pattern.* She could see exactly what his face had done when he decided it: the chin tucked, the eyes very wide, the small private nod he did not know he did. It was the face she had watched him make a hundred times about other people's feet and had never once thought he would make about hers. And the worst of it, the thing she did not want to look at, was that she might have done the same, seeing a thing in his fighting once, and waiting to see it again, and calling it being careful.

But she would not have left it out of a *report*. Not after he had given her every other exchange so carefully, in the dusk, with the old men in the third row put in for evidence. It had looked like everything he saw, when it had been everything he saw except one thing, and he had decided which. A woman at a desk had once decided that a thing Lira had done in front of witnesses did not count, and Lira had stood there with her chin up and asked *what was the exam actually testing*, and that had been the end of the academy. She did not like that a boy who was the opposite of that desk in every way had put her in mind of it.

The skewer woman was setting up at the corner of the widest street, and she looked across at Lira standing still in front of the board, which Lira never did, and then looked away, which was kind of her. Lira turned her back on the board and walked on.

The other thing came up under the anger as she walked, slowly, the way the warmth comes up in your feet after you have been standing in snow. She had not let it come up in the yard because she had not wanted him to see it. She let it now.

*When you're tired, you go too far on your right.*

She went over it as she went down through the triangle: the backhand, and the weight going on past the end of it, and her right foot coming down hard and wide to catch her. She had felt that foot come down a thousand times and never once thought about it, because it was simply what her right foot did at the end of a long morning, like her hair coming loose on one side. You did not think about your hair coming loose. It came loose.

And then the academy, and the seventh exchange against a girl who was not as good as her. Lira had gone over that bout for weeks, lying on her bed in the long dormitory with her eyes open, trying every step of it from every side, and she had never found it. She had decided in the end that the girl had been lucky, which was not a good answer, only the only one left.

It had been her right foot, and a hand's width, there the whole time in plain sight at the end of every long morning, and nobody had seen it: not her instructor, not the woman at the desk, not her.

A boy from Denvash had seen it, from a rope, on a cold afternoon, while his own heart was going so hard against his ribs that he had printed the rope into his palms.

She found that she had stopped again, in the middle of the street, and that she was smiling, and that she was still angry, and that both were true at once. A man with a barrow had to go round her, and said something she did not hear.

It was the strangest feeling she had ever had about another person, and she stood in the street and made herself look at it straight, because she did not believe in looking at things sideways. Somebody had looked at her and seen her exactly, all the way down to her feet. Nobody had ever done that before, not once, not anybody, not in her whole life. And then he had kept back the one thing he saw that she most needed to know.

Both of those were him; that was the trouble. You could not have the first one without the kind of person who did the second.

She thought, then, because she was honest with herself when nobody was looking, about a row of three things she kept in her own head.

The first was a hand at the board on the first day that had moved before it should have. The second was the third exchange against Renn on the first night. The third was a boy dropping under her backhand in the grey half, lower than she had ever asked him to go, and coming up standing in a way that was not his. She had never told him about the row, or that she had gone into the yard alone, three mornings running, before he came, and tried the drop eleven times, and learned that it was easy to do and impossible to do in time.

She had kept that for a month, and told herself she was waiting until he had something he wanted to tell her.

It did not make what he had done all right. It took some of the heat out, that was all, the way a bucket of water takes some of the heat out of a forge without putting it out.

She walked on, and did not go anywhere she had meant to go. She went where her feet had been going all along, two streets over from Torvin's, to a narrow lane she had walked down nine times in the last month on purpose and pretended each time that it was on the way to somewhere else.

---

The chandler's shop was at the end of the lane, with its door open and a smell of tallow coming out of it, and candles hanging in bunches from the beam over the counter by their wicks, like onions.

The widow who kept it was sitting behind the counter dipping tapers. She was a small woman with a face as round and pale as one of her own candle ends, and she had told Lira, the first time Lira came in to ask about the room upstairs, that she did not let to fighters. Then she had looked at Lira's boots, which had been resoled twice, and her jacket cuffs, which were turned back and stitched, and said that she supposed fighters had to sleep somewhere, and that she would want two weeks paid first and no visitors after the lamps.

"I'll hold it till Wednesday," she had said, a week ago. "Then I'll let it to whoever asks."

It was Wednesday.

"I've come about the room," said Lira.

The widow looked up from her tapers.

"I said Wednesday, and you've come on Wednesday." She seemed faintly surprised by this, as if people seldom did. "Two weeks first."

Lira took the tin out of the inside of her jacket.

It was a small flat tin that had once held throat sweets, and the picture on its lid had worn away to a brown smudge. She had carried it ever since she came to Ardenmere, in the inside pocket where it pressed against her ribs when she sat down, and she opened it on the counter between the bunches of candles. Inside were the weeks of door, in small worn coins: the purse from Brenna, and the two summer purses before that, and every coin from every week she had eaten at Amrit's for half price because he had decided she was his. There was the money from the sparring hour across the district that she did not tell anybody about. There were three coins she had found in the groove at the Cinder House since the summer, dropped by people at the rope, and had kept because they felt like the yard's own.

She counted out two weeks, twice, the way Torvin did, not because she did not trust the coins but to check her own attention, and pushed it across the counter. There was not very much left in the tin afterward, and she shut the lid on what there was.

The widow counted it once, quickly, and swept it into a drawer, and took a key off a nail behind her.

"Stair's round the side. Mind the fourth step. It talks."

---

The room was at the top of a stair so narrow and steep that Lira had to go up it with one hand on the wall, and the fourth step did talk, a long creak like an old man complaining.

There was a door at the top. It was a plain door made of four boards with a crosspiece, painted a long time ago in a green that had gone grey, with a latch, and a keyhole below the latch, and a scuff at the bottom where somebody had kicked it shut for years. She stood on the top step with the key in her hand and looked at it for a long time.

Then she put the key in and turned it, and the lock was stiff, and then it was not, and she pushed the door open and went in, and shut it behind her, and stood with her back against it.

The room was very small, smaller than the roof room in the tall house in the third lane, which she had shared with two sisters from the dye sheds who talked in their sleep about indigo. There was a bed frame with a straw mattress rolled up on it, and a hook on the back of the door, and a shelf. There was one window, looking down into the lane, with a crack across one pane that somebody had stuck over with a strip of paper. The ceiling sloped down on the window side so that she would have to remember not to stand up too fast in the morning. It smelled faintly of tallow from the shop below, and the floorboards were worn pale down the middle where somebody had walked from the door to the window, and from the window to the door, a great many times.

She stood there with her back against the door and listened to it be shut.

That was all she had wanted, and she had not been able to say it to anybody, not to Yeni, who knew everything anyway, and not to Cael. When he asked what she was saving for, all she had been able to say was *a door*, and then tell him not to make a face about it. It had sounded like nothing, and it was nothing, four boards and a crosspiece and a lock that stuck. But it was hers, and it shut, and when it was shut there was nobody on the other side of it who had any right to open it.

She had not had that since she left the academy, nor at the academy either, in the long dormitory with its rows of beds. And before the academy—

She did not think about before the academy. She had a rule about that, and she kept it, the way you keep a door shut in a house you have moved out of.

She sat down on the floor, in the middle of the pale worn strip, with her back against the bed frame and her knees drawn up. She sat there for a long time in the thin light from the cracked window and let herself think about the six weeks, for the first time sitting still long enough to do it.

They were half gone: twenty-two days spent, and twenty left. She had counted the days without meaning to, the way he would have.

In twenty-two days he had fought three times. He had beaten a boy who held his breath, and Renn, and nearly nobody had believed it about Renn until they saw it. He had lost to a Bronze in the sixth exchange, and in the third he had done the thing she had been trying to teach him for two months. He had done it badly, stiff in the hips, but he had done it, in front of the whole yard, against a woman the whole yard was afraid of. He had said *gone* thirty-four times at her post with his ears going red. Then he had said it once more at a Bronze with his ears not going red at all, and half the rope had heard him.

She had stood at the barrel with her hands on the rope and watched every one of those, and watched him get better. It was not the slow, careful getting better of the first weeks, when every morning bought a hand's width and cost a bruise, but something faster. He had found something, or it had found him, and he was coming up through the circle the way she had said people came up who went down faster, and he was not going down, because he was being careful, and she had made him careful.

She thought about being his teacher, which she had never been to anybody before. She had been somebody's student and failed, and somebody else's student and been right in front of witnesses, and had walked the yards of this district on her own for months, losing. She had not known, when she put her name to a vouch for a boy from Denvash who said a word nobody had ever heard out loud, that it would feel like this. She had not known it would feel like watching something she had planted come up through the ground and turn out to be a different plant from the one on the packet.

And she thought, sitting on the floor, about being seen.

She had told him at the board on the first day that the examiner had failed her for being right in front of witnesses, which was true but not the whole of it. The whole of it was that she had stood in front of that desk wanting, more than she had ever wanted anything, for somebody who understood to look at what she had done and say *yes, that, that's real*. Nobody had; the examiner had not understood it, or had not wanted to. The witnesses had understood it and said nothing, because it was not their exam.

Then a boy from Denvash had stood at a rope, three weeks ago, and watched her skip the middle against Brenna in front of the whole yard. He had given it back to her in the dusk afterward, exchange by exchange, flat, with nothing added. *You caught the chop and sent it with no middle. Three times. She went after it every time.* He had not said it was good, and had not needed to. He had seen it exactly as it was, and said it exactly as it was, and that had been better than *good*.

And this morning he had seen the other thing, the bad thing, the thing that had cost her a bout and all the time since of not knowing. He had seen it just as exactly.

It was the same eyes; that was what she kept coming back to, on the floor. The eyes that saw the skipped middle were the eyes that saw the right foot. You could not have one without the other. You could only ask the person who had them not to keep half of what they saw in a pocket.

She would ask him tomorrow, and not like this morning, with her voice going up and the bread whole on the post and *I'll see you*. She would ask him properly. She would tell him the rule, plainly, the way she told him everything, and let him pick it up, and it would be heavier than what other people gave him, and it would be the right weight.

And then she would show him the door.

She got up off the floor and went to the window and stood under the sloping ceiling without hitting her head, and looked down into the lane, where a dog was asleep on a step and a woman was hanging a sheet out of a window opposite. Two streets away, over the roofs, she could see a corner of Torvin's chimney with the smoke going straight up, and she found that she had chosen this room partly for that, without knowing it until now. She stood at her own window, in her own room, with her own door shut behind her, and let herself be proud, all the way through, without anybody there to see it.

Then she went down the stair, and the fourth step talked, and she said *I know* to it out loud. She went to her sparring hour across the district very late, and was shouted at, and did not mind at all.

---

He did not know whether she would be there.

He went up to the yard in the dark on Thursday morning anyway, the way he always did, by the lane where the ground began to rise, with two loaves from underneath in his arms. He told himself that it was what he did, and that Hesk had said not to change what he did, which was true and was not why he went.

The gate in the wall was open and the frost was white along the foot of the east wall, and in the grey half, at the post, somebody was striking the canvas, low, twice, and stepping round it, and striking again.

He stopped in the gateway.

Lira did not stop. She struck the post twice more and stepped round it, and he understood from the strikes that she was thinking, and that the strikes were where she had put her hands while she did it. Then she stopped, with her palm flat on the canvas, and looked at him over her shoulder.

"You're late."

"I'm not."

"You're standing in the gate. That's late." She took her hand off the post. "Come here."

He came across the yard. She did not take the bread, but stood in front of him in the grey half with her hands loose at her sides and her weight a little forward, and she looked at him the way she looked at a bout between exchanges, quickly and completely.

"Here's the rule. You see a thing about me, you tell me. When you see it. Not when you're sure. Not when you've decided I'm ready. Not after Sunday. When you see it." She did not raise her voice. "My feet. My fighting. Anything. If it's wrong, it's wrong, and I'll tell you it's wrong, and we'll both have learned something. But you don't keep it. Not my things. I'll put up with you keeping your own things as long as you like. I've told you that. I'm still not asking. But mine are mine."

"All right."

"Say it back."

He said it back in his own words, the way he had said Vell's terms on the first morning, slowly, checking each one before he set it down. If he saw a thing about her, he told her when he saw it. Not when he was sure. Not when he had decided anything. He did not keep her things.

She listened to every word, and when he had finished she nodded once. "Good. That's done, then." She took one of the loaves off him at last and tore it and gave him the larger half, and the morning brightness came up in her face, slowly, as if it had been waiting behind a door. "It was still the best thing you've ever given me. I meant that too."

"I know."

"I'm going to fix it. Tired or not." She bit into the bread. "I've already started. I did the step at the post twenty times before you came, at the end of an hour, and I felt it go every time. Every time, Cael. I can *feel* it now. All that time—" She stopped, and shook her head, and went on more quietly. "A long time I couldn't find it. And now I can feel it every time."

He did not say anything, because he did not think there was anything to say that would be the right size, and he ate his bread.

Then she said, not looking at him, in the voice she used when a thing mattered more than she wanted it to look, "I took a room."

---

It was two streets from Torvin's, at the end of a lane he had walked down perhaps twice, over a chandler's shop with candles hanging from the beam by their wicks, like onions. There was a widow behind the counter who looked at Cael and said, "No visitors after the lamps," before he had said anything at all.

"It isn't after the lamps. It's the middle of the afternoon."

"I'm saying it now," said the widow, "so it's said."

The stair went up round the side, so narrow that he had to go up it sideways, and the fourth step creaked under him like an old man complaining.

"It talks," said Lira, behind him. "Every time. I'm going to name it."

At the top there was a door.

She stopped him on the top step before he could reach for the latch, and went past him, squeezing between him and the wall, and took a key on a string out of her jacket, and let herself in, and shut the door in his face.

He stood on the top step looking at the door. It was green gone grey, four boards and a crosspiece, with a scuff at the bottom.

"Knock," said Lira's voice, from the other side.

He knocked.

There was a pause, a long one, longer than it needed to be, a pause somebody was enjoying.

Then the latch lifted, and the door opened, and Lira stood in it with one hand on the edge and the other on the frame. She looked at him as if he were somebody who had come to the wrong house.

"Yes?" she said.

He did not laugh, though he nearly did, and he saw her see him nearly do it, and her face went very straight indeed.

"I've come to see the room."

"Have you." She considered him, and then stood aside and held the door open. "You can come in. Mind your head. The ceiling comes down on the window side and it's not sorry about it."

He went in, and minded his head.

It was very small, as he had known it would be. There was a bed with a straw mattress on it and a blanket folded square at the foot, and a shelf. On the shelf there was a tin cup, and a comb, and the canvas roll, and a little flat tin with the picture worn off its lid. There was one window with a crack across the pane and a strip of paper over the crack, and a hook on the back of the door with nothing on it, because she was still wearing her jacket. He thought she had not taken it off since she got the key.

He stood in the middle of the room on the pale worn strip of floor and looked at all of it, one thing at a time, the way he looked at everything.

"Well?"

"It's yours."

He had not known that was what he was going to say until it was out. She did not say anything, but shut the door behind him, very gently. It made a small sound as the latch dropped, the smallest sound, a click like a figure coming right.

"It shuts."

"I heard."

"It *locks*." She showed him the key on its string. "From inside. And from outside. Both. I can be in here and lock it, or out there and lock it, and either way it's locked." She seemed to think he might not have understood. "Nobody else has a key. The widow has one, for fires. Nobody else."

"Lira. It's a good door."

She looked at him, and then sat down on the end of the bed, quite suddenly, as if her legs had decided it for her, and looked at the door. She did not say anything for a long time, and he did not either. He stood under the sloping ceiling with his head bent, and the light came in through the cracked pane, and two streets away over the roofs, through the window, he could see the top of Torvin's chimney with its smoke going straight up.

"You can see Torvin's from here."

"Can you? I hadn't noticed."

---

Vell put him on Sunday's card with a Copper Rank 4 named Talis, Stone Path.

"He's young," she said, at her table on Thursday. "Younger than Dessa. A good deal less patient. Stone like hers, and strong, and he's had a good season." She turned her tea by the handle. "You'll want to watch him tonight. He's fourth on the card."

"It's four days since Sarel," said Lira, who had come up beside him. "He's still green across the back."

"I know how long it's been. I wrote it down." Vell looked at Lira. "Survivable?"

Lira looked at Cael's back, as though she could see through his coat to the colours on it.

"Survivable. If he doesn't go down on the ribs."

He watched Talis fight that night from the rope, near the barrel, with the Log open on his knee.

Talis was about eighteen, a big fair young man with a neck as wide as his head and a way of rocking on his heels between the exchanges, impatient, as if the circle were a queue he had been made to stand in. When Vell said *begin* he did not wait for anything, but went in, and Cael watched the grit come up off him as it had come up off Dessa, shivering all at once over his arms and shoulders and lying down flat. Then he was a gatepost with a temper, and he walked through a Copper 3 Blade in four exchanges, and the rope cheered.

Cael wrote it all down: the shoulders, which dipped a little before the right hand came; the hands, which Talis held low; the impatience, the rocking on the heels, the way the young man's face went red when a strike did not land. Then he wrote *frustration — let him*, because that had been a true thing about Brenna and might be a true thing again.

He did not write anything about the ground, or look at it. He had never once in his life looked at the ground.

---

On Sunday the frost had gone off by the middle of the afternoon, and the dirt in the circle was soft on the surface and hard underneath, and Talis came over the rope as if he were late for something.

"Talis. Copper Rank 4, Stone Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

The grit came up off him and lay down flat, and he came.

Cael did what he had done in the third exchange against Sarel, and moved, small and quick and unplanned, with his hips loose this time, as Lira had shown him on Wednesday. But Talis was not Sarel, and the watching was not too slow for him, so Cael watched while he moved, and he began where the Log told him to begin, with the shoulders. The shoulders dipped. The right hand came. Cael was somewhere else.

But Talis was somewhere else too.

Cael had not seen him go there. One breath Talis was in front of him, and the next he was a stride to the left with his weight already planted, and the right hand that the shoulders had promised never came at all. It was the left forearm that came, heavy and grey with the grit, across the upper arm like a mallet on a stump. Cael went sideways and kept his feet, just, and Talis was already rocking back on his heels, impatient, waiting.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

*First exchange,* said Cael to himself. *The shoulders dipped before the right, and I read it, and he didn't throw the right. The shoulders told the truth about a strike he never threw.* He crossed them off in his head. *Not the shoulders.*

In the second he watched the hands.

Talis held them low, loose, at the level of his belt, and Cael watched them and nothing else, all through the exchange, as he had watched Renn's heel once. They told him nothing until they were already moving. They did not set, or close, or draw back; they simply went, and by the time a hand had begun to go, Talis had already been planted somewhere for half a beat, and the hand was only the last thing to arrive. Cael got out of the first one. The second came from a place a stride off to his right that he had not seen Talis reach, and caught him on the hip with a short blunt thud like a door kicked shut. He felt it go right through him into his back, into Sarel's bruise.

*Not the hands,* he thought, on his mark, with his hip aching. *The hands come last. Everything's already decided by the time the hands know about it.* He looked across. *He knows where he's going to plant before I do. Of course he does. It's his foot. But there should be something before it.*

In the third he watched the face.

It was the last place left above the belt, and it had been true about Brenna. Talis was impatient, and impatient people told you things. So Cael went in on purpose, once, early, and put the one strike he owned onto Talis's forearm, short and straight from the hip, a touch and no more, to make him angry. It worked. Talis's face went red from the collar up, and his mouth went tight, and Cael thought *good, let him*, and watched the red face for whatever it would give away.

It gave away nothing. Talis did not look where he meant to go, the way the kiln boy had; his eyes stayed on Cael's chest. His temper did not make his shoulders talk, or his hands. It only made him plant harder, so that the next strike, when it came off the planted foot, came with more of the ground behind it. Cael got his forearms in the way of it and felt it jar them both to the shoulder.

*Not the face,* he thought, back on his mark. *Not the temper.* He had gone down the whole of Talis from the shoulders to the belt, the way you go down a column, and every line was empty. There was nothing left to look at, and Talis was still going to plant somewhere he could not see, and hit him from it.

In the fourth he watched everything at once, and it made no difference. Talis planted on Cael's left, where Cael had not seen him going, and the gatepost forearm came round low into his side under the ribs, on the left, the side Sarel had not touched. It did not land like a mallet, or a door. It landed like a beam swung end-on into a wall, all of it at one point, with the whole weight of the planted leg and the ground beneath the leg pushing it through. It was the strike he never saw building. He was looking at all of Talis, and all of Talis was still, and then he was on the ground.

He went down on his side with his cheek against the dirt and every bit of air gone out of him at once.

"Match," said Vell.

He could not get up yet. He lay with his face on the cold packed earth of the circle, and breathed, or tried to, and listened to the yard shouting above him. He thought about nothing at all for a moment.

Talis walked back to his mark.

Cael felt him go.

He felt it in his cheekbone, where his face lay against the ground, a faint, quick buzz, very small, like the hum in a table when somebody sets a heavy glass down at the other end of it. It came up out of the dirt into the bone of his face, and then, a half beat after it, Talis's foot came down, a stride away, and planted.

Talis took another step. The buzz came first, and then the foot.

Cael lay very still with his cheek on the ground.

*Again,* he thought, and Talis took a third step, and it came again: the hum in the dirt, and then, half a beat behind it, the foot. Every time. The ground knew where Talis was going to plant before Talis's foot got there. It said so, if you were listening with the right part of you, and Cael had not been. He had been listening with his eyes, at shoulders and hands, the way he listened to everybody. It had never once occurred to him that a tell could live in the ground.

He got up eventually, slowly, with his left side feeling as though somebody had driven a wedge into it, and something catching under the ribs, sharp, whenever he breathed in all the way. He walked to the table, and Vell wrote. He did not hear what she said, and then he did.

"Result stands as called. Talis, Copper Rank 4, Stone Path, over unrated. Fourth exchange. Match." The pen moved. "Witnessed and entered."

Lira was waiting at the barrel. She had her hand on the iron stake at the corner of the rope, where it went down into the ground, and she did not take it off when he came up. She was looking at him with an odd expression. "Did you feel it?"

He stared at her.

"Through the stake." Lira tapped the iron with one finger. "I didn't, at first. I was holding the rope. In the third exchange I put my hand down on the stake to lean, and it was humming. Every time he was going to plant. A half beat before he put his foot down." She looked at him. "I thought you'd have seen it."

"I felt it. After. With my face."

Lira looked at the side of his face, where the dirt was.

"Well," she said, after a moment, "that's one way to find it."

"I wasn't looking at the ground."

"Nobody looks at the ground." She took her hand off the stake at last. "That's why it's such a good place to keep something."

# Chapter 11 — Homework

They came in by ones and twos at first, and then in knots, through the gate in the wall and round the side of the public house, until the empty yard of the morning had turned, without any single moment when it happened, into a place where something was going to happen.

Somebody strung a rope round the circle on short iron stakes, an arm's length outside the groove, so that the crowd had a line to stand behind. Somebody else came out of the back of the public house with a bucket and a ladle and went round the rope selling water, and then a boy came out with a tray of hot pies and did better. The lanterns along the walls stayed dark.

It was afternoon, and the light came down into the yard over the west wall, and lay across half of the circle and left the other half in shadow. The shadowed side was where people chose to stand. Cael worked out why after a minute, when he saw a man shading his eyes on the sunny side: from the dark side you looked into the light, and saw everything, and from the light side you looked into the dark.

Lira took him to an upturned barrel at the corner of the rope, on the shadowed side, where the wall of the public house met the yard wall. From the top of it he could see the whole circle and most of the crowd.

"Best seat," she said. "Don't tell anyone."

"Why is it empty, then?"

"Because it's next to the drain." She pointed with her chin at a grating in the corner, and the smell came up to meet the pointing. "Everything good has a cost. You'll get used to it."

A woman came out of the back door of the public house carrying a folding table.

She set it up in the shade of the back wall, on the edge of the yard nearest the circle, without looking at anyone. She unfolded its legs one at a time and pressed each into the dirt with her boot to seat it. Then she went back inside and came out with a stool and a book. The book was a thick ledger in a dark cover, worn soft at the corners and darker still where hands had held it, and she set it on the table and squared it with the table's edge, as though the angle mattered.

She was somewhere in her fifties, not tall, with grey working through dark hair that she wore pulled back hard from her face. She moved without hurry and without wasting anything, and when she walked out to the circle and along the groove, slowly, looking down at it, Cael saw that she walked heel first and then flat, every step the same length, measuring.

He knew that walk. He had last seen it on a white sheet in a back room in Denvash, on a man nearly twice her size.

"That's Vell," Lira said, unnecessarily.

Vell walked the whole groove. Twice she stopped and pressed at the ground with the toe of her boot, and once she called something over her shoulder. The boy who had sold pies came running with a rake and smoothed out a patch where the dirt had rucked. Then she went back to her table and sat down and opened the ledger to a clean page, and only then did she look at the crowd, a single sweep from one end of the rope to the other, as if she were counting it.

Her eyes went past Cael on his barrel without stopping. He was nearly sure they had paused.

A large man with very clean hands was working his way along the rope with a slate hung round his neck on a string.

He was dressed better than anyone else in the yard, in a dark coat with good buttons, and he was perfectly friendly to everybody. People kept putting coins in his hand and he kept writing on the slate with a stub of chalk and saying something low and pleasant to each of them. He laughed a good deal. Every few minutes he wiped the slate with his sleeve and wrote it again.

"Marrow," said Lira, in a voice like a closed door.

After a while Cael took out the notebook and, without being sure why, began copying the slate each time Marrow wiped it and wrote it again: two names, a number beside each. As people paid, the numbers moved toward the name more of them were paying on, which made sense, but they moved a little further each time than the money did. Cael could not have said how he knew that, but the numbers had a rhythm, and the rhythm leaned, very slightly, the same way every time, like a wheel with one spoke a hair too long.

"What are you doing?" Lira said.

"Counting."

She looked at his page. She read it twice, and then she looked across the yard at Marrow, who was laughing at something a man had said and writing on his slate.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't let him see you doing that." She did not take her eyes off Marrow. "I mean it. People who count Marrow's numbers have a way of finding out that the yard's less friendly than they thought."

Cael closed the notebook. He had seen what he wanted to see, and it had been what she said it was, a hair the wrong side of fair. He was obscurely pleased about it, in the way he was pleased when a measurement came out where he expected, and he was also a little frightened, and the two feelings sat side by side without either one making room.

Vell stood up behind her table.

She did not shout. She simply said, in a voice that carried over the whole yard without seeming to be raised at all, "First bout," and the yard went quiet in a wave, from the rope nearest her out to the far wall, as if she had dropped a stone into it.

---

The first two fighters were both Copper.

Vell gave them out as they stepped over the rope, in the same flat, carrying voice. "Copper Rank 4, Stone Path." A woman of about thirty, broad through the hips and shoulders, with short hair and her sleeves rolled up past the elbow. "Against Copper Rank 2, Blade Path." A lean young man, quick in every movement, who came over the rope in a single hop and rolled his neck from side to side as he walked to his mark.

They stood on opposite sides of the circle, just inside the groove.

Cael was writing before Vell had finished speaking. *Stone, Cu 4. Weight low and centred. Hands open, not fists. Feet—* He looked at her feet. They were flat, and wide, and very still, and the loose grit round her boots shivered once and lay down, as the grit round Joren's boots had done at the low wall, a world away and a week ago.

*Rooted,* he wrote. *Or something like it. Higher rank than Joren's.* He looked at the young man. *Blade, Cu 2. Weight forward. Right arm low and back, behind his hip.*

"Begin," said Vell.

The young man brought up his blade.

Cael had read about Blade Path and had seen it once, on a flickering grey sheet, too fast to follow. He had never seen it close. It came out of nothing along the young man's right forearm, from just below the elbow and out past the knuckles, a narrow blade of pale light, steady as a ruled line, with none of a flame's flutter in it and none of a lamp's spill. It simply stood there in the air, hard and clean, with a thin bright line along its leading edge, and when the young man turned his wrist it turned with him and made a faint high sound, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass.

It looked about a hand long past the fist.

"Longer than that," Lira said quietly beside him.

He looked at her.

"Whatever your eye just told you," she said, without looking away from the circle. "Longer than that. Everyone's eye lies about it. It's light, it's not steel, it doesn't look like it should reach as far as it does." She tapped two fingers on her own forearm, in the air beyond her fist. "About eight inches past where his hand ends. Remember that."

He wrote *Eight inches past the fist. My eye says less.* And underlined *says.*

The Stone woman moved first.

That surprised him. He had expected the quick one to open, the one with the reach, and instead the woman whose whole plan must depend on getting close came off her mark and crossed the distance in two long steps, low, her hands coming up open in front of her. The young man answered at once. It was a short, tidy cut, aimed at her leading shoulder, not to finish anything but to mark her, so that the next time she tried to close she would remember what it had cost.

She took it.

Cael saw her decide to take it. It was a half-second before the blade landed, and it was very small: her shoulder turned a little toward the cut instead of away from it, and her chin came down, and she did not slow. The edge of light went across the top of her shoulder and opened her sleeve and the skin under it in a short, shallow line. She made no sound. She was already inside his reach by the time he had finished the cut, and her hands closed on his extended forearm, one at the wrist and one above the elbow, just behind where the blade began.

The young man pulled. Nothing happened. Her boots were flat on the packed dirt and the grit round them lay still, and he might as well have tried to pull his arm out of a wall.

He broke the hold the only way he had, by letting the blade go out like a lamp and twisting his arm small, and he slid free and skipped back three steps, and the first exchange was over. The crowd made a noise that was half a groan and half a laugh. The Stone woman went back to her mark with a dark line across her shoulder, rolled the shoulder once, and stood there.

*She let him cut her,* Cael wrote, fast. *On purpose. To get inside. The cut cost less than the distance.* He stopped, and thought, and wrote: *If she'd dodged it properly she'd have been two steps further out, and he'd have cut her on the way in next time anyway, deeper, because he'd know her line. She paid now, small, instead of later, big.*

The second exchange took four seconds.

She came in exactly the same way, and this time he did not cut at her shoulder, because she had shown him she would take that. He cut lower, at her lead leg, to stop the step. But she had not been going to step. She had been going to drop, and she dropped under the cut onto one knee and came up inside it with both hands already on his arm, and this time she did not hold.

She turned. She took his whole extended arm, blade and all, and turned it over and out against the joint, using his own reach as a lever. He went down in the dirt on his side with his arm locked straight above him and his face in the groove.

He slapped the ground twice with his free hand.

"Called," said Vell. "Stone Path."

The crowd's noise was very different this time. It was mixed. Some of it was a groan, from people along the rope who had paid Marrow on the quick young man with the reach, and some of it was a long, appreciative murmur from people who seemed to have understood something about what they had just watched. Cael could tell which was which by where the people stood. The groaners were mostly on the sunny side, and the murmurers mostly in the shade.

The Stone woman let go, stood up, and offered her hand, and the young man took it and was pulled up, and they touched fists. Then she walked to the table, with her sleeve dark across the shoulder, and stood there while Vell wrote. It was a short entry. Two lines.

After the bout ended Cael kept writing for a full minute, so as not to lose the shape of it.

*Two exchanges. She lost the first one on purpose to learn where he'd cut. She won the second because he learned it too, and changed, and she'd guessed what he'd change to. He was faster. She was a step ahead the whole time anyway. The reach was his advantage and she made it the thing she held on to.*

He looked at what he had written, and then he added a line underneath, in the smaller hand he used when he was not sure.

*The second exchange is questions.*

---

The second bout took the rest of the afternoon, or felt as though it did.

Vell gave them out as Bronze. "Bronze Rank 2, Iron Path. Against Bronze Rank 1, Stone Path." Cael looked at Lira when he heard *Bronze*, and she knew what he was asking before he asked it.

"Vell's Bronze," she said. "Not the registry's. Neither of them could get past the gate on the bridge with a card that said Bronze, I'd bet. But they're Bronze in that book."

"What's the difference in a fight?"

"Watch," said Lira, "and tell me."

It was a long bout, and nothing in it was fast in the way the first one had been fast. The Iron man was tall and heavy and very calm, and when he struck, he struck short and hard, with no reach to it at all, and everything he hit he seemed to hit in exactly the same place.

The Stone woman was older than the first one, grey at the temples, and she did not let him hit her anywhere if she could help it. She gave ground. She gave ground along the groove in a long slow curve and let him follow, and every time he had nearly caught her up she stopped, and set her feet. The grit lay down round her boots, and he struck her guard as if he were striking a wall.

It went five exchanges like that. Then six.

Cael filled two pages and started a third. He wrote down how each exchange began and how it ended and what each of them tried, and he began to see something he had not had words for before, which was that both of them were changing their minds. In the first exchanges the Iron man had come straight at her, every time, and every time he had been met by the wall of her stance.

By the third he had stopped coming straight. He came in at an angle, so that she had to turn to meet him, and turning meant lifting a foot, and when she lifted a foot the grit did not lie down. By the fourth she had seen what he was doing, and she had stopped turning; she let him come in at his angle and met him side-on, which was weaker, but which kept both her feet on the ground.

*She's giving up strength to keep her feet,* Cael wrote. *He's giving up power to make her move. They're both paying for something.*

In the sixth exchange something changed in both of them at once, and it took him a moment to see what it was.

They were tired. They had been tired for some exchanges already, he realized, but in the sixth exchange it started to make decisions for them. The Iron man's strikes were still in the same place every time, but he took a breath between them now, a short one, and she saw the breath and began to time her steps to it.

The Stone woman, for her part, had begun to set her feet a fraction later each time, so that there was a moment, at the start of every stance, when she was not quite rooted yet. He saw that too. Cael watched them both see it, one after the other, and he watched them both try to use what they had seen, and it was like watching two people play a board game who had each just noticed the other's mistake.

In the seventh exchange the Iron man struck at the moment before her feet set.

He did not strike hard. He did not need to. He hit her guard while she was still settling into it, and she went back a whole step and then another. Her heel came down on the edge of the groove and slipped, and for an instant she was off balance with her weight on one foot and the Iron man was already turning to bring the next strike round.

She held up her hand.

It was not a slap on the ground. It was an open palm, raised, as you raise your hand in a room to say that you want to speak. She held it there, breathing hard, with her heel on the edge of the groove.

The Iron man stopped his strike in the air.

"Called," said Vell. "Conceded. Iron Path."

And the crowd, which had been quiet for most of the bout, did something Cael had not expected. It applauded. It did not cheer, as it had for the Stone woman's lock in the first bout. It clapped, steadily, all along the rope, the shady side and the sunny side together, and the clapping went on while the two of them touched fists and walked to Vell's table, and the person it was for was not the winner.

"Why are they clapping for her?" Cael said.

"Because she read it right." Lira was clapping too, not very loudly. "Another breath and he'd have had her down, and maybe hurt, and she'd have been out for a month. She saw it a strike early, and she stopped it, and she'll be back in the circle next week." She stopped clapping. "She'll get more bouts offered off the back of losing that than he will off the back of winning it. He's dull. Strong and dull. She's interesting."

"How you lose matters," Cael said.

"How you lose matters," Lira agreed. "You were listening. I was starting to think you only listened to people's feet."

At the table, Vell was writing. It took longer than the entry for the first bout, three or four lines, and when she had finished she said something to the Stone woman that made her laugh. The Stone woman went off to the water bucket with her shoulders down and her hands loose, as though it had been a good afternoon after all.

He had six pages by the end of it, more than he had written about anything since the archive.

Lira was watching him read.

"You're not watching it like everybody else," she said.

"How am I watching it?"

"Like you're going to be tested on it." She leaned back on her hands on the barrel lid. "Everybody else on that rope was watching it to see who won, and to see if they'd been right, and to have something to talk about at dinner. You were watching it like homework."

He thought about that. It was not untrue. He thought that the difference was simply that he did not know yet what he was going to need, and so everything might be the thing he needed, and so he had to keep all of it. He did not say that.

"Is that bad?"

"No," Lira said, and then, after a pause, more quietly: "No. It's not bad."

She did not say anything else about it. But on the walk back down from the yards, through the long gold light of the end of the day, he noticed that she was walking a little closer to him than she had in the morning, and that she was looking at him from time to time out of the side of her eye, as he imagined she might look at an opponent she had begun to take seriously, rather than at a curiosity she had decided to be kind to.

---

She went with him as far as the crossroads.

The board stood in the middle of the triangle with the evening light on it, orange and low, so that every notice threw its own small shadow across the one beneath it. The skewer woman had gone. The man with the weighted cords had gone. The triangle was full of people walking home, and none of them stopped at the board, and Lira did not stop either. She stopped at the edge of the triangle and turned round and folded her arms.

"You're going to put your name down," she said.

It was not a question, and he did not treat it as one. "Probably."

"Not probably." She shook her head. "You've decided. You decided in the second bout. Somewhere about the fourth exchange you stopped writing and you just watched, for about ten breaths, and when the pencil started again it moved differently. Faster. Shorter. Like a list of things you were going to need." She said it with plain satisfaction, the satisfaction of a person who liked being right about people and saw no reason to hide that. "I'm not showing off. You ought to know what your face does when you've made up your mind, because you plainly don't, and somebody should tell you."

"What does it do?"

"Nothing. That's how I could tell. Everything else about you is always doing something." She tipped her head. "So you can't bluff me. Which is going to be either very useful or very annoying, depending on what you end up wanting."

He did not know what to say to that. He was not even sure she was wrong.

"How do I put my name down?"

"Go to the Cinder House tomorrow, before the bouts, and find Vell at her table. Tell her you want a bout. Unrated. She'll match you against somebody." Lira's voice had gone practical. "She'll ask your age. She'll ask your Path. She'll ask who vouches for you."

"Do I need somebody to vouch for me?"

"You don't *need* it." Lira looked at the board, not at him. "Nobody can stop you walking up to that table on your own. But an unrated debut with nobody speaking for them gets matched carefully, which means cheaply, which means Vell puts you against somebody who won't teach you anything, because nobody knows whether you'll fold in the first exchange or last the whole bout, and she won't spend a real fighter's evening finding out." She was quiet a moment. Then, quickly, as if she wanted to say it before she had finished deciding to: "I'll vouch for you. If you want."

He had not expected it. It took him half a breath too long to answer, and she saw that half a breath, and for once she did not say anything about it.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I don't think you'll waste the yard's evening." She shrugged, the easy shrug that looked as if it cost nothing. "And because I don't vouch for many people, and I'd like to be right about one of them. Most people I could vouch for, I'd be wrong about. Most people don't stand in front of a board for eleven minutes."

"I'd want that," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." The corner of her mouth went up. "If you go down in the first exchange I'm going to tell everybody I've never seen you before in my life and you must have followed me there."

"Would they believe you?"

"I'm very convincing," said Lira. "Tomorrow. Cinder House, the middle of the morning, before Vell's had too much tea and started being kind to people. Bring the notebook. You'll want it after, whatever happens."

She walked away across the triangle toward wherever she went in the evenings, and did not look back. Cael stood at the edge of the crossroads and watched her go until the widest street had taken her.

Then he looked at the board, and at the word *vouch* on the bottom notice, written in brown ink by somebody who had pressed hard enough to dent the paper. *Ledger-verified bouts only. No vouch, no walk-in.*

He remembered Lira's voice in the yard that morning, with her face turned away from him, stretching her arms over her head: *It's not a thing you do for just anybody.*

He had been offered something, and he had taken it, and he had no idea what it cost.

---

Torvin was at the kitchen table that night, which Cael had not seen him do before. He was sitting at the end of it with a ledger of his own, a thin one with a cracked spine, and a stub of pencil, and he was adding columns with his lips moving. His wife was at the range. Yeni had the far end of the table, with a shirt across her knee. The woman who slept in her boots was eating soup with her boots on.

Cael sat down across from Torvin and waited until he had reached the bottom of a column.

"What does a vouch cost?" he said.

Torvin looked up. He looked at Cael for long enough that the pencil, which had been about to start the next column, came to rest on the page.

"Who's paying?" he said.

"The person who gives it."

"Then it costs them." Torvin went back to his column.

That seemed to be all. Cael waited, and Torvin's lips moved down the column, and reached the bottom, and he wrote a figure under it and drew a line. Then, without looking up, he said, "A vouch is a loan. You're the one borrowing. They're the one who's out of pocket if you don't pay it back."

"Out of pocket how? It isn't money."

"Didn't say it was money."

At the other end of the table, Yeni's needle had stopped.

"It's their name," she said. She did not look up from the shirt either. Cael was beginning to think that nobody in this house ever looked up when they were saying something important. "Round the yards, everybody's got a name, and the name's worth something. It's worth what people think it is. If somebody puts their name next to yours and you go down in a heap the first time you're touched, then the next time they put their name next to somebody, people remember the heap." The needle went in and out. "Do it twice and their name's worth nothing. Then nobody'll take their word for anything. Not for a fighter, not for a debt, not for what they saw happen."

"How long does it take to make a name worth something?"

"Depends who you are." Yeni bit the thread. "Who's vouching for you?"

"Lira."

Yeni's hands stopped. Across the table, Torvin's pencil stopped too.

"The Wind girl," Torvin said. "Four months."

"You know her?"

"Everybody knows her," said Yeni. "She lost her first four bouts in that yard and kept coming back. Nobody keeps coming back after four. Now people bet on her." She looked at Cael at last, a long, measuring look, the same look she had given him the first night when she had seemed to be taking his measurements for a coat. "Four months is a short time to build a name, for her. It's all the name she's got."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Torvin's wife scraped something in a pan. The woman in the boots finished her soup.

"Well," Torvin said, and turned the page of his ledger, and started on the next column.

---

Upstairs, with the lamp still lit and the brothers not yet in from the river, Cael sat on his cot and turned back through the notebook, past the day's six pages, to the entry he had written on the rise three days ago in the last of the light.

*Find somewhere to sleep. Find work. Watch everything. Show nobody anything. Be dull. Be competent. Be still here.*

He had found somewhere to sleep. He had not looked for work, and the pouch he counted twice every morning would not last while he put it off. And tomorrow he meant to walk into a circle in front of sixty strangers, which was about as far from *show nobody anything* as he could get without standing on the board. Hesk had said it more plainly in the side room at Weaver's Row, leaning close: *Don't let anyone see you move. Not until you know what you're doing.*

He set both rules against what he actually meant to do. He did not mean to show anything. He meant to last, and watch, and keep his hands to himself, and come out knowing more than he had given away. The circuit was the one ladder in this city that did not ask to see a card, and the only way onto it was across that rope. He was bending a rule, and he knew which one, and how far. He told himself that bending a rule you could see was a different thing from breaking one you had forgotten, and he very nearly believed it.

He thought about the Stone woman in the first bout, choosing a small cut now over a larger one later, and he understood, sitting there, that this was closer to tomorrow than any of the morning's rules. He was not brave. He was fairly sure of that. He was going to walk into the circle because staying out of it would cost him more, over time, than walking in. He would be paying for something. He would just be paying now, small, instead of later.

But he would not be the only one paying.

He turned to a clean page and wrote at the top of it, in the private hand, smaller than the rest.

*Lira's vouching for me tomorrow. Torvin says a vouch is a loan and I'm the one borrowing. Yeni says it's her name, and four months of it is all the name she's got.*

*She didn't tell me that. She made it sound like nothing.*

He looked at the two lines. Then he wrote one more under them, and underlined it, once.

*Find out what it costs her before I let her spend it.*

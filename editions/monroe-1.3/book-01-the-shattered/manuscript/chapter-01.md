# Chapter 1 — Page Seven

Nobody described Kindling the same way twice.

Cael had collected eleven. They were on page seven of his notebook, the current one, in the order people had given them to him. He had started the list in spring without meaning to start anything, because it turned out that when you asked people a question they liked answering, they told you, and if you wrote down what they told you, you had a list.

*Sunlight,* the fruit woman told him, from behind her trays of dates in the Outer Market. She had carried an Ember Path for eleven years and had never once doubted it, and she said it while weighing his dates, without looking up.

*A door opening,* the Merchant Row gate guard offered. He was Copper-tier, Shield Path, and he had a great deal of time between carts. He said it twice, the second time slower, in case Cael had missed it.

*Like remembering something you never knew,* announced the girl from three streets over. She was two years past her own Kindling and explained it with the confidence of someone to whom a thing has happened exactly once.

*Like a wheel seating on its bearing, so you feel it go home,* said Garrik, who fixed carts.

*Warm,* said Ressa at the bakery. Then she thought about it and added, *Like the oven catching.*

*Like every timetable you own being right at once,* said the man who sold hand-copied transit schedules, and when Cael laughed, it turned out the man had not been joking.

*Cold water, and then not cold,* said the lock-keeper at the canal gate.

*Like a bell you feel in your teeth,* said the boot-mender.

*Brief,* said the woman who ran the reference room at the community hall, and went back to her ledger. Cael had thought it the most honest answer on the page, until the last one.

*Heavy, good heavy,* said Joren, two days ago, on his way home from Weaver's Row. He had said it too fast, as if handing over something hot.

*I don't know how to describe it,* Hesk had said, and nothing more.

Cael had starred that one. Only that one.

---

This was the morning before his Kindling.

There was no way to skip it and no way to ask for more time. In the year you turned fourteen, you stood in the circle at a certification office, and your Path woke there whether you wanted it to or not. The only thing anyone could choose was the day, and the city chose that for you, a year ahead, because the district made so many fourteen-year-olds that the offices would drown otherwise. Cael had turned fourteen five days ago. His appointment was at eight tomorrow, on Weaver's Row, and he had spent most of the past year not thinking about it, which had not worked.

He ate breakfast at the kitchen table while Hesk read the city notice sheet. The east window let in light at a low angle for about twenty minutes each morning, clear and sideways, so that it lay across the table like a ruler. The bread was from Ressa's bakery on the corner, where she kept the side door open while the ovens were going. The smell carried half a block in every direction, and walking home in the morning was mostly a matter of following your nose. Under the bread was the workshop smell of solder flux and machine oil and brass filings, which did not really have a smell, except that they did.

"Weather's turning," Hesk said, from behind the notice sheet.

"It says that?"

"No, it says the canal authority is raising the lock fees." He folded the sheet. "I can tell about the weather myself."

Hesk was fifty-four, with a square, weathered face and hands that looked too big for the fine work he did with them. He was Iron-tier, retired from active practice for longer than Cael had been alive. He made instruments now: pressure gauges for the canal authority, level meters for the survey office, and once, memorably, a very small clock for a man who wanted it hidden inside a walking stick.

He looked at Cael over the folded sheet. "You're not eating."

"I'm eating."

"You're holding a piece of bread, which isn't the same thing."

Cael took a bite to prove something. Hesk watched him chew it with the patience of a man who had raised a boy and knew exactly how much a single bite proved.

"Workshop, when you're done," Hesk said. "I want your numbers on the lock gauge housing before we test it."

Cael knew it was a kindness, and he took it anyway.

The workshop was the back half of the house, with a long bench under two windows, a stove, racks of brass stock sorted by gauge, and a wall of small drawers labelled in Hesk's square hand. *Springs, fine. Springs, coarse. Springs, do not use.* Cael had asked about that last drawer when he was six, and Hesk had said, "Those are the ones that taught me something," which Cael had not understood then and did now.

The housing sat in the test rig. It was a brass case for a canal pressure gauge, about the size of two fists, and it would bolt to a lock wall and hold its dial steady while tons of water leaned on the gate beside it. Hesk had built the case. Cael had done the bracket calculations three days ago, at this bench, with the city's appointment letter tucked into the back of his notebook so that he would not have to see it.

He opened the notebook to the page.

"Walk me through it," Hesk said.

"Wall bracket, four bolts, and the load is the housing's weight, plus vibration from the gate, plus a margin for when somebody bumps it with a boat hook." He ran his finger down the column. "The allowed load on each bolt comes from the stock sheet. I used the supplier's figure for the bracket alloy, and with the margin you like, it holds at three times working load."

"Three times, good, let's see."

The rig was simple: a lever arm, a hanging tray, iron weights stacked in fives, and a dial gauge touching the bracket at its weakest point, so that if the bracket bent the needle would move, and if it bent too far and did not come back, it had failed.

Hesk added the first stack, then the second, and the needle stayed near zero. At the third stack, which was working load, the needle twitched and settled, and Cael found that he had been holding his breath and let it out. Hesk added a fourth stack, slowly, and then a fifth, and Cael leaned in over the dial so close that he could see his own eye in the glass. At five and a half stacks the bracket made a small sound, not loud, a tick like a knuckle cracking, and the needle swung across the dial and hung there and did not come back.

Hesk lifted the weights off one stack at a time. The needle stayed where it was. The bracket had a slight new curve to it, as if it were thinking about bowing. It had failed at less than twice working load.

Cael felt his face go hot, which was ridiculous, because it was a bracket and nobody had been standing under it.

"Well," said Hesk, with interest. "That's a lie somewhere."

"My numbers were right."

"Then find out what was wrong."

Cael went back down the column twice. The arithmetic held. He had weighed the housing himself; the bolt spacing was right; the margin was right. Then he came to the one number on the page that he had not made.

*Allowed load, bracket alloy, per supplier stock sheet.*

He had copied it, and he had not tested it, and he had not even looked at where the supplier had got it from.

"The alloy," he said.

Hesk said nothing, which meant *go on.*

So Cael took the bent bracket to the bench, clamped a scrap off-cut of the same stock in the vice, and spent the next forty minutes doing the small, boring test that would have taken forty minutes three days ago. File a sample, measure it, load it, read the needle, and do it all again. The real figure, when he had it twice, was about two-thirds of the supplier's.

"They printed the number for a different temper," Hesk said, when Cael showed him. "Softer stock with the same name, and it happens." He turned the off-cut over in his fingers. "It's a good calculation, Cael. Everything you made yourself was right."

"But I used their number."

"You did."

"Because it was printed."

"Because it was printed, and it looked like a number. Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from," Hesk said, and set the off-cut down on the bench between them.

Cael had heard him say it before, at eight, over another failed bracket, and had filed it away with everything else Hesk said, without any idea yet how many things the sentence would turn out to be about. He wrote it down anyway, at the top of a fresh page, with the alloy figure he had measured underneath it, and the date.

"Redo the bracket in thicker stock. You know the number now."

"I know *a* number."

Hesk looked at him for a moment. Then he smiled, which he did not do often, and which changed his whole face.

"That," he said, "is the right answer."

The new bracket went into the rig an hour later and held at three times working load, and at three and a half, and Hesk stopped there because there was no reason to break a good part to prove a point. Cael watched the needle sit still at the top of the stack. He had made something, and it held, and he knew exactly why, and for nearly a whole minute he did not think about tomorrow at all.

---

He went out afterward, because the house had begun to feel small.

Denvash's Outer District was a long ring pressed against the inside of the interior wall: wide up here in the north, where the transit depots stood, and squeezed thin in the east, where the lanes knotted up against the Inner District. The northern quadrant was where Cael had grown up. He knew its depots and its market, its community hall, and the canal gate that sighed when the locks let water through. He knew which streets flooded and which dogs bit. He walked north toward the market with nowhere particular to go, his breath showing in the cold. He counted things, because his own thoughts were too loud: fourteen carts between Fen Street and the market road, six stalls open and three still setting up, and one man disputing a delivery's weight with his supplier in the tone of a quarrel both of them had enjoyed every week for years.

At the end of Tallow Lane, the Inner District wall came into view over the rooftops.

It was higher than any wall had a reason to be. Cael had five pages in his notebook about that wall, covering its height, the grade of the road on each side of it, how many street lamps stood per hundred paces on each side, and the gate. The gate mattered most, because every city in Valdris was built in rings, and the rings were built on rank. The Registry kept your classification, and your classification decided where you could go. Copper-tier got the outer districts and part of the markets. Iron-tier got the whole city in general, and the guild districts if you carried the right papers. Bronze opened the administrative core, Silver opened every district and the roads between cities, and Gold opened whatever was left, which was mostly the kind of thing people put on posters.

Hesk was Iron-tier, so he went through the Inner gate twice a month to deliver gauges, showed his registration, and was waved on, and he never talked about it, in the way people never talk about the doors that open for them.

Below Copper was Unranked, and Unranked could not legally pass that gate at all.

Most of the people Cael knew were Copper, because about six in ten practitioners settled there for life, according to the Registry's public summary; another quarter reached Iron, perhaps one in ten reached Bronze, and Silver was rarer still. Each tier had ten ranks, which you climbed by use, by doing what your Path did again and again until a registered Arbiter station evaluated you and agreed that you had climbed. Rank 10 was the doorway to the next tier, which most people never went through and some went through once.

He had read the public summary three times at the long table under the community hall's window. He had read the sections on tiers and ranks, on declarations, on district access and guild enrollment, and the part near the end, under a heading called *Non-standard designations and legacy classifications*, which was four lines long and which he had needed to read only once.

*[SHATTERED]: Classification indicating fractured Path architecture, historically associated with unstable practice and practitioner safety risk. Currently deprecated. Four (4) instances recorded in the history of the Compact registry.*

Four.

Not a percentage, and not one in a hundred thousand: four people, in the whole history of the Registry, and Cael, who wrote down everything, had not written this down.

His preliminary assessment had been in the spring. Every child in the district had one. You sat in a chair at the district office while an assessor laid two fingers on your wrist and her other hand flat on your chest and frowned at nothing for a minute, and then she wrote something down. Most children got *standard coupling*, but Cael's assessor had looked at him a moment longer than she had looked at the boy before him, and written *irregular coupling patterns*, and under it, kindly, *uncommon variation, no action required.* He had looked up *irregular coupling patterns* that afternoon, and there had not been much, except those four lines a page further on in the same summary.

He turned off Tallow Lane before the wall could fill the whole sky, and let his feet take him toward Weaver's Row.

---

The certification office on Weaver's Row had been a dye-house once, and you could still smell it when the wind was wrong. Now it had grey stone facing, the Compact Registry seal over the door, and tall windows that let in light without seeming to want it.

Alis Trent was standing outside it with her mother.

Alis lived two streets over, had turned fourteen early in the autumn and Kindled three days ago, and was holding her certificate in both hands and reading it as though it were in a language she was still learning. Her mother was crying quietly into a handkerchief. Cael looked at the mother once and then not again, because people's crying was their own.

Alis looked up when his boots scuffed the step. "Tide Path. Copper, Rank 1."

"Tide's good for healing," Cael said. It was true, so he said it.

"That's what everyone says."

"Everyone's right."

"My brother got Blade Path."

"Also good."

"He's Silver Rank 3 now."

There was a space after that, and Cael tried several things in it, and none of them fit. Tide was a real Path with steady guild demand, and in ten years Alis could be an Iron-tier healer with her own rooms and a waiting list. But Copper Rank 1 at fourteen was not the same as a Silver-tier brother with travel papers and a guild sword, and Alis was clever enough to know it. Pretending otherwise would have been an insult.

"In two years you'll have more choices than it feels like you have today," he said.

"You sound like a pamphlet," she told him.

"I'm quoting my grandfather."

"Is he right?"

Cael thought about the needle sitting still at three and a half times working load. "Usually."

Her mother had gone inside to ask the clerk about the guild enrollment inquiry. Cael knew the order of it from the procedural summary in the community hall: certificate, family notification, the residential registry updated, and then a visit to the guild of your Path to ask whether they had room.

"What does it feel like?" he asked. "Having one."

Alis considered the question properly, which he liked her for, because most people answered before they thought.

"Like being able to hear my own pulse in my hands, all the time," she said. "I touched my mother's arm yesterday and I could tell she had a headache." She frowned at the certificate. "It's not very dramatic."

"You didn't want dramatic."

"I wanted it over." She looked at him squarely. "And tomorrow it's yours."

"Yes."

"Are you worried?"

It was a direct question, and he gave it a direct answer. "Yes."

She nodded once, as if he had confirmed something she already knew. "Me too. Before. Three days ago." She looked down at the paper again. "It's—I can't explain it. Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted."

Cael took out his notebook, turned to page seven, and wrote it below the eleven descriptions, standing on the office step with the cold coming up through his boots.

*Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.*

He starred it.

"Are you writing me down?"

"Yes."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's the second star on the page."

She almost smiled, a tired, real almost-smile. "Who's the first?"

"Hesk."

"Then I'll take it." She folded her certificate in half, and then, after a moment, unfolded it and held it flat again, as though folding it had been the wrong thing to do to it. "Good luck tomorrow, Cael."

"Congratulations," he said. "On the Path."

He meant it, and he hoped she could tell.

---

Everyone in the northern quadrant knew Joren's result, so Cael did too. Joren had spent a year predicting his Kindling, starting with *something fast* and narrowing it week by week until, by midsummer, he was telling people at the market *Blade Path, Silver-track, probably Rank 3 by sixteen*. He had told Garrik, and Ressa, and even the guard on the Merchant Row gate, who had said *a door opening* and then *good luck, son* in a voice that made Cael wince. The whole quadrant had followed his certainty with the close attention people give to certainty they suspect is wrong.

The certainty had been wrong. He had come out Stone Path, Copper Rank 1.

It was not a bad Path. Cael had looked it up the day Joren Kindled, because he looked everything up, and Stone rewarded patience; it built slowly and held well, and at the higher tiers it did things with ground and weight that the guilds paid good money for. Joren had patience underneath the noise, and in ten years, if he worked, he could be Bronze, which was more of the city than most of the Outer District ever saw. But it was a place to start from, not a place to have got to, and Joren had spent a year promising everybody he would get there.

Cael found him at the low wall.

It stood at the corner where Fen Street met the canal path, waist-high, made of old grey blocks, and it held nothing up. It had been part of a building once, and the building was gone, and the wall had stayed because nobody could think of a reason to take it down. When they were seven, Cael and Joren had sat on it every afternoon arguing about which Paths they would get. Joren had always wanted Blade, while Cael had wanted whichever Path would let him make things that held up for years, had never worked out which Path that was, and had at some point stopped wanting anything in particular.

Joren was standing in front of the wall with his feet planted wide and his arms folded, wearing the fierce, private look of someone concentrating on something nobody else could see.

"You look like you're waiting for a cart to hit you," Cael said.

Joren turned only his head. His feet stayed exactly where they were. "Cael, good, good timing." He grinned, remembered not to grin too much, and grinned anyway. "Come here and push me."

"No."

"Push me as hard as you can, and I'll tell you why after."

"You'll tell me why before, or I'm going home."

Joren sighed the long, wounded sigh of a person whose friend had always been like this. "I got my declaration, my first one, Rank 1. It came with the word, the same morning." He glanced up the street and lowered his voice. "You're not supposed to show anybody."

Declarations were private; the Registry summary said so, and everybody knew it anyway. When your Path gave you an ability, your Arbiter showed it to you and only you, and sharing one was allowed. But it was the kind of thing you did with family, or with someone you would trust with a door key.

"Give me your notebook," Joren said, holding out his hand.

Cael hesitated for one heartbeat. Then he gave it over, open to a clean page near the back, and Joren took the pencil from the spine and wrote slowly, his tongue at the corner of his mouth, the way he had written everything since they were small. When he handed it back, the page said:

```
PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1
[Rooted Stance] — Passive. While both feet are planted flat, your stance resists displacement by force. The effect ends when either foot lifts.
```

Cael read it twice. "That's the exact wording?"

"Exact, because I've read it about four hundred times." Joren tapped his own temple. "It shows up right there. Not in your eyes, sort of behind them, like words written on the inside of your eyelids, except they aren't really words, and your eyelids are open." He scowled. "That's a terrible description."

"It's better than *Brief.*"

"Who said *Brief*?"

"The reference room."

"Of course she did." Joren set his hands on his hips without moving his feet. "*Passive* means it's always on. I don't do anything. I just stand, and it's there. You know what that means?"

"It means you're hard to push over."

"It means I'm *impossible* to push over." He lifted his chin. "Go on, before it gets dark."

"It's ten in the morning."

"Before it gets dark, Cael."

So Cael put the notebook on the wall, set both hands flat on Joren's chest, and pushed.

It was like pushing a doorframe. He had expected it to be hard, but he had not expected it to feel like that. Joren's body still felt like Joren's body under his hands, warm and thin, a boy's shoulders under a wool coat, and yet behind it there was something else, as if the push went into Joren and kept on going, down through his legs and into the paving stones, and the paving stones were not interested. Cael leaned and put his weight into it and dug in his heels. Joren did not move. Around his boots, the loose grit on the paving shivered once and lay flat, every grain of it, as though the ground had settled itself in the way a dog settles when it has decided it is staying.

"Huh," said Cael.

"Right? Right?" Joren was beaming, his face a little red. "I've been out here both mornings since. You have to use it to rank, that's what the Stone Guild woman said. An hour a day, feet down, let people push." His voice dropped. "Nobody else wants to push me, and my mother won't, because she says it feels like pushing the house."

Cael stepped back and looked at him. Not at his face; at his whole shape.

Joren's weight was low and wide, his knees a little bent, and everything about him was set. It was a stillness Cael had never once seen in him, because Joren had never in his life stood still for longer than it took him to think of the next thing to say. But he was talking now, about the Stone Guild woman and the hour a day and the Rank 2 threshold, which was nowhere close. She had told him, he said, that Copper Rank 1 to Rank 2 could take a year for Stone and that this was normal, and *normal* was a word he was going to have to get used to—

And as he talked, his left heel came up.

Only a little, and only at the end of each sentence, when he wanted the sentence to land. It was the bounce he had always had. At seven, telling Cael he would be a Blade Path champion, he had bounced on his toes so hard he had fallen off this exact wall.

*The effect ends when either foot lifts.*

Cael did not decide anything. He simply waited for the end of the next sentence.

"—so it's not the Path I wanted, but I'm going to be the best Rooted Stance in the whole—"

The heel came up. Cael put two fingers on Joren's shoulder and pushed, gently, as you would close a door that was nearly shut already.

Joren sat down in the street.

He sat down hard, with his mouth still open around the word he had been saying, and for a second he looked so astonished that a cold flash of worry went through Cael that he had hurt him. Then Joren began to laugh. It was not his market laugh, the loud one he used when people were listening. It was the old laugh, from the wall, from when they were seven, and it came out of him in a rush and kept coming until he was lying flat on his back in the grit with his arms flung out, wheezing at the sky.

"You—how did you—"

"Your heel."

"My *what?*"

"It comes up when you talk, at the end of a sentence, and you've always done it." Cael sat down on the wall. "The declaration says *either foot lifts.* A heel is part of a foot."

Joren stopped laughing. He raised his head off the ground and looked at his own boots as if they had betrayed him personally.

"That's not fair."

"It's in the wording."

"You read my declaration once."

"Twice."

Joren let his head fall back on the paving and lay there looking at the sky, with some of the laugh still in his face and something else coming in underneath it.

"So that's the whole Path," he said at last, very lightly, very carefully. "I'm very hard to move, as long as I don't move. Great, good, I can be a door."

Cael did not say anything clever, because there was nothing clever that would not have been worse. He got down off the wall and held out a hand, and after a moment Joren took it. Cael pulled him up easily, because Joren weighed what a fourteen-year-old weighed and the ground did not hold him at all when he did not ask it to.

"It's Rank 1," Cael said. "It's the first thing it does, not all it does. Higher ranks build on the stance, in the Stone section—ground reading, weight transfer, things that make other people's footing go bad." He brushed grit off Joren's sleeve. "And the heel's fixable. You only have to stop being you for an hour a day."

Joren snorted. Then he looked at Cael for a long moment, and the carefulness went out of his face, and what was left was only Joren.

"Nobody else would have seen that," he said. "The heel."

"You'd have seen it eventually."

"Not for about a year, and not before somebody at the guild sat me down on my backside in front of everyone." He shook his head. "You notice stuff, you've always noticed stuff, and it's really annoying."

"Thank you."

"It's not a compliment." He grinned. "It's a little bit a compliment."

He set his feet again, flat, and Cael watched the grit shiver and settle around his boots.

"Again," Joren said. "And this time I won't talk."

"You're always going to talk."

"Then I'll talk without my heel. Push me."

They did it six more times. Joren talked through four of them and kept his heels down for three. On the fourth, Cael waited him out, and Joren began a sentence about the Stone Guild woman's terrible tea, and you could see what it cost him to finish that sentence flat-footed, but the heel stayed down and he stayed up, and he crowed about it as if he had won a tournament. The sixth time, Cael pushed until his arms shook. Joren did not move, though his face went red and sweat came out at his temples, until at last he said, "Stop, stop, it's on all the time but holding it isn't free, it's like holding your breath in your legs," and Cael stopped, and Joren let go of whatever he had been holding and nearly fell over anyway, from relief.

They sat on the wall afterward, side by side, the way they used to, and Joren rubbed his trembling calves and pretended they were not trembling.

"So," Joren said. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"What do you think you'll get?"

"I don't know."

"You'll get something good, Cael." He said it with all the old certainty, as though his certainty had never once been wrong. "Something smart. You notice things, so they have to give you something that goes with that."

Down the canal path, the lock-keeper was opening the upper gate. The water leaned, and the gate sighed, and the sound went out across the rooftops like a long breath. Cael thought about four lines in a summary at the community hall.

"Maybe."

Joren looked at him sideways, and for once he did not fill the silence. He sat in it with his trembling calves and let it be there, and it was the most patient thing Cael had ever seen him do.

"Come by after," Joren said finally, "tomorrow, whatever it is, and I'll show you how to fall down properly; I've had a lot of practice these two days."

"I'll come by."

Joren slid off the wall, set his feet on the paving, and stood there, flat-footed and stubborn, in front of a wall that did not hold anything up. Cael could see in his shoulders that he meant to stand there for the whole hour.

Cael picked up his notebook and looked once more at the page near the back, at the declaration in Joren's round, careful hand. Then he closed the book over it gently, as you close a door on someone's room, and walked home the long way, and did not count anything at all.

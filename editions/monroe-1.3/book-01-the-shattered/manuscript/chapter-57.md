# Chapter 57 — The Crossing

Somebody had been at the board at the triangle in the night.

Vell's card was still in the middle of it, where the light was best, on the back of its lamp-oil bill. *LAST BOUT, SUNDAY FORTNIGHT. DARROW INNES (Bronze 1, Iron Path) against unrated (vouched).* But under it, on the bare grey wood of the old door, somebody had written in charcoal, in large uneven capitals that sloped downhill toward the cooper's shed, *HESK-WARD*. Somebody else had underlined it twice. A third person, in chalk, had added *13 TO 1* beside it, and a fourth had crossed that out and written *SHAME*.

Cael stood and looked at it on the way up, at noon, with Lira beside him and his wraps over her arm.

"Red Cap," said Lira. "He can't do a *W*. Look at it."

The *W* was two *V*s that had not quite met. Cael looked at it. In seven months the board had known him as a line in brackets, and now it had his name on it, spelt by a boy who could not do a *W*, in a hand that would wash off in the first rain.

"Come on," said Lira. "You're not fighting the board either."

---

The pie boy had been raking since first light, and he told them so at the gate in the wall, leaning on his rake with his face shining, before they were through it.

"New groove," he said. "Wider. She had me cut it a whole pace out, all the way round, and the rope after it. Then she had me rake it four times. Four. Then she came and looked at it and had me do it again." He pointed with the rake, as if they might miss it. "Benches on three walls. Trestles from the cooper. Two buckets. And three slates on the nails, look. Two men come up from Fenrow with their own chalk."

It was all true. The yard had put on a different stance in the night. The circle in the middle had grown, and its groove was fresh and dark and combed smooth, and the rope stood a pace further out on every side. Benches ran along three walls, not one, with a row of trestles behind them. On the nails by the gate there were three slates, each in a different hand, and two of the hands belonged to men Cael had never seen, sitting on upturned crates beside them with their chalk in their laps and their hats tipped forward.

He counted, because he could not help it. Sixty-three people in the yard at two hours out, most of them on benches they meant to keep. The girls along the top of the wall in a row, swinging their heels. A short queue in the lane already, with a man selling hot nuts up and down it out of a tray.

"How many?" said Lira.

"Sixty-three."

"Keep it." She was looking at the benches, not at him. "Tonight you'll want to know how many there were before."

He kept it.

The three slates all said eleven. Marrow's was on the first nail. Marrow himself was on his crate below it, writing out an over and under on how long the boy would last, and when he looked up and saw Cael going past he stopped writing, and looked at him for a moment, and put the chalk down on his knee. He folded his arms, as if there were some sums a man did not do in front of the person they were about.

---

His corner was on the shaded side by the iron stake. Somebody had swept it. He sat on the end of the nearest bench and held his hands out, and Lira knelt in front of him and began on the left wrap, and he watched the yard fill past her shoulder.

The bread woman had shut her stall. He saw her come in at the gate with a shawl over her head and her chin up, and pay, and go to the third row, where people moved along for her without being asked. She sat down with her arms folded, as if she meant to see for herself whether all this was worth a morning's bread. Sella was on the end of the second bench in her laced boots, and Torvin's wife was beside her with a cloth bundle on her knee that was almost certainly a bun, and almost certainly not for Sella. Torvin had not come; Torvin did not come to the yard. *If you're carried home, they're to bring you in by the yard door.*

Doss stood at the back on account of the knee. Renn was on the sunny side, with his barge friends round him, and when he saw Cael looking he cupped his hands round his mouth and shouted across the whole yard, "*Feet!* Not his face! His *feet!*" and his friends cheered as if he had said something clever, and Renn bowed to them. Corbin stood by the buckets with his arms folded, square, saying nothing to anybody. Amrit had come in his apron, which nobody had ever seen happen; somebody must be minding his pot. The mender was up on the wall among the girls, sitting very straight with his spectacles on, looking alarmed to be so high. The boys were everywhere at once. They were running chalk for the slates now as well as cards for Vell, threading through the crowd in two directions with white hands, shouting prices at each other as they passed.

Dessa was on the third bench, where she had sat before, with her hair tied tight. She was not looking at the circle. She was looking at the ground in front of his mark.

The old man was not there. Cael had not thought he would be. The old man stood in his gate at the top of the lane of yards on every day of the year, and whatever happened in the district went past him or did not.

"Other hand," said Lira, and he gave her the right.

Feryn found them while she was on the third turn. He came along the rope with his cold gone down into his chest and his coat buttoned to the throat, and dropped onto the bench beside Cael and coughed and took a slip of paper out of his pocket.

"Marrow's," he said, and held it up. "Eleven, first thing this morning, before he'd had his breakfast. He wrote it out with a face like a man passing a stone." He folded it carefully and put it back. "That's a coat. There's a tailor by the lower bridge has the cloth put by. Grey, with a proper collar." He plucked at his own frayed cuff. "Lose, and I'm in this one another winter, and you'll have to look at it every time I'm in town."

"I'll try not to."

"Do." Feryn leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, and the cough and the coat went out of his face, and what was left was the man who had gone down the river for a week to watch somebody else fight. "One question, and then I'll go and find a bench. What's the first exchange for?"

"Finding out whether I drew him right."

"And if you didn't?"

"Then I've got three more to find out what's there instead."

Feryn said nothing. Then he nodded, slowly.

"One other thing. The salt-flats woman. She said she didn't remember deciding to stop." He stood, and looked down at him. "Whatever you decide in there, remember deciding it."

He went off along the rope, coughing, toward the sunny side, and Renn's barge friends made room for him on a bench as if he had always sat there.

When she had tied off the right wrap, Lira took the Log out of his coat on the bench and opened it at the page he had written the night before, and held it up in front of him without a word, so that he would read it once more and not have to hold it.

*The crossing.*

*Claim. Wherever it goes in, it goes to the left foot. He nails that foot down. There is one way down on that side: low left, where the ribs end and the hip starts. Everything crosses there on its way. He has to stop each time to send it down.*

*Evidence. Three down the river (F., heard). Doss, "he'd wait" (heard). The chalk-board woman, the bucket (heard). Week two's Bronze, one place, seven exchanges (seen). A washer bill.*

*Ruling.*

There was nothing after *Ruling*. He had left the line empty on purpose, because everything above it was other people's knuckles, and there was not one thing on the page yet that had come through his own.

Lira shut the book and put it back inside his coat, and put the coat on the bench behind him.

"Look at me," she said. "Not at the book."

He looked at her.

She put her thumb on his own left side, low, under the ribs and above the hip, and pressed, not hard.

"Here," she said. "On him. Nowhere else. I don't care if he leaves his whole head open for you like a door. If he does, it's because he wants you to look at it." She took her thumb away. "If I touch my chin, he's reading your nod, and you'll stop nodding. If I put two fingers here, I'm asking. You don't answer me with your head."

"Thumb on my hip."

"Thumb on your hip." She sat back on her heels. "And the third exchange I don't want anything from. Not one thing. You stay standing. That's all the third's for."

"And after the third?"

"After the third is his," said Lira. "We've not got a say in it yet."

---

Darrow Innes came in by the gate in the wall like anybody else, and paid.

Cael saw him before the yard did, because he was watching the gate, and because nobody else was expecting a Bronze to stand in the queue. The pie boy tried to wave him through. Darrow shook his head and put a coin in the pie boy's hand and closed the boy's fingers over it, and only then came in, and the yard found out who he was a moment later and went quieter all at once.

He was not big. That was the first thing everybody seemed to notice, because the sound went down on it like a breath let out. He was of middle height and no more, and about thirty, and broad through the middle as a cask is broad, all of a piece, so that it was hard to say where his chest stopped and the rest began. His hair was cut close and was the colour of wet sand. His nose had been broken long ago and set a little to the side, and his ears were thick, and his hands were square and quiet and hung at his sides as he walked without swinging. He had a plain brown jacket on and a canvas bag over one shoulder.

He went to Vell's table first. He said something to her and she said something back, and he nodded, once, with what looked from across the yard like real courtesy. Then he went to the empty corner on the sunny side and put the bag down and took the jacket off and folded it twice and laid it on the bag. He asked the pie boy for water, and said something that made the pie boy laugh, and drank it slowly, standing, looking at nothing.

Then he looked across the circle at Cael.

It was a short look. It started at Cael's feet and stayed there longest, and came up to his hands and stopped there a moment, and only then, last of all, to his face. It went back down to the feet. Then Darrow looked away, at the groove, at the ground under his own mark, and that was all.

*Feet first,* thought Cael. *He reads feet first. Then hands. The face is last, and he hardly bothers.* He put it in the book in his head, in the margin, where he put things about other people's habits.

Lira had seen it too. She did not say anything.

At the nails, the Fenrow men had stood up off their crates. They had been watching the Bronze pay at the gate and fold his jacket and drink his water, and whatever they had seen in it, they wiped their eleven off with the sides of their fists and wrote thirteen. Marrow watched them do it. Then, after a while, without unfolding his arms any further than he had to, he did the same.

Then Vell stood up behind her table.

She did not call for quiet. She stood up and waited, and the quiet came to her, from the benches nearest her first and then the rope and then the wall and last of all the lane outside the gate, where the people who had not got in were standing on each other's toes to see in over the heads.

She said the rules for the witnesses as she always did, without raising her voice; and three hundred people leaned toward her to hear them, which was why she did not raise it. It went until somebody put a hand up, or could not go on, or she called it. Her call stood. Once a fighter had come over the rope, nobody stopped it but her. Nobody was killed in her circle. That was older than any law there was, and anybody who did not like it could go back out through the gate they had paid at.

Nobody moved.

"Darrow Innes," said Vell. "Bronze Rank 1, Iron Path. Eight years at it, for money."

The sound that went round the yard was hungry, and under the hunger a little afraid.

Then she looked down at her book, and up again, at the shaded side, at the corner by the iron stake. For seven months, at this place in the rules, she had said *unrated*, and *vouched*, and stopped there.

"Cael Hesk-ward," said Vell. "Assessed-Copper. Vouched."

It went round the yard in a different way. He heard it go. Somebody on the wall said it again, trying it, and somebody along the rope said it back, and then it was gone under the noise as if it had always been there.

She had never said it at the rope before.

He went over the rope and out across the new groove, and the circle was wider than any he had stood in, and the walk to his mark was long and was nothing. Darrow came from the other side without hurrying and stopped on his own mark a pace and a half away. Close to, he smelt of plain soap and the canvas of his bag, and he was breathing as a man breathes in a chair after supper.

"Afternoon," said Darrow.

Cael crouched, and put his hand flat on the dirt in front of his mark, and felt the yard come up through it, three hundred people standing on one floor. Then he stood.

"Begin," said Vell.

---

Neither of them moved. Three breaths went by, and in them Cael watched Darrow arrive.

It was what Feryn had shown him on the wall. The right foot came forward, light, and rested. The left went back and down, and set, and then set again, a small second tap of the heel as though to be sure of it. The hands hung loose. And the weight went down, all of it, lower and lower, until the man in front of him looked less like somebody who had walked into the yard than like something the yard had been built round, the way the old man's grey post stood in the middle of his square of dirt.

Darrow did not come. He had not come in eight years.

So Cael went to him. But he did not go in with his best.

He had thought about it on the straw for two weeks, and in the end it was Hesk's bench that had decided him, not the straw. When a housing came back from a customer cracked, Hesk did not put the whole weight on it at once to see where it broke. He put a small known weight on one joint, and watched where it went; and then on the next joint; and the next. *Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.* You traced it by loading it a little at a time, in order, and watching.

So Cael loaded Darrow Innes a joint at a time.

One to the right side of the chest, short and true, Lira's strike, the one strike he owned. He felt it land and felt it go: in, and then sideways, as if someone behind the ribs had taken it from him and passed it along to the left. One to the point of the right shoulder: in, and across, and down. One, reaching, to the jaw, which he did not expect to matter and which did not; it went down through the thick neck and across like the others. One to the belly: in, and down, and left.

Every one of them landed. His knuckles told him so through the wraps, plainly. And every one of them went away into that broad still body like a stone dropped down a well, without a sound coming back up.

There it was, then. Doss's thing. His hands said *landed* and his eyes said *nothing happened*, and the two of them disagreed in the back of his head like two people arguing in another room. *Stop believing your hands,* said Doss's flat voice, *and you're done.*

He chose his hands. They were the instrument. He could trace what they told him.

Darrow let him. He did not block, or slip, or seem to mind. Once, after the shoulder, he put out his open right hand and tapped Cael twice on the side of the head with the flat of it, lightly, the way you would tap a melon in the market, and the yard laughed. It was not a cruel laugh, but Cael felt his ears go hot. It meant *I could have, there*. Then, not hard, Darrow put the side of his foot into the outside of Cael's thigh, low, and the leg went dead to the knee for a moment, and Cael stood on it anyway and did not let the leg tell his face.

And then he put two together, close, to the low left side, where the ribs stopped and the hip began.

They were different.

It was very little. It was nothing he could have shown anybody, or said in words to anybody but Lira. But the four before had gone into Darrow the way his fist went into the straw. These two went into something that was already doing something. He thought of the floor of the reading room under the post, over the river, where you could put your palm on the boards on a spring morning and feel the water moving under the building, a long way down, all the time. That. There was something moving under that place on Darrow's side when he got there, still carrying the shoulder, or the belly, down toward a foot nailed into the dirt.

It was one feeling in two knuckles. It was not proof. But it was the first thing in the whole business that was his own.

Darrow took a long step back, without hurry, a little before the exchange need have ended, like a man ending a conversation when it has said what it came to say.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

The yard let its breath go, and the noise came up off the benches like birds off a field.

"*Five!*" somebody at the slates was shouting, delighted. "*Five on him, and he never moved his feet!*"

Cael walked back to his mark with his thigh coming back to life in pins and needles. On the way he looked at the barrel.

Lira was where she always was, with her arms folded on the top of the rope and her face giving nothing to anybody who did not know it. As he looked, she unfolded one arm and laid two fingers, lightly, against her own left side, low, under the ribs.

He did not move his head. He put his thumb against his left hip, as if it were stiff, and took it away again.

At the nails a boy went past with white hands, and the chalk squeaked, and the first slate said fifteen.

---

In the second exchange Darrow stopped giving anything away.

He did not get faster. He did not seem to get anything. He simply began to walk forward, and kept walking, with the right foot doing all of it and the left coming after and setting, every step, tap and tap again, as if every new piece of the yard had to be nailed down before he would stand on it. He walked Cael backward across the circle, and Cael went, because there was nothing in front of him to go through. In six steps Cael's back was a forearm's length from the rope by the iron stake, and three hundred people were very quiet.

And then Darrow began to hit him, and he did it the way the Bronze in Cael's second week had done it to the Stone woman. He hit him in one place.

It was the right side, low, under the arm. A short one, with no reach in it at all. Then the same place. Then, when Cael had got his elbow down over it, the same place again, under the elbow, so that the elbow itself was driven into his own ribs. They were not big. None of them was meant to end anything. They were meant to add. Cael understood that with a kind of cold amusement even while they were landing, because he had spent two weeks on the straw learning to do the same thing, and here was a man who had been born knowing it. *They do it themselves,* he had said to Lira at the post. *It's how their own bodies work.* He had not thought that he would be the post.

It hurt. It hurt more each time, and it would go on hurting more, and that was the point of it.

So he went for the crossing from the rope.

There was only one way to it from there. He had to come up under Darrow's right arm, while it was busy with his ribs, and go across the front of the man to reach the left side, which meant going past the right hand on the way and taking whatever it gave him. He did it. He took a short one on the forearm that rang the bone from the wrist to the elbow, right over the old burn, and he knew that by the evening it would be the colour of a plum. He put a short strike into the low left side on his way across, the third of the afternoon. Then he was out along the rope, and Darrow was turning after him, and the left foot came off the floor and went down again, *tap*, and *tap*.

And Cael put the next one in on the second tap.

He had not planned it. He had seen it on the straw, perhaps, without knowing he had seen it, in a hundred afternoons of the chalk-board woman's bucket and Doss's polite Iron man. The second tap was the setting down. That was when the left foot took it all, whatever was coming down the road. If there was a road. If anything was carrying anything.

So he went on doing it. He let Darrow walk him, and every time the left foot came up and went down and tapped, he put one into the low left side on the tap, short and dull and true, from whatever angle he could get, whatever it cost. He did not try to make any of them count. He could not have made any of them count if he had tried; most of them came out of a scramble, thrown going backward or going sideways along the rope, with Darrow's short ones landing in his own right side in return, add, add, add. One was a knee in a clinch he should never have let happen. One was an elbow as Darrow shoved him off, ugly and short, the kind of strike Lira would have made him do again. Nobody watching could have thought it was a plan. It did not feel like a plan from inside it. It felt like a boy being beaten slowly against a rope, and flailing.

He was counting them. He had been counting since the first two, because counting was the one thing he could do in there without spending anything. Five. Six.

On the sixth the place was slow.

The others had gone quiet under his knuckles as quickly as a door shuts. The sixth did not. The sixth arrived and found something still moving underneath; the hum under the boards was going when he got there, and went on going for half a beat after. Something in there was still carrying the fifth down to the floor when the sixth came in on top of it.

He did not let himself think about it. There was no room to think. A part of his head that was not doing anything else wrote *Evidence* in a column, very small, and moved the empty line beside *Ruling* along by about the width of a pencil, and was quiet.

The seventh he put in going off the rope at last, sideways along it, out from under Darrow's arm, as he ducked a short one to the head. The same place.

And Darrow stopped.

He did not stagger. He did not do anything that the yard could see as a thing being done. He only stopped walking, in the middle of a step, with his right foot forward and his left foot set, and stood there a moment. Then he took his own left hand, without any hurry, and laid it flat against his own left side, low, under the ribs, as a man in a crowd lays his hand on the place where his purse is, not because he thinks it has gone but because he has felt something and wants to be sure.

He took the hand away. He looked at Cael. It was the first time that afternoon he had looked at his face first.

"You've found yourself a place," said Darrow quietly. His voice did not reach the rope; it was not meant to. "Some of them do. The ones that think." He shook his head, slowly, without any unkindness in it at all. "There's nowhere on me that's further from the ground than anywhere else, son. All of it goes down. All of it."

Cael did not answer. He had no breath to answer with, and nothing he wanted to spend it on.

*Yes,* he thought. *All of it goes down.*

*All of it goes down through one place.*

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

# Chapter 17 — About Speed

"Petra Voss," said Vell, at the table, on the morning after Amrit. "Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Sunday, second bout." She looked at his arm. "That'll be well enough by Sunday. Hers won't be your problem. Hers'll be the speed."

He went from the table to the low wall at the side of the yard, where Lira was sitting with her knees drawn up, waiting for the shadow to reach the post. She had the look of somebody who has been saving a piece of news since the night before and has finally decided she cannot hold it any longer.

"There's a wager on you," she said.

He looked at his forearm. It had come up in a long red patch over the old scrape, shiny and tight, with a blister the size of a thumbnail near the wrist that he was not breaking, because everybody had told him not to. He had slept with it outside the blanket.

"Marrow's always got a wager on me."

"Not Marrow." Her mouth was doing something at one corner that she was plainly trying to stop. "You know the two errand boys who work the east lanes? One's got a red cap. The other one's got a brother in the carters'?"

Everybody had seen them. They ran messages and small parcels from the river up to the yards and down again, in competition with each other, all day, at a dead sprint, shouting abuse at each other across whatever street they happened to be crossing. He had once watched the red-capped one leap a handcart rather than go round it, because the other one was going round it.

"They hate each other. Properly, since they were about six. And now they've got a bet on how many more bouts you'll need before your first win. Red Cap says two. The other one says never." She looked at him. "They've each put half a mark on it, which is more than either of them earns in a day. They told the pie boy, and the pie boy told the woman at the water bucket, and the woman at the water bucket told me."

"Which of them's going to win?"

"Red Cap, I think. He hates the other one more than he loves money, so he picked the opposite of whatever the other one said." She considered. "That's not a good reason to back somebody. But it's not the worst reason anybody's ever backed you."

Cael looked at the post for a moment. "I don't like it."

"I didn't think you would."

"I didn't come here to be talked about."

"You came here to fight in a yard in front of sixty people every week, and you keep lasting longer than anybody thought you would." She said it quite gently. "That's what being talked about is made of. You can't do one without the other. Not here."

He had known that without letting himself know it all the way. Not three weeks ago, standing on the rise above this valley, he had written *Show nobody anything* and underlined *still here*, and since then he had walked into a circle three times with a crowd round it.

Now a fish man nodded at him, and two boys he had never spoken to had put a day's wages on him, and somewhere at a water bucket a woman he did not know was telling people what he was.

"In Denvash I was the boy who wrote things down. That was all anybody knew about me. Garrik knew me, and Ressa, and that was a whole street, and it took fourteen years."

"This district watches its fighters the way farmers watch the sky. Nobody loves the fighters. It's that news out of the circuit is worth something, and anything worth something gets passed round. You can't stop it." She let her feet down off the wall. "The only thing you get any say in is what story they tell."

"What story are they telling?"

"Right now? The boy who keeps losing and won't stop coming back, and takes longer to put down every time." She shrugged. "It's a good story. People like it. It's the kind they cheer for when it finally comes good."

"What would be worse?"

Lira did not answer for a moment. She was looking at the post, where the sun had just touched the top of the canvas.

"The boy who's dangerous," she said, "and nobody knows why."

She said it lightly, but with great precision, every word set down in its place, and he understood that she had not made it up just now. She had been carrying it, and he thought he knew which night she had picked it up, and which exchange.

She stood up before he could say anything, and held out a hand. "Come on. One arm today. You can't catch with the burnt one."

He wrote it that night in the front of the Log, because it was a fact to be managed and not a fear to be sat with, and he wanted it to stay that way.

*Being watched is information other people get to use. Losing is a story. When I stop losing, it will be a different story, and I need to know which one before it starts.*

---

In the afternoon of that same day, on the cooper's roof, Lira told him about Petra Voss much as Vell had. She did not draw anything on the tar this time. She sat with her arms round her knees and looked out at the first wall of the Ranked core going up the hill in the haze.

"She's seventeen. She's been in the circuit a year and a half. She's very good and she knows it, and she doesn't talk much, and she hits like a door slamming in a high wind."

"Force."

"Force at Copper isn't strength, not really, not the way people think." Lira held up her hand and made a short, flat push with it at the empty air. "It's more as if everything she hits gets an extra shove at the end, after her hand's already stopped, as though somebody behind you kicked you at the same moment she hit you. You've seen it. You said you saw a plank jump out of somebody's hands on your first day in the lanes."

He had: a woman throwing a short punch at a plank held up by her partner, and the plank leaving his hands as though it had been kicked from behind. He had written *Force, or something like it.*

"That was her?"

"That's her yard. She trains there every afternoon." Lira glanced at him. "Go and watch her if you like. It won't help."

"Why not?"

"Because she's quicker than you, and watching her won't make you quicker." She said it without any unkindness at all, just as she had said *that's not a skip, that's a hole*. "Whatever's there, you'll see it. You'll see her lace her boots and know which foot she favours; I'm not worried about your seeing. But you'll see things and not have time to do anything about them. That's what she does to people. She doesn't hide anything. She doesn't have to."

He went and watched her anyway, that afternoon, from the lane outside her gate. She was small, smaller than Lira, wiry and freckled, with her reddish hair in one tight braid down her back, and she was working alone at a sand-bag on a chain, hitting it with short, straight punches that did not look like much.

Every time she hit it, it jumped. It did not swing back and forth as a sack swings when you punch it; it leapt away from her fist on its chain as if somebody much larger had hit it, and hung at the end of its swing for a moment, and came back.

He watched her for an hour and wrote two pages, and on the way back down the lane his hands were not quite steady, and he could not tell whether it was the hour of watching or the thought of Sunday.

---

The next morning, on the way up to the Cinder House, they passed the gate of the old man's yard. The old man was in his corner in the shade, in the stance that was like nothing else in the lane, and the woman with the shoulder was there too, going through her sequence alone. She reached the fourth position and held it, and her shoulder stayed down, and she went on to the fifth.

The old man's pale eyes came round to Cael as they went by, went to the red forearm, and came back to his face.

"Six," the old man said, and his eyes went back to the wall.

Lira said nothing until they were past the next gate. Then she said, "Four months, and he's never said a number to me either," in a voice that was trying hard to sound offended and not quite managing.

---

On Sunday the yard was fuller than it had been for Brenna or for Amrit.

He noticed it when he climbed onto the barrel. The rope was two deep on the sunny side and three deep in the shade.

There were faces in the crowd he had begun to know: the fish man with his beard; the two errand boys, standing as far apart from each other as the yard would allow and both glaring; the woman who slept in her boots, with a pie; and, near the gate with her arms folded, not Torvin but Torvin's wife.

He found he did not want to know how many of them had come for the second bout.

On Marrow's slate the line had moved again. *Over-under: four exchanges.*

And Marrow, for the first time, looked at him.

It was only a glance. Marrow was going along the rope with his chalk and his pleasant voice and his clean hands, taking coins, and as he came level with the barrel his eyes went up from the slate to Cael's face and rested there for the length of a breath. It was a perfectly friendly look, the look a shopkeeper gives a scale that has begun, for no reason anybody can find, to weigh against him.

Then Marrow smiled and went on.

"Don't," said Lira quietly.

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. Keep not doing it."

"Second bout," said Vell.

He stepped over the rope.

Petra was already on her mark, looking not at the crowd or at Vell but at him, steadily, with a calm, narrow interest. She was standing very lightly, with her weight forward on the balls of her feet and her hands up in front of her face, loose. She looked like somebody waiting for a door to open so that she could go through it first.

"Petra Voss. Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause. "Begin when you're ready."

The first exchange taught him how little he knew.

She came off her mark so fast that he was still deciding which way to go when she arrived. A short straight punch came at his face. He got his right forearm up in time, barely, and caught it on the outside, the way Lira had taught him a hundred times in the grey half of the yard.

The catch held. Then the shove came.

It came a heartbeat after her fist had stopped, out of nowhere, as if somebody behind his arm had put a shoulder into it, and it drove his forearm back into his own face and threw him off his feet. He went back three skidding steps and sat down hard on the packed dirt inside the groove.

He was up again before he had finished thinking about it, which was the one thing he did right in the first exchange. The rope laughed. Petra did not. She was already back on her mark, waiting, with her hands loose in front of her face.

*There's no reset,* he thought, on his own mark, with his forearm ringing. *There's no time for one. You catch it and the shove comes before you've finished catching.*

In the second exchange he saw the lean.

It was very small, and he would have missed it if he had not spent an hour in the lane watching her hit a sand-bag on a chain.

Before a punch she meant, a punch she was putting the shove into, her shoulders tipped forward. Not her head. Not her hands. The shoulders, both together, perhaps the width of a finger, as if she were leaning into a wind only she could feel. Her jabs did not have it. Her feints did not have it. Only the ones she meant.

He saw it for the first time halfway through the second exchange. He saw the shoulders tip, and he knew what was coming, and he started to move.

The punch hit him anyway.

It caught him on the left shoulder, the Renn shoulder, because he had moved, and moving had taken his face out of the way. But it had not taken the rest of him. The shove came after the fist and spun him half round, and he stayed up, and stood there with the shoulder singing and understood something that he did not like at all.

He had seen it. He had seen it clearly, a long way off, the way he had seen the dip in Brenna's shield and the shimmer in front of Amrit's hands. And the seeing had bought him almost nothing. It had bought him the difference between being hit in the face and being hit in the shoulder. She was simply faster than his seeing.

He saw the lean three more times before the end of the exchange. Each time he started to move the moment he saw it. Each time he was hit, or nearly hit, or hit a little less hard. Once he got all the way clear and heard the shove go past his ear like a slammed door in the next room. Once he did not get clear at all.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

In the third exchange he tried moving earlier.

He reasoned it as he stood on his mark. If the lean came and he moved and she was still quicker, then he had to move before the lean finished. He had to move on the very start of it, the first tilt of the shoulders, before it had become a lean at all.

She came in. Jab, jab, nothing in them. Then the shoulders began to tip.

He went. He went on the very first tilt, so early that he was moving before he was sure, and her real punch went past his cheek into the empty air where his face had been. It was the first time all night she had missed with one she meant.

The rope noticed. He heard it notice.

So did Petra.

In the fourth exchange she did something new. She leaned, and he went on the lean, and her punch was not where his face had been. It was where his face was going. It met him coming the other way. It caught him on the side of the jaw, not hard, a glancing blow, and the shove came after it, and he went down on one knee and his teeth clicked together and he tasted blood where he had caught the inside of his lip.

He got up. He looked at her across the circle, and she looked back at him, with the same calm, narrow interest she had had at the start.

She did not know about the lean. He was almost sure of that. Her face had no idea that her shoulders were telling him anything.

But she knew that he was moving before her punches came. She had seen it in the third exchange. And she had done what Renn had done with the sweep. She had not tried to find out how he knew. She had simply stopped hitting the place he was and started hitting the place he would be.

*She's learning faster than I am,* he thought. *She's learning me while I'm learning her, and she's quicker at that too.*

The fifth exchange he tried everything he owned.

He tried Brenna. He gave ground along the groove and let her follow, and waited for her to get angry, so that she would get bigger and slower and louder. She did not get angry. The longer he ran, the calmer she became, and the calmer she became the quicker she was.

He tried Amrit. He watched her breath for the moment it stopped. It never stopped. She breathed in short easy puffs through her nose all the way through every punch, like a girl running for a cart she knew she would catch.

He tried the catch. He caught one punch she meant, square on the forearm, and held, and the shove came, and he leaned into it with everything he had, and it moved him anyway, a full pace backward, his heels plowing two short furrows in the dirt.

Nothing he owned was quick enough. Every piece of it worked. Every piece of it was a half-beat too slow.

The sixth exchange he lost by inches, over and over. He saw the lean and moved and was nearly clear. He saw the lean and moved and was clipped. He saw the lean and moved and she was waiting where he moved to, and he saw her waiting and changed his mind and moved again, and her second punch, which had no lean in it at all, only a jab, caught him under the ribs on the right side, the Renn side, and stopped his breath for a moment.

He was tiring, and he could feel it; his feet were slower than they had been in the first exchange and his hands were lower. She was not tiring at all.

The only thing he saw her pay was a small one: between exchanges she shook out her right hand at the wrist, once, as though the shove she put into other people came back a little way into her own arm.

At the rope the noise had gone strange. Some of it was the noise for a beating, the low pleased murmur of people watching what they had paid for. But under it there was something else. He heard somebody say "Seven," and somebody else say "He won't," and the first say "He will," and he understood that they were betting on whether he would last another exchange.

Vell called the end of the sixth.

He stood on his mark and breathed. He did not look at the rope. He looked at Petra's shoulders.

*I can see it,* he thought. *I can see all of it. I just can't get there.*

"Begin," said Vell.

The seventh exchange was very short.

She came. She jabbed, twice, nothing in either one. He let them go past and watched the shoulders. They began to tip. He went, on the first tilt, as early as he had ever gone. He went left, not right, because she had been waiting on his right for two exchanges. He did everything right. He would go over it afterward, line by line, and he would not find a single thing he had done wrong.

Her palm was there anyway.

It was not a fist. It was the heel of her open hand, flat, driven straight into his middle, below the ribs, and it arrived before he had finished going left. It arrived as if she had known he would go left before he did. And then the shove came after it, and it was the biggest shove of the night, and it lifted him.

He was in the air. Then he was not. He came down on his left hip across the groove, half in the circle and half out, and his face hit the packed dirt, and he lay there with his mouth full of the taste of earth and blood and no air in him anywhere.

"Called," said Vell. "Force Path."

---

He reported to the table with his lip swelling and his hip a single deep ache and his breath still coming back to him in pieces.

Vell wrote, and blotted it, and looked at him, at the swelling lip and at the way he was standing on one leg.

"Seven," she said.

He nodded. He could not have said anything just then without his voice doing something he did not want it to do.

"Four," said Vell. "Five. Six. Seven." She said each number flatly, as if reading them out of the book, which perhaps she was. "Every time one more." She closed the ledger and laid her hand on the cover. "You'll come to the end of that sooner or later. Everybody does."

"What happens then?"

"Then you'll have to do something besides last. Go and get your lip seen to."

Petra was at the water bucket. She had a cup in each hand, and she held one of them out to him, which surprised him so much that he took it without thinking.

"What were you looking at?"

He drank, and the water stung his lip. "You."

"No." Petra shook her head, once, so that her braid swung. "Everybody looks at me. You were looking at something. From the second exchange. You kept moving before I'd started." She frowned at him, not angrily. It was the frown of somebody looking at a lock she had not seen before. "What was it?"

He thought about the Log. *Never tell anybody their tell unless I'm sure I don't need it.* He had written it the night he told Lira about her left foot and watched her take it away from him in an hour. He was not sure he did not need this one. He was fairly sure he would need it very badly, one day, in this circle, against this girl.

"I don't know," he said. "I was just watching."

Petra gave him the narrow, considering look a moment longer. He could see that she did not believe him, and he could see her decide that it did not matter yet.

"You're quick at it. The watching. Quicker than anybody I've fought." She drank her own water. "Not quick enough."

"No," he agreed.

"That's all right," said Petra Voss, quite seriously. "Nobody is, the first time." And she put her cup back on the bucket's edge, and walked off to her friends at the gate, and did not look back.

---

Lira had a wet cloth for his lip and nothing to say about the bout, and he was grateful for both. She walked him home through the dark by the long way round the market, where it was quieter, at his own pace, which was slow because of the hip, and at the well with the iron cover she stopped.

"You saw her shoulders."

He looked at her.

"From the second exchange. Before every real one. Your eyes went to her shoulders." She was not asking. "I've fought her twice. I never saw it."

"She doesn't know she does it."

"No. I don't think she does." Lira looked at him in the dark. "You didn't tell her."

"No."

"Good." And then, after a moment, as if it had surprised her to say so: "Good." She turned toward the four lanes. "Cold on the hip. Not long. Count it."

*5. Petra Voss. Copper R3, Force Path. Called, seventh exchange. Down on the left hip.*

*Claim: before a punch she means, her shoulders lean forward, about a finger. Jabs and feints don't have it. Evidence: second through seventh exchanges, every committed strike I could see. Ruling: watch the shoulders.*

*Claim: she doesn't know she does it. Evidence: her face; she asked me what I was looking at. Ruling: don't tell her.*

*Claim: the shove comes after the hit. You can catch the fist and the shove still moves you. Evidence: first exchange, fifth exchange. Ruling: don't catch her. Don't be there.*

*Claim: when I moved early she moved her target to where I'd be. Evidence: fourth exchange, and the seventh. Ruling: she learns in the bout, as fast as I do or faster.*

He stopped there and looked at the column of claims, and then wrote one more line across the bottom of the page, under all three columns, because it did not belong to any one of them.

*I'm losing on speed now. Not on seeing.*

He closed the front of the book and opened the back, and then he sat with the pencil until the lamp had burned down a finger's width.

*Nought and four. Four, five, six, seven.*

*I can see everything now. I saw the heel, I saw the dip, I saw the shimmer, I saw the shoulders. And tonight I saw every single one of the things she did, a long way off, and I still couldn't get there.*

*Hesk wrote: your body is going to learn before your mind does. I think that's backwards for me. My mind's ahead. My mind's in the right place every time, and my body gets there after, and after is no use.*

*I don't know how you make a body faster. I don't think watching does it.*

He looked at that last line and nearly crossed it out, because it frightened him, and he did not cross it out, because it was true.

*Find out how,* he wrote under it. *Lira knows. Ask her.*

He put the book under his pillow and tried to lie on his right side, because the left hip would not have him, and his ribs on the right would not have him either. In the end he lay on his back under the beam, and Torvin's voice said *Lamps* through the floorboards, as it did every night, and the last line of light under the door went out.

Seven exchanges: he had lasted longer than he had ever lasted and seen more than he had ever seen, and he had never felt further from being able to do anything about it.

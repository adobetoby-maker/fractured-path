# Chapter 8 — Watching's Not Nothing

The brothers were gone before it was light. Cael heard them go, a great deal of whispering that was louder than talking would have been, and a boot dropped on the boards and complained at in a mutter he could not make out, and then the door. When he opened his eyes properly the window had turned grey, the wheezing man was still asleep with his mouth open, and Yeni's cot was empty and already made, its blanket folded into a square so exact that it looked like a joke about blankets.

He dressed under the coat, the way you dress in a room that is not yours, and went down the turning stairs to the kitchen.

It was a long, low room at the back of the house, with a black range at one end that somebody had already lit. A table scarred with knife marks ran down the middle, and at the far end a door stood open on a yard with a pump in it.

A woman was sitting at the end of the table lacing a pair of heavy boots. From the creases in her skirt and the state of her hair it was plain that she had slept in her clothes, and probably in the boots too, and had only taken them off to put them back on more tightly. She nodded to him without stopping. At the range, a broad, grey-haired woman in an apron was scraping out the bottom of a big iron pot with a wooden spoon.

"There's a bowl of this left," she said, without turning round. "It's yesterday's oats, so it's free, but if you burn the pot you scrub it."

"Thank you."

"I'm his wife." She tipped the spoon toward the ceiling, toward the house and the man who owned it. "He'll tell you he owns the place. He owns the sign."

The woman in boots snorted into her laces.

Cael ate the oats standing at the counter by the yard door, where he could see the pump and the wall and a square of sky over the wall that was turning from grey to white. Then he took the pouch out of his coat and counted it, all of it, on the counter, in small stacks of marks and half-marks, twice.

It came out the same both times: one week at Torvin's gone, one night at the waystation, a bowl of beans and half a loaf. What was left would last, though not comfortably and not long enough for him to stop counting it, so long as he did not waste it, and wasting things was not one of the skills he had ever needed to practise. He wrote the figure in the back of the notebook and drew a line under it.

"You count like Torvin," said the woman in boots, standing up and stamping her heels down into the leather.

"He counts three times. I only count twice."

"Give it a month," she said, and went out through the yard.

Yeni came in from the yard with a basin while he was putting the pouch away, and she stopped in the doorway and looked at his bag, which he had brought down to repack.

"Your strap's going."

He looked. Across the strap, where it lay against his hip, there was a pale scuffed band he had last seen at the bottom of a dip in the road, between two hedges, with a turnip lying in the grass at his feet. Overnight the scuff had begun to fray. Two threads stood up out of the weave like hairs.

"It caught on something," he said. "On the road."

"It'll catch on something else and go." Yeni set the basin down, went upstairs, and came back with the sewing kit before he had worked out whether he was supposed to say anything. She sat down at the table, took the strap across her knee, threaded a needle by feel without looking at the eye, and laid a row of small tight stitches across the frayed band and back again. It took less time than he took to tie a boot.

"There," she said, biting the thread. "Now you owe me a favour."

"What kind of favour?"

"I haven't decided. I'll collect it when I need it, and you won't like it, and you'll do it anyway." She put the needle away. "That's how favours work here. Do they work differently where you come from?"

Cael thought about Ressa's extra loaf, which she had not let anyone pay for, and Garrik's afternoon of wheel bearings when he was eight.

"Mostly people didn't collect."

"Then they were either very rich or very soft." Yeni shut the kit. "Which?"

"Neither. They'd known me since I was born."

She looked at him for a moment, and something in her face went less brisk without becoming anything else. "Well," she said, "nobody here has. So you'll owe me." She stood up with the kit under her arm. "If you're posting that letter, the carriers are at the bottom of the fish steps, by the lower bridge. The west bag goes at noon."

He had not said anything about posting a letter. He thought back over the evening, and found the place: she had seen him write, and asked who to, and he had said his grandfather, and she had heard *letter* and *west* in that and filed them.

*She watches,* he thought. *She just doesn't write it down.*

He went to the market first, because the market was on the way and because he had thought about the boy's argument while he was falling asleep and had found it held.

The nut stall was open. The sister was building the charcoal up under the drum, and the boy was sitting on an upturned crate eating an apple. When he saw Cael coming down the row he finished the apple in three bites, threw the core over the awning into the next row, and stood up as if a bell had rung.

"You lost a day," he said.

"I know. I've come to buy the rest of the week."

"Price of a twist of nuts." The boy held up a hand. "Plus the bit."

Cael paid it. The bit turned out to be about half the price of the nuts again, and he paid it without arguing, because the sister had told him not to and he wanted to see what happened when he did. What happened was that the boy took the coins, looked at them, looked at his sister, and put one of the smaller ones back in Cael's hand.

"For not haggling," he said. "Nobody never haggles. It's unnatural."

"It's nobody *ever* haggles," said the sister to the charcoal.

"That's what I said."

Then he talked, and Cael had been right to pay. He talked fast and without stopping, and pointed with a paper twist as he went, and what he gave Cael was not a list but a map, with people on it instead of streets. The bread woman at the top of the second row sold yesterday's loaves with this morning's on top, so you asked for the one from underneath and watched her face.

The man with the pies was honest about the meat and dishonest about the weight, and the woman with the pies two stalls along was the other way round, and the trick was to buy pies from her and gravy from him. The fish was good if the fishmonger was the one with the beard and bad if it was the one with the hat, because they were cousins and shared a stall and only one of them knew anything about fish. The fruit woman by the steps gave short weight to anyone who called her *auntie* and full weight to anyone who called her by her name, and she had never told anybody her name.

"So what do you call her?"

"Nothing. You point." He pointed, to demonstrate. "She likes that best."

Cael had the notebook out by then and was writing as fast as he could, and the boy watched him do it with the deep, uncomplicated satisfaction of a craftsman whose work was being taken seriously.

"And past the market," Cael said, when the boy ran out of stalls. "The rest of it. What's where."

"That's not the week. That's extra."

"Then I'll find it myself."

The boy weighed this, and then shrugged, in the manner of somebody giving away a thing he had never been sure he could sell. "Houses are all round here, this side. That's where people sleep. Down by the water are the workshops, people who fix things and make things, the ones who can't pay what they charge for a bench across the river. Up north, where the ground starts going up again, that's the yards. Fighters, mostly. People training." He sniffed. "Some of them are good. Most of them shout."

"And?"

"And there's the other market." His voice did not drop, but it slowed down. "South of here. It isn't a market, really. It's people standing about. You can buy things there that you couldn't buy anywhere with a wall round it. Gear. Old stuff. Things nobody asks where they came from." He looked at Cael. "And answers. There's a woman who sells answers."

"What kind of answers?"

"Whatever kind you pay for." He picked up his scoop. "Don't buy from her unless you really want to know. People always think they want to know. Then they find out."

His sister turned the crank on the drum, and the nuts inside rattled round like rain on a roof, and she said, without looking at either of them, "That one's free. He tells everyone that one."

---

He found the carriers at the bottom of a flight of broad stone steps that went down to the water, slick with fish scales and river weed, beside the lowest of the bridges. It was a stone hut with a counter open to the street, two sorting tables inside, and a row of heavy canvas bags hanging on hooks along the back wall, each with a direction painted on it in tall black letters. WEST was the third one along. A young man with ink on his fingers took the letter, read the address aloud in a flat voice, weighed it on a little brass balance, and named a price that made Cael wince and pay without saying anything.

"About a week," the clerk said, putting the letter in the bag. "Bit more if the roads are bad. Bit less if they're not."

"And letters coming back. Do I come here for them?"

"Torvin's?" The clerk glanced at the address again. "No. Runner takes them round. He knows Torvin's." He looked at Cael for the first time. "Everybody knows Torvin's."

So it was done. The letter was in a canvas bag on a hook. The bag would go up onto a carrier's cart at noon and out along the merchant road, past the waystation and the dip in the hedges, and in about a week it would be in Hesk's hands. Cael stood on the bottom step with the river going by at his feet and felt the weight of that more than he had expected to. It was as if he had put down something he had been carrying since the waystation without noticing how heavy it was.

The bridge went up from beside the carriers' hut in a long stone hump, wide enough for two carts, with a low parapet on either side. He climbed it as far as the top of the hump, where he could see the far end, and stopped there with his elbows on the parapet like a boy watching the river, which, as far as anyone could tell, was all he was.

At the far end of the bridge was the gate he had watched close the night before.

By daylight it was smaller than it had looked by lamplight, a stone arch in a stone wall with two guards on the near side of it and a little roofed booth where a clerk sat with a ledger. Everyone who crossed the bridge stopped at the booth. Everyone held out a card. The clerk looked at each card, and looked at the face above it, and either nodded or did not.

Cael watched them for a long time, and he counted.

A woman with a basket showed a card and was nodded through. Copper, he guessed, from the quickness of it; the outer ring was Copper's, and the clerk barely looked. A man in a guild coat showed his and was nodded through faster still, and walked on up the road toward the second wall without breaking his stride. Iron, Cael thought, or better, and going higher than the outer ring. Two porters with a handcart showed theirs and were let through to unload in the shadow of the arch, and no further, and they seemed to know exactly where the line was and did not step over it.

Then a young man in a patched jacket came up to the booth with a sack on his shoulder and held out a card, and the clerk looked at it and shook his head.

The young man said something. The clerk shook his head again, not unkindly, and pointed back along the bridge. One of the guards shifted his weight from one foot to the other, nothing more than that, but the young man saw it and stopped arguing. He turned round with his sack and walked back over the bridge toward the Unranked side, past Cael on the hump, with his face set and his eyes on the stones.

Cael watched him go down the steps toward the fish market.

*Unranked can't legally pass.* He had read it in the community hall, at the long table, and copied it into a notebook in the careful hand he used for facts. Copper got the outer districts. Iron got the city and, with papers, the guild districts. Bronze got the administrative core. Silver got everything and the roads between cities. It had been a table in a summary, and he had learned it the way you learn a table, by its columns.

Now he had watched it work on a person, and it was not a table. It was a man with a sack walking back the way he had come.

He took the registration card out of his coat and looked at it. Name, number, district of issue. On the classification line, where Pellin's stamp had come down, there was one word in square brackets, and nothing else: no tier, no rank, no Path. He thought about holding it out to the clerk at the booth, and he thought about the clerk's face, and he put the card away again without getting any closer to the gate than the top of the hump.

He did not need to try it. He could see where the line was from here.

---

The workshops along the river were all doors.

Every building on the waterfront had its ground floor opened up to the street, shutters folded back or taken down altogether, and inside each one somebody was making or mending something with the light coming in over their shoulder. A cobbler sat in the first doorway with a last between his knees and a mouthful of nails.

A woman in the next one had a dozen lanterns hanging from her ceiling, all of them broken in different ways, and was soldering a hinge on a thirteenth with a fine iron that she warmed between her palms. There was a cooper, and a man who made nothing but buckets, and a woman with a loom so large it filled her whole shop and pushed her out to work on the doorstep.

At the end of the row, in a doorway with no sign at all, a man was mending something that Cael could not at first identify. It turned out to be the iron frame of a mangle, the kind used for wringing out laundry, bent in two places as though somebody had dropped a cart on it. The man had it clamped to a bench with blocks of wood under it, and he was bringing it back to true by hand with a long lever and a straightedge, a little at a time. He would lean on the lever, then lay the straightedge along the frame and squint down it, then lean again. He did not hurry and he did not stop.

Cael stood in the street and watched him longer than he meant to, because it was the bracket on the rig again. The man was not guessing. Every time he leaned on the lever he checked afterward what he had done, and when he had done too much he leaned back the other way, a little less, and checked again. On the shelves behind him stood a cartwheel with three spokes gone, a clock with no hands, and a child's wooden horse with one leg missing and the other three very carefully mended.

On the doorframe, at eye level, somebody had cut four words into the wood with a knife and filled them in with black paint.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE*

That was all. Cael read it twice and could not decide whether it was the beginning of a sentence or the whole of one, and whether it meant the thing or the person who had brought it. He thought that perhaps the man had cut it that way on purpose so that he would not have to decide either.

The man laid the straightedge along the frame one more time, squinted down it, and grunted. Then he looked up and saw Cael in the street, and looked at him the way he had looked at the frame, checking.

"Something of yours broken?" he said.

"No," Cael said. "I was just watching."

The man nodded, as if that were an answer he got often enough not to mind it, and went back to the lever.

---

The ground began to rise toward the north edge of the district, and as it rose the houses gave way to walls, and the walls had yards behind them, and the yards were full of people hitting things.

That was how it looked at first, from the street: noise and dust and the flat smack of hands on canvas, coming over the walls from every side. The yards were rented by the hour, according to a chalked board at the mouth of the first lane, which also gave the prices and, underneath them, a line that said NO PATH DAMAGE TO WALLS — YOU PAY. Some yards were bare dirt with a post in the middle, and some had a roof on poles at one end for the rain. One had a long row of straw targets along the back wall, most of them burnt black in the middle, and another had nothing in it but a single heavy chain hanging from a beam with a sack of sand at the bottom of it, which a woman was punching with her eyes closed.

Cael walked slowly along the lane and looked through the gates.

He had come here meaning to watch, and he found that he could do more than that now. He could read some of it. A young man in the second yard was throwing strikes at a post, and every strike started in his hips and his feet and arrived at the post a quarter of a second after his shoulder had told it to. Cael saw the quarter-second each time, and thought of a grey picture on a sheet and Hesk's voice in the dark: *the hips have to go where the weight goes.* A pair of girls in the next yard were drilling a block, one striking and one turning the strike aside with her forearm, over and over. The one who was blocking had learned to watch the striker's back foot instead of her hand, and the one who was striking had not yet noticed.

Paths were everywhere, if you knew what to look for. In one yard a man stood perfectly still with his feet flat while a friend shoved him, and the dust around his feet shivered once and lay flat, and Cael thought of Joren with a sudden sharpness that surprised him. *Stone.* In another a woman threw a short punch at a plank held up by her partner, and the plank did not break so much as jump out of his hands as if it had been kicked from behind. Force, or something like it. Nobody here wore a Path on a badge, and nobody needed to. They wore it in their shoulders and their feet.

He stopped at the gate of a yard near the top of the lane where a woman was working through a stance sequence alone. It was a slow sequence, six positions, each one held for a breath before she flowed into the next, and in the fourth position her right shoulder came up a finger's width too high.

She knew it, too: she would reach the fourth position, feel it, drop the shoulder and hold, and then go back to the beginning and start again, and the shoulder would come up again, and she would drop it again. Cael watched her do it eleven times. On the twelfth, the shoulder stayed down, and she held the position for a long breath with her eyes shut, and then went on to the fifth position as though nothing had happened.

He found that he had been holding his own shoulder very still.

"You training somewhere?"

The voice came from the far corner of the yard. An old man was there, Cael saw now, in the shade of the wall where he had not noticed him. He was lean and grey and somewhere past sixty, in a shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, with his feet set in a stance that was nothing like the woman's and nothing like anything else in the lane. He was not moving. He had the stillness of something that had been standing there long enough to become part of the wall, and when he spoke he did not turn his head.

"Not yet," Cael said.

"Watching's not nothing." The old man shifted his weight. It was a very small shift, from one foot to the other, so small that Cael would not have seen it at all a week ago, and he could not tell why the man had done it. "Half of training's watching. The other half's being pig-headed enough to keep at it once the watching's shown you where you're bad."

"Is that what she's doing?" Cael nodded at the woman with the shoulder.

"That's what she's doing. Twelve times today. Yesterday it was thirty." The old man's eyes moved to Cael at last. They were pale and steady and not at all old. "You saw the shoulder."

"In the fourth position."

"Most people watching her look at the sixth. That's the one that looks good." He shifted again, the other way. "You didn't look at the sixth. You looked at the one where she's wrong."

"Is that bad?"

"It's not where a bored person looks." Something that might have been a smile went across his face and did not stay. "What you're looking for, I couldn't tell you. That's your business. But you're not looking where most of them look, and that's worth more than you'd think in a lane full of people pretending to be serious."

Cael did not know what to say to that, so he said, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm telling you a fact." The old man settled back into his stance, and the stillness came down over him again like a lid. "Come back with coin for yard time. There's always room for one more person who means it."

---

The other market was where the nut boy had said it would be, south of the food market, and it was not a market.

It was a long, wide lane with a wall down one side, and along the wall, at no particular intervals, people were standing or sitting, and some of them had things laid out in front of them and some of them did not. A man had a blanket of practice weapons, wooden blades and padded sticks and a pair of iron gauntlets with the rivets ground off, all of it worn and mended.

A woman had armbands in guild colours, with the guild marks unpicked so carefully that only the needle holes showed. Someone had a crate of ledgers with their covers torn off. Nobody called out prices. People came along the lane in ones and twos, stopped, spoke quietly, and went on, and mostly nothing changed hands that Cael could see.

He walked it once, from end to end, at the pace of someone going somewhere else. He did not want to be seen lingering here before he knew what lingering meant.

Near the far end, behind a bare low table, sat a small old woman.

She was small and very upright, with her hands folded on the bare wood and a grey shawl round her shoulders. She looked as though she had been sitting in that exact position for years and saw no reason to change it now. A young man was standing in front of the table. Cael slowed a little as he came up behind him, the way you slow when someone ahead of you on a path has stopped. The young man bent down, said something too quietly to hear, and put a small stack of coins on the table between the old woman's hands.

She did not look at the coins. She looked at him. Then she spoke, and Cael counted without meaning to: three sentences, short ones, in a low, even voice, not one word louder than it needed to be.

The young man straightened. All the colour had gone out of his face at once, as if somebody had pulled a plug, and he stood there a moment with his hands hanging. Then he turned and walked away up the lane, fast, without speaking to anyone, and Cael had to step aside to let him pass.

The coins were gone. Cael had not seen her take them.

He walked on past the table without stopping. As he passed, the old woman's eyes moved and followed him for three steps, unhurried and entirely unconcerned, and then went back to the lane. He felt the attention leave him the way you feel a draught stop when a door is shut.

Three sentences, a small stack of coins, and a man who had thought he wanted to know.

He had nothing to ask her that was worth the price. But he understood, walking on, that he had just watched the whole of a trade with no goods in it, and that this told him more about how the district worked than anything else he had seen since the rise. In Denvash you bought information from the record office, slowly, with forms. Here you bought it from a person, in a minute, and you could not give it back.

---

The lane came out at a crossroads where three narrow streets met at odd angles, so that the open space in the middle was a kind of lopsided triangle, larger than any of the streets that made it. It was late in the afternoon by then. His legs had started reminding him that they had walked two days on the merchant road and a third one today, and he meant only to cross the triangle and find his way back toward Torvin's.

In the middle of it, on a post, there was a board.

It was a slab of weathered wood about waist high on a post that had been replaced at least once, because the new wood was a different colour from the old. Notices covered it, and notices covered the notices, layer on layer, so thick in places that the bottom ones had fused into the grain and become part of the board. Some were printed. Most were written by hand, in every kind of ink and every kind of handwriting, and pinned with nails, tacks, splinters and in one case a bent fork.

He read the top one because it was at eye level.

*Open card, Thirdweek. No tier floor. Unrated taken. Terms at the Cinder House.*

He read the next one, and the one after that.

*Fenrow, circuit bouts, the usual nights. Betting through the usual hands.*

*New blood wanted. Ask for Dava.*

*Rematch: Tamsin and the Ashwood boy. This time it counts. Cinder House, Fourthweek.*

*Ledger-verified bouts only. No vouch, no walk-in.*

None of them said what they were for. That was the first thing he noticed, and it stopped him. Every one of them was an advertisement, and not one of them named the thing it was advertising, as though everyone who needed to read the board already knew, and anyone who did not know was not meant to.

So he read it the way he would have read a load sheet with the headings torn off: by its numbers and its repeated words. *Card. Bout. Rematch. Unrated. Tier floor. Ledger. Vouch.* Some notices had names in them, and some of the names had letters and numbers after them in brackets, the way the registry wrote a classification.

*Renn (Cu 3, Blade) wants work. Any evening. Cinder House.*

*Result, Secondweek: Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), third exchange, called.*

He looked at that one for a long time. *Cu 5, Stone.* Copper, Rank 5, Stone Path. The shape of it was the registry's shape, the same tier and rank and Path that Pellin would have written on a form, and yet the notice had nothing official about it at all. It had been written in pencil on the back of a bill for lamp oil.

*Over.* Somebody had been over somebody else. *Third exchange. Called.* Somebody had called it.

People were fighting each other. That was the thing the whole board was talking around. People were fighting each other on purpose, for money or for something else, in named places on named weeks, and somebody kept a ledger of it, and you could not walk in without someone to vouch for you. The people who fought had tiers and ranks after their names that looked like the registry's and could not be, because nobody in the registry would have written them in pencil on a lamp-oil bill.

Two men walked behind him while he stood there, and he heard them without turning his head.

"—won't take Brenna again. Not after the first one."

"She will. She'll take it because she can't stand that it went to three."

"That's exactly why she won't. You don't take a fight you only won by being stubborn."

"Dessa? Dessa's never done anything in her life except by being stubborn."

They went on down one of the three streets, still arguing, and Cael did not know either name, and he filed them both: *Dessa. Brenna.* He had learned in the last year that a name filed early was a name you did not have to scramble for later, when it suddenly mattered and you could not work out why it sounded familiar.

He went back to the board and read it again from the top, more slowly.

There were tallies in the margins of some notices, little strokes in groups of five, in different inks. The rematch notice had been circled, once in black and once in red and once in something brown that might have been charcoal, by three different hands. From the layers of old notices under new ones he could guess how often the fights came: often enough that the board was never short of a fresh notice, and not so often that any one stopped mattering.

He thought about the man with the sack turning round at the booth on the bridge.

If there was a place in this city that weighed people by what they could be seen to do, and not by what was stamped on their cards, then it was here, behind this board, two streets from where he stood. That might be the most useful thing he had found since he walked out of the side room at Weaver's Row. It might also be the quickest way to get badly hurt by people who had been hitting each other for years while he had been reading about it at a long table under a window.

He suspected it was both. He was beginning to suspect that a great many things were both.

The light had changed while he stood there. It had gone long and gold across the triangle and then started to thin, and the board's shadow lay out across the dirt toward him like a finger. He had not written anything down. He had his notebook open in his hand, and the page was blank, and he had no idea how long he had been standing there.

That was strange enough that he noticed it. He always knew how long he had been standing somewhere.

He shut the notebook and walked back to Torvin's in the dusk, along streets that were not yet his but had begun, here and there, to be streets he had walked before. In the morning he meant to come back to the board, to check, he told himself, for new notices.

He knew while he was telling himself that it was not the whole reason, and he let himself believe it anyway, because he was tired and his legs hurt and it was easier.

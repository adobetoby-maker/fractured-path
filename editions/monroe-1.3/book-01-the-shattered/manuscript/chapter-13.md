# Chapter 13 — Tonight's Answer

Air came back to him in pieces, as though someone on the far side of a door were handing it through a crack a mouthful at a time.

The first mouthful was tiny and hurt all the way down. The next was bigger. By about the fourth he could get an elbow under himself, and then a knee. He stayed there in the dirt with his head hanging and took stock, because taking stock was a thing to be doing that was not lying on the ground.

Right side, the ribs: a deep, bright band of heat from under his arm to the bottom of his chest, so that each breath had to go round it instead of through. But each breath did go, and nothing ground against anything when it did. So, probably, nothing broken. Probably.

Left shoulder: duller now, and warming by slow degrees toward the kind of ache that would be worse tomorrow than tonight.

And the left forearm, which he had not counted at all. He turned it over in the lamplight and found a long graze down the outside from wrist to elbow, crusted with the circle's dirt and stinging now that he knew about it. Where it came from, he could not say. It annoyed him in a remote, bookkeeping sort of way, like a figure in his own column in a hand that was not his. It might have been the last fall, or the sweep, or the middle of the third exchange, where his feet had been and he had not been with them.

He did not want to go near the third exchange. He laid the arm down.

Someone stood between him and the lamps. It was Renn, without the blade now, his right hand held out open at the height of Cael's face.

Cael took the hand. Renn brought him up off his knee in one easy pull, with the unthinking strength of a man who had spent years shifting loads a great deal heavier than any fourteen-year-old, and did not let go straight away. He kept hold until Cael's feet had agreed to stay where they had been put.

"Four exchanges," Renn said.

He said it flat, the way it would go in the book, but his eyes were busy on Cael's face the whole time.

"Do you know how many I mostly get, off a debut with no rating? One. Two, if they've got some nerve. Never four." His glance went to Cael's ribs, and to the way Cael was holding himself round them, and back. "And the third."

He stopped there and gave one short shake of the head. It was not a man refusing to believe something. It was a man putting a thing on a high shelf to come back to when he had the leisure to look at it properly.

"I threw the whole of that one at you," Renn said. "It doesn't miss. It missed."

Cael kept quiet. He had nothing to say that would have been accurate.

"What's your Path?"

There was no suspicion in it whatever. It was what one tradesman asks another across a bench when a piece of work has come out better than the tools should allow: *what did you use?*

"Nothing to register," Cael said.

Renn looked at him a while longer. Whatever he made of that, he kept to himself, except that the corner of his mouth went up a little, as if it was the best thing he had heard in the yard all night.

"Tell Vell you want me again," he said. "Leave it a month. By then you'll be worth my while." He let go of the hand at last. "And I want the second look."

It was not the kind thing a winner says to a loser on the way past. Renn meant it, and said it without any decoration at all. He gave Cael one pat on the right shoulder, the one that had not been hit, tipped his head toward Vell's table, half a courtesy to her and half a direction to him, and walked off to the rope. The crowd let him through and shut behind him, and its ordinary noise closed over the place where he had been.

---

Win or lose, you went to the table after, and you stood there until she was done with you. Lira had told him so on the barrel. He went, holding his right arm tight to his side.

Vell was writing, and he waited, and watched the pen. Four times now, from the barrel and from the rope, he had seen her close a bout in the book: the names, how it ended, which exchange, anything owed. Usually it took two lines. The Bronze pair had taken three, because of the concession.

This pen was well past three. It went, and paused, and went on, and not in the steady way a hand copies out something it already knows. It went as his own pencil went when he was trying to get a thing down before it faded on him, choosing each word as it came and crossing nothing out, because nothing had been written yet that was wrong.

Once she raised her eyes to him without lifting her head.

The look was flat and gave nothing back, and it was very thorough. It was the look of someone checking a sum she had not yet decided to believe. It rested on him the length of a breath, went back down to the page, and the pen added a little more.

She blotted the page and shut the ledger on it, and whatever she had written stayed in there.

"Result stands as called," Vell said. "Renn, by the flat, in the fourth." She rested her hand on the closed cover. "He wants a return in a month, and I'm minded to let him have it. When you've mended, come and find me, and we'll settle terms."

"Yes."

For a moment her look was almost something other than flat. Then it was flat again.

"You lasted longer than the book said you would," she said.

Over by the rope Marrow was wiping his slate clean with the side of his hand, and his face had nothing on it at all.

"Is that good?" Cael said.

"It's what happened." Vell squared the ledger to the table's edge and looked past him to whatever was next, and he understood that he had been sent away.

He was halfway back across the yard before it reached him: three people who knew what they were looking at had watched him move tonight. Renn across the circle, Vell from her stool, and Lira at the rope. He had not chosen to show any of them anything, and it had happened in front of them regardless.

---

In the Cinder House yard, Vell did not open the ledger again until she had the place to herself.

That took most of an hour. The crowd went first, in a long slow drain out through the gate, still arguing about the second bout, which had gone the way the book said it would and had therefore been a disappointment to nobody but the loser. The pie boy went next, with his tray empty and his pockets heavy.

Marrow came and stood at the end of her table for a minute, as he did every night, and told her what the evening had taken, and she wrote the figure where she always wrote it, in the back. She did not ask him how he had come by it; years ago she had decided that Marrow's book was Marrow's business and the circuit's book was hers, and that the two must never be allowed to touch.

"The debut," Marrow said pleasantly. "That cost me."

"Did it."

"Everybody had him at one." He smiled. He had a very good smile and he knew exactly how much it was worth. "Over pays long, on a debut. I didn't lay it off."

"Then you'll lay it off next time," said Vell, writing, and after a moment Marrow went away.

The boy from the public house came out with a pole and began taking the lanterns down from their hooks, one at a time, starting at the far wall, so that the yard went dark in stages, from the outside in. Vell sat on her stool and let him. When there were only three lanterns left, the ones nearest her, she opened the ledger at the page and read what she had written.

*Renn (Cu 3, Blade) over unrated debut (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.), fourth exchange, by the flat. Called.*

That was the result. There was no name in it. The book did not carry the names of unrated debuts, only what they were; a name went into the book when the yard had a reason to need it, and most debuts never gave it one. It was two lines. It was all the book required, and it was all anybody coming to the book in a year, or ten years, would need in order to know what had happened in the yard tonight and who owed whom what.

Under it she had kept writing.

*First exchange: debut gave ground on the curve, did not answer. Took the flat on the left shoulder off the true strike. Stayed up.*

*Second exchange: moved on R.'s rear heel, before the strike. Early. Read it. R. answered with the sweep; debut cleared it, was offered the opening after, saw it (I watched his eyes), did not take it.*

*Third exchange: R. threw the full figure.*

She stopped there, as she had stopped when she was writing it, with the yard full and the boy standing in front of her holding his ribs.

She had kept this book, or one very like it, for longer than some of her fighters had been alive. Before that she had fought in yards like this one, and in better ones, for long enough to reach a rating nobody gave lightly and a body that told her about it every wet morning. She had seen, at a rough count she had once made on a winter night with nothing better to do, something over four thousand bouts. She had called nearly all of them. She did not remember most of them, and that was as it should be. The book remembered them. That was what the book was for.

She remembered Renn's figure.

She had watched him build it, over two seasons, a piece at a time. The first time he threw it, it had not been finished, and a woman with a Shield Path had walked through the middle of it and put him down. The second time it had been nearly finished. By the end of last season it had been finished, and she had watched him throw it nine times since, and she had written it down nine times.

Nine times the third strike had come up the lane, and nine times somebody had been standing in the lane, because that was where the first two strikes put you. That was the whole of its design. Nobody had to guess wrong for it to work. Guessing right was what delivered you to it.

She had written it down on a night in the summer against the name of the Wind girl, the one who had stood in front of her table tonight with her chin up and said *I do.* The girl had read the first two strikes as cleanly as Vell had ever seen anyone read them, and the third had put her in the dirt, and she had got up, and come back the next week, and the week after that.

Tonight it had not put anybody in the dirt.

Vell had been looking straight at it. She looked at every third exchange straight, because the third was where fighters stopped asking questions and told you what they meant, and it was where things happened that the book needed to know about. She had watched the boy's eyes, because a reader's eyes went to the place he was reading, and she had seen him go to Renn's heel in the second exchange like a man going to a door he knows. And in the third exchange she had watched his eyes go to the heel again. She had watched the heel settle.

And then the boy had not been where the strike was. And his eyes had still been on the heel. He had moved before they had left it.

She did not know what she had seen. She had sat at the table with the pen in her hand and the boy standing in front of her, and she had known that she did not know, and she had known that the book did not care whether she knew. The book cared what had happened.

There was a rule she kept for herself, which was not written down anywhere, because there was nowhere it belonged. She had made it on the night she called the Ash Path man for the third time, and he did not stop, and a woman had been carried out of this yard to wait for a healer from across the river.

Vell had written that entry afterward, and it had run to a page and a half, because she had written down everything she had seen, in order, including the two calls before and how she had made them and what she had seen in his face when he did not stop. And when people came to the table in the weeks after, wanting to know whether she had called it too late, or too early, or whether she had a grudge, she had opened the book and turned it round and let them read.

Nobody had argued with the page and a half. The circuit had survived it, and so had she.

*Write what you saw,* the rule went. *Not what you understood. If you only write what you understand, the book is only as honest as you are clever, and nobody is that clever.*

She dipped the pen.

*Defender off the line before R.'s strike began. Not a read. I watched for the read and there was none. Eyes still on the heel. Off the second strike the same. Was not in the lane for the third. A hand's width.*

*Debut threw one punch, untrained. R. turned it with the forearm.*

*Fourth exchange: R. threw the figure without probes. Debut read the first fold, part of the second, and nothing after. Down by the flat, right side. Got up.*

She read it back. It was eight lines more than the book needed, and every one of them was a thing she had seen with her own eyes from this stool, and not one of them said what any of it meant.

That was correct. She did not know what it meant. Neither, she thought, did the boy. She had looked at him while she wrote, with the yard still noisy round them, and she had seen a fourteen-year-old holding his ribs and waiting, as he had been told to wait, with nothing in his face at all.

Most of them had something in their faces at the table, after a first bout: shame, or fury, or a grin they could not stop, or the dazed look of somebody who had not known until tonight what it was to be hit by a person who meant it. He had had nothing. He had had the look of somebody who was keeping very still around something, so that it would not spill.

She thought about the margin, where she had written *nothing to register* that morning. She had heard that phrase before, now and then, from people who did not want to say their Path out loud in a yard, and it usually meant a bad draw they were ashamed of. It usually did not mean anything she needed to know.

She thought about him saying the terms back to her.

She had made a great many people say the terms back to her, over the years. Most of them said them in her words, in her order, as fast as they could, like children saying a lesson. He had said them in his own, slowly, as if he were checking each one before he set it down, and when he had finished she had known that he had heard every word and weighed it, and had decided to come in anyway. That was rarer than talent. She had seen a great deal of talent.

And she thought about the Wind girl.

Four months. Four bouts lost and then a long run that had made people start betting on her, and in all that time she had not vouched for anybody, though Vell knew for a fact she had been asked. Tonight she had stood at the table and said *I do* as if she were afraid somebody would take the words out of her mouth before she could get them said. *Because I asked him what he was, after eleven minutes at the board, and he didn't lie to me.*

Vell had not asked what he was. She had decided, in the morning, that she did not need to know.

She thought now that she had been right not to ask, and that she would not ask, and that she was going to watch very carefully all the same.

There was one more line to write, and it was not one the book required, and she wrote it anyway, at the bottom of the entry, smaller, in the space she used for things that belonged to her and not to the circuit.

*Lasted four. Marrow's slate said one. Match him with care. Not soft. Against people who'll teach him something.*

She blotted it, and closed the ledger, and sat for a moment with her hand flat on the cover in the light of the last three lanterns.

The boy from the public house was waiting with his pole.

"Go on," Vell said, and he took the last lanterns down, one, two, three, and the yard went dark, and she picked up the book and her stool and went in.

---

Lira held her tongue for a block and a half, which Cael would later learn was close to a record.

She had come off the rope an hour earlier, while Vell's yard was still roaring, and found him at the gate and fallen in at his side with no word at all. The two of them had gone out into the dark of the district together. Lamps were few in the lanes and far apart, and the dark between them was the real kind. Two corners on, the yard had dropped away behind them, and there was only the sound of their boots, a dog somewhere, and the river a long way off. Cael was walking carefully round his ribs. Lira was walking at exactly his pace, which was slower than her own, and she said nothing about that either.

Then it came out of her, aimed at the dark in front of them rather than at him.

"You almost had him in the second exchange. Once the sweep went by. Half a beat, he was crouched and turned and wide open to you. Put a hand on him there and it counts, even with arms as short as yours." She breathed out hard through her nose. "I nearly bit through my knuckle."

"I wasn't trying to win. I was trying to learn how he moves."

For three steps she said nothing. He could feel her changing her mind beside him, a shift in the air, like the side of a stove when somebody has just shut the damper.

"Oh. That's actually smarter." Her voice had altered entirely.

"Two probes," Cael said. "Always the same two, every time he starts." He had the whole bout stacked in him, page on page, and every line he gave her he was also checking for himself. "High first, then across. Front foot. Nothing behind them; they're for looking with. When he means it, the weight goes back onto the heel before anything else. He sits in it. A quarter of a second, less by the end. He rolls his shoulders when he changes his plan: once after the sweep, once before the last one. And the sweep is for somebody who's just found the heel and is pleased with himself. He was waiting for me to get clever."

"He was," Lira said.

"And when he stops testing and simply comes," said Cael, "that's the one to be frightened of. That was the fourth."

"That," said Lira, with feeling, "was the fourth."

She had watched it from the rope, and he could hear in her voice what the watching had cost her.

Then she was not beside him any more. He had gone two steps on before he noticed. When he turned back she was under one of the few lanterns in the middle of the lane, hands at her sides, stopped, and he understood that the talk had come round at last to the thing she had meant to say all along.

"The third exchange," Lira said.

He waited.

"On the roof I said I wouldn't tell you what that figure looks like, because knowing it doesn't help." She did not move. "I'll tell you now. The whole thing is built for the third cut. The first two aren't trying to hit you. They're steering you. Read the first one right and you go where it puts you. Read the second right and you go where that one puts you, and where it puts you is the exact lane the third one comes up." She gave it to him without any colour in her voice, like a reading off a gauge. "In my second month I read the first two as well as I've ever read anything, and the third laid me on my back staring up at the lamps. Everybody he's ever thrown it at has finished up staring at the lamps."

The lantern caught in her eyes. They had not left his face.

"Tonight it went by a boy on his first night in the circle, with a hand to spare." She put her head on one side. "Somewhere after the second cut you went where that figure doesn't send anybody. So I've a question. It's a small one, and I think it's fair."

He had known what it was since Vell's table.

"How?"

He had walked the whole way from the yard without readying an answer, and he knew he had done that deliberately. Readying one would have meant turning round to look the thing in the face and take its measure, and here in the dark he could admit, a little, that he did not want to. He had nothing to measure it with. In the fourth exchange he had reached for it and his hand had closed on nothing.

There was no showing it to her. He could not have done it again on demand, here between these walls, because she asked; he could not have told her what it was made of or promised it would ever come back. It was a single stone in a heap he could otherwise count, and it would not be counted, and every time his thoughts drifted near it they remembered urgent business somewhere else.

"Instinct," Cael said.

It was a true word that stopped a hand's width before the truth did.

Lira's eyes stayed on him. He was beginning to understand that she could tell, very exactly, when someone had handed her a real answer, or a false one, or a true one with its legs sawn short. He suspected she had a second sense as well, rarer than the first, for whether to collect on the difference now or leave the account open.

"All right," she said. "Instinct."

She walked on, and he went with her. Three streets went by, lanterns coming and going over them, before she spoke again, and when she did it was lightly, in the voice of someone making a note in a book of her own.

"That's tonight's answer," said Lira. "I'm writing down that it was tonight's."

---

They walked.

The district went about its late business round them: shutters going up, lamplight under doors, somebody in a window two streets over arguing cheerfully about the odds on something. A cart went by with its lamp swinging. A woman on a doorstep called a cat in by a name Cael did not catch, and the cat did not come.

His body had sorted its pains into their long arrangements by then, the shoulder stiff and the forearm stinging, and the ribs a hot band that made every breath a small negotiation.

He had lost, and it was in the book in two lines, and he had not touched Renn once. But he had walked out of the circle owning the working of one man. Bruises healed. What he had bought with them would not. A month from now, at Vell's table, he meant to have every piece of it ready.

Nearly all of it.

One piece of the evening he left unsorted. He did not so much as pick it up. He let it lie where it had fallen, whole and unexamined, in a corner of himself where he would know it was there without having to look at it. He had been doing that with a turnip cart since the road. Now there were two things in the corner, and he did not let himself count them, because counting them would have been the first step toward a word.

So he went home at Lira's side through the cooling dark: fourteen, beaten in four exchanges, his shoulder and his ribs charging him for every step. And he was certain, quietly and against all the evidence of the book, that he had won something tonight that nobody on the rope had been watching for.

At the corner by the well with the iron cover, Lira stopped.

"I go this way," she said, with a nod at the dark beyond the well that could have meant any of four lanes. Then she looked at him, at the way he was holding his side. "Torvin's has a yard with a pump. Put the cold on the ribs before you lie down, or you won't get up in the morning. Not too long. Count it."

"How long?"

"You'll know how long." She almost smiled. "You count everything."

"Lira."

She had already turned. She turned back.

"You didn't lose your name tonight," he said. "Did you?"

For a moment Lira only looked at him through the dark. Then she laughed, short and real, the laugh from the yard in the morning that had surprised her.

"No," she said. "Marrow did. He'd laid nothing off on the over." She walked backward a few steps down the lane, without looking behind her, without hitting anything. "Go and put your ribs under the pump, Cael. I'll see you at the yard."

And she was gone into the dark, and he stood at the corner by the well for a moment longer, holding his side, and then went home.

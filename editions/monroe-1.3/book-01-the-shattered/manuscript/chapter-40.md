# Chapter 40 — Tier Equivalent

Corbin came over the rope like a man stepping over a low wall in his own garden.

It was Thursday, the ninety-second day, the last week of the six, with the lanterns lit on their hooks along the three walls and the yard fuller than Cael had ever seen it on a Thursday. The benches had gone out along the shaded wall because there was nowhere else to put people, and on the end of the front one the errand boys were defending their places from a pair of dyers. Up on the wall the two girls sat with a bag of roast chestnuts between them, not laughing at anything.

Renn was on the sunny side by the gate, and had waved, and pointed at his own front foot, and laughed. Dessa was on the end of the third bench, beside an old man in a scarf, with her hair tied tight and her hands folded in her lap. He had looked at her once and she had looked back and given him nothing at all, and he had understood that she had come to watch him from the benches, as she had said she would, on her own schedule, and that she would tell him nothing about it.

Marrow's slate said *Corbin*, and a short price, and *unrated (vouched)*, and a price that was not long at all. Under it there was no over/under, because Marrow had rubbed it out, and Cael could see the ghost of a *5* under the chalk where it had been.

"He doesn't know," said Lira at the barrel. "First time since you came. He couldn't make up his mind."

Corbin stood on his mark across the circle with his arms at his sides, square and not tall, with his bald patch catching the lantern light and his short beard going grey at the chin. He did not roll his shoulders or rock on his heels, or look at the crowd, or at Vell. He looked at Cael's feet.

Cael had known he would, and had written it down on Sunday night in a hand that would not keep still. He looked down at his own feet on his mark, as if checking them, and set them.

He set them to say *left*.

He did it the way Renn had settled his heel in the middle yard on the lane, on purpose, for somebody to read. He put his weight a little onto his right foot and turned the left toe out a hair, along the groove, the way a boy stands who means to go sideways to the left on the word. Every person in this yard had heard by now that the boy from Torvin's went sideways against Renn, and Corbin would have heard it, and watched it.

"Corbin. Copper Rank 3, Shield Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Corbin read his feet.

Cael watched him do it, and let him, and did not move them.

"Begin."

He went left, exactly where his feet had said, honestly, because the first exchange was a handshake and he wanted to see what Corbin did with a true read before he gave him a false one.

Corbin was there.

He was there before Cael was. He had moved on the word as if the word had been his own idea, and he was standing a stride to Cael's left, in the place Cael was going, with his weight settled and the little patch of shine coming up off his left forearm. It was hardly bigger than a dinner plate, and Cael's strike from the hip went into it out of habit, like a fist into a door that somebody has just shut, and stung all the way to his elbow.

Corbin's right hand came round, short, not fast, and hit him in the middle of the chest with the flat of the fist.

It was not a hard blow but a sensible one, and it knocked the breath out of him and put him back two steps and told him, as clearly as words, *I was here first*. Then the shine went out of the air off Corbin's forearm, as if he did not want to waste it on nothing, and Corbin walked back to his mark.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

*First exchange,* Cael said to himself. *I told him the truth with my feet. He read it. He was there first. He spends the shield only when he needs it.*

He looked across at Corbin, who was looking at his feet.

Cael set them again to say *left*, exactly as before, with the weight a little onto the right foot and the left toe turned out along the groove, so that they said the same thing they had said a moment ago, when they were telling the truth.

"Begin."

He went right.

He went right on the word, against his own feet, against everything his feet had said, and Corbin went left. Corbin went exactly where the feet had told him, a stride to the left, smooth and early, to the place where a boy who meant what his feet said would be. The boy was not there.

For half a breath Cael was on Corbin's right side, the open side, the side without the shield, with nothing between his hands and the man's ribs.

He threw the strike.

The shine was there. He did not see it come; it was not there, and then it was there, the little plate of it, a hand off Corbin's forearm, which had come across the front of his body faster than anything about Corbin had looked. Cael's knuckles hit it and stopped. Corbin's elbow came round after it, short, into the top of Cael's left arm, and Cael went sideways and kept his feet.

But he had seen Corbin's face.

It was only for a moment, as the elbow came round. Something had gone across it and been put away again almost at once, not anger but surprise, a small clean surprise, the look of a man who has put his foot down on a stair in the dark and found it a stair lower than he thought.

Along the rope, on the shaded side, the old men in the third row made a low noise.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

Cael went back to his mark rubbing his arm. *Second exchange. I lied with my feet. He believed me. He was on the wrong side. He's quicker with the shield than he looks. Much quicker.* He looked across. *And it surprised him. Somebody lying with their feet.*

Corbin stood on his mark.

He did not look at Cael's feet, but at the middle of Cael's chest, steadily, the way you look at the middle of a page when you have decided not to read it line by line. He had stopped reading the feet. One false read in forty fights, and he had stopped.

*He's learned it,* thought Cael. *Already.*

He had expected that, but not so fast.

"Begin."

Corbin did not move first.

That was the change, and it was all the change there was: he stood on his mark and did not move until Cael did. Cael moved the way Lira had made him move every morning for three weeks, small and quick and unplanned, and Corbin did not try to read it, because he did not need to. He simply waited, square, with his weight in the middle of his feet. Wherever Cael went, Corbin turned to face it, a quarter turn and no more, without hurry, and the little plate of shine came and went off his forearm. It came when Cael was near and went when he was not, as if it were a lamp somebody kept turning up and down.

Cael went in twice. Twice the shine was there before his strike, and twice Corbin's fist came round after it, short and sensible, into his shoulder and then into his side. The second one found the left side under the ribs, where Talis had put his gatepost of a forearm eleven days ago. Something caught there, sharp, and he felt it all the way up into his teeth.

He did not let his face do anything. Corbin watched him not let it.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

He got to his mark and made himself breathe, four and four, with the catch coming at the top of every breath in. He had thought it had healed, and it had not quite healed.

*Third,* he said to himself. *He's stopped reading me. He waits. I can't get through the shield when he's waiting. And he's found the ribs.*

He looked at Corbin, and Corbin was looking at his left side, not at his feet or his chest but at the place under the ribs where the catch was. Corbin had seen the Talis bout, of course. Everybody who used Corbin for a ruler had been at the rope for everything else, and Corbin had been at the rope too, for forty fights' worth of seasons, watching.

The fourth exchange was the bad one.

Corbin came for the ribs without hurrying it, forward a step at a time, square, with the shield coming and going, and every time Cael moved, Corbin's short fist went for the left side, under the arm. Cael got his arm down for the first. The second came in over it, and the catch went through him like a nail. He went down on one knee in the dirt and came up again before Vell could say anything, which he had learned from Lira: you came up before anybody could think you were not going to.

He went backward round the inside of the groove, toward the long side, toward the drain, with his left arm held in against his ribs and his breath coming short. Corbin followed him without any hurry at all, the most patient thing Cael had ever seen, more patient than Dessa, who had waited for you to beat yourself on her. Corbin was not waiting for that; he was simply walking a tired boy round a circle, a step at a time, toward the edge of it.

Cael felt the groove come up under his heel.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

He was on his mark without remembering getting there, and the catch was there at the top of every breath, and his left arm did not want to come away from his side, and somewhere along the rope on the shaded side somebody had begun, quietly, to count. *Four.* Somebody else said *five's what Marrow had*, and somebody told them to be quiet.

At the barrel Lira had let go of the rope and folded her arms tight across her chest, the way she had stood at the post on the morning she heard his whole name, as if she were cold all at once. He understood from her face, which had nothing on it, that she was not going to call anything, or say anything, or tell him what to do. It was his to carry.

He looked across the circle.

Corbin was on his mark, breathing a little harder than he had been at the start, not much, and looking at Cael. And then, as Cael watched, his eyes went down.

They went down to Cael's feet.

Cael stood very still on his mark.

*He's reading them again.*

Of course he was. He had stopped reading them in the third because the boy had lied with them once. But the boy was hurt now, and tired, with his arm in against his side and his breath coming short, and tired people did not lie with their feet. Tired people stood the way their bodies needed to stand, because they had nothing left over for standing any other way. Forty fights had taught Corbin that. You could not lie with your feet when you were on your last legs. It took too much.

So he was reading them again. He was reading them the way he had always read them, for forty fights, because it had always worked.

Cael looked down at his own feet.

He set them to say *left*.

He did it slowly, so that it looked like what it was, which was a tired boy setting his feet. He put his weight onto his right foot and turned the left toe out along the groove. It was the same shape as the first two exchanges, exactly the same. Once it had been the truth, and once it had been a lie.

He saw Corbin read it.

He saw Corbin see the shape, and know it. It was the lie from the second exchange, the shape that said *left* and went right. And Corbin was a man who had been lied to once by that shape and had learned it, as he learned everything, in a single exchange. Cael watched him settle very slightly onto his own left foot, ready to cut across to his right. That was the place a boy would go who said *left* with his feet and meant *right*.

"Begin," said Vell.

Cael went left.

He went left, exactly where his feet had said. He went the way he had gone in the first exchange when he was telling the truth, because that was what he was doing. He was telling the truth to a man who had just learned not to believe it.

Corbin went right.

He went to the right, smooth and early, into the place where the liar would be. It was a very good move, the move of a man who had read a lie correctly and answered it before it had finished being told. To cut across to his right he had turned a quarter away from the boy, and his left arm, the shield arm, had gone round with him across the front of his own body. For half a breath, for less, he was a stride away from where Cael was, on the wrong side, with his weight going the wrong way, his shield on the far side of himself, and all of his square solid back turned a quarter toward the boy.

Cael was inside.

He did not remember deciding to be. He was simply there, on Corbin's left side now, close, closer than the shield, with the catch in his ribs going through him like a nail and none of it mattering. Corbin's shield arm was on the wrong side of Corbin. To come back across, the shield would have to come all the way round the front of the man who owned it. It was hardly bigger than a dinner plate, and very quick. But it was on the wrong side, and there was a whole square man in between.

The strike came up from the hip. Back foot driving, shoulder following, fist arriving last.

He stopped it a finger's width from the hinge of Corbin's jaw, under the ear.

He felt the shine come up beside his wrist, too late. It was there, the little plate, a hand off Corbin's forearm, which had come round the front of him faster than anything had a right to. It was a breath behind the fist. It had come round in time to guard a jaw that was not going to be hit, because the fist had already stopped.

Then Cael's left hand, which he had not been able to lift away from his ribs for an exchange and a half, came away from them on its own and went flat against the side of Corbin's chest. It did not strike. It was simply there, resting on the man's ribs, under the arm, exactly where Corbin had been putting his fist into Cael's all evening.

Corbin stood quite still.

Cael stood quite still.

They stood like that, in the middle of the circle under the lanterns, close enough that Cael could hear the man breathing, and the yard had gone so quiet that he could hear the lantern hooks creaking on the walls.

Then Corbin lifted his right hand, slowly. He opened it, palm out, and held it up toward the table.

"Called," said Vell. "Conceded. Unrated."

---

Corbin laughed.

It was a short laugh, and not a loud one, and it came out of him as though it had been waiting a long time for a reason. He stepped back from Cael, out of the strike's reach, and let the shine go off his forearm, and stood looking at him with his hands on his hips.

"You told me the truth," he said.

"Yes."

"The last time. With your feet." Corbin shook his head slowly. "The first time you told me the truth, and the second time you told me a lie, and I learned the lie. And the last time you told me the truth again, because you knew I'd learned the lie." He seemed to be going over it, step by step, the way a man goes back over a sum to find the place where it went wrong. "That's forty-one," he said. "Forty-one fights. Nobody's ever done that to me with the *truth*."

"I couldn't have done it twice."

"No," Corbin agreed. "I'll have learned that by morning." He held out his hand, and Cael took it, and Corbin's hand was hard and square and dry and steady. "Go and stand at the table. They'll want to know how long it took."

"Five."

"Five," said Corbin, and nodded, as though the number confirmed something he had always suspected about the world. Then he turned and walked off toward the water bucket, square and unhurried, a man stepping over a low wall in his own garden. On his way he passed Dessa's bench, and Dessa said something to him that Cael could not hear, and Corbin laughed again.

The yard came up behind Cael like weather.

It came up all at once and from everywhere, the shaded side and the sun and the benches. The old men in the third row were stamping on the boards, and the river workshops were shouting, and the errand boys were standing on the end of the front bench defending it from nobody. Up on the wall the girls had both stood up, and the bag of chestnuts had gone over and was rolling along the top of the wall, and neither of them was trying to catch it. Renn was shouting something on the sunny side by the gate, with his arms up. It sounded like *mine*. His friends were shouting him down.

Cael walked to the table with his left arm against his ribs, because it had gone back there as soon as it was allowed to, and stood in front of it.

Vell was writing. She did not look up. She wrote for a long time.

"Result stands as called," she said at last. "Unrated, over Corbin, Copper Rank 3, Shield Path. Fifth exchange. Conceded." The pen moved on. "Witnessed and entered."

Then she put the pen down, which he had never seen her do before a result was blotted, and looked up at him.

"Go home," said Vell. "Put that side in cold water. Not butter." She looked at him a moment longer. "And come to my table on Tuesday, after the afternoon card. Not before."

---

He did not get far toward home.

He got as far as the gate in the wall, with Lira on his left side now, the rib side, without being asked. Then he had to stand still in the gateway for a while and breathe, carefully, with his hand pressed flat against the catch, while people went past him out into the street.

They went past him talking.

He had never heard so many people talking about a bout on their way out of the yard. They were not talking to him, and nobody stopped, though a few of them looked at him as they passed, the way you look at a thing you have just been told about, to see whether it matches. But mostly they talked to each other, walking out into the dark street under the lanterns, and he heard pieces of it go past.

*—Corbin, though—*

*—five, I said five, I said it at the third—*

*—conceded, he put his hand up himself—*

*—that's past Corbin, then, that is, that's—*

Lira stood beside him in the gateway and listened to it go past, with her head a little on one side, the way she listened to the yard when she was reading it.

"There," she said quietly. "Hear that?"

"I hear it."

"That's not about you." She was not smiling; her face had the look it had at the barrel before a bout, quick and still at once. "Listen to it. Nobody's saying you're good. Nobody knows what you are. They're saying *past Corbin*. That's a thing they already know how to say. They've been saying it about other people for years." She looked at the street, at the backs going away under the lanterns, talking. "By tomorrow it'll be at the fish steps. By Sunday somebody's cousin across the bridge will have it. *The boy at Torvin's went five with Corbin and Corbin put his hand up.*" She turned and looked at him. "That's the one they'll repeat. I told you."

"You did."

"I'm always right about the yard." She took his right arm, the good one, and put it through hers, which she had never done before. Without making anything of it she began to walk him out into the street, slowly, at his pace, which was the pace of an old man crossing a frozen yard. "It's the only thing I'm always right about. Come on. Torvin's wife will have put something on with real vegetables in it, and if we're not in before the brothers there won't be any."

They went along the dark street together, slowly, through the people going home talking. At the corner by the board somebody had already been with a stub of charcoal, and under the card for Sunday, in large untidy letters that were not Vell's, it said *CORBIN PUT HIS HAND UP*. Under that, smaller, in a different hand, *five*. The errand boys were standing in front of it arguing about which of them had written it.

---

It was a Saturday, the six weeks' thirty-ninth night, very cold, and nothing happened in it at all until it did.

He was at Torvin's, on his cot under the window, with the Log open on his knee, late. The brothers had gone to sleep early because the river had frozen at the edges, just as they had said it would, and there would be no barges in the morning. They had argued about it until they fell asleep in the middle of the argument, one and then the other, and Yeni's lamp was turned low on the shelf, and the wheezing man was whistling by the door, long and thin. Downstairs Torvin had said *Lamps* up through the floorboards an hour ago, and the house had gone quiet round it.

He was going back through the six weeks.

He had begun it after supper because he could not sleep on his left side yet, or his back, and lying on his right side he could read. So he went back through the Log from the morning at the board, entry by entry, the way he read a column of figures before he signed it, from the top, slowly. Seventh, the kiln boy. Eighth, Renn, the front heel. Ninth, Sarel, two, with the longest entry in the book. Tenth, Talis, and the ground. Eleventh, Dellin, *nothing happened*. Twelfth, Corbin, five, conceded. Four wins and two losses. Six bouts in six weeks, as he had said under the pear tree, which he had not been sure his body would take, and it had taken them, just.

He was in the margin of the Dellin entry, at the line about putting his hand on the ground at his mark, when something changed.

He did not know what it was; that was the first thing about it. It was not a sound, and it was not a feeling, not quite, but nearer to the quiet that comes before a thought arrives whole, the moment when you have stopped working at a thing and it suddenly turns over in you, by itself, and shows you its other side. Except that there was no thought, only the quiet, and the turning over, and nothing on the other side yet.

But he knew that something had changed. He knew it the way he had known, in Pellin's office, that the sigil at the edge of his sight was looking at him.

He put the Log down on the blanket.

He sat very still on the cot, with his back against the cold wall under the window, the way he had sat in the circle at Weaver's Row with his feet on the two worn places, and waited.

It came from behind the breastbone.

It came from the place where the sigil had gathered on the morning of his Kindling, small and risen, before it spoke one word and went out. That place had been dark ever since; he had gone looking for it in Torvin's yard on the twenty-eighth night, for an hour, and found only the place where a door should have been. He could feel that it was still dark. Nothing lit. No sigil came up out of it to the edge of his sight. But in the dark, the way words come up on the inside of your eyelids when your eyelids are open, something was written.

He read it.

He did not read it with his eyes, and he did not know what to call what he read it with. He read it the way he had read the archive entry in the green book at a table in Denvash, under a north window: once, and then again, and then a third time, slowly, checking each word against the one before it, before he let himself believe any of it.

It stayed. It did not go out, the way the word had gone out, but sat there in the dark behind his breastbone, quiet and plain, in the way a figure sits on a page once it has been written down.

*Wind-adjacent.*

He knew where it had come from.

He knew it at once, before he had finished the third reading, as surely as he had known that something had changed, and it did not come as an argument but as a fact, like the river being cold.

It had come from the grey half. It had come from two months of mornings in the shadow of the east wall, at a canvas post, with a girl who would not tell lies to anybody she trained. It had come from watching her step a hundred times from the inside with his arms in the wrong place, and once from the rope. It had come from the drop under her backhand that was lower than she had ever asked him to go. It had come from the fourth exchange against Dessa, going across, into the closing space, in a way that was not his. It had come from the third exchange against Sarel, when he had stopped watching and moved without deciding, the way she moved. That was the thing she had been trying to teach him for two months, and he had thought he was only learning it.

He had been gathering it. He had been gathering it all along, mornings and bouts and the long minutes at the rope, and he had never had any word for what it was he gathered. Now it had a name, or the beginning of one. *Adjacent.* Next to Wind, but not Wind. Lira's, and not Lira's. Partial. Undetermined. Unknown.

It was not an answer. It was a page with more on it than yesterday's.

He sat on the cot for a long time with the notice there in the dark behind his breastbone, and did not write anything.

Then he thought about Lira.

He thought about her in her room two streets over, behind her green door that locked from both sides, with Torvin's chimney in her window, and he thought about going there. He could see the whole of it, the way he had seen the whole of it on the night of the summons, when he had shut the Log on the kitchen table and gone up through the dark to the window at the bottom of the roof stair. The habit of telling had begun that night, and he had been proud of it, and he could go now. He could knock on her green door, three times, soft, and she would open it with her coat on and say *what happened*. He could tell her.

*I think I've got something of yours.*

He tried the sentence in his head and it came out wrong, and he tried it another way, and it came out wrong again.

He had promised her on the step on the night of Dessa: *when I know what it is, really know, you'll be the one I tell first.* He did not know what it was. He knew what it was called, now, a little, and where it came from; he did not know what it did, or why, or how a thing could be collected by watching, or whether it would answer if he called it. He did not know whether it was a thing he had done or a thing that had been done to him.

And in the grey half, on the morning after he had named her right foot, she had stood in front of him with her hands loose at her sides and said *mine are mine*. *You don't keep my things.*

This was hers. Or some of it was. He did not know how much.

He took the Log up off the blanket and turned it over to the back, and wrote.

*Not yet. I'm not hiding it. I just can't explain it yet, not even to myself. If I tell her now, she'll help me work it out, and afterward I'll never be sure which parts of the working out were mine.*

He read it back.

He was not sure that it was honest.

He read it a second time and was less sure. It had the shape of a reason, and he could see the shape quite clearly, and he could not see whether there was anything inside it. It might be true. It might be the kind of true that has its legs sawn short. It might be a boy on a cot who had been told once, plainly, in the grey half, *you keep everything till you're sure*, keeping one more thing till he was sure, and writing down a good reason for it in his best hand.

He did not cross it out, but drew a line round it instead, a box, so that when he came back to it he would know he had not been sure.

---

Then, because he could not sit still with it any longer, he got up.

There was a narrow space between his cot and the wall, perhaps two strides long and one wide, where his bag stood and the cold came in under the window, and he stood in it in his stockinged feet on the boards. The brothers were asleep. Yeni was asleep, or was lying with her eyes shut and listening, which with Yeni was the same thing.

He thought about Lira's step.

Not the whole of it, with the touches and the backhand, but only the beginning of it, the part he had watched a hundred times from the inside and never once been able to see. The first drop of the hip, which started everything, a small dip and turn, so slight that from the inside it looked like nothing at all.

He did it.

He did it on purpose, slowly, at half the speed Lira would have done it. He dropped his hip the way he had seen her drop hers, and turned the way he had seen her turn, and put his foot down where he had seen her put hers.

It worked.

It did not work well. It worked so badly that anybody watching would have thought he was trying to step over something on the floor and had misjudged it. It was clumsy and slow, and nothing like the drop under her backhand, or the going across against Dessa, which had come out of him whole and perfect and not his. But for a moment, for one step, the boards between the cot and the wall were very slightly less firm about how far apart they were. It was the width of a hand, perhaps less: the space between where his foot had been and where it landed was a little shorter than it should have been, and he felt it go short under him, like a stair that is not quite the height you expected.

He had done it. Not had it done to him. Done it. On purpose. He had chosen to.

He stood there afterward in the narrow space, breathing.

He was breathing much harder than one slow step in his stockings had any right to make him, and his hip ached, the left one, deep in the joint, as if he had carried something heavy up a long stair on that side. It cost something, he did not know what or how much; he only knew that it had cost more than it should have, and that he had paid it, and that he did not know yet what it had bought.

He did it once more, to be sure.

It was worse the second time, slower and clumsier, and the boards were only a very little less firm, hardly at all, and the hip ached more after it. He did not do it a third time. He sat down on the end of the cot with his hand pressed to the hip and his breath coming short, and turned the fact over in his mind like a stone he had picked up off a road and could not yet tell the weight of.

Then he picked up the Log and wrote under the box.

*I did it on purpose. Once. Badly, slowly, and it cost something in the hip and the breath that I can't measure yet. Twice, worse. But on purpose.*

*Every time before, it came when I didn't ask for it and wasn't there when I did. Tonight I asked, and it came. Not much. A little. Badly.*

*I think that's the thing that matters. Not that it's there. That it's started to answer when I ask.*

He read that over, and it was true all the way through, and he left it.

---

He wrote to Hesk before he slept, because it was Saturday, and the letter would go in the west bag on Monday and be in Denvash by the Monday after. And he had found, sitting on the cot with his hip aching, that he wanted very much to write to somebody, when there was only one person it could be, and he could not write it to him.

He wrote it short.

*Hesk,*

*Six weeks nearly done. Six bouts in them, four won. The last was a man everybody here uses to measure people by, and he put his hand up in the fifth. People were saying it in the street on the way out. Lira says that's the one they'll repeat.*

*Lira's drawn a line under the forty. I'll tell you about the forty another time; it's a long story and most of it is me saying one word.*

*Tell Joren I'm putting my hand on the ground at my mark before every bout now. He'll know why, or he won't, and either way he'll like it.*

*Tell Alis I laughed.*

*I'm all right. The ribs are mending.*

*— Cael*

He read it over, and it was true all the way through, and he folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

Then he sat with the leather book closed on his knee and counted, as he always did now, what he had not told Hesk.

The cart. Renn. The drop. Dessa. And tonight.

It was five.

He sat with the number for a long time in the cold, with the wheezing man whistling by the door. It was not that it had gone up. He had known it would go up the moment he read the notice; he had made a promise on a step about who would hear first. It was that tonight it had gone up and the other number had not gone down. There was one person who was meant to be first. She was two streets away behind a green door, and he had not gone.

He blew out his small piece of the light by turning his face to the wall. He lay a long time with the notice quiet behind his breastbone and his hip aching, and listened to the district's late sounds come up through the window, and did not sleep for a while.

---

On Tuesday the afternoon card went off without him, three bouts, and he stood at the rope and watched all three and wrote them down. Then the yard emptied, and the pie boy began to rake the circle, and the crows came down along the eaves, and he went to Vell's table.

She was finishing an entry, and did not look up until she had blotted it. "Sit down."

There was nowhere to sit but the dirt and the barrel, so he stood, and she did not seem to mind.

"Assessed-Copper," said Vell.

He waited.

"It's not a real rank. I want that said first, so you don't go thinking it is." She turned her tea a quarter turn by its handle. "It's a word I use. It means the circuit has watched you for long enough to say you fight at about Copper 5, or 6, on a good night, whatever the registry calls you. And the registry calls you nothing." She looked at the ledger. "Take it to Denvash and they'd not know what it meant. Take it across the river and they'd laugh. Nobody outside this district would give you a mark for it."

"But inside it," said Cael.

"Inside it, it's the difference between a boy people bet on for sport and a fighter people plan for." She picked up her pen and wrote a short line beside the long-standing one at the top of his page, which he could not read upside down, and she did not turn the book. "That's worth something here. It's worth more here than you know yet."

She blotted it.

"Is it worth anything to you?" said Cael.

He had not known he was going to ask it until it was out. Vell looked up at him, and something went across her weathered face, very nearly amusement, and was put away again.

"Liking a fighter has nothing to do with how I rate him. The book's honest or it's nothing, and an honest book's all that stops this circuit tearing itself to pieces over who's good." She looked at him a moment longer, and then said, more quietly, in a different voice, the one she kept for things that were hers: "For what it's worth. I do."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She went back to the book. "You did the fighting."

He went.

Lira was sitting on the low wall at the side of the yard with her knees up and her coat round them, in the last of the light. She had waited, and had not come to the table, but had sat on the wall, as she had sat on the river wall on the day of the six weeks, with the look of somebody who had been happening to pass a particular place for about an hour.

"Well?"

"Assessed-Copper."

She did not jump off the wall, but sat quite still for a moment, and then the slow smile began, the one that started behind her eyes and took its time getting out.

"Copper 5. Or 6. On a good night." She got down off the wall. "That's higher than mine, you know. My circuit rating. You're past me." She did not sound as if she minded; she sounded as if she had won something herself. "I vouched for you. Three months ago, near enough, with my name on it, for a boy who said a word nobody had ever heard out loud. And now you're past me." She held out her good hand to pull him up off nothing, out of habit, and then laughed and dropped it. "Come on. It's the forty-second day. You've spent them all."

They went out through the gate in the wall together, and down the lane in the dusk toward the well with the iron cover, where she would stop, and go one way, and he the other.

He walked beside her and did not say it.

He had it with him, behind his breastbone, quiet and plain, in the dark place that was not quite as dark as it had been. *Wind-adjacent.* It had been there at Vell's table and on the wall and in the gate, and it was there now in the lane. Every step he took beside her, it was there, and every step he did not say it.

The six weeks were spent. Somewhere on the merchant road, or at a desk two days west, somebody more senior than Coss was being found and sent, with a file that was too thin and a question nobody had told Coss. A girl with a green door was walking beside him in the dusk, pleased about a word that was not a rank, a girl who had drawn a line under forty and had told him, plainly, *mine are mine*.

At the well she stopped, as she always stopped.

"Same time tomorrow," said Lira.

"Same time tomorrow."

She went off up the lane toward the chandler's, and he stood at the well and watched her go until he could not see her, and then turned toward Torvin's, two streets the other way.

He did not know how long he could carry it, only that it would not be long. He thought, walking home with his hip aching, that it might be two weeks, if he was careful, and that he did not want to be careful, and that the thing behind his breastbone was partly hers. He thought that every morning he kept it from her in the grey half, it would get heavier, the way a thing does that has been held up too long. Nothing that has to be held is free.

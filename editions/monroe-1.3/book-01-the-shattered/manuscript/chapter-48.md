# Chapter 48 — An Ordinary Tuesday

Properly, it turned out, meant a column.

Lira was at the post when he came in through the gate on Thursday with the bread, and she had already chalked it. Beside the *gone* column, which was ruled off at forty, and the falls column, which had stopped at nine, there was a third column now, narrow and new, with nothing at the head of it but a small *W* and nothing under the *W* at all.

"That's yours," she said. "Every time you ask for it, a stroke. Every time it comes, a ring round the stroke. And every time it costs you something, you tell me what, and I'll put a word beside it." She took the loaves off him and tore them and gave him half. "You said on Sunday you'd write it down every time. You haven't."

"I've written it."

"You've written that it costs. That's not the same as writing what." She pointed the chalk at him. "*A little* is a word. *Worse* is a word. *Can't feel my foot* is three words, and if you ever say it to me I'll want to know. But it goes on the post, where I can see it, and not only in the back of your book where I can't."

He looked at the empty column, and then at her.

"Is this the rule?"

"This is the rule." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Eat. Then ask."

So he ate, and then he stood up in the grey half with his left arm held against his chest, because it would not yet hang without hurting, and asked.

The step came slowly, as it always came when it was asked for, as if he were reading it aloud from a page in poor light. His hip dropped and his body turned and his foot went down, and the frozen dirt between where it began and where it landed went short by the width of a hand. Then his breath went, and the old dull ache came into the joint of his left hip, and he stood there with his good hand pressed against it, breathing as if he had run up from the river.

Lira made a stroke on the post and put a ring round it.

"What?"

"Breath. The hip. About the same as Sunday."

"*Same*," she said, and wrote it beside the ring in small letters. "Again."

He did it again. It was worse, as the second always was, and the ground hardly changed under his foot.

"*Worse*," said Lira, and wrote that, and made a ring that was only half a ring, because she said that was fair. Then she looked at the two strokes, chewing, with her head on one side.

"And the other one," she said.

He had known she would ask. He had known it since Tuesday, on the low wall, when she had read the second notice twice with her lips moving and said *two doors* in the voice she kept for things she would come back to. He had thought about the answer on the long walk to the brickworks and back yesterday, and he gave her the one he had found.

"I haven't tried it."

"Why not?"

"Because it doesn't go anywhere in particular." He looked across the yard at her, and at the girls coming along the far wall with their stolen breakfasts. "Feryn's went on everything in front of him. All at once. The Wind's only my feet. If I ask for the other one, it'll go wherever I'm facing." He did not look away. "And you're in front of me."

Lira stood quite still with the bread in her hand.

He thought for a moment that she would laugh, as she had laughed at the eel woman, and he would not have minded. She did not. She looked at him as she looked at a guard she had not seen before, the whole of it at once, and then she nodded, slowly, as if he had told her the right answer to a question she had not quite known she was asking.

"Then not me," she said. "Not the girls on the wall. Not the post, either; it's Vell's." She turned and looked at the empty end of the yard, where the grey half ran out against the drain. "Somewhere with nobody in front of it. When your arm can lift a cup."

"When my arm can lift a cup," he agreed.

"Good." She went to her mark. "Box drill. Slowly. And keep that arm out of it, or I'll tie it to you."

---

He posted the letter to Hesk at noon.

He had finished it the night before at the kitchen table, after everybody had gone up, with his left arm lying on the table beside the paper like something he had brought home from the market and not yet put away. It had not been a long letter to write. He had promised in the last one to tell the next thing as it came. This was the next thing, and it had come only two days ago, and he knew exactly what it had to say.

*Hesk,*

*I said I'd tell you the next thing when it happened, and not five things later. This is the next thing.*

*I lost to the Bronze on Monday, in the fourth, on Vell's call, and she was right to call it. The arm's stiff and the ribs catch; nothing's broken; it'll be a week. He bought me supper afterward and told me about a goat.*

*That night, after midnight, on my cot, there was another one. I'll copy it exactly, as I did the first.*

He had copied the three lines letter by letter from the back of the Log, and checked them twice against the page, and then once more against the place in the dark behind his breastbone where they had been written first.

*I think it came from him. Not from watching; I'd only watched him for one bout, and the first took three months. In the last exchange he threw me, and his hand stayed on my shoulder after the throw was finished, a second longer than it needed to. That's the only thing that doesn't fit anywhere else. It's one data point, and I've written that in the book where I'll have to look at it.*

*Lira knew first. I told her on Tuesday in the grey half before the light, and she read it twice and said she didn't know what it meant either, and gave me the bread.*

*I'm all right. I'm more than all right, most of the time. I lost and learned more than I've learned from most of the things I've won. I don't know what any of it is yet. I'm going to keep finding out.*

*— Cael*

At the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps, the clerk with the inky fingers read the address out to the hut as he read every address, and weighed the letter on his little brass balance, and looked at Cael over it.

"Thinner than the last," he said.

"Yes."

"Torvin's," said the clerk, as he had said it every week since the autumn, as though it were a small private joke between the two of them, and hung it on the third hook along, under WEST.

Cael stood and looked at it. It was a single folded sheet, and it had in it two plain lines of figures copied from the dark, and in a week a man would sit down with it on a workshop step in Denvash and read it twice and not drink his tea. Cael had decided, at the kitchen table, that this was better than the other thing. He found, standing in the hut with the river going by outside and the barges knocking at the quay, that he had not changed his mind.

He went back up the steps one at a time, with his good hand on the rail.

---

The arm got better, slowly, the way arms did.

By Saturday he could close the left hand round a cup, though he could not yet lift the cup. By Sunday the swelling had gone down enough that he could see the shape of the bone again under the skin. The bruise had turned from black to a dark wet purple, and at its edges it had begun to go the colour of a pear left too long on a sill. The ribs caught at the top of every full breath, both sides, the left more than the right. The right hip, where the ground had come up, stiffened every night and loosened every morning on the long walk up the hill to the grey half, and he learned to leave early enough to let it.

On Monday he went down to the mender's.

The mender was at his bench with his loupe in and a clock on its back in front of him, and he did not look up when Cael came in under the four black-painted words on the doorframe. He did not look up for some time. Then he took the loupe out of his eye and looked at Cael's left arm, which Cael was holding a little away from his side so that it would be seen to be holding itself.

"Still there," said the mender.

"Still here."

The mender considered him. Then he put the loupe back in, and pushed a tin of washers across the bench with one finger, and Cael sat down on the stool at the end and began to sort them by size, slowly, with both hands. It hurt a little to do it with the left. He did it with the left anyway, because he had been told to work it in cold water every morning and he thought that washers were very nearly the same thing.

Nobody said anything for the rest of the afternoon. At the end of it the mender counted the tins with his eyes, the way he always did, and nodded once over his clock, which meant Wednesday.

---

The summons came on Tuesday, which was an ordinary day.

That was what he remembered about it afterward. Nothing had happened that morning to warn him. He had gone up to the grey half and done the box drill slowly and asked for the step twice, *same* and *worse*, and Lira had chalked them. He had gone down to the market and bought two loaves from underneath and a twist of nuts from the boy who never haggled. It had rained a little at noon, a thin spring rain that had not been able to decide whether it was still winter. He had sat on the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard for an hour in the afternoon, working his arm in a bucket of cold water and drawing Corvane's figure in the wet dirt with a stick. Everything in the day had been the same size as everything in the day before it.

He came in through the yard door at dusk with the bucket, and Torvin's wife was at the range with her back to the table, and there was a letter propped against the salt crock.

It was not from Hesk; he saw that from the door. Hesk's letters were a little crooked and soft at the corners from a week in the bag, and they had *ROOMS (the O leans)* on the front in Hesk's square hand. This one was long and white and very straight, with a seal on the back the colour of dried blood. In the middle of the seal was the small hard shape he had seen once already, in the autumn, on a summons Torvin had held out to him at arm's length.

He put the bucket down.

"Boy from the post brought it," said Torvin's wife, to the range. "At noon. I made my mark for it." She stirred something. "He said there was no answer wanted. I said I hadn't asked him."

Cael picked it up; it weighed nothing. He broke the seal with his thumb and opened it standing up, by the light from the range.

*To CAELEN HESK-WARD, registration 41-7843-V, resident at the lodging house known as Torvin's, Unranked District, Ardenmere.*

*You are required to attend a senior evaluation, convened under the District Handbook, Part Six, at the post, Ardenmere, on the Thursday of this week, at the second hour after noon.*

*Under the closed-pending order of the fifty-fifth day, this evaluation discharges the senior authorization then applied for.*

*Ilsev, Assessor, Regional Evaluation.*

*Countersigned: Coss, field agent.*

He read it twice, and then a third time, slowly, holding each word up against its neighbours, as he read everything he could not afford to get wrong. It did not change, and it did not say what would happen on Thursday at the second hour after noon, or what an assessor did, or what Part Six was. It said a time and a place and a name, and that was all it needed to say, because the time and the place and the name were the whole of it.

His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had thought for weeks that when it came he would feel it come, like the air leaning in Corvane's yard. Instead it had come against the salt crock on an ordinary Tuesday, between a bucket of cold water and a bowl of soup, and he felt mostly a kind of plain recognition, as you feel when a cart you have heard coming all morning finally turns the corner.

The six weeks had run out on the forty-second day, six weeks ago now. He had known it was coming, on every one of those days, and on most of them he had been too busy to look.

"Thursday," said Torvin's wife, who had not turned round.

"Thursday."

She stirred the pot. Then she reached behind her without looking and took a bowl down from the shelf and filled it and put it on the table at his place, and put a spoon in it, and put a heel of bread beside it.

"Eat that," she said. "Then go and tell the girl. Before the lamps, mind. That widow's a terror about the lamps."

---

The widow was asleep in her chair behind the counter with her chin on her chest, among the candles hanging by their wicks, and she woke up as he came in, which she had never done before.

She looked at him over the candles with her round pale face.

"Lamps in an hour," she said. "I'm saying it now."

"Yes."

She shut her eyes again, and he went round the side and up the stair, and the fourth step talked, and he said *I know* to it without thinking.

Lira opened the door before he had finished knocking. She had heard the step, and the voice, and the not-quite-running on the stair, and she stood in the door with her hand on the edge of it and looked at his face and then at the white paper in his hand, and stood aside without a word.

She took it out of his hand and read it standing up, under the sloping ceiling, with her lips moving very slightly. Then she read it again, sitting down on the end of the bed.

"Part Six," she said.

"Yes."

"I've never read Part Six." She looked up. "Have you?"

"No. I read Two and Three and Four. Four was the one that mattered." He sat down on the floor with his back against the shelf, on the worn pale strip, and was careful of his ribs. "Nobody's read Part Six. That's what's wrong with it."

"An assessor." Lira looked at the name at the bottom. "*Ilsev*. That's a woman. That's a woman who's come from regional to sit in that little room and do something Coss couldn't." She turned the paper over, as if there might be more on the back, and there was not. "She'll have read your objection."

"Yes."

"She'll have read it the way Coss did. Three times, with her lips moving. Except she'll have had weeks." Lira put the summons down on the blanket beside her, very carefully, as she would have put down the throat-sweet tin. "So she's coming with an answer to it. She'd be a fool not to, and she isn't a fool, or they wouldn't have sent her."

"I know."

"Then you need her answer before she gets here."

"I know." He looked at the paper on the blanket. "The reading room's mornings. Tomorrow. I'll go at first light and sit there till Halden puts me out."

Lira nodded slowly. He could see her doing the sums: what there was, and what it would cost, and what was left over.

"I can't come," she said. "Wednesday's my hour with the dyers' girls, and it's the rent, and if I miss it they'll go and find somebody cheaper who doesn't know what a foot is for." She frowned at the summons as though it had done that on purpose. "And you read faster when I'm not there asking what words mean."

"You don't ask what words mean."

"I ask what they mean *here*. It's slower." She almost smiled, and then did not. "Thursday morning. Before the post. I'll come to Torvin's step at first light, and you'll read me whatever you've found, out loud, the way you did before, and I'll tell you which bits sound like you're frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"I know you're not. That's not what I said." She stood up, under the slope of the ceiling, and he stood up too. "Go home. The widow's got a candle in her lap that she's going to light the minute your foot's off her bottom step, and she'll count to the top of it, and if you're still on the stair she'll tell the whole lane."

He went. On the fourth step, going down, he heard the door shut above him, and the key turn in it, and then, after a moment, the key turn back again, as though she had locked it from habit and then thought better of it, or worse.

---

Halden looked up from his table when Cael came down the three steps into the reading room, and looked at him for the length of a breath. Then he looked at the clock on the wall above the shelves, which said that it was very early indeed for anybody to want the law.

"Halden," said Halden.

"Part Six," said Cael.

Halden did not say anything for a moment. Then he got up from his table, slowly, as he had got up in the autumn, and walked down the long room to the left-hand shelves, and took down the thick district Handbook that Cael had read through twice in a day and most of a night. He did not bring it back to the long table. He turned it over in his hands and opened it from the back, and looked at what he found there, and came back along the room with it held open in both hands like a tray with something on it that might spill.

He set it down under the lamp.

The back of the book was a different paper. It was thicker and whiter and had been sewn in after the rest, and the pages had never been cut. They were still joined at the top and the outer edge, folded sheets, as they had come from the binder, so that you could look between them as into a row of little paper rooms and see that there was print inside, but not read it. At the head of the first sheet, upside down, it said *PART SIX. THE SENIOR EVALUATION. RESERVED TO ASSESSORS.*

"It's bound in with the district edition," said Halden. "Nobody's ever cut it." He took a paper knife from his own table, a thin bone thing worn yellow, and laid it beside the book. "Reserved to assessors means that an assessor is expected to know it. It doesn't mean anybody else may not. Anybody the registry has a number for may read anything in this room." He looked at the uncut pages. "Nobody has wanted to before."

"Thank you."

"I said I'd find you the next book." Halden went back to his table. Halfway there he stopped, and said over his shoulder, without turning round, "Begin at the beginning. Copy it from the first line. The people who start in the middle are always wrong about something."

So Cael began at the beginning.

He cut the pages one at a time, with the bone knife, carefully, slitting along the folds. The paper made a soft dry tearing sound in the quiet that seemed louder than it was. He read each page as it opened, and then he copied it into the front of the Log in his careful hand, all of it, every line, from *Section 1: Definitions* down. His left arm ached from holding the book flat, and he swapped hands, and it ached the other way.

It was dull, and it was long, and he understood very little of it on the first reading and a good deal more on the second. There were definitions, and who might ask for a senior evaluation, and how an assessor was to conduct one, and what happened when none of the rest applied. Section four was a long dry paragraph about practitioners whose classification could not be assessed by ordinary means, and a temporary category they could be entered under, and a facility they could be required to attend. He copied it line by line like all the rest, and thought, *that's for people the Arbiter couldn't read*, and went on to five.

By the time the light from the high windows had moved all the way across the long table and begun to go up the far wall, his hand had cramped twice and the Log had eleven more pages in it than it had had at dawn.

Halden came down the room at noon, when the reading room shut, to take the book back.

He looked at the cut pages, all of them slit now down their folds, lying open and loose, and then at Cael, and then at the Log.

"All of it?"

"From the first line."

Halden nodded slowly. He closed the book and held it for a moment with his hand flat on the cover, as if feeling whether anything inside had changed.

"Go home and read it again," he said. "Tonight. Slowly. You'll find something you didn't see here." He tucked the book under his arm. "People always do. It's why I tell them to copy it."

---

He read it again that night, at Torvin's kitchen table, after the lamps.

Torvin had said *Lamps* through the floorboards an hour ago. The range had been banked and was ticking as it cooled. Torvin's wife, going up, had looked at him sitting there with the Log open and a candle end in a saucer, and had not said anything, and had left the saucer. He had read the five sections through once, slowly, as Halden had told him to, and then again, more slowly. He had found nothing he had not found in the morning, and he had begun a third time.

Somewhere in the middle of the third reading, two hours before dawn, by a candle end gone down to a stub, section four stood up off the page.

It did not change. The words were the words he had copied in the reading room, every one. *Where the primary classification of a practitioner cannot be assessed by ordinary means, the senior evaluator may enter the practitioner under a temporary non-standard category for the period of assessment.* He had read it that morning and thought, *that's for people the Arbiter couldn't read*. He read it now and heard it in a different voice: in the voice of a woman who had read his objection three times with her lips moving, and had had weeks.

*Cannot be assessed by ordinary means.*

He had said that. He had handed Coss a sheet of paper that said exactly that, three months ago, at the desk that rocked. A standard evaluation could not assess his classification. That was the whole of his objection. That was the thing that had bought him six weeks.

And it was the first half of section four.

He sat very still at the kitchen table with the cold going through his shirt.

It was not fear, quite. It was the thing that came in the circle when a real opponent showed him what she meant to do, the cold clean alertness that sat under the ribs and made everything else very quiet. He had built the door himself, in the autumn, and walked through it, and been glad of it. Now he could see, from the other side, that it opened both ways. A careful assessor would only have to read his own objection back to him, and then turn the page.

*Enter the practitioner under a temporary non-standard category. A practitioner so entered shall attend at a regional assessment facility within twenty-eight days, and remain there for the period of assessment.*

Remain there. That meant not here. That meant a room in a building he had never seen, in a city two days west or further, for as long as somebody else decided an assessment took, and Lira and the grey half and the Cinder House and Torvin's and the mender's tins of washers all a long way off on the other side of it. It would all be done by procedure, correctly, with a stamp. Nobody would have to do anything a clerk could be ashamed of.

He made himself breathe, four and four, with the catch at the top.

Then he turned back three pages, to the beginning, because Halden had told him the people who started in the middle were always wrong about something, and because he did not yet know what else to do.

*Section 1: Definitions.*

He read them one at a time, with his finger under the line. *Assessor.* No. *Senior evaluation.* No. *Primary classification: the classification returned by the practitioner's Arbiter at Kindling and entered in the registry, including any entry made in the Supplementary Register.* He stopped. He read it again, and his finger did not move.

Then he read the next one.

*Temporary non-standard category: the category under which a practitioner without primary classification is entered pending assessment.*

He sat back.

The candle end guttered in its saucer and steadied. Somewhere upstairs the wheezing man began his long whistle at the top of every breath, and one of the brothers said something in his sleep about a rope.

*Without primary classification.*

The category was not for anybody whose classification could not be read. It was for people who did not have one. Section four gave an assessor the power to put a practitioner into the category, and section one, three pages earlier, said who could be in it. A boy whose Arbiter had returned nothing would be in it. A boy whose return had been lost, or never recorded, would be in it. And a boy whose Arbiter had taken eleven seconds and then returned a word, a real word, a word with four instances and a statute of its own in the Supplementary Register, and a brass plate that had lit eleven seconds late, had a primary classification. He had more of one than anybody in the district. It simply was not one the standard framework could use.

That was not the same as not having one. Section one said so, in the Compact's own words.

He did not let himself feel anything about it yet. He had learned that in the circle too. A plan that felt good at the mark was a plan you had not yet tested. He took a clean page at the front of the Log, and ruled it, and began to build the answer in four steps, as plainly as he could.

*One. Section four lets the assessor put a practitioner into the temporary non-standard category when his classification can't be assessed by ordinary means. Mine can't. I said so myself.*

*Two. Section one, three pages before, says what that category is: it's for a practitioner without primary classification.*

*Three. Section one also says what a primary classification is: what the Arbiter returned, entered in the registry, including the Supplementary Register. Mine was entered there. Statute nineteen. Four instances. It resolved; the plate lit.*

*Four. So I have a primary classification. Section four gives her the power to put people in the category, and section one keeps me out of it. Not being assessable isn't the same as not being classified.*

He read the four steps through. Then he read them from the bottom up, the fourth first, to see whether each one stood on the one below it or only leaned. They stood. He read them aloud under his breath, and they still stood.

Then he copied them clean onto the next page, without the crossings-out, in his best hand, short enough to say and not read. A thing said from memory across a desk sounded like knowledge, and a thing read from a page sounded like a hope.

When he looked up the window over the range had gone from black to grey, and Torvin's wife was standing at the foot of the stairs in her shawl, looking at him. He did not know how long she had been there.

She did not ask him anything. She went to the range and opened it and woke the fire, and put the oats on, and when they were done she put a bowl in front of him and stood over him with the wooden spoon until he had eaten all of it.

# Chapter 12 — Terms

Lira was waiting for him at the board in the morning, sitting on the edge of a water trough with her canvas roll across her knees, eating an apple.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. I just make it look like I happened to be passing." She threw the core over her shoulder into the trough without looking, where it bobbed, and stood up. "Come on. Vell sets her table out at ten, and the first one there gets her before she's tired of people."

They walked up toward the yards side by side through a grey, cool morning, under a thin high cloud that took the colour out of everything, and the district was busy in the ordinary way of a weekday, carts and barrows and the smell of bread. Cael let two streets go by. He had settled in the night what he was going to say, and how, but walking now every version he had built in the dark came out as an accusation or an apology, and he meant neither.

He said it anyway, plainly, at the corner where the lane started to climb.

"What does it cost you?"

Lira did not pretend not to understand him, and he liked her for that before she had answered.

"Who told you it cost anything?"

"Torvin. And Yeni." He kept walking, and so did she. "Torvin says a vouch is a loan. Yeni says it's your name. She says four months is all the name you've got."

"Yeni talks too much."

"Yeni hardly talks at all."

"Then she picked a strange thing to say it about." Lira walked half a street without saying anything, and when she did speak, the lightness had gone out of her voice, and what was left was level and careful, the voice she had used to tell him about the examiner. "All right. If you go into that circle and fold in the first exchange, people will remember that I said you wouldn't. Next time I vouch for somebody, Vell will look at me a little longer before she writes it down. Next time I say a fighter's good, people will say, *she said that about the boy who folded.* It won't ruin me. I've lost four bouts in that yard and I'm still standing in it. But it'll cost me something I worked for, and I'd rather it didn't."

"Then why do it?"

"Because I don't think you'll fold." She looked sideways at him. "I could be wrong. I've been wrong before. But I've watched you for two days, and I don't think so, and if you want to know what my name's actually *for*, Cael, it's for this. It's for saying what I think in front of people who'll hold me to it. Otherwise it's just a thing I keep in a drawer."

He thought about the drawer marked *Springs, do not use,* and did not say anything about it.

"I'm not going to win," he said instead.

"No."

"I mean I'm not going to try to. I'm going to try to last. And I'm going to watch him. And I'm going to come out of there knowing more about how he moves than anybody on that rope." He heard himself saying it and realized he had not known until this moment that it was a plan, rather than a hope. "That's what I can do. That's all I can do. I wanted you to know before you put your name down, in case it's not what you thought you were vouching for."

Lira stopped walking.

They were halfway up the lane, outside a yard where two men were taking turns throwing each other over a hip onto a pile of straw, and the steady soft thump of bodies landing came over the wall like someone beating a rug. Lira stood in the middle of the lane and looked at him for long enough that he began to wonder whether he had made a mistake.

"That's exactly what I thought I was vouching for," she said. "Nobody wins their debut. Nobody sensible tries. The ones who try to win are the ones who get carried out." She started walking again. "And listen. Don't do anything stupid in there to protect my name. You get hurt doing something stupid for my sake and that costs me more than you folding would. I'm not paying for that."

"What counts as stupid?"

"Refusing to go down when you're down. Standing up when you should stay on the ground. Trying something you saw somebody else do on a sheet once." She shook her head. "Keep your feet, watch him, and when Vell calls it, it's over. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job," he said.

"And Cael."

"Yes."

"It's a good question," said Lira, not looking at him. "Asking what it costs. Most people never ask. They just take it." She lengthened her stride. "Come on. She'll have had her first cup by now."

Near the top of the lane they passed the gate of the yard where the woman had worked on her shoulder. She was not there, but the old man was, in his corner in the shade, set in the same stance as if he had not moved since the day before, and as they went by his pale eyes came round to Cael, then to Lira's canvas roll, then to the way the lane ran on toward the Cinder House.

"Tonight?" he said.

"Tonight."

"Then watch his feet, not yours." The old man's eyes went back to the wall. "Yours'll do what they do."

Lira waited until they were past the next gate. "You know him?"

"He told me watching's not nothing."

"Four months I've walked past that gate," said Lira, "and he's never said a word to me." She looked back over her shoulder, frowning, and then at Cael, as if he had grown an inch while she was not looking.

---

Vell was watching the empty circle when they came in at the gate, and she went on watching it all the way across the yard, as if it might do something interesting the moment she looked away. Her table stood in the shade of the back wall where it had stood the afternoon before. The ledger was squared to its edge, the pen lay across the open page, and a cup of tea steamed at her elbow. Apart from the crows, there was nobody else in the place.

She moved her eyes only when they had stopped in front of her. They went to Lira, then to Cael, and then back to Lira for a moment, and Cael knew that look from the workshop. It was the look of somebody checking a column and finding a figure entered where she had not expected one.

"Name," she said.

"Cael."

The pen moved, but not for long enough to be his name. It wrote something shorter. She did not ask for a second name, and he did not offer one.

"Age."

"Fourteen."

The pen stopped. It stopped for about one breath, and in that breath Vell lifted her head and looked at him properly, and he made himself stand there and be looked at. It was a thorough look, not an unkind one. It went over his face and his shoulders and his hands and the set of his feet. Partway through it he realised it was doing to him exactly what he had done yesterday to the Stone woman and the Blade fighter from the barrel yard, and doing it a great deal better.

She laid the pen down and folded her hands.

"You'll have had the terms explained to you."

"Yes."

"Explained and agreed are different animals. So you'll hear them from me, and then nobody can stand in my yard afterwards and say they didn't." She did not raise her voice; she did not seem to need to. "You come in unrated. You fight who I put across from you. It ends when I call it. Not when you've had enough, not when the rope's had enough, not when your friend there can't bear to watch." Her glance went to Lira as she said it, and returned. "Once you're over that rope, I'm the only one who stops it. Whatever happens in there, you carry it out of here yourself, whichever way it falls. Nobody's making you. If any of that sits wrong with you, tell me now and I'll strike the line, and there's not a soul in this yard who'll hold it against you."

"It doesn't sit wrong."

"Then say it back."

He looked at her.

"It isn't that I think you're deaf," said Vell. "I've had grown men nod along to all of that, and then stand in the middle of my circle shouting that nobody told them. Nobody goes in until I've heard it come out of their own mouth. I don't care how old they are."

So he said it back, slowly, and checked each part before he set it down, the way Hesk had taught him to call a load figure aloud. Unrated. Whoever she chose. Over when she said it was over, and for no other reason and at no other time. Nobody stopped it but her. Whatever happened, his to carry, either way. Nobody was making him. He did not use her words. He used his own, so that she could hear he had understood the terms and not just memorised them.

When he had finished, the corner of Vell's mouth gave a little. It was gone again before it could grow into a smile.

"Path," she said.

"Nothing to register."

Her eyebrows went up, slightly, and came down. That was all the comment it got. She did not ask him what he meant. She picked up the pen and wrote three or four words, not in the column but out in the margin of the page, and laid the pen down again across the book.

"Who speaks for you?"

"I do," said Lira.

It came out fast and rather loud, as though she had been holding it ready and was afraid the chance might go by. Then she stood with her arms folded and her chin up and dared the table to say anything about it.

Vell considered her for some time.

"Speaking for a boy of fourteen," she said. "Unrated. Nothing to register."

"Yes."

"You've been in my yard four months." Vell's voice stayed exactly where it was. "In four months I've not once seen you vouch for anyone."

"No."

"So why this one?"

Lira opened her mouth and then, to Cael's surprise, shut it again and thought. He watched her do it, with Vell's eyes on her, and he watched her put down the easy answers one by one, the shrug and the joke about being bored, until there was only the true one left.

"Because I asked him what he was," she said, "after eleven minutes at the board, and he didn't lie to me."

Vell held her eyes a breath longer. Then she dipped the pen and wrote a line beneath whatever it was she had put down for him. It was short, and he could not read it upside down.

"There's a Blade called Renn who keeps at me for work," Vell said, still writing. "Copper Rank 3. This season he's had eleven bouts and lost three of them. For an unrated debut tonight, he's what I have. Anybody easier isn't fighting, and nobody who's fighting is easier. He'll not go gentle on you for your age. That's not how this yard works, and you'd not thank me if it were. Somebody goes soft on you the first time, you learn something about this place that isn't so, and you pay for it later."

Cael thought of the card in the corner of the board, near the lost boot: *Renn (Cu 3, Blade) wants work. Any evening. Cinder House.* He thought of Lira in her second month, going down to Renn while Marrow collected.

"Renn, then," he said.

Vell nodded the way a man nods when a reading agrees with the gauge. "Fourth bout. After the lamps." She set the pen across the page. "Don't be late. And don't thank me. All I've done for you is put a line in a book."

---

Lira took him to the quietest place she knew, which turned out to be the roof of a cooper's shed two lanes over from the Cinder House, reached by a ladder that the cooper apparently did not know she used. It was flat and tarred and warm from the morning, with a low parapet, and from it you could see the tops of three yards and, beyond them, the first wall of the Ranked core going up the hill in the grey light. Cael thought of a different roof, with a chimney and a water tank, and a grey dog barking at a drainpipe, and then made himself stop.

"Blade," said Lira. She sat cross-legged on the tar with a stick of charcoal and drew on the roof between them. "You saw it yesterday."

"Pale light. Along the forearm, from below the elbow. Out past the fist."

"How far past?"

"Eight inches. My eye says less."

"Your eye's spent fourteen years learning how long an arm is, and it won't unlearn that in an evening." She drew an arm on the tar, and a fist, and a long thin shape past the fist, and then drew the shape again, longer. "Add the eight inches every time you look. The moment you stop adding them, you'll get hit."

"What does it do? When it hits."

"Depends how he holds it." She turned the charcoal in her fingers. "It's got an edge and it's got a flat. The edge cuts. You saw the edge yesterday, on the Stone woman's shoulder. The flat just hits, like being hit with a strap of something hot." She looked at him. "Renn will use the flat on you. On an unrated debut, everybody with a blade uses the flat, unless you give them a reason to stop. It's a courtesy. It still hurts."

"Does he give reasons to stop?"

"No. He's careful." She drew a small circle on the tar beside the arm. "That's what eleven bouts and eight wins means. Not fast, or not only fast. Careful. He doesn't take risks he doesn't need to take. Against you he won't need to take any." She looked up. "Which is good. A careful fighter is a fighter who repeats himself."

"You've fought him."

"In my second month." She drew a line through the small circle. "He put me down in the third exchange. I'd read everything he did in the first two. I'd read it perfectly. I knew exactly what he was going to do, right up to the moment I was on the ground looking at the lamps." She put the charcoal down. "He's got a thing he throws in the third exchange, when he's decided you're worth throwing it at. I'm not going to tell you what it looks like."

"Why not?"

"Because knowing what it looked like didn't help me, and it won't help you. You'll be watching for it, and you'll miss everything else, and you'll still go down." She shrugged. "If he throws it, you go down. Everybody does. That's not losing, that's arithmetic. If he throws it at you, it means you made him want to, and that's a better result than most debuts get."

Cael wrote that down. *If he throws it, you go down. That means you made him want to.*

"Watch the first exchange," Lira said. "Don't try to do anything in it. Give him ground and look. Everybody on that rope will be watching to see whether you last. You watch what he does while he still thinks you're no trouble."

"And if I can't tell the difference? Between watching and just losing?"

Lira considered that seriously, which he had not expected.

"You'll be frightened," she said. "That's fine. Everybody's frightened. I'm frightened every time, and I stopped counting the times a while ago. Being frightened's not the problem. The problem is when you get so frightened you stop looking. If you notice you've stopped looking, that's when it's not watching any more. That's when it's just being hit."

He did not say anything for a moment, because he was thinking about a page in a leather book, in Hesk's hand, which he had read three times on a wall by the merchant road with crumbs on his knees. The fifth entry. It said that fear could be a tool, so long as it kept you looking, and that the only kind worth worrying about was the kind that closed your eyes. Lira had never seen that page or heard of Hesk. She had arrived at the same place by a different road, through four lost bouts in a yard in Ardenmere, and she had said it to him on a cooper's roof in almost the same words.

This steadied him more than anything she had said all morning. Two people who had never met, and who had learned it in two different ways, had told him the same thing, and that made it a figure he could trace.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For telling me that."

Lira looked at him as if he had said something odd, and then shrugged, and picked up the charcoal again. "Hips," she said. "Do you know about hips?"

"Hands lie," said Cael. "Hips have to go where the weight goes."

She stared at him. "Who taught you that?"

"My grandfather. On Tuesdays."

"Then your grandfather," said Lira, "has saved me about an hour," and she drew a pair of feet on the tar instead, and started on those.

---

By the time the lamps were lit, the yard was full.

Lira had warned him on the walk down from the roof that it would be an ordinary sort of night: sixty people, perhaps a few more, and none of them there for the fourth bout. They had come for the second, a grudge rematch between two Iron-rated fighters. Cael found this a relief, because he had not wanted anyone to come for him.

The lanterns hung on their hooks along the three walls and threw a warm, uneven light, bright in the middle of the circle and soft at the edges, and outside the rope the faces of the crowd came and went in the swing of the lamps, so that he could see a mouth and then not see it, an eye and then not.

He did not watch the first three bouts; he watched Renn.

Renn was on the far side of the yard, by the wall, with two friends, and for most of the evening he did not do anything at all. He stood with his weight on one leg and his arms folded and watched the bouts with mild interest, and talked to his friends between them, and laughed twice, a man of twenty or close to it.

He was built heavily through the chest and shoulders, not tall, and he stood like a man who had carried heavy things for a living, with his knees a little soft and his back straight. When he walked to the water bucket and back, his stride was short and even, each foot set down as though he meant to stand on it a while.

Once, while the third bout was being called, he looked across the yard and caught Cael looking at him. He nodded, the nod you give somebody across a market, not unfriendly and not especially interested, and then he went back to talking to his friends.

Marrow was working the rope with his slate, and when he passed the barrel at the corner, Cael read the slate without moving his head. *Renn*, it said, and beside it a number so small it was barely worth betting on. *Unrated, debut*, and beside that a much larger number. And underneath them both, on a line of its own, *Over-under: one exchange.*

"What does that mean?" he said quietly. "The last line."

Lira had seen it too. Her face did not change. "It means Marrow's taking bets on whether you last more than one exchange," she said. "And his book says you don't."

"Is that a good bet?"

"For most debuts," said Lira, "it's free money."

At the table, Vell stood up. "Fourth bout," she said, and the yard did not go quite as quiet as it had for the first one, and somewhere along the rope a man laughed at a joke Cael never heard.

Cael stepped over the rope.

The groove was a hand deep and he felt it under his boot as he crossed it, a dip and then level ground. Inside the circle the dirt had been swept and watered, and it was firmer than it looked, packed down by years of feet into something nearly as hard as a floor. The lamplight was very bright in the middle, brighter than it had looked from the barrel, and he walked to the near side of the circle, stopped just inside the groove, and turned round.

Renn was on the far mark before him, working each shoulder round in turn, unhurried.

From the cooper's roof, Lira's voice came back to him in its own clipped rhythm. *Add the eight inches. Out loud, in your head. First exchange, you look. Give him ground. Look.*

"Renn," said Vell. "Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Against unrated, debut. Vouched." A small pause, so small that probably nobody else in the yard noticed it. "Begin when you're ready."

Renn brought the blade up. It came out of nothing along his right forearm, as the young man's had the afternoon before, but it was not the same: it was steadier, and it did not make the faint high singing sound when he turned his wrist. It simply stood there in the lamplight, pale and hard and straight, with a thin bright line along one edge. To Cael's eye, before Renn had moved at all, it stopped about a hand past the knuckles. He made himself say that twice in his head, and disbelieve it twice.

*Plus eight inches.* He said it. *Plus eight.* He saw it, then, the way a drawing on a page changes when somebody tells you what it is: the real length of it, the place where the edge actually ended in the air, a long way further out than his eye wanted to believe.

Then Renn started toward him, strolling, and the first exchange taught him three things, in order.

The first was that an amble from a Copper Rank 3 covered ground faster than other people walked. Renn did not seem to hurry, and then he was there, inside the distance Cael had thought was safe, and the blade was coming.

The second was that Renn opened with probes. Two of them, loose, one high toward the head and one across toward the body, thrown off his front foot with no weight in them at all, like a man tapping a wall to find out where the hollow part is. They were meant to be seen, and Cael saw them. He gave ground along the curve of the groove, left, and the first went past his face with a faint hiss of air and the second went across the front of him with room to spare, hands high, knees bent, his arms doing nothing except being in the way.

At the rope, someone said something about a rabbit, and somebody else laughed.

The third thing was the important one, and he nearly missed it, and then he did not.

Renn's weight went back.

It went back through his rear foot, into the heel, and settled there, and his whole frame seemed to gather itself onto that heel the way a spring gathers when you press it. The probes had been front-foot work, light as talk, and this was something else: the frame loading. And in the instant after the heel settled—

The blade came round.

Cael saw it start and was a breath late, so that most of him got clear and not all of him. The flat caught him across the meat of the left shoulder as it went past, and it landed like an iron bar left too long in a stove, a broad hard slap that spun him a half-turn on his feet and drove the breath out of him in a grunt. Along the rope the crowd made its first real sound of the night, a pleased murmur that meant the evening was going the way everyone had paid for.

He did not fall. He finished the turn three steps back with his left arm hanging and his shoulder singing, and he was still on his feet, and still inside the groove.

Renn did not come after him but walked back to his mark and waited for Cael to find his, and he rolled his shoulders again while he waited, in no hurry about anything. Cael could find nothing in his face to be afraid of, and nothing to be flattered by. Cael was a few minutes' exercise for a rated man between bigger bouts, the kind of evening a careful fighter takes to keep his arm warm, and Renn bore him no ill will and spared him no thought.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

The shoulder hurt in a bright, exact way, as though it had been drawn on him with a hot wire. Cael put the hurt in a box and set the box down, and kept the heel out where he could see it.

*Probes off the front foot,* he thought. *Truth off the back heel. Between heel and blade there's a gap, and that one I spent finding it.*

*Next time it's mine.*

The second exchange began the same way: Renn ambled in, and the probes came, high and across, off the front foot, loose and conversational, and Cael gave ground on the curve, and did not look at the probes at all. He looked at the heel.

It went back. It settled.

And this time Cael went when the heel went, without waiting to see the blade it was loading. He went left and back, early, before the blade had started to come round, a whole quarter of a second sooner than he had moved the first time—

The strike did not come round.

Renn dropped instead. He went low, very fast, his blade arm flung out wide and high as a counterweight, the pale edge drawing a bright line up into the dark above the lamps, and his whole heavy body swinging round, leg first, through the place where Cael's ankles ought to have been. He was going for the legs. There was no harm meant in it. It was the answer a careful fighter keeps for a beginner who has worked out one trick and is too pleased with it, and it ends with the beginner flat on the ground and the rope laughing.

But Cael had already gone, early, before the sweep had committed to anything, and the leg came round through an empty place where his ankles had been half a beat before. He jumped the tail of the swing, clumsily, arms flung out like a boy clearing a ditch, and landed with both feet under him, and found Renn right in front of him.

The bigger man was still crouched from the swing, twisted half round, his hips facing Cael full on and his blade flung up and out on the far side. A short arm could have reached him. For the space of half a beat there was nothing at all between Cael and the middle of him.

Cael saw it. He saw all of it, the hips and the turned shoulder and the blade on the wrong side, and he wrote it down in his head as clearly as if he had had the notebook in his hand.

He did nothing with it.

Then the moment shut. Renn came up out of the sweep in one smooth motion, stepped back two paces, and stood looking at Cael across the bright middle of the circle. And then he rolled his shoulders: left, then right, slowly, settling them, as he had before the first exchange. Cael filed it at once beside the heel. *Shoulders when he changes his mind.*

There was a half-smile on Renn's face, not for Cael exactly but for the situation.

Something had shifted in the sound along the rope; it was no louder than before, but it had lost the comfortable note of people who already knew the ending, and here and there someone had stopped talking to watch.

At the table, Vell's pen moved again, and for longer.

When Renn came in for the third exchange, he did not amble.

He came balanced and quiet, with his weight in the middle of his feet, and the probes, when they came, were crisper, shorter, with something in them now. Something had been decided across the circle: Renn had stopped exercising, found a puzzle in front of him, and meant to finish it.

The probes came, high and then across, and Cael gave ground, and watched the heel.

The heel settled.

And then he was somewhere else.

He was off the line of the strike without knowing when he had left it. His weight was already through, his feet already placed, and the blade went past through the place where his chest had been, and he had not seen it go. He had not read it. Two exchanges and a shoulder he had paid for that quarter-second between heel and blade, and when the heel came down the quarter-second was simply not there to use. There was the heel, and then there was this.

The second strike grew straight out of the end of the first: the same cut, turned and sent back, slanting low across into the place where anyone who had got clear of the first would have to be standing.

He was not standing there.

He was standing outside its arc, with his weight already turning, and he had not chosen that either. His mind arrived at each new place after his body was already in it, and found it lit and waiting, and could not account for having come.

The third strike came up from below.

He did not see it start. He only felt the air of it, close, very close, a hand's width from his ribs on the left side, ripping upward past him into the empty space above the circle, and he heard the blade's faint hiss die away at the top of its arc. The yard had gone so silent that he could hear the lanterns creaking on their hooks.

He stood still, his heart hammering hard enough to feel in his teeth, with no notion at all of what he had just done.

*The watching,* he told himself, because whatever had happened needed a name, and any name would do for now. *Two exchanges of watching. The body learns.* He set the explanation over the thing and saw at once that it did not fit, as a lid cut for a smaller jar does not fit. Two exchanges' study of a single fighter could not have bought that. Nothing he could put his finger on came to that. And the middle of a bout was no place to stand doing arithmetic.

He left the lid where it sat, crooked, and got his hands up.

Because Renn was still there, a pace and a half away, finishing the figure, his weight carried all the way through and up onto his front foot, his blade high and his ribs open. It was the second time tonight Cael had stood within reach of a fully committed Copper Rank 3. He had read nothing this time and got out anyway, and now the man was open, and Cael did the only thing he knew how to do, which was nothing he had ever practised.

He threw a short straight punch at Renn's shoulder.

Renn did not even turn toward it. His left forearm brushed it off, idly, as a man brushes off a fly, without breaking his stride and without bothering to make a guard of it. Then he stepped off, back to his mark, and the third exchange was over.

Cael had not touched him all night.

He knew that, standing on his mark with his fist still stinging from the forearm. He had read two exchanges and paid for one of them with a shoulder. Then something had happened in the third that he could not account for, and in the middle of it he had been handed the cleanest opening of the evening and had answered it with a punch a child could have swatted. Seeing a strike coming was one craft and doing anything about it was another. He had spent two days, and an hour on a cooper's roof, and two exchanges in this circle building the first. He owned none of the second.

Across the circle, for the first time all night, Renn was paying attention to him: the narrow, careful attention of a tradesman holding up a part that has come off his own lathe in a shape he did not cut. He said nothing, because fighters did not talk mid-bout, but his shoulders went round again, left and right, and something in the way he stood packed itself tighter, lower, more closely round its own centre.

*There's his serious weight,* Cael thought. *Eight wins in eleven, with the holding-back taken off.*

The fourth exchange lasted four seconds.

This time there were no probes, no amble, no tapping at the wall. Renn came straight off his mark and threw a figure that was cousin to the one in the third exchange, strikes folded into each other, each one coming out of the end of the last. But it was stepped up past some point where reading it stopped being any use, because every strike shut the door the one before it had left open, and the whole of it came so fast that everything earlier in the evening seemed, looking back, to have been good manners.

The first fold Cael saw whole, heel and strike, and he moved for it, late.

He saw the second fold partly.

And then, though he had not decided to, he was reaching for the thing that had moved his feet in the third exchange, feeling about for it like a man patting his pockets for a key he had been holding a moment before.

There was nothing there. There were only his own plain reflexes, the ones that had been late all evening, and they were late again, and they had nothing to spare.

The flat of the blade caught his right side across the ribs. It was a clean, driving, expert blow, with all of Renn's weight behind it this time, and it lifted him off his feet and turned him in the air and put him down on his side in the packed dirt with every bit of breath gone out of him at once.

The ground pressed cold against his cheek, and overhead the lamps rocked on their hooks, or else the yard did. The crowd had become a long way off, much further than the rope, and it was shouting.

"Match," said Vell.

She did not raise her voice, and did not need to. The one word reached every corner of the yard, the shouting and the lamplight and the boy lying in the dirt, and after it nothing else that happened in the circle counted.

# Chapter 56 — Being Seen

He had written to Hesk on the night of the fourth stair, all of it, by the bead of Yeni's lamp, and posted it in the west bag at noon the next day. Darrow Innes and the board and the fourteen names. Vell's three things, and the one he had kept only to the letter. Lira's room, and what he had said in it, which had been harder to write down than to say. *I'm telling you the next thing as it comes,* he had written at the end, *including the part where I did it wrong.*

So when the letter from Hesk was against the salt crock on the Monday, he thought for a moment, coming in at the kitchen door, that it was the answer. Then he saw the date in the corner, in Hesk's square hand, and counted, and knew that it could not be. It had been written the night after Coss's pie stall and the cooper's roof. It had been a week on the road. It had passed his own somewhere between here and Denvash, two letters going opposite ways in two leather bags on two carts, and neither of them knowing.

He took it out to the low wall by the pump. The pear tree had finished with its flowers. The last of them lay brown in the corner of the yard where Torvin's wife had swept them, and the tree had put out small green leaves in their place, all at once, as if it had changed its mind about something.

He broke the seal.

*Cael,*

*You asked me a question a month ago and I said I'd answer it when I had an answer I'd sign.*

It was the question. He had asked it in the Ilsev letter, at the end, on the night of the roof after the post. *If whoever made that mark wanted me gone, would any of that matter?* Hesk had kept it. *Your question I'm keeping.* And now here it was, answered, in a letter written before Hesk could possibly have known that the question had stopped being a question and become a card on every board in the district.

He read the clock. Three families, and a card woman, and one loose spring the size of a fingernail paring. He could see Hesk's bench as he read it, the lamp and the drawer marked *Springs, fine*, and the back off a thing that everybody else had only told stories about. Then he came to the part that was the answer, and stopped, and read it again with the pear leaves moving over the page.

*It isn't a wall. I won't tell you it is. A wall would keep things out, and nothing you've got there will keep anybody out who's set on coming in. But a thing that's known can't be put in a drawer without somebody asking where it went.*

*A drawer.*

Hesk did not know about the drawer. He could not know. Coss had said it at a pie stall in Ardenmere, on the same day Hesk was writing this in Denvash, and nobody had told either of them what the other had said. And Hesk had reached into the dark from his own bench, a week behind, and put his hand on the very word.

He sat very still on the wall for a while. Then he read on.

*A thing that's talked about gets brought to a bench, in the end, and somebody takes the back off. The quiet things are the ones that go out with the ashes.*

*Being seen is not the same as being safe. But it's not nothing.*

He read that twice. It was the plainest sentence in the letter. It did not promise him anything. Hesk never promised him things he could not put his name under, and he had not done it here. He had said exactly as much as was true and stopped, the way he stopped a measurement at the last mark he trusted.

*Keep being known. Keep me told. I'm proud of you past anything I've the words for, and I'll say so properly when I've found them.*

*— H.*

He sat with it on his knee. *I'll say so properly when I've found them.* That was like Hesk too. He would not say a thing until he could say it right, and he would rather leave it in a letter as a promise than send it half made. Somewhere in Denvash, at a bench, a man was still looking for words, and had told him so.

He did not copy anything into the Log. He folded the letter along its own folds and put it in the front of the Log, inside the cover, on top of the other one, so that the two lay together. *You're describing a home.* And *it's not nothing.*

Lira read it that evening on the side wall of the Cinder House yard, after the post, with the bread. She read it twice, as she read all Hesk's letters, and when she came to the drawer she stopped and looked at him over the paper and did not say anything. When she came to the end she folded it and gave it back to him.

"He doesn't know about Darrow," she said.

"Not yet. Mine's on the road. They'll have passed each other."

"He doesn't need to." She tore the bread and gave him the bigger half. "He's answered it anyway. He's only answered it before you asked."

---

He tried them both together on the Tuesday night, in Torvin's yard, facing the trough.

He had not meant to. It was not in the plan; nothing borrowed was in the plan, and he and Lira had settled that on the first morning of the straw post without either of them having to say it. But it had been sitting in him since Feryn's wall like a question at the end of a page. There were two of them now, side by side in the dark behind his breastbone, the leaning one and the heavy one. He had only ever picked up one at a time.

So he stood with his back to the house and the moon on the whitewash, and went in.

He found the Wind first, as he always did, and held it, the readiness, the tilt. It was slow coming, but it came, a little, the ground going short under him by a finger's width. Then, without letting go of it, he reached past it for the other one.

The Wind went out of his hands as soon as he reached. It did not fade. It was simply gone, the way a thought goes when somebody says your name. He stood there with nothing.

He tried again. This time he went for the heavy one first, and began to gather it, the long breath, the middle going still and thick; he could see it start in his shadow on the wall. Then, half through the breath, he reached back for the Wind. The gathering broke. It did not go forward, or anywhere. It only came apart in the middle of him, like a stack of bricks with the bottom one pulled, and went nowhere at all, and the water in the trough lay flat and still with the moon in it and did not so much as shiver.

His arms went heavy anyway. That was the worst of it. He had got nothing, and the wet sand came into both arms from the shoulder down all the same, as if he had.

He sat on the low wall for most of an hour with his hands in his lap and thought about it.

One at a time. Slowly. The Wind came when it liked, and when he asked it came late and small. The other needed a whole breath, and showed in his middle like a stone in a sack, and cost him his arms. And the two of them would not sit in his hands at the same time; reaching for one let go of the other. He could not do it. He was not sure there was any *it* to do.

In the morning in the grey half he told Lira. She listened with the chalk in her hand. Then she went to the canvas hanging on its nail on the wall, where the post's columns had gone to keep them safe from the straw. Under the *P*, below the last ring, she made one stroke with no ring round it, and beside it, in her smallest letters, *both. nothing. arms anyway.*

She looked at it.

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"Now we know." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Now neither of us is going to stand at that barrel on Sunday hoping for something that isn't there." She went back to the straw post, and slapped it once with her open hand, so that it went *whuff*. "This is what's there. Five. Same place."

Feryn came back up the river on the Thursday with his coat buttoned to the chin and a cold, and three pages of notes folded in his pocket. He spread them out on the low wall at the side of the Cinder House yard, weighted with stones, while the Thursday card went on behind them in the circle.

"I went down and watched him," he said. "Saturday, at the coal wharf. Against a Force woman from the salt flats, Copper Rank 8, very good. He knew I was there. He didn't care." Feryn sneezed. "And I found three people up and down that river who've been in a circle with him and were willing to talk about it for the price of supper. Which you owe me, by the way. Both of you."

Lira had come round the rope. She sat on the wall on Feryn's other side and pulled the nearest page toward her.

"Tell it," she said.

"First thing. He gives you the first exchange." Feryn put his finger on the top of the first page. "Free. He hardly blocks and he hardly slips. He stands there and lets you empty your pockets into him, and watches you do it. The Force woman put a shove into him that would have moved a cart. He went back a foot. That's all." He looked at Cael. "He wants your best on the table early. All of it, where you can both see it. So that you both know."

"Know what?"

"That it's no good." Feryn sneezed again, and wiped his nose, and did not smile. "That's the whole of him, the way I see it. The Path's only what he stands on. What actually beats people is what they start thinking in the second exchange, when they've given him their best and he's still standing there looking mildly interested. The woman from the salt flats was good. She was better than good. By the third exchange she was hitting him like she was trying to wake somebody up, and by the fourth she'd stopped trying at all, and he put her down very gently and helped her up." He tapped the page. "She told me afterwards she didn't remember deciding to stop. She only remembered noticing she had."

Cael thought about Doss at the kitchen table. *You stop believing your hands.*

"You've lost to that," said Lira. It was not a question.

"Not to him." Feryn was quiet for a moment. He folded the corner of the page over, and then flattened it out again.

"To an Iron at Copper," he said, when I was twenty, in a tannery yard in a town you've never heard of. He wasn't very good. He wasn't a patch on Darrow. And I hit him for three exchanges and in the fourth I stood there with my hands up and I couldn't make them go. I'd stopped believing they'd do anything." He looked at the circle, where a Blade was getting up off the dirt. "I've never told anybody that. I've been telling people for four years I lost because the yard was wet."

Nobody said anything for a while. Lira, who had been looking at the notes, looked at Feryn instead, and then back at the notes, and Cael saw that she had put it away somewhere to be careful of.

"What did they say it felt like?" he said. "The three. Hitting him."

Feryn turned to the second page.

"They all said the same thing, and none of them had met each other, and none of them had the words." He ran his finger down it. "It goes away. That's all any of them could tell me. You hit him and it goes away from you, *in* and *back*, and *down*, one of them said, toward the back of him. Like hitting a man who's leaning on a door you can't see."

"Which side?"

Feryn looked up.

"Which side of him does it go to?" said Cael. "When it goes back and down. Left or right?"

"I didn't ask." Feryn frowned at the page. "I didn't think to. Does it matter?"

"How does he stand?"

"Right foot forward. Left back, and set. He sets that back foot as if he's nailing it to the floor; you can see it go down." Feryn lifted his own left heel a little off the stones, and put it down hard, to show. "He never moves it much. The front foot does all the walking."

Cael had a bill of the mender's in his pocket, for washers, and a stub of pencil. He turned the bill over on the wall and drew a man on the back of it as Corvane had taught him to draw a man, a stick figure, with the right foot forward and the left foot back and planted. Then he drew a line from the right shoulder, where a strike might land, across the chest and down. He drew another from the right ribs, across. Another from the jaw, down the neck and across. Another from the left shoulder, straight down. And all of them, if they were going back and down toward a left foot nailed to the floor, had to go through the same narrow place to get there: the low left side, where the ribs stopped and the hip began. Below that there was only one leg for any of it to go down.

He looked at it. Everything he had drawn ran downhill to one drain.

"You're drawing him like a gutter," said Feryn, looking over his shoulder.

"Everything has to get to the back foot," said Cael slowly. "Wherever it goes in. And there's one way down to the back foot, on that side. So everything crosses there." He put the point of the pencil on the low left side of the stick figure, where all the lines met. "There."

Lira had leaned over too. She looked at the drawing for a long time without saying anything.

"The chalk-board woman," she said. "He'd stand a moment after a good one. Setting the bucket down."

"Doss said it too."

"Putting it down that leg." Lira touched the drawing with one finger, just above the pencil point, very lightly, as if it might hurt it. "If it all goes through there, and he has to stop a moment every time to get it down—"

"Then if you put the next one in there while he's still putting the last one down," said Cael. "While it's still crossing."

Feryn looked from one of them to the other.

"That," he said, "is either the cleverest thing I've heard about Iron in eight years of listening, or the two of you have drawn a man on the back of a washer bill and fallen in love with the drawing." He sneezed. "Which is it?"

"I don't know yet," said Cael. He wrote, small, beside the point where the lines met: *the crossing.* Then, under it, because he would not call a thing true that he had only drawn: *Claim. Evidence: 3 (h), Doss (h), Stone woman (h). Ruling: pending.*

"Then find out," said Feryn, and folded his notes, and blew his nose. "Somebody owes me a supper."

On the tenth night they sat on the front steps of Torvin's after the lamps, which the widow would have said was too late, except that it was Torvin's steps and not hers.

It had been a long day. The five white marks on the straw would fit under one of Lira's fingers now, more often than not, and in the afternoon she had started making him do it moving: back three steps, in two, five strikes to one place while she pushed him about with her free hand, and then out again before she could catch his wrist. His knuckles were raw under the chalk. His shoulders ached in a new place, low, from throwing short. He sat on the third step with his forearms on his knees and the street going quiet in front of him: a cart being backed into a yard somewhere, a window shutting, a dog asking a question at the end of the lane and getting no answer.

Lira sat on the step above him with her knees drawn up. She had been quiet since the lamps, and he had learned that her quiet usually had something at the far end of it.

"I've been adding a thing all day," she said at last. "I can't make it come out."

"Say it."

"Say you win." She was looking at the street, not at him. "Say it all goes the way you drew it on Feryn's bill, and you put a Bronze down in front of three hundred people, and Vell writes your name. Then it's Monday." She turned her head. "I don't mean Monday at the post. I mean Monday in a drawer at regional. What's different in there?"

He thought about it properly, because she had asked him to.

"Nothing," he said. "The drawer's the same drawer. The mark's the same mark. Legal's as slow the Monday after as the Monday before." He turned his raw knuckles over and looked at them in the lamplight from the window behind. "Whoever put a mark on a docket isn't going to rub it off because a boy won something in a yard by the river."

"Then why?"

"Because—" He stopped. He knew the answer. He had known it at the board. But he could not find the shape of it out loud, and he sat there on the step with it half made in his mouth.

"It doesn't change what you are," said Lira. "It changes who'd have to explain themselves if something happened to you."

He turned round on the step and looked up at her.

She was still looking at the street. "It's his letter," she said. "Your grandfather's. I've been turning it over since Monday. *A thing that's known can't be put in a drawer without somebody asking where it went.*" She said the words carefully, as if they belonged to somebody else and she was handing them back. "Right now, if you went, the only person who'd have to explain it would be a man at a desk, to a drawer. After Sunday, if you went, he'd have to explain it to the bread woman. And Vell. And the boys. And three hundred people who paid at the gate." She put her chin on her knees. "That's not safe. He said it isn't. But somebody would have to stand up somewhere and say where you'd gone. And they'd have to say it to a lot of people."

He looked at her for a long time.

"You had it first," he said.

"I always have it first when it's you." She did not smile. "Now say the other part. The part you've not said to anybody."

He looked back at the street.

"I'm frightened I've read it wrong," he said. "Not Darrow. Them. I've read the whole district like a stance, and I've decided what it'll do if I go missing, and I've never once seen it do it." He made himself go on. "And there's a worse one. That I'll have made myself the easiest thing in Ardenmere to find. That somebody I haven't even thought of will look up because of the noise. And I'll have done that, and not you, and not Hesk."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"Good," she said.

He looked round.

"You'd be a fool if you weren't." She drew her knees in tighter. "Put it on the post. A stroke for it, and a ring, and *frightened* beside it in small letters. Then hit the straw anyway." She looked down at him then, straight, for the first time since they had sat down. "And you didn't do it, by the way. We did. On Feryn's bill. I'm in this as far as you are, and I chose it, on my own bed, in the dark, and nobody asked me to."

"I should have."

"You should have." She bumped his shoulder with her knee, not hard. "I'm still checking, you know. About that. Every day. I'll go on checking for a while."

"I know."

"Good."

The dog at the end of the lane asked its question again, and this time somebody's door opened and somebody told it to be quiet, and it was. They sat on the steps a while longer without saying anything, and it was not the kind of quiet that needed filling.

On the Wednesday before the bout, at first light, Coss was standing at the gate in the wall of the Cinder House yard.

Cael saw him from the straw post and did not know him for a moment, because he had never once thought of Coss in this yard. Coss had never come near it. In the autumn he had walked every lane of the district and asked nothing about the yards, and in the spring he had sat at a pie stall in his Sunday coat. He had not once come up the lane to the place where the boy actually fought.

He was here now, in the brown coat, and the brown coat was creased all down one side as if he had slept in it on a bench. He had not shaved. He stood in the gate with one hand on the post of it and looked at the yard as if it were somewhere he had read about and had not expected to be so small: the groove, the rope on its stakes, the barrel, Vell's empty table in the shade of the back wall. Then he looked at Cael, at the straw post, and at the chalk on Cael's knuckles.

Lira had seen him too. She looked at Coss, and then at Cael, and without a word she took the chalk and went down to the far end of the grey half and began going through her own corners alone, where she could see everything and hear nothing.

Cael went across the yard to the gate.

"I came on the night mail," said Coss. His voice was hoarse. "From regional. I've two days without pay, and I've spent one of them on a cart, and I'll spend the other going back on the noon one." He looked past Cael at the post. "Is that what you do?"

"Some of it."

Coss nodded slowly. Then, as if he had decided to stop putting it off, he took a folded paper out of his inside pocket and held it out.

It was a district return. Cael had seen the form at the post, at the clerk's counter: a long sheet that went to regional every week, with boundaries and permits and pigs. Halfway down it, in the clerk's careful hand, under *Public notices posted in the district*, somebody had copied Vell's card word for word. *LAST BOUT, SUNDAY FORTNIGHT. DARROW INNES (Br 1, Iron Path) against assessed Cu (vouched).* Somebody else had drawn a single neat line under it in red.

"Your post's clerk copies every board in the district into his return," said Coss. "He always has. It's in the form. Nobody's read that column in twenty years." He took the paper back and folded it. "Somebody read this one. My section head put it on my desk on Monday, with that line under it, and went away. He didn't say anything. Last time he said a view. This time he didn't say anything at all."

He put the paper away.

"That's what I came to tell you. That nobody's saying anything. You don't know these people, and I don't either, not really, but I know the floor I work on. When it stops talking, it's because somebody's been told it needn't." He looked at Cael. "You've made it loud. Loud gets answered. I don't know what the answer is. I don't know who'd give it. I only know there's a desk somewhere that's read this, and I can't see it from mine."

"I know."

"Then don't do it." Coss's voice cracked on it, and he stopped, and started again, lower. "Not like this. Pull out. Say it's the ribs. Say anything you like. Nobody in this world ever blamed a boy for a sprain. Vell would take it. The Iron man would take it; he's touring, there's a gate every Saturday on that river." He put his hand flat on the gatepost, hard, as he had put his hands flat on the table either side of a pie. "Go back to being a line in brackets for a season. Let legal sit. Let them forget."

Cael looked at him.

He had never seen Coss like this. He had seen him level at a desk with a stamp, and careful in a reading room, and very still over a pie he had not eaten. He had never seen him not know what to do with his hands.

"I was quiet," Cael said. "All autumn. I did everything you'd have told me to. I kept my head down and my Path to myself and won where nobody was looking, and I didn't fight anybody anybody had heard of." He held Coss's eyes. "And a man walked a sweep through the market five weeks after I came, that you couldn't find the order for. And a mark went on my file the hour it was opened, that Ilsev couldn't find the end of. And then you came here in your own coat to tell me there was a view." He kept his voice level. "Quiet didn't stop any of it. Quiet's only what made it easy."

Coss did not answer.

"Somebody else wants me gone," said Cael. "Not you. Lira worked that out on a roof the day you came, and I think she's right, and I think you think so too. Somebody I've never met has decided I'd be better in a drawer, and they don't need a reason anybody can read. I can't out-quiet that." He looked at the creased brown coat, and the hand on the gatepost. "I've tried. I was better at it than anybody. It's the one thing they've already got."

Coss stood there for a long time with his hand on the post of the gate.

Then he took his hand away, and looked at it, as if he had not known it was there. He sat down on the end of the trestle bench just inside the gate, the one the girls from the wall used when there was nobody fighting, and put his elbows on his knees.

"I've a daughter," he said.

Cael did not say anything.

"She'll be fourteen at midsummer. She does her lessons at the kitchen table with her tongue between her teeth." Coss looked at the dirt between his boots. "When I go out on a file she asks me at the door whether this one's going to be interesting, and I tell her they never are. I told her that in the autumn, about yours." He was quiet a moment. "I haven't told her anything since. There's nothing in it I could tell a child."

He turned his hat round in his hands.

"I sit and watch her do her sums, and I think about your file. Thin, and odd, and walking off its road in three places. And I think, if it were hers, what would I want?" He shook his head slowly. "I don't know. What I'd want her to do, I couldn't tell you. I know what I'd want done for her. I'd want somebody to come and say it to her face, whatever they had, in their own coat. Not across a desk." He looked up. "That's all this is. It's no use to you. I know it's no use."

"It's some use."

Coss looked at him for a while longer. Then he stood up, slowly, a man who had been on a cart all night, and put his hat on.

"Then don't do it by halves," he said. "If you're going to be loud, don't stop at loud enough." He looked once more round the yard, at the post and the straw and the groove and Lira going through her corners at the far end without once looking their way. "I'll not come again before Sunday. I can't. I've no days left."

He went out through the gate. Cael watched him go down the lane in the grey light, in the creased coat, without looking back this time, toward the river and the noon cart.

Lira came back up the grey half when he was out of sight.

"He's frightened for you," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She held out the chalk. "Somebody up there ought to be."

That night, at the very back of the Log, below the line about the drawer, he wrote: *Coss has a daughter. Fourteen at midsummer. He came on the night mail on his own days to tell me nobody was saying anything.* He sat looking at it. Then he wrote one more line under it, and did not know quite why: *He asked me what he'd want if it were her. I think he was asking himself, and he came all this way to do it out loud.*

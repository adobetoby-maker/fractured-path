# Chapter 25 — Spending and Banking

"Quiz," said Lira, on the morning of the fiftieth day, in the grey half, with the frost still white along the bottom of the east wall.

They had stopped the box drill early. She had not given him a report that morning. She had said, coming in through the gate, that there was nothing in him today worth reporting except nerves, and she did not count nerves, because everybody had them and they told you nothing.

"Tired Dessa," she said. "What gives her away before the frame's up?"

"Back foot comes down late. Half a beat. The frame's up and the foot isn't under it."

"When?"

"At the end. Once a bout. After she's re-formed it so many times that the last one comes late."

"How many times have you seen it?"

"Twice."

"Once each bout."

"Once each bout."

"Never twice in the same one."

"No."

Lira walked round him once, slowly, in the grey half, the way she walked round the post before she struck it.

"And if she goes low instead? If she sweeps before you've done anything?"

"She doesn't. She only sweeps to take back ground somebody's taken off her. She did it four times in two bouts and every time she'd just been moved."

"Two bouts," said Lira.

He waited.

"Two bouts isn't a pattern. It's two bouts." She did not say it unkindly. She said it in the voice she used for his feet. "You're good at this, Cael. You're better at it than anybody I've ever watched. You're also fourteen. Twice is all you've watched her, from a plank, and neither time was it you across from her." She stopped in front of him. "Keep a corner for a Dessa you've never met."

All through the strikes at the post he kept turning it.

Lira had had him at the post every morning for the two weeks, after the box drill and before the bread. She had given him one strike, only one, short and straight from the hip, with the back foot driving and the shoulder following and the fist arriving last, a hundred times a morning. It was the first thing she had ever taught him that was for hitting somebody rather than for not being hit. She had said, on the first morning of it, that if he was going to have one strike he had better have it properly, and that one strike he owned was worth ten he had seen.

He had it properly now. He could feel it in the morning when he woke, in his back foot and his hip, the way he could feel the box drill.

"What is she waiting for?" he said, between strikes.

Lira looked at him.

"I wrote it at the bottom of the fourth page. She waits better than anybody I've seen. I don't think she does it to win. I think she decided to be like that first and the winning came after." He struck the post. "If I knew what she was waiting for, I'd know more about her guard than any number of feints will tell me."

Lira did not answer for a while. She took the bread off the top of the post and tore it, and gave him the larger half.

"Everybody's waiting for something," she said at last. "Most people don't know what. I'm not sure she does." She ate. "But that's a better question than anything else in your four pages. Keep hold of it."

---

The yard was full by the time the lanterns were lit, and the benches were out though it was a Thursday, because the public house knew a full yard when it saw one coming.

He came early, the way he came everywhere, and stood by the water bucket in the corner by the drain and made himself look at things.

The rope was three deep on the shaded side and two deep in the sun, though there was no sun, only lanterns and the cold coming down out of a clear sky. The benches were full to the third row. There were faces he knew in all of them now, and that was a new thing, and he looked at it as carefully as he had looked at anything.

The fish man with the beard was in the second row with his arms folded. Torvin's wife was by the gate, and beside her the woman who slept in her boots, eating a sweet bun. The two errand boys were at opposite ends of the rope, as far from each other as the yard allowed, and both of them were watching him and not each other, which he thought was probably the first time in their lives they had both looked at the same thing. Further along the rope stood the girls from the wall in the lane of yards. Brenna was near the front with her two friends, her face already red, though she had done nothing yet but stand there.

Doss was by the gate in the wall, leaning on it, with his bad leg out straight and his pipe unlit in his hand. He must have come before his night at the warehouses. He saw Cael looking, and did not nod, and did not look away either.

Marrow came along the rope with his slate.

Cael read it without moving his head. *Dessa*, and a short price. *Unrated*, and a long one. But the long one was not as long as it would have been a month ago, and he could see why. Somebody had been paying on him, a little, all week, and Marrow had moved the number to meet them. Under the two names, on its own line, *Over/under: 5 exch.*

Marrow came level with the water bucket and looked at him for a moment, pleasantly, and for a moment longer than pleasant needed. Then he wrote something on the slate with his chalk and went on.

"Second bout," said Vell.

The yard did not go quiet. It went lower. He had heard it go lower for Dessa on both Sundays, and now it was going lower for her again, and he found it strange to stand inside the sound and know that some small part of it was for him.

Dessa came across the yard toward him before he had reached the rope.

Her eyes never once went to the rope or the benches. She came straight to where he stood by the bucket and stopped in front of him, square and solid, with her hair tied back so tightly that it pulled at the corners of her eyes. Close to, she was older than he had thought from the benches, nearer twenty than not, with a small white scar through one eyebrow and a face that did not seem to do very much.

"Vell will have told you," she said. "I want to hear the terms from you."

"She told me."

"Then say them."

So he said them, to her face, the way he had said them to Vell on the first morning, in his own words and his own order, slowly, checking each one before he set it down. Unrated. He fought who Vell put across from him. It ended when Vell called it, and not before, and not for any other reason. Nobody stopped it but Vell. What happened in it was his to carry, whichever way it went. Nobody was making him.

Dessa listened to all of it without moving. When he had finished she looked at him for a breath longer, the way he had seen her look at the Fenrow man's feet.

"Third row," she said. "Both Sundays. I saw you."

"Yes."

"With a book." It was not a question. "Good." She turned and went to her side of the circle.

He stepped over the rope.

---

"Dessa," said Vell, from her table. "Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause, which was the same small pause it always was. "Begin when you're ready."

Dessa set her feet. The grit round her boots shivered and lay flat. Her hands came up a little, loose, in front of her hips, and her eyes went to his feet and stayed there.

The first exchange cost him the skin off two knuckles and bought him one number.

He had decided on the bench, on the first Sunday, that he would not be quick with her. The Fenrow man had been quick. He had thrown everything he owned at her in his first exchange and walked back to his mark afterward flapping his hands as if he had shut them in a door, and Dessa had not so much as shifted a heel. Whatever you brought her in a hurry, she kept. So Cael went in at three-quarter pace, and asked.

He asked the high line first, a touch toward her face with nothing behind it. Her left forearm was waiting before the touch got there. He let it land anyway, lightly, to learn what it was like, and it was not like a block at all; it was like putting his hand against a gatepost somebody had sunk in the dirt. He asked her left side, a step across, and the whole frame came round to face him in one piece while her boots stayed exactly where they were. He asked her body, low, a feint he never meant to finish, and her weight went over by the width of a thumb and came straight home.

The same feint again, and it moved her less. A third time, and it did not move her at all.

*Three,* he thought. *Three, the same as the Fenrow man got. Lie to her once and she believes you. By the third time she knows your lies better than she knows your name.*

Two more touches, one on each forearm, to be sure. They were like knocking at a house where everybody was at home and nobody was coming down. She did not answer with a strike. She did not follow him when he drew off. She stood on her mark with the grit flat round her and let him spend, and he spent, and when he circled back to his own mark all he had to show for it was a sting across the right knuckles and the count of three.

Along the benches a man laughed. It was the laugh a yard gave a mismatch in its first minute, the small one dancing round the big one and getting nowhere, and Cael did not turn his head for it. He watched Dessa's lips at her mark. Four and four.

*I spent,* he thought. *She banked. That's what everybody's first exchange with her looks like. That's all right. It was meant to.*

The second exchange was the one he had made, and it started with a mistake he meant.

Its whole idea had come to him on a plank bench, and been turned over on the wall by the pump, and finished at the mender's with his palms flat on the bench while a beam came to rest. Nothing in four pages said that her guard could be broken, by anybody, least of all by a boy lighter than she was, so he would not try to break it. He would make her put it up instead, over and over. Each time her frame turned to meet him it had to be built again where it now faced, shoulders and forearms and hips coming round and settling and locking, though her feet never shifted. She did it fast and she did it beautifully, and it was not free. He had watched her count stretch from four to seven across a long bout on a Sunday, and that stretch was the bill.

So the bill would be run up on his clock, not hers.

He came at her and stopped short, and held still a moment longer than anybody would. He gave her two touches where one was due, and then nothing at all where she had braced for two. He stepped across to her left and was back on her right while she was still coming round to the left, and he did it again the other way. There was no beat in any of it that she could lean her weight against. Every time he changed his mind, her frame had to change to meet the new one.

It did. She turned and built and locked each time without haste or waste, and she never once came after him, and her eyes never once left his feet. She would not spend.

But he could hear her breathing now.

Once, coming out of a stopped step, he drifted a thumb's width nearer than he had planned, and knew without seeing it how much of her was gathered and waiting for exactly that mistake. It was like leaning out over the lip of a well and feeling the cold breathe up at your face. He was not eager. He took himself back out by the road he had come in.

He broke off, with his own chest heaving harder than he liked. From the benches it must have looked like nothing at all: her frame standing where it had stood when Vell spoke, and a boy hopping round a wall for most of a minute and never once landing anything worth the name.

"Hit her, then!" somebody shouted, and laughed, and somebody else told him to hush.

Cael did not look at either of them. He watched Dessa go back to her mark, because he had nothing better to spend the next breath on, and he watched her the way he had watched the Fenrow man's feet and the Blade man's blade from the third row. She reached her mark and set herself, and the grit shivered, and her back foot, which ought to have been the last bolt shot home, came down a half beat after everything else.

He saw it.

It went through him like cold water. This was the second exchange, not the end of a long night. She was not tired the way she had been tired after six exchanges with the Blade man, low on her left side, her count drawn out long. But he had made her build that frame something like thirty times in a minute, to a rhythm she had not chosen, and the thirty-first had come down late.

On two Sundays he had seen it once a night, and only at the end. He had not thought he would see it tonight before the end, if he saw it at all. Whether it would ever show itself twice in one bout, he had no idea.

At her mark Dessa's lips moved. Six and six.

He stood on his own mark with his hands loose and his face as still as he could make it, and he did not move.

Every part of him wanted to go now. The window had been there three breaths ago, in front of his own eyes; if he went straight in, hard, while she was still counting, he might be inside the frame before the back foot had come down. His legs were already telling him so.

And that was exactly what the four pages said she lived on.

He had written it on the first Sunday, with the cold off the wall coming through his coat: *She doesn't beat people. She stands there while they beat themselves.* Every eager fighter in his notes had seen something and gone for it and found her at the end of it, weight already low and the sweep already on its way. Hurry was her income. He had not spent two Sundays on a plank to hand her his now, because his legs were impatient.

The late foot was not an order to go. It was a promise that the window would open again the next time she had to raise her frame to his count, and that he could make it open.

So he banked it.

He breathed out. He let his shoulders drop. He looked at her feet as if they had told him nothing, and when Vell said, "Begin," he went in exactly as he had the first time, at the same polite pace, as though he had learned nothing and planned nothing and had only questions left.

---

To anybody watching, the third exchange began as a copy of the first.

The same three-quarter pace. The same polite touch at the high line, which her forearm met. The same step across to her left, which her frame turned to follow.

And then, before it had finished turning, the step back to her right, the one he had used in the second exchange. Her frame came round to meet it, as it had come round thirty times already, and began to build itself again where it had turned. Her back foot came down to lock it.

He was not waiting for it. He was already going.

He had timed it from the third row, the gap between the frame and the foot. He had clapped its rhythm on his knee on a plank bench until the old man beside him had asked him to stop. He went into it the way you go through a door you have walked through in the dark a hundred times, without looking for the handle.

For a half beat the frame was up and the foot was not under it. It was the only half beat in three bouts of watching when she was standing on the dirt instead of built into it.

He hit her.

It was the strike from the post, the only one he owned: short and straight, the back foot driving, the hip turning, the fist arriving last. It went in under her left forearm, which had come round a hair too high, into the soft seam below her collarbone where the frame had not yet arrived to cover it. It did not stop at the surface of her. It went on through, and he felt the whole of her give round it, the way a person leaning on a door gives when somebody strikes the door.

She went back half a step.

She went back off her mark, the mark nobody moved her from. It was the first ground she had given in the bout, and he had seen what came after the first ground she gave, four times, in two bouts, from the third row.

He was already going left.

He went off her line before her weight had finished dropping, while the yard was still drawing its breath at the half step. Every eager fighter in his notes had followed that half step in. He went the other way, outside, two long steps along the curve of the groove. Four times on the Sundays he had seen that sweep, and it came now the way it had come then, low and flat and much faster than anything else she owned, a long arc along the dirt that reclaimed the half step and whatever stood on it.

Nothing was standing on it. It went through the place where a fighter following her in would have been, and he was two strides wide of it, off her line, with nothing touching him, and already turning back to face her.

Dessa came up out of the sweep and onto her mark and set her feet. The grit shivered and lay down.

Then she looked at his face.

It was the first time she had looked anywhere but his feet all night. It was very quick. Her eyes came up from his boots to his eyes and stayed there for the length of a breath, and he saw something go across behind them. It was not anger and it was not surprise. It was the look of somebody adding a new figure to a sum she had thought she had finished. Her eyes dropped to his feet again. The frame went up. That was the end of the exchange.

The yard was making a new sound.

It was not loud. It was a low, turned-head murmur that ran along the rope and up into the benches and back. Eighty people were revising something all at once, and some of them had money on it. On the second bench a man wanted to know what he had just seen, and the man beside him told him, and got it wrong. Near the front, Brenna said something to one of her friends, sharp and short, and did not laugh.

He read her lips while she counted at her mark. Eight and eight. It was the longest count he had seen from her on any night.

She did not look at the yard.

---

His notebook was empty for the fourth exchange.

He knew it, standing on his mark with his knuckles throbbing and his breath coming hard. Two Sundays on a bench had bought him one tell and one way to make it come, and both of them were used up. She would close that window now. Nobody as good as she was left the same window open twice. She had looked at his face, and she would not let a frame come late on his clock again tonight, not if he made her build it a hundred times.

What he had left was patience. Against Dessa, patience beat having no plan only as a cup beats bare hands when the river is coming up.

Still he went, no faster than before, braced to pay dearly and be given almost nothing back.

She advanced.

Every step forward he had ever seen her take, on both Sundays and in the first three exchanges tonight, had been ground taken back from somebody who had taken it. This one was not. She took it, and then another, and then a third. She came frame first, unhurried, with the grit shivering flat under each foot as it came down, and even while she came he could see the sense of it, the thrift. Patience had never been the point with her. Spending was the point. And the cheap thing now, the thing that cost her least, was to finish a tired boy before his notebook grew another page.

It was quiet and complete. It was a wall deciding to arrive.

He gave ground.

He gave it along the long side of the circle. That was the one thing he had kept back, the one line in four pages he had not needed yet. He had noticed it on his first morning in the yard with Lira, and written it down on the first Sunday from the third row. The groove was not quite round. It was longer one way than the other, as if all the fighters who had walked it into the dirt had preferred the same side. Going back along the long side, he would have two more steps before the groove arrived at his heel.

He took the first of them. And the second.

Dessa did not follow him round the long side. She cut across it.

She did not hurry. She simply walked the short way, straight across the middle of the circle toward the place where he would be when he ran out of long side, and the two steps he had banked were gone before he had finished taking them. The groove was at his heel. He could feel its edge through the sole of his boot.

The yard had gone silent. It was the silence of eighty people all doing the same sum at once and not liking where it came out. He could hear the lantern nearest the gate creaking on its hook.

He searched her advance for a rhythm. He had refused her rhythm all night, and he looked now for one he could refuse, some step he could make come early or late. He found none. There was no rhythm left in her to refuse. She was only coming, frame first, with her eyes on his feet and her lips not moving at all.

She rocked onto the front foot, and all her weight went with it.

What happened next, he did not decide.

His body was already going. It was not going back, though every line of the exchange said back; back was the groove, and the rope, and the dirt. It was not going sideways either, along the curve, where he had gone in the third exchange and where she would be waiting for him now. It went across.

His lead foot planted, and the whole of him turned on it, low, into the closing space beside her advancing shoulder. It was the space her frame was coming forward to fill, the space that was not there yet. Then it was there, exactly, for exactly as long as he needed it, and he was through it before it closed.

No thought went into it. Hardly any of him went into it.

It came the way a word comes when somebody says the first half of a sentence and you know the rest before they finish, in a voice you cannot place. And it had come once before, on a grey morning in this same yard, with Lira's backhand going over the back of his head and nobody watching but the crows.

He came out of it inside her frame.

Her forearms were behind him. He was at her right side, at an angle no guard of hers had ever had to cover, since no one had ever got there to need it. Her weight was on her lead foot, still going forward into the place he had been. His was under him.

The strike was his. That part he could account for. He had made it a hundred times a morning for two weeks at a canvas post in the grey half, and his hip knew it and his back foot knew it, and it went where he sent it: short, rising, up into the hinge of her jaw under the ear, where nothing was guarding anything because nothing had been expected there.

Dessa went down.

She did not fall the way the Fenrow man had fallen, all at once. She went down in stages, a knee and then a hand, and sat back onto the dirt inside the groove. An arm went under her; she sent word to her legs to stand. Her legs did not answer. She tried once more, with great care, the way a carpenter tries a joint he has just glued, and they did not answer again.

She sat on the dirt and looked at nothing for a moment. Then she lifted her right hand off her knee and held it up, open, the palm toward Vell's table.

"Called," said Vell. Her voice was exactly as level as it always was. "Conceded. Unrated."

---

He had stood inside that noise four times while the yard made it for somebody else, and now it was coming the other way.

It reached him in pieces. A shout from the benches, a groan from the rope, coins clicking along from hand to hand, somebody laughing at the face of somebody who had lost. Near the gate a voice he knew went up high and broke, one of the errand boys, though he did not look to see which. And under all of it ran a quieter, stranger thing, which was his own name in the mouths of people he had never met, here and there along the rope, as though somebody had just told it to them and they were trying it for size.

He did not know what to do with any of that, so he did the one thing he did know, and crossed the circle to her.

Dessa was up already. She stood working her thumb along the line of her jaw, feeling the hinge under the ear the way a joiner runs a thumb along somebody else's seam to see whether it will hold. When the woman from the water bucket came toward her with a cup, she waved it away without looking.

"Both Sundays," she said. "Third row. A book on your knee." She did not make it a question. "That window was measured. And you'd left my line before the sweep came, so you'd counted that as well."

"Yes."

"There'll be people tell you it was luck." She moved her jaw left and then right, once, and let it be, as if she had decided it would keep. "It was nothing like luck. Since I came to this yard nobody's read me that clean, and you did it sitting on a plank for a fortnight." She looked at him then, properly, the way she had looked at his face in the third exchange. "What's your Path?"

"Nothing to register."

Both her eyebrows went up a little, the scarred one and the other, a small and honest surprise.

"I had you ready," she said slowly. "Unrated, four losses, lasts a long while, gives ground. I know what you do with a boy like that. I'd planned it out, and I came with it." She looked down at the groove, where her own sweep had scored a long curve through the dirt across her mark. "Only there's nothing written on you. No Path to learn the habits of. No school's lines to cut. Nobody but you has read those four pages, and every bit of tonight came out of them." Her eyes came back up. "The registry might call that a hole in you. I don't. I'd sooner face a Path I know, any night of the week, than a fortnight of you sitting on a bench."

"Rematch?"

He had not known he was going to say it until it was out.

"No." There was no heat in it at all. "Not yet." Her glance went down to his hands, and he saw that they had begun to shake; something close to warmth crossed her face and did not stay. "Go and fight some problem that isn't me for a while. I'd like to sit on the benches for once and watch you work it. Then we'll talk."

She turned to go, and turned back.

"And eat something," she said. "A win empties you worse than a loss. Nobody tells you that."

She went off to her side of the yard, and on the way, exactly as the four pages had her, she never once looked at the crowd. She did stop at the water bucket. She took the cup the woman had held out to her before, and drank, and handed it back, and gave the pie boy a nod.

Then he was alone in the middle of the circle, with dirt down one side of his neck and his right knuckles split, and his hands shaking.

It was the ordinary sort of shaking, he thought, spent muscle and nerves that had finished their work, the same as after Petra. It climbed his forearms into his shoulders and he let it climb. Under it he looked for anything else, and found nothing.

So he reached. He stood quite still with the yard going on round him and reached for whatever had turned him across that closing space, the way you reach after a word you had on your tongue a moment ago. He had reached like that twice in the grey half after the drop, and come back with empty hands, and he came back with them now. Whatever it was had gone back into its room and shut the door behind it, and had not even left an ache to prove it had been out.

What it had left was how he was standing. His feet were set wider than he set them, his hips turned further round than he turned them, and his weight was on the foot he did not usually trust with it. It was somebody else's coat again, with the sleeves ending where he did not expect.

He put himself right on purpose, a piece at a time, feet in and hips round and weight back where it lived, the way he would have reset a gauge that had been knocked.

Then he filed it, in his head, where nobody could see him do it. *Second instance.* He knew while he was writing it there what number it really was, and he left that for later.

---

He went to Vell's table, because you went to the table win or lose, and read the second line upside down, the way he had learned to read at nine across the bench from Hesk, with his hands still shaking and the lantern swinging a little on its hook.

*Built the third exchange on the lateness of her frame. Timed it from the benches over two bouts, then made it come early in the second by working her frame to his count. Does his studying before he fights, and more of it than a few weeks in this yard explain.*

Above it sat the line Vell had spoken aloud while she wrote, because the record was always spoken. "Result stands as called. Unrated, over Dessa, Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Fourth exchange. Conceded from the dirt." The pen had paused, and gone on. "Witnessed and entered." At the head of that line, where a name would have stood, there was still no name, only *Unrated (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.)*, the same as every other time.

The second line she had not read out to anybody.

She blotted it, and shut the ledger on her thumb, and lifted her eyes to his with nothing in her face at all.

"It's a compliment. Don't spend it."

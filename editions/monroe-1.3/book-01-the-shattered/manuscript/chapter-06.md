# Chapter 6 — The Rise

The dog came out of a farm gateway in the second hour, as though it had been waiting there for him by arrangement. It was thin and brown, with a white bib on its chest and one ear torn at the tip, and it fell in at his left side without looking up, matched its trot to his walk, and asked for nothing at all.

"You're not the grey dog," Cael told it.

The dog did not argue the point.

He was glad of it, which surprised him. The first hours out of Denvash had been farmland: stubble and plough on both hands, low stone walls dividing it, a farmhouse every mile or so with its morning smoke going straight up. Carts had passed him going the other way into the city with cabbages and split wood. A family had gone by on foot, pulling a handcart with a baby asleep among the sacks, and two merchants on horseback had overtaken him at a trot without once looking down. None of it had felt like company. Every walk he had ever taken had been inside a city that answered him. Each street had a name, each distance stopped somewhere, and round every corner was a thing he already knew. Out here the road simply kept going and had no opinion about him. The only regular thing in the whole landscape was the sound of his own boots.

So he had looked at things, because looking was the one tool he could count on having brought. The mist had lain in the hollows between the fields and gone off unevenly as the sun got up, the high ground clearing first and the low places staying white long after, as if they were keeping hold of the night on purpose. A farmer two fields away, setting stones back on a broken wall, had lifted one hand to him between one stone and the next, and Cael had lifted his back. The road had felt less empty for as long as that lasted, and he had been surprised at how much less.

The dog stayed most of an hour. It went where he went, at the easy distance of an animal that belonged to the road and not to anybody walking it, and at a crossroads with a milestone it turned off toward a farmhouse without a backward look, exactly the way it had come. Cael watched it out of sight. He minded more than made sense.

By the middle of the morning his legs had started to speak up. It was not pain so much as a dull insistence on being noticed, which the city had never asked of them, because city distances were short and crowded with things to look at. The road notes advised a pace you could keep all day rather than one that felt bold. He let himself slow, and his breath came back. Hurrying would buy him nothing. Ardenmere was two days off, and it would wait for him whether he came in spent or only tired.

---

The cord round the leather book had a smooth place in it, worn almost to a shine, where it had been knotted and unknotted more times than Cael could estimate. He sat with the book on his knee and his thumb on that place for some while before he did anything about it.

He had made himself wait for a wall. *Not until the road*, Hesk had said, and a mile out of the gate had felt like a technicality, so he had walked on until late in the morning, when a low grey wall ran along the road's edge with a stubble field behind it, waist-high and good for sitting. It made him think of the other one, at the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, with a folded paper pushed into the gap between two blocks. Joren would have found it by now and stood there for his hour. Cael hoped he had kept his heels down.

He ate Ressa's loaf first. It was still faintly soft, and it tasted of the corner and the side door and every early morning he could remember, and he made it last. When it was gone he brushed the crumbs off his knees for the sparrows, who had been watching him from the wall with great attention, and untied the cord.

The book was smaller than it had looked in Hesk's hands. It sat in his palm. The leather had gone soft with handling, and the writing inside was Hesk's but not the square, careful hand that went up on the workshop slate. This one was rougher, and it changed from page to page: pale brown ink that had aged, black that had not, a hard-pressed line here and a light one there, the work of a man who had come back to the same few pages over a great many evenings.

On the first page there were only a few lines.

*If this is in your hands, then I was right, and I'm sorry. I'd have given a good deal to be wrong. I can't take the word back off you. What I can give you is what I've managed to work out, most of it by watching, since there was nobody I could ask without being asked why I wanted to know.*

*None of it is sure. Read it anyway.*

He turned over.

*First: I don't know what [SHATTERED] actually means. I'm putting that at the top so you can't miss it, because everything under it is guesswork in advice's coat. Here is what I think, for what a thought is worth against a word with a registry behind it. Frightened people chose that word, and being frightened enough to name a thing is not the same as being right about it. Broken is one reading. Another is a country nobody has mapped yet, which is a different thing from no country at all. I can't tell you which. I've turned it over for years and I have nothing better, and anyone who sounds sure is spending money they haven't got.*

Cael read it a second time. Only then did he notice what he had been waiting for. Somewhere below the parts of himself he kept an eye on, he had believed that a man who had spent years getting ready for this day would have come out the other end with an answer, and that the answer would be in here, in Hesk's hand, the way the answer to a load problem sat at the foot of the slate.

There was no answer. There was a grown man writing down that he did not have one.

Cael sat on the wall with that. It took a while, and it turned out to be more use than an answer would have been. Answers could be wrong. This was a figure taken by hand and written down honestly, and you could build on a figure like that, because you knew exactly what it would bear.

The next page.

*Second: your body is going to learn before your mind does, and I mean that as plainly as I've ever meant a measurement, so don't go looking for poetry in it. Nobody is going to give you a card with a neat box on it saying what you can do, the way they gave Joren his. If anything comes, it'll come as your body doing a thing before you've chosen to, and you working out afterwards what happened. When it does, keep it to yourself. Watch it. Learn it the way you would learn a new tool, by using it where nobody can see, and only then try to say what it is, even to yourself.*

*Third: don't perform weakness, and don't perform strength. Perform competence. Look strong, and people start measuring you for a fight. Look helpless, and they start measuring you for something worse. Plain, quiet competence is the only face I know that buys you time, and time is what you'll want most. Let them find you dull until you know.*

Joren's declaration was copied out at the back of Cael's own notebook. It had its heading, PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1, and its name in brackets, and one sentence that said exactly what Joren could do and exactly where it stopped. Joren must have read it four hundred times by now. He knew where his edges were, because his whole Path had been handed to him as a set of edges with the measurements written on.

There would be nothing like that for Cael. Not at Rank 1, not at Rank 10. No box, no line saying where the effect ran out. He would have to find his edges by walking into them, quietly, with nobody looking, and the two entries were telling him so from two directions at once.

The ink went paler on the next page, and the writing slower and more careful, as if the man holding the pen had put his weight down on each word.

*Fourth: if the abilities do come, they will not come labeled. No notice, no box, no voice telling you what it is or how you came by it. Here I've the least to offer, because I've never seen it happen to anyone; this is reasoning, not watching. My guess, then. Mind what you can do when it's real, not what you can do when you're practising. I don't think whatever this is turns up because you've asked it nicely in a quiet yard. I think it turns up when it has to.*

*Fifth, and this one matters more than the others, so read it twice: you are allowed to be afraid and competent at the same time. I've watched good people talk themselves into believing those two can't share a room, that being scared means you aren't ready. That's wrong. Fear that keeps your eyes open is a tool like any other in the drawer. Worry about the other sort, the sort that shuts them. Yours won't. I've watched you for fourteen years, and you have never once known how to stop looking. Of everything about you, that's the part I'm least afraid will let you down.*

He read the fifth twice, as instructed, and then a third time because he wanted to.

He had always thought of the watching as a kind of hiding: a way of standing a half-step outside things so they could not quite get hold of him, and something he would have to grow out of sooner or later. In the circle he had counted eleven seconds. On the morning of the notice he had named what his own hands were doing so that he would not have to feel it. In the archive he had copied the entry out in his best writing so that he would not have to just sit there and know it.

And Hesk did not call any of that hiding. Hesk called it the tool. Cael let that sink, and it did not sink all the way, but it went a long way down.

After the fifth there were many more pages. There were notes on the Registry, and lists of cities with most of them struck through, and single unnumbered sentences in fading ink. Near the middle, alone on its page, in ink so new it had hardly had time to dull, there was one more.

*When you don't know what you can do yet, watch someone who does. Your body will learn before your mind does.*

It had no number. It had been written lately, a few months ago at the most, perhaps only weeks.

He looked at it for a long time. Then the small snag he had felt in the side room at Weaver's Row, when Hesk said *a few years*, caught again, and this time he could follow the thread to its end.

*A few years*, in the side room. *Some years*, under the lamps on Chandler Street, about the folding chairs. They were the same span of time. For as long as Hesk had known what Cael's coupling looked like, he had been going to sit in the dark behind a curtain every Tuesday and learn how a body moves when it carries a Path, so that he would know what he was seeing if it ever happened to Cael's. Then, on the night before Kindling, with nothing left to learn and no time left to explain it, he had taken Cael along. He had sat him down and said *watch his hips*, and *the third exchange is where they tell you the truth*, and *there*, out loud, over and over, so that one day, with Hesk a week's post away and no chair beside him in the dark, Cael would be able to say *there* for himself.

It had not been a treat, or something to keep his mind off the morning. It had been the one lesson Hesk was sure enough of to give. He had given it at the last moment it could be given, and then gone home and written it down in new ink in case it did not take.

Cael tied the cord and sat holding the book against his knees with both hands.

*This isn't nothing,* he thought. *I'm starting from this.*

It helped. It did not help all the way. True things had always held more weight than comfortable ones; he had known that for years without ever having to lean on it, and it held now, even with *I don't know* written across the top. He put the book in the inside pocket of his coat beside the bracket, and stood up, because Ardenmere was still the best part of two days east and the afternoon would not wait for him.

---

The road dropped after midday into a long shallow valley with a stream at the bottom, and here the walls gave way to hedges, high and thick, so that the road ran between them like a corridor. It narrowed and bent, and you could not see more than fifty paces either way, and the air was still and close and smelled of wet leaves and turned earth.

Cael walked with his head down, counting milestones. He had passed nine since Denvash, and he was trying to work out his pace from them and from the sun, and getting different answers, and enjoying the problem the way he had enjoyed the bracket.

He stepped sideways.

He did not decide to. One moment he was walking in the middle of the road with his eyes on the ruts. The next, his left foot had gone out onto the grass verge and his weight had followed it, and he was standing in the long grass at the bottom of the hedge.

The cart came round the bend behind him.

It came quickly: a high-sided farm cart heaped with turnips, pulled by one big dun mare going downhill at a trot that was not quite under control. Its wheels were rimmed with iron, but the road was soft dust here, and they made almost no sound at all. The driver was slumped on the bench with the reins slack in his lap and his hat over his eyes.

The cart went through the place where Cael had been walking. It went through it so closely that the near wheel hub brushed the strap of his bag where it hung at his hip, and he felt the brush, and the wind of it on his face, and a turnip bounced off the top of the heap and rolled into the grass at his feet.

Then the cart was past, and the driver came awake with a jerk and hauled on the reins, and the mare slowed, snorting, and the cart stopped twenty paces on, half across the road.

The driver twisted round on his bench, a red-faced man with a grey moustache, and stared back at Cael in the long grass with the turnip at his feet. "Lad!" His voice cracked. "Lad, are you hit?"

"No."

Cael looked down at himself. He was not hit. His bag strap had a pale scuff across it where the hub had touched, and his heart was going very fast, faster than it had in the circle; he could feel it in his throat.

The driver climbed down and came back up the road at a stiff old man's half-run, and stopped in front of Cael and looked him up and down as if counting his arms and legs. "I never saw you. I swear I never saw you. I was—" He took his hat off and wrung it. "I was resting my eyes. The old girl knows the road better than me, and I never thought there'd be anybody walking the bottom of the dip this time of day." He swallowed. "You'd have been under her. You'd have been right under the wheel."

"I heard you coming," Cael said.

"You never."

"I must have. I stepped out of the way."

The driver looked at him with his mouth working, and then at the road, at the soft dust and the iron-rimmed wheels standing quiet as a pair of plates. He did not say anything. Cael could see him not saying it.

"Well," the man said at last. "Well. Thank the sky for good ears, then. Mine are no use to anybody."

He would not let it go after that. He was going as far as the waystation with his turnips, a few miles on, and he would not hear of Cael walking it. It was the least he could do, and a good deal less than the least, if you asked him. Get up, lad, get up on the bench, and here, have the turnip, it's yours, it's got your name on it now.

So Cael got up on the bench beside him with the turnip in his lap. The dun mare took them down the dip and up the other side at a walk this time, because the driver held the reins in both fists now and did not once close his eyes.

He was a talker. Before they were out of the valley, Cael had learned that he had four daughters and a son, and that the son was no good with turnips but very good with ledgers. He learned that the waystation woman's stew was better than it looked and her bread was worse, and that he did not hold with the new lock fees on the Denvash canal.

"Where are you headed, then, on your own, at your age?"

"Ardenmere."

"Ah." The man nodded as though that explained a good deal. "The big one. Family there?"

"No."

"Work?"

"I'll find some."

The driver glanced at him sideways. "What Path are you, lad, if you don't mind my asking? You've got a look about you. Fresh. Kindled recent, I'd say."

Cael felt the question land and go all the way down.

He could lie. He could say *Copper, Force* and watch the man nod and move on. He found he did not want to, not because lying was hard, but because he had already watched Pellin's hand go to the back of a drawer, and the gate guard's face draw back, and he did not want to do that same thing to himself from the inside. But he did not have to hand the word over either, not to a turnip farmer on a road, not today. *Don't perform weakness. Don't perform strength. Perform competence.*

"Still finding out," he said.

The driver considered this, and then laughed, a big comfortable laugh. "Aren't we all. Aren't we all. I'm sixty-one and Copper Rank 4 and I'm still finding out what Stone Path is for, besides knowing when the turnips want lifting." He shook his head. "Ardenmere's full of people like that. You'll fit right in."

Cael looked down at the turnip in his lap. He did not think about the bend in the road, because there was nothing to think about. The cart had made more noise than the driver thought, and he had heard the wheels without noticing that he heard them, as you hear a clock, and then he had moved.

Anyone would have. He was sure of that. He was nearly sure. He did not take out his notebook, because there was nothing to put in it; *a cart went past close* was not information. It was only a cart.

---

The waystation was where Hesk had said it would be, on the south side of the road: a low timber building with a mossy roof and a lamp over the door, already lit, though it was not yet dark. The driver dropped him at the gate and shook his hand very hard and told him twice more to thank the sky for his ears. Then he drove round the back with the mare and the turnips, where the woman who kept the waystation was apparently expecting him.

Inside there was one long room with a stove at one end, a table with benches, and a row of cots along the far wall with blankets folded at their feet. Two were taken already, one by a drover asleep with his boots on and one by a thin woman knitting, with a small girl asleep against her side.

The keeper came in from the back, tall and bony, with her sleeves rolled up and her hair tied in a cloth, and named a price, and Cael counted it out of Hesk's pouch in the small worn coins. She counted them again herself, and then a third time. She did not once look at his face, not when he paid and not when she pointed him to the cot by the window and not when she said, "Stew's at dark. Bread's on the table. Don't eat the bread." It was not unkind. If anything it was the opposite, he thought. She did not want his face, only his coins to be right, and here that was all anyone needed to be.

He sat at the end of the table near the stove with a clean sheet of paper and his pencil, and wrote *Hesk*, and then looked at the word for a while before he wrote the rest.

*I'm at the waystation. It's where you said, and the lamp's lit. The woman counts coins three times and says not to eat the bread, which I think is good advice.*

*Ressa put an extra loaf in. I ate it on a wall. I think you knew she would, and that's why you didn't take any for yourself this morning. I noticed.*

*Garrik didn't look up. I think he wanted to and couldn't. I don't think it's about me.*

*I left a note for Joren in the wall, in the gap at the canal end. Tell him, if he asks, that I meant it.*

*The lock gauge is holding. I watched the bracket the whole time the chamber filled.*

*I read the book. I read the fifth one three times. I read the one about watching someone who does.*

He held the pencil over the page for a moment, and then went on.

*A carter gave me a ride the last few miles. He grows turnips and gave me one. He's Copper Rank 4, Stone Path.*

He looked at that line for a while. He had not written how the ride started. It had been nothing, only a cart on a bend. If he wrote it down, Hesk would read it a week from now at the kitchen table and worry about something that was already over, with nothing he could do about it except worry.

Cael held the pencil very still.

It came to him, sitting there, that this was exactly the choice Hesk had made, for years. *Don't make a thing real before it is. Don't hand him weight he can't do anything about.* Cael had been angry about that choice three days ago, in a room with a crooked tree in the window, and here he was a day out of Denvash making it himself, about a turnip cart.

He did not know what to do with that, so he set it aside with the other things he did not know what to do with yet, which was getting to be a long list.

He did not write the cart in.

*I'll send this from Ardenmere when I know where I'm staying, so you'll have somewhere to write back to. You'll get it about a week after I send it. I'm sorry about that. I would make the post faster if I could.*

*I'm all right. I'm tired, and my legs hurt, and I'm all right.*

*— Cael.*

He folded it and put it in the leather book between the pages to keep it flat.

The stew came at dark and was better than it looked, and the bread was worse than she had said. The thin woman shared her tea with him without asking whether he wanted any. He thanked her, and she said, "Long road, is it?" and he said, "Not as long as yesterday," and she seemed to think that was a good answer. He lay on the cot by the window afterward and listened to the room: the drover snoring in a long, patient rhythm, the stove ticking as it cooled, something moving in the hedge outside and going quiet. These were not his sounds, and he did not know any of them, and he lay awake a while learning them, and then he slept, without meaning to, before he had learned them all.

---

The second day was longer than the first. His legs had stiffened in the night and took most of the morning to forgive him. The road climbed out of the farmland into rougher country with fewer walls and more sheep, and long slow hills that looked short until you were on them.

The sheep watched him go by with flat, unimpressed faces. There were no more turnip carts. He walked by himself and watched the milestones, and for nearly half an hour he watched a hawk working a field without ever seeing it catch anything, and found that by the end he was on its side.

He did not take the leather book out again. He did not need to, because he had read it often enough on the wall that its lines came up by themselves as he walked, the way the old descriptions on page seven did.

And the record came up by itself too, the green book and the four entries, and this time he made himself do something with it instead of just letting it come.

He did not know their names or how old they had been, or where they had come from, or what they had found themselves able to do before whatever happened to them happened. He did not know whether any of them had had a Hesk, or been given a book. But he knew the one word in the entry that he had read past in the archive, because the other words had been louder.

*Weeks.*

Not years. Not after a long life of trying and failing. Weeks. Whatever had happened to three of them had happened fast and early, before they could have found out what they were, before they could even have found their feet. He thought about the phrase *practice-related*, and about how little practice anyone could do in a few weeks, and about someone, thirty-two years ago, choosing which kind of true to write down.

---

Late in the afternoon, the road began to climb in earnest. It went up a long bare hill in a series of slow bends, with no hedges, only grass and stones and the wind, which had picked up out of the west and was pushing at his back. The sun was low, and his shadow went up the road ahead of him, long and thin, with a bag on its shoulder.

He came over the top and stopped.

Ardenmere lay below him in a wide river valley, and it was enormous.

He had read about it three times in the reference section on the eastern cities. He had known it was larger than Denvash, but he had not known what *larger* meant until he stood on the rise and looked. The river came in from the north, wide and slow and copper-coloured in the low sun, and bent through the middle of the valley like an arm.

On the far bank, in the crook of that arm, rose the Ranked core, walled and stepped, ring inside ring, each ring higher than the last. He could see the districts climbing toward the centre, Copper, he thought, and Iron, and Bronze, and at the very top something white with towers catching the last of the light. He had five pages in a notebook about one wall in Denvash. He could see six walls from here.

And around them, outside them, spilling past every one of them, was the rest.

The Unranked District was not a district. It was a city of its own, running along both banks of the river and up the slopes on either side and out across the flat land to the south. It had no walls at all, and no shape that he could find except the shape of a great many people deciding where to live one at a time.

There was old stone in it, solid and square. There were things that looked built and things that looked stacked, roofs going up on top of roofs, and three or four bridges crossing the river with movement on all of them.

The sun went down while he watched. The gold went out of the river, and the white towers on the Ranked hill held the light a little longer than everything else and then let it go too, and the sky turned grey-gold and then a deep, clear blue.

And the fires came out.

They came out one by one at first and then all at once, cookfires in yards and on roofs and in doorways and along the river, hundreds of them and then more than hundreds, little orange points of light across the whole Unranked District, each with a thin grey thread of smoke going up from it and leaning east in the wind, until the whole valley below him was lit like a field of embers that somebody had banked for the night and would come back to in the morning.

A thousand fires. He could not count them, and for once in his life he did not try. Each one was somebody's dinner, somebody not Ranked enough for the walls, making do.

He stood on the rise with the wind at his back. He was very tired, and his legs ached all the way down. He had a turnip in his bag and a bent bracket in his pocket and a book against his chest that said *I don't know* in his grandfather's handwriting.

He thought about the green book in the archive, with its four entries, three of which had ended in a few weeks and one in a line that said nobody knew. He was the fifth.

On the walk home from the record office he had promised himself there would be no fifth entry in that green book with his name at the top. It had been easy to promise, the way things are easy on a walk home. He said it again now, to himself, and it was not easy anymore, because he could see how big the thing was that he would have to say it to.

He took out his notebook, opened it to a clean page, and wrote in the last of the light:

*Three of them were dead within weeks. They didn't get time to find their feet. Whatever happened, it happened before they knew what they were.*

*So that's the first job. Not to find out what [SHATTERED] means. Not yet. First, get past the weeks.*

*Find somewhere to sleep. Find work. Watch everything. Show nobody anything. Be dull. Be competent. Be still here.*

*Then find out.*

He looked at it, and underlined *still here*, once, firmly.

He did not know what was down there. He did not know where he would sleep tonight, or who the first person to be kind to him would be, or whether he would be right to trust them. He did not know what the Unranked District did to people like him. He did not know what he was.

He knew he was not going to be an entry somebody else wrote. He decided that, standing on the rise at the end of the second day with a thousand fires coming up out of the dark below him, and he decided it properly, the way you set a part into a rig and load it to see whether it holds.

He put the notebook away.

Down in the valley the road ran on toward the river, pale in the dusk, and where it met the first houses there was no gate at all, only a wide space in the dark with fires on either side of it, standing open.

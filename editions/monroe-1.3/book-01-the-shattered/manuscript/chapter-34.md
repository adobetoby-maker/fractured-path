# Chapter 34 — Efficient

The frost had not gone off the yard when they came in through the gate, and the east wall's shadow lay across the dirt in its long grey bar with the post standing up in the middle of it like a man waiting for a cart.

Lira set the rest of the bread on top of the post and did not take her coat off; her right arm was still inside it, not in the sleeve, and she had stopped pretending that this was a choice.

"Go on, then," she said. "You've had it in your head since the board. Say it."

He said it. He had worked it out under the pear tree the evening before and again on the walk up, and it came out in order. There were forty-two days. The Cinder House ran a card on Thursdays and on Sundays and most Tuesdays in the afternoon, and Vell would not put him in twice in a week, and his body would not let him even if she did. At the very most that made six bouts, and he thought it was more honest to say five. Each one had to count, and each one had to be against somebody the yard would talk about afterward, so that his name went into more mouths than it had been in before. A boy whose name was in a great many mouths was a boy whose going would be noticed, and asked about, on the day a more senior man came along the merchant road with a thin file.

"So I want to fight," he said. "As often as she'll let me. I told her so."

"I know. She told me." Lira leaned her good shoulder against the post. "She said you stood in front of her table and asked for six weeks of bouts like a man asking for six weeks of bread."

"What did she say about it?"

"She said *hm*." Lira gave him the long look she gave him sometimes even now, after seven weeks. It was not suspicion. It was more like a person reading a contract she has already signed, to see what exactly she has agreed to. "You've got five bouts in the book. One of them's a win. That isn't a foundation, Cael. That's a first course of stones."

"Then help me lay the rest faster."

"Faster." She said the word back to him as if she were weighing it in her hand. "All right. But we do it my way, and you don't argue with my way until it's failed you at least twice." She held up one finger of her good hand. "Faster doesn't mean reckless. It means efficient."

"What's the difference?"

"Reckless is fighting six times because six is the biggest number on the page. Efficient is fighting as many times as you can and still walk into the next one whole." She pushed herself off the post. "You can lose. You've lost four times and you're standing here, and losing's cheap if you learn something. What you can't do is get hurt in a way that costs you the bout after. Every morning from now on is for the next one. Every night the Log turns round before you sleep, not two days later when you've forgotten what your feet did." She looked at the bread on the post and did not pick it up yet. "And you don't go in against anybody I think will break you, whatever Vell says, and whatever you say. I'll tell you. You'll listen."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong carefully." She almost smiled. "That's the other half of efficient. I've watched people come up through that circle in a hurry. Most of them came up fast and went down faster, and then they weren't in the yard any more, and nobody could tell you where they'd gone." She stopped, and he saw her hear what she had just said, and what it sounded like to somebody who had sat under a pear tree last night working out who would notice if he went. "I don't mean like that."

"I know what you meant."

"Good." She took the bread off the post at last and tore it, one-handed, with her teeth, and gave him half. "Eat. Then the deflection, slow, while I watch your left foot. It's still turning out on the back corner."

"I fixed it."

"You fixed it on Tuesday. It's Wednesday."

---

The mender was at his bench when Cael came down the row of workshops after noon, exactly as though nothing had happened on Monday and nobody had ever stood in his doorway apologizing.

The lantern woman next door had all thirteen of her broken lanterns out on a plank in the thin sun, and was warming her soldering iron between her palms and talking to it, while the cooper beyond her knocked a hoop down. The mender's own doorway had no sign over it, only the four words cut into the frame at the height of a man's shoulder and filled in with black paint, as they had been on the first afternoon Cael ever stopped there.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE.*

He had read them perhaps fifty times. He had thought they were a joke at first, and then a warning to people who left things too long, and then simply the mender's way of answering the question every customer asked, *when will it be ready*. He read them again now on his way in, and they meant something else, and he did not stop to look at what.

"Wednesday," said the mender, without looking up.

"Wednesday."

On the end of the bench there was a crate of forty or fifty door hinges, black with old paint and rust, which had come out of a house by the dyers' that was being pulled down. The mender wanted to know which of them still swung true. He did not want them cleaned, or oiled, or mended; he wanted them sorted into two heaps, one that could go into somebody's door tomorrow and one that would only ever be iron again.

Cael sat on the stool at the end of the bench and opened and shut hinges.

It was slow work and he liked it, because you could not tell a true hinge by looking at it. Paint hid the pin, and rust hid the knuckle, and a hinge that looked like scrap would sometimes open as sweetly as a new one, while a hinge that looked clean would bind at the halfway and grind and stop. You had to hold it and work it and feel for the place where it caught, and after the first dozen his hands knew before his eyes did. Something in the first quarter-turn told him which heap it was going in, before he had finished turning it, and he made himself finish turning it anyway, because he did not trust that yet.

He thought about the six weeks while his hands worked, because there was nothing else in his head that afternoon to think about.

A hinge that was still true would go into a door, and somebody would hang it and swing it and forget about it for twenty years. A hinge that was not would go on the scrap heap and be carried off to the smith by the bridge, and in a week nobody in the district would remember it had ever been a hinge. He did not like where the thought was going. He turned the next hinge over in his hands and worked it, and it caught at the halfway, and he put it on the scrap heap and did not think about it any more.

At the end of the afternoon there were two heaps on the bench, and the true one was smaller than the other. The mender came down the bench with his loupe pushed up on his forehead and picked a hinge out of each, one after the other, without any hurry. He worked them both, and put them back on their heaps exactly where they had been.

He looked at Cael for a moment, not with the long look he gave a frame before he leaned on the lever but with something shorter than that, and plainer.

"Monday," he said.

"Monday," said Cael. "And Wednesday. For six weeks, at least."

The mender considered this, as if it had been a measurement.

"And after?"

"I don't know yet."

The mender nodded slowly, as he had nodded at *I don't know yet* once before over a tray of keys, and put his loupe back in his eye and went back to his clock. Cael went out under the four words in the black paint, and he did not read them, and he heard them anyway all the way up the row.

---

Vell was at her table on Thursday morning before the yard had properly woken, with her tea and the ledger and her stool set square to the back wall, and she went on writing when he came across the dirt.

"Tuesday," she said. "The afternoon card."

He stopped in front of the table. "Who?"

Vell turned the ledger a little toward the light and laid her finger on a line. He could not read it upside down at that distance, and she did not turn it far enough for him to try.

"There's a boy on tonight's card," she said. "Second bout. He's on again Sunday, the first, before the benches fill. Copper Rank 2, Ember Path. He works the kilns down by the brickworks, and he fights the way you'd expect from somebody who stands in front of a kiln all day." She took her finger off the page. "You'll have him on Tuesday if you still want him after you've watched him twice."

"Why him?"

"Because he's on the card twice before Tuesday, and you'll want to watch him." Vell picked up her tea. "And because you've already been taught Ember once. Amrit taught you. I'd like to see whether it stayed."

"Not because I asked."

"No. Not because you asked." She drank. "Go away. You're standing in my light."

He went to the rope that evening on the shaded side, by the barrel near the drain, with the Log open on his knee and the pencil going, and he watched the kiln boy fight.

He was about seventeen, broad through the shoulders and narrow at the hip, with forearms burned all over in small shiny patches, old and new together, and he came over the rope as if he were angry with it. When Vell said *begin* he went straight at the other fighter, a Force youth a little older than him, and Cael watched the air in front of his right hand go thick and wavering, as air goes over a road in summer.

Then the boy stopped breathing.

It was not a long stop, only the length of a swallow: his chest went still, and his mouth shut, and a half-beat later the heat came. Cael felt it from the rope, on his face, like an oven door opened across a kitchen, and the Force youth flinched sideways out of it and lost his footing on the groove.

*Shimmer,* Cael wrote. *Then the breath stops. Then the heat. Same as Amrit.*

He wrote it again after the second exchange, and after the third, and he made a mark beside it each time. Amrit had stopped his breath before the real heat two times in three, and Cael had had to learn to tell the third time apart, and had paid for it with a burned arm. This boy stopped his breath every time, not two times in three or four in five but every single time, as surely as a door creaks on the same hinge.

There was a second thing, which he saw in the fourth exchange and nearly did not believe. Before every strike the boy looked at the place he meant to hit. He did not glance there; he looked, the way you look at a nail before you bring the hammer down, and then he hit it.

The Force youth went down in the fourth. The kiln boy stood over him breathing hard, with the shimmer still going off his hand, and the crowd along the rope cheered him in the comfortable way people cheer a thing that has gone as it was meant to.

Cael looked at his page. It said *breath, every time* and *looks where he's going to hit*, and nothing else, because there was nothing else.

He sat with the Log on his knee while the next pair went over the rope, and he did not like it at all, and he could not have said why.

---

"Gone," said Lira.

"What?"

"That's what you say." She stepped back out of the box drill and stood in the grey half with her good hand on her hip. "Every time a plan dies. The moment it dies. You say it out loud, so I can hear it, and then you do something else."

It was Friday. Her shoulder had gone from purple to a colour like old mustard, and she had taken her arm out of her coat that morning for the first time, and she was using it carefully, as if it belonged to somebody who might want it back. He had been building a counter to her step since the end of the month before, and this morning he had built it four times, and four times it had been wrong in the same way. He had seen the two touches and started back and out, and found her already inside, and gone on with the counter anyway, back and out, with her backhand stopped beside his ear while his forearm was still on its way up to a place she had left.

"I knew it was wrong," he said.

"I know you knew. I watched you know it." She tapped her own temple. "You saw me inside, you knew the counter was dead, and you finished it anyway, all the way, every step, as though you'd paid for it and you weren't going to waste it." She shook her head. "It's dead, Cael. You don't owe it anything. You don't get to keep a plan because it was a good plan five breaths ago."

"So I say *gone*."

"Out loud. Every time. Before you do the next thing."

"That's ridiculous."

"It's very ridiculous." She brought her hands up. "Do it anyway."

The first time, it came out of him as a mumble, half under his breath, a beat too late to be of any use, and she stopped and made him say it again. The second time he said it clearly, and felt his ears go hot. Along the top of the far wall the two girls who sat there in the mornings to watch the yard and eat whatever they had stolen from their mothers' kitchens put their heads together and laughed.

"Gone," said Cael, a third time, standing in the wrong place with his arm half up.

"*Gone*," said one of the girls on the wall, in a deep solemn voice, and the other one fell against her shoulder.

Lira did not laugh. She took a stub of chalk out of her pocket, made one short line on the side of the post where the canvas ended, and came back to her mark.

"That's three," she said.

By the end of the morning it was nine. He said it every time he finished building something and saw it fall down, which was nearly every time. Some of the times he said it before he had properly understood that the plan was dead, and then stood blinking while his body worked out that he was right. The word did not feel like a tool; it felt like a boy announcing his mistakes to a yard.

She did one more thing before the sun reached the post.

"Watch," she said, and stepped back, and became him.

She did it so exactly that for a moment he did not understand what he was looking at. She stood on her mark with her feet a little too square and brought her hands up and looked across at an opponent who was not there, with her chin tucked and her eyes very wide and very serious. Then she nodded, slightly, to herself, a small private nod, the nod of somebody who has just solved something; and only then, a full breath later, she moved.

The girls on the wall had stopped laughing and were watching her with their mouths open.

"Do I do that?"

"The nod?" Lira dropped out of him and was herself again, all at once, loose and quick. "Every time. You see it, you work it out, and you agree with yourself. Then you go." She picked up the last of the bread. "Anybody who's watched you twice could time you by it. I could. Renn will have."

He had not known the nod was there. He had felt the agreeing, the small click of a figure coming right, and he had never once thought that it showed on the outside, where anybody standing in a circle could see it.

"Gone," he said, to nobody, to the nod.

Lira looked at him, and then took out the chalk and made a tenth mark on the post.

"That one counts. That was a good one."

---

Sunday was the first of the new month, and Renn was not in the yard.

Cael had not expected him to be, exactly, but he had expected him somewhere. He had looked for him at the fish steps that morning among the men unloading, and at the market, and at the yard gate. The river had come up again in the week, and the barges that should have come down from the upriver farms on Friday had not come. Torvin's brothers had been at the table every night saying so, loudly, with their elbows on the boards.

He went to Vell's table before the benches filled.

"It's the turn of the month," he said.

"I know what day it is." She was ruling a fresh page. "He's not back. The river's holding everything above the weir. He could be three days, or ten. I don't keep a man's slot open for ten days on the chance of a river."

"So the hold's gone."

"The hold's gone." She drew the line to the edge of the page and lifted the ruler. "I said I'd hold him to the turn of the month. It's turned."

He stood there a moment longer. "When he's back, I'd like to ask again."

"Then ask him." Vell blew on the ink. "Not me. I made you a promise once already and the river broke it. If you want Renn, you go and find Renn, and if he says yes, you both come to me, and I'll see." She looked up. "He'll come up the fish steps like everybody else, when it stops raining up the valley."

So he went and stood at the rope on the shaded side, near the barrel, and watched the kiln boy fight for the second time.

It was the first bout, with the benches still half empty and the pie boy not yet going with his tray. The kiln boy was against a Blade this time, a tall girl of about sixteen with a long reach, and he went at her across the circle the same way he had gone at the Force youth on Thursday, angrily, as if the circle had insulted him. The shimmer came up off his hand, and his breath stopped, and the heat came.

Cael made a mark in the Log.

It happened again and again: the boy stopped his breath before every real heat and looked at every place he meant to hit. The tall girl worked out the second thing in the third exchange and moved her ribs away from where he was looking. The boy noticed nothing, and kept looking, and kept hitting where he had looked. In the fifth exchange she slipped one of his looks and put the flat of her blade across the back of his neck, and Vell called it.

Lira had come up the rope while he was writing, and she stood beside him with her arms folded on the top of it.

"That's two," she said.

"Every time. Both nights. He holds his breath, and he looks."

"Then he'll do it on Tuesday."

"I know." He looked at the page. "That's what's wrong with it."

She turned her head and looked at him properly.

"There's nothing wrong with it. He's seventeen. He's rated Copper 2 because that's what he is. Some people have a tell like a fire bell and nobody's ever told them." She looked back at the circle, where the tall girl was helping the kiln boy up and he was letting her, red in the face. "You're not used to easy. That's all. You've never had one."

Marrow came along the rope with his slate. He had not put the Tuesday card on it yet. He saw Cael reading the slate anyway, and smiled the good smile, and said nothing at all, which was not like him.

---

On Tuesday afternoon the yard was thin.

It was a working day, and cold. Along the rope there were perhaps forty people, most of them men from the yards and the river workshops on their way home early, with the girls from the wall, and the errand boys, and two of Brenna's friends, who had come because Lira had told somebody who had told somebody. Lira was at the barrel with both arms out of her coat now, and she did not wave.

The kiln boy came over the rope as if it had insulted him.

"Copper Rank 2, Ember Path," said Vell, and gave his name, which Cael heard and did not keep. "Against unrated. Vouched." She looked at her book. "Begin when you're ready."

The shimmer came up off the boy's right hand before he had left his mark. He looked at Cael's left shoulder, hard. Then he came.

Cael watched his chest.

It went still, and the mouth shut, and Cael went right, away from the shoulder the boy had been looking at, a full step outside the reach of the hand, and the heat went past his left side like a door of air pushed open and slammed. It was hot on his ear and on nothing else.

The second exchange was the same. The boy looked at his ribs, held his breath, and struck where he had looked, and Cael was a step outside it. He did not strike back, because he had planned not to; he wanted to see whether anything in the boy changed when he missed twice.

Nothing did.

The boy's face went darker. His breathing got louder at the top of every breath, but it still stopped dead before every heat, as if a hand had been put over his mouth. On his mark between the exchanges he glared across at Cael and rubbed the burned side of his right hand against his trouser leg.

In the third exchange Cael went in.

The boy looked at his head, and his chest went still, and Cael stepped left and forward on the stillness, before the heat. The heat went over his right shoulder and singed the air beside his collar, and he was inside it, close enough to smell the brick dust on the boy's shirt. His right hand came up from the hip, short and straight, the one strike he owned, the one Lira had made him throw at the post until his knuckles knew the canvas by heart. He stopped it with his knuckles touching the boy's jaw, just under the ear.

The kiln boy stood there with his breath still held, and the shimmer going out of his hand, and his eyes very wide.

"Called," said Vell. "Unrated."

It had taken perhaps three minutes.

The rope made a pleased noise, the ordinary kind. The kiln boy let his breath go at last, all at once, and looked at Cael's fist by his jaw, and then at Cael. He did not look angry any more. He looked mostly surprised, like somebody who has been told a thing about his own face by a stranger.

"How'd you know where I'd be?"

Cael took his hand away.

"You look," he said. "Before you hit. You look at where you're going to hit."

The boy stared at him.

"Everybody does that."

"No. Not everybody."

He walked to the table and stood in front of it, as you stood, win or lose, until she was finished with you.

"Result stands as called," said Vell, writing. "Unrated, over—" and she gave the kiln boy's name again, and then, "Copper Rank 2, Ember Path. Third exchange. Called." The pen went on for a line, and stopped, and did not go on. "Witnessed and entered."

That was all; there were no extra lines tonight, or none that she wrote while he was standing there. He had been listening for them without meaning to, and he noticed that he had been listening and did not like it.

When he came off the dirt the errand boys were arguing about something, and the one in the red cap held his hand out flat, and the other one put something in it with a very bad grace. Marrow had paid out, by the look of him, and had paid out cheerfully, which meant he had taken more than he gave.

Lira met him at the barrel. "Well."

"It was easy."

"It was easy." Her face did not do anything. "And you don't trust it."

"No."

"Good." She handed him his coat. "Easy happens. Some days you get a boy who holds his breath and looks where he's going to hit, and it goes exactly the way you wrote it. It doesn't mean the hard ones have gone anywhere. They're still out there, waiting their turn. Don't start thinking you've got a method that works on people. You've got a method that worked on him."

"I know."

"I know you know. It's getting said all the same, so it's been said." She bumped his good shoulder, which was both of them, today. "That's one."

He wrote it up at the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard before he went in, while the light went. It was the seventh entry in the Log and only his sixth bout, because the Log had counted Baro, whom he had only watched, and Vell's book counted nothing it had not seen fought. It was the shortest entry he had ever made.

*Seventh. The kiln boy (I didn't keep his name), Cu 2, Ember. Claim: holds his breath before every heat; looks at where he means to hit. Evidence: two bouts watched, every time, both nights. Ruling: step outside the look on the stillness; in on the third. Won, third exchange, called.*

He sat for a moment with the pencil. Then he added, under the ruling, where he would see it:

*It worked because he had never been told. That's not the same as it working. Don't learn the wrong thing from this.*

He looked at the line and did not cross it out, and went in to supper.

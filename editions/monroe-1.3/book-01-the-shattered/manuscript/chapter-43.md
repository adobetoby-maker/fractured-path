# Chapter 43 — Three More Weeks

He wrote to Hesk that night, at the kitchen table at Torvin's, after everybody else had gone up.

He had not meant to do it then. He had meant to sleep on it, and write on Sunday, and post on Monday as he always did, with the west bag at noon. But when he came in from the lane in the dark, Torvin's wife was banking the range and the table was empty and scrubbed, and there was a lamp on it, and he sat down at it with the Log and a sheet of paper and could not get up again.

He had told Lira. That had been the promise: when he knew what it was, really knew, she would be the first. He still did not know what it was. But he had found out, sitting on her floor, that not knowing was not a reason to keep a thing, only a reason to say *I don't know* beside it. And she had been the first, and now there was somebody second.

He sat with the pen over the paper while the range ticked as it cooled. Then he wrote.

*Hesk,*

*There are five things I haven't told you. I've counted them every time I sealed a letter since the end of the first month, so I know the number exactly. I'm going to tell you all five now, in order, as plainly as I can, and then I'm going to tell you why I didn't before, and you can decide what you think of that.*

*The first was on the road, on the third day, before I ever got here. A cart came round a bend behind me and I stepped out of its way before I heard it. I told the carter I'd heard it. I don't think I did.*

*The second was my first bout, against Renn, in the third exchange. He threw a figure at me that should have caught me whatever I did, and I wasn't where it went. I didn't choose where I went. I tore the page out of the book where I'd written it, and kept it in my coat for a month, and didn't read it.*

*The third was in the yard, in the mornings, with Lira. She threw a backhand and I dropped under it, lower than I've ever dropped, in a way that wasn't mine. She asked me how I knew it was coming and I said I didn't know, and that was true.*

*The fourth was the Dessa fight. I told you I built the third exchange, and I did. I didn't tell you about the fourth, because I didn't build it. It happened the way the drop happened. Your line about a win you built sat on the page right over the place where I hadn't written it, and I read it three times.*

*The fifth came two weeks ago, three nights before the six weeks ran out, while I sat on my cot at Torvin's. Something came up where the Arbiter used to be, written in the dark. I'll copy it exactly, because you'd want it exact.*

He copied the three lines from the back of the Log, letter by letter, and checked them twice.

*I think it's Lira's. Some of it. I think it came from watching her every morning for three months, but I don't know that, and I've spent the last fortnight finding out how much I don't know. I can make the step on purpose now, badly. It costs something in the breath and the hip that I can't measure yet. I tried to get something from a second person, a Force fighter, by watching him two whole days. Nothing came that I could stand on.*

*I told Lira today. I'd promised her she'd be the first, before you, before anybody, and she was. She didn't flinch. She said it wasn't theft, and that she'd checked, and she hadn't got less.*

*Why I didn't tell you. At first it was because I didn't know what it was, and I didn't want to send you something that would frighten you a week late, with nothing I could do about it from here. You know what's in the archive better than I do. Then it was because I'd promised Lira first. Those are both true. But the first one is the same reason you gave me across the bench on the second day, and I was angry with you for it then, and I think I was right to be. So that reason's finished, for me.*

*You told me once to tell you as it comes. This is how it came. I'm sorry it came all at once.*

*I'm all right. I'm well. I'm not frightened, most of the time. I'll tell you the next thing when it happens, and not five things later.*

*— Cael*

He read it over, twice, by the lamp. It was the longest letter he had ever written, and there was nothing in it that he did not know to be true. When he had finished the second reading, he folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for Monday, and then sat at the scrubbed table with the lamp turned low and counted, as he always did now, what he had not told Hesk.

There was nothing to count.

It did not feel as he had thought it would. He had thought it would feel like putting down something heavy, and it did a little, but mostly it felt like having opened a door in winter: colder, and a great deal more air. Somewhere in Denvash, in a week, a man would sit on the step of his workshop with this in his hands, and be frightened, and there was nothing Cael could do about that. He had decided it was better than the other thing anyway.

Torvin said *Lamps* through the floorboards from upstairs, though there was only one lamp left lit in the house, and it was Cael's. He turned it out.

---

On Sunday morning, in the grey half, Lira did the step for him on purpose.

She did not say she was going to. She was at the post when he came in through the gate with the bread, as she always was, and she took the loaves off him and tore them and gave him half. Then, while he was still eating, she went to her mark and stood there in the frost with her hands loose at her sides and looked at him.

"Watch," she said.

He stopped chewing, and she did it slower than she had ever done it for him, slower even than on the first morning she showed it to him, when he had not known what he was looking at. She did the two empty touches, high and light, so slowly that he could see her fingers open at the end of each, and then the drop of the hip and the turn, the beginning of everything, the part he had watched a hundred times from the inside and never once been able to see. Then came the ground folding, which he could see now, from out here, as a kind of shortening in the air between her front foot and where it landed, and the backhand coming round at the height of his head, slow as a door swinging in no wind, and stopping by his ear.

She stood there with her hand by his ear and looked at him.

"Again?"

"Lira—"

"I'm not doing it so it'll go into you." She dropped her hand. "I don't know if that's how it works. You don't either. It might be watching, it might be something else, it might be the weather. I'm doing it because yesterday I found out something of mine had been going across to you for three months without either of us knowing, and I'd rather it went across with both of us looking." She shrugged, a little stiffly, as though the shrug were new to her. "If it's going to happen anyway, I want to be in the room. That's all."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and watched.

She did it again slowly, and then at half speed, and then at the speed she used against Brenna, so that the ground went short between one breath and the next and the backhand was by his ear before he had seen it start. On the tenth time her weight went a hair past the end of the backhand onto her right foot, and he said "one finger," at once, and she said "one," to herself, pleased, and pushed her hair off her face with her wrist. Up on the wall the two girls had stopped eating to watch.

"They'll be doing it on the wall by tomorrow," said Lira, without looking round. "Badly." She came and sat on the low wall beside him and took her own bread back off the post. "Now you. The bad one, the one by the cot. I've seen the other one against Dessa and Sarel, from the rope. I want to see the one you can ask for."

So he stood up in the grey half and showed her.

It was as clumsy as it had been every night. He dropped his hip and turned and put his foot down, slowly, stiffly, and the dirt between his foot and where it landed went short by the width of a hand, and his breath went, and the ache came into the joint of the left hip. He stood there afterward breathing as if he had run up the stairs at Torvin's. She said *again*, very quietly, and he did it again, and it was worse, as it always was the second time.

Then she got up and went to the place where his foot had come down, and crouched, and put her palm flat on the frozen dirt there, as she had put it on the iron stake at Talis.

"It's mine," she said. "The shape of it. Badly. The hip's wrong, and you're far too slow, and you stop at the end as if you're waiting to be told you can." She looked up at him from the dirt. "But it's mine. I'd know it anywhere. It's like hearing somebody hum a tune you made up when you were small, a long way off, out of tune."

"Does that bother you?"

"No. It ought to. I keep waiting for it to, and it won't." She stood up and dusted her hand on her trousers. "It costs you, though. Your breath went, and you went white round the mouth. What does it cost?"

"I don't know. Breath. The hip. More the second time."

"Write it down. Every time. How many, how much, how long till it goes." She went back to her mark. "I'm not telling you it matters. I don't know if it matters. I'm telling you that a thing that costs you something you can't measure is going to cost you more than you think one day, at the worst possible time." She brought her hands up. "Box drill. Full speed. And don't you dare do that in it."

---

The ledger was shut, and he had never once seen it shut. On card days and empty days alike it lay open at Vell's elbow, squared to the table's edge, whether or not there was anything to put in it. This morning she sat square to the back wall with her tea and the closed book in front of her, and her hands folded on its cover, and that was how he knew before she said a word that whatever had brought them here was not ordinary.

The pie boy had found them in the grey half, before Cael could get down to the carriers' hut with his letter. Vell wanted the pair of them at her table before the yard opened, he had said, and she had said to say *not after the bread*.

She waited until they were standing in front of her.

"There's a man coming through in three weeks," she said. "Feryn. Bronze Rank 2, Pressure Path. I've put my name to one fighter for him." She nodded toward Cael without looking at him. "You."

Beside him Lira went entirely still; he felt it more than saw it.

"Assessed-Copper, a fortnight since, in my own hand," said Vell. "Mind, assessed is not rated. The book still has you unrated, and so will I at the rope, because the registry gives me nothing to put there. Assessed is my word for how you fight, no more than that. But a keeper's word is what he posted for." Her eyes went to Lira. "I'd sooner you had it from me than off Marrow's slate."

"What's he doing in Ardenmere?" said Lira.

"Passing through. He's from nowhere in particular, these days. He has his own evaluation next season, and he's going round the circuits first, a bout in one town and on to the next. He likes to try himself on people he's never met. He's posted for interesting opponents, any rating a keeper will sign for, and there's a purse on it most people in this yard wouldn't see in a season, and a gate besides. He'll fight here the once, a Monday at noon, because that's when the road brings him past us, and then he'll be gone." She laid one finger on the closed cover. "I don't put anybody in against Feryn. I tell them he's coming and what he is. If they want him, they come and ask me, and then I decide. That's the whole of this."

Cael looked at the shut book. "What's Pressure like?"

"Heavy." Vell gave it some thought. "Five years I've watched him, in three cities. The first time he was nineteen and still Copper, and a man with one arm beat him on a wet night, and he laughed about it all the way to the water bucket. He's come on a long way since. What he does isn't a strike. It's nearer to weather. The ones who've stood in front of it say it feels as if the air itself were leaning on them." Only then did she lift her eyes and really look at him. "I don't know anybody in this district who's fought it and been glad afterward."

Lira said nothing. He could feel her not saying it.

He went at it as he went at anything that mattered, from all the sides he had not tried yet, and he did not stop to ask whether he wanted it, because he could feel that he did, all through him, and wanting was not a reason. Three weeks would do for watching a man, so long as the man fought somewhere a person could watch him. It would not do for building anything like what he had built against Dessa, or Dellin, or Corbin, and Pressure he had never seen, not once.

"Is there anyone else he's meeting," he said, "somewhere I could get to?"

Vell looked at him.

"On the tour," said Cael. "Before he comes here. Whoever he's fought, anywhere close. Whoever's due to fight him between now and the Monday, if I could get there to see it."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Lira laughed, and it went up into the cold over Vell's table and made the crows on the eaves shuffle along the gutter.

"That's all?" she said. "A Bronze 2 is coming in three weeks, a man who was fighting when you were still learning your letters, and you don't say *am I ready* or *is it too soon*. You ask who you can study."

"Is that the wrong thing to ask?"

"It's the right thing exactly. Only most people your age would ask something else." The smile stayed, but something grave had come in under it to stay as well. "You're fourteen, Cael. I don't say it to stop you. I say it so you'll have heard it said out loud, once, before you choose. You've a very bad habit of doing the sum as if your age weren't one of the figures. It's one of the figures. It needn't be the one that decides it."

He considered that, and could see that it was true.

"So I need three more weeks," he said.

Vell made a small noise in her throat which, in anybody else, might have been a laugh.

"There's nobody to watch," she said. "He fought two days west of here a fortnight back, and he's gone south since, and who he meets between there and here, I can't tell you and neither can he. The first time you see him fight will be in my circle, if you see him at all." She opened the ledger at last, found her page, and took up her pen. "You've until Friday to tell me if you want him. I'd give it longer than you've given it so far."

"I'll think about it."

"You'll think about it, and then you'll say yes." She was already writing. "I've watched you think. Off you go. You're standing in my light."

---

They walked down the lane together in the frost, and Lira did not say anything for a whole street. Then, at the corner by the board, she stopped and turned round and looked at him.

"Survivable," she said.

"You haven't said that."

"I'm saying it now. I'm going to work out whether it's true, and if it isn't, I'll tell you, and you'll listen. That's been the rule since the first morning of the six weeks, and it didn't stop being the rule because the six weeks ran out." She looked at the board, where Sunday's card was already nailed up in Vell's upright pencil, and took her chalk out of her pocket and turned it over in her fingers, though there was no post here to make a mark on. "If this is happening, it happens right. Not with momentum. You've been going on momentum since Corbin, and momentum's a very good way to walk into a Bronze with nothing in your hands. I want everybody within two days' walk who's stood up to a Bronze for three exchanges and come away with something to say about it, Pressure if there's one. You take Torvin's. I'll take the yards and the eating house, and we'll meet at the well tonight."

He posted the letter to Hesk at noon, at the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps. The clerk read the address out to the whole hut, as he read every address, and weighed the letter in his hand and looked at Cael over it, and said "Thick one," and hung it on the third hook for the west bag. In a week a man would sit down on a workshop step in Denvash with it. There was nothing to be done about that now, and Cael was glad there was nothing, and went back up the steps.

Torvin's was Doss. He found him at supper at the far end of the long table where nobody sat, with his bad leg out stiff along the bench and his pipe unlit beside his bowl. Cael sat down across from him, which nobody did either, while the brothers stopped arguing to watch him do it and Torvin's wife looked at them both from the range with her spoon in the air.

"Three exchanges with a Bronze," said Cael. "Does anybody in the district know about that?"

Doss looked at him with his flattened face doing nothing, while one of the brothers asked for the salt and was ignored.

"I was one," he said at last. "For a season and a half. I went more than three with a good many people." He picked up his pipe and looked at it. "That's not what you're asking. You're asking who went three with a Bronze and wasn't one. Pressure?"

"Yes."

Doss set the pipe down beside his bowl, very precisely, as if he were laying a tool on a bench.

"There was a boy, few years back, from the river workshops. Went four with a Pressure man at Fenrow, and walked out. He's gone to the coast, I heard." He took up his spoon. "And there's Corvane. Lives down past the brickworks, at the far end. Trains young ones for coin, when she can get them. She lost to every Pressure fighter on this river for ten years, and then she stopped fighting them and started studying them. She'll know more about it than anybody this side of the bridge." He looked up from his bowl. "She won't want to talk to you. She doesn't want to talk to anybody."

---

Lira came to the well that night with the same boy gone to the coast, which they agreed did not count twice, and one name more. It was a woman who sold eels on the fish steps, who had fought a Bronze at the Cinder House eight or nine years ago and lasted, the girls on the wall said, longer than anybody expected. They went to her on Tuesday morning after the grey half, because Lira said there was no point keeping a name in your pocket, and meant to go on from there to Corvane's.

The eel woman was broad and red-faced, in a sacking apron, with her sleeves rolled up past the elbow in the cold and her arms in a tub of eels to the wrist, and she laughed when Lira told her what they wanted, a big laugh that made the men unloading the barges look round.

"Three exchanges," she said. "With Hollow-eyed what's-his-name, the Bronze from across the bridge. I was a Copper 4 and I thought a great deal of myself. He put me down in the first exchange and I got up. He put me down in the second and I got up. In the third I didn't get up for a quarter of an hour, and when I did, I'd forgotten what day it was." She beamed at them. "That was the last time I ever stood in a circle, and I've never once missed it."

"What did you learn?" said Cael. "From the three."

She thought about it seriously, with her arms in the eels.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Just don't. That's the whole of it, my love. Three exchanges with a Bronze, and that's what they taught me, and it's the best thing anybody ever taught me." She lifted an eel and held it out to Lira. "Do you want one of these? Half price for fighters."

They did not want one. Lira kept her face perfectly straight all the way to the top of the fish steps, where it gave out, and she had to lean on the post there with her shoulders shaking and wipe her eyes with her wrist. "We should put it on the board under Vell's card," she said. "*Bronze, Monday at noon. Don't.*" Then she straightened up. "That's one name used up and one gone to the coast, which leaves your Doss's. Everybody wants to talk to somebody. You just have to work out what about."

Corvane lived at the far end of the district, an hour's walk past the brickworks, where the kilns made the air taste of soot and the lanes were built of the cracked bricks nobody had wanted. Her house was the last in a low row with their backs to the river, and beside it, behind a wall, there was a yard, and somebody in it was giving instructions in a dry level voice that did not carry while somebody young answered *yes* every time.

Lira stopped him at the gate. "Wait here. She's an old fighter who doesn't want to talk to anybody, and you're a boy everybody's started talking about, and if you come in with me she'll decide you're a show and shut the gate." She took her jacket off and folded it over her arm, so that she would look like what she was, a fighter come to ask a fighter something, and went in.

He waited in the lane by the wall for most of an hour. A young man came out through the gate rubbing his shoulder and walked off without looking at him. Then the dry voice started again, lower, talking to Lira, and he could hear only the shape of the conversation going back and forth across the yard: Lira's voice going up once, sharply, and coming down, a long silence, something short from the dry voice, and then Lira laughing as she laughed at Marrow when she had got the price she wanted.

She came out with her face pink with cold and something else.

"Thursday," she said. "The afternoon. Two hours. She wants a mark for it, which is too much, and I've paid half already and you'll pay the rest." She put her jacket on. "And she wants to look at you."

"What did you say to her?"

"I asked her what she'd studied for ten years and never once had anybody worth showing it to." Lira did her buttons up one by one. "And then I told her what you do, which I hope you don't mind, because it was the thing that did it. She'd heard *the Denvash boy at Torvin's*, and *past Corbin*, and thought it was talk. She asked whether it was true you watched Renn for two months and found his heel, and whether you'd quit watching in two exchanges against Sarel and done something else, and I said yes to both. And she was quiet, and then she said that she had never in her life met anybody who could do both, and that she supposed she'd better see one before she died."

"And you laughed."

"She meant it to be funny. I think. She's harder to read than Corbin." Lira started back down the lane toward the kilns, and glanced back at him. "You'll like her."

That night, on the cot, he wrote at the top of a clean page in the front of the Log: *Feryn. Bronze Rank 2, Pressure. Three weeks.* Under it he wrote: *Pressure: heavy. Like weather. Like the air leaning on you (Vell, h). Don't (the eel woman, h). Corvane, Thursday.* There was nothing else he knew, and he had stopped pretending long ago that a short page meant a lazy one.

On Wednesday morning, before the grey half, he went to Vell's table and told her yes, two days before she had asked him to. She wrote it down.

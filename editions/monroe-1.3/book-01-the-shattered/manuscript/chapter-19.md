# Chapter 19 — Absence

Torvin was at the kitchen table again that evening, with his thin ledger and its cracked spine, adding columns with his lips moving.

It had become a thing he did on certain evenings, though not on any evenings Cael could predict. He would come in from wherever he went in the afternoons and sit at the end of the table with the ledger and a stub of pencil.

His wife would put a bowl at his elbow without asking, and nobody would speak to him until he reached the bottom of a column.

Tonight the whole house seemed to be at the table. Yeni had a shirt across her knee, the woman who slept in her boots was eating soup in her boots, and the brothers had come in early, because the river was high and nobody was moving barges on it. They were arguing in low voices about whether the river was high or only looked high.

Cael ate his soup without tasting much of it. He kept feeling the drop in his legs, in the long muscles at the backs of his thighs, where it had stretched something he did not normally stretch; it was not pain so much as the memory of having been somewhere.

Torvin reached the bottom of a column, wrote a figure under it, and drew a line.

"Man came by this afternoon," he said, to the ledger. "Asking after the circuit kid staying here."

The brothers stopped arguing.

Cael put his spoon down, carefully, so that it did not click against the bowl.

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing." Torvin turned the page. He seemed perfectly satisfied about this, in the way a man is satisfied with a wall he has built that has not fallen down. "Said I don't keep a list of who fights. Said I don't want to see anybody's registration and I don't. Said if he wanted to know about circuit kids, he could go and ask the circuit." He started down the next column. "He went."

"What did he look like?"

Torvin's pencil paused while he considered the question from several sides, as he considered most things.

"A man. Polite. Not from round here, I'd say, by his boots. Didn't give a name and I didn't ask for one." The pencil went on. "Didn't say why. I didn't ask that either."

That was all. He did not seem to think it very important. As far as Cael could tell, he thought the important part of the story was his own discretion, and had told it mainly so that the table would know how well he had done.

"People ask about the fighters," said Yeni, without looking up from her shirt. "They always have. Somebody sees a bout and wants to know where the boy lives, so they can tell their friends they saw him buying bread." Her needle went in and out. "It means you're worth a bet. That's all it usually means."

"Usually," said Cael.

Yeni's needle paused for half a stitch, and then went on.

"I'd have told him you were seven foot tall," said the woman in the boots, through her soup. "And lived on the roof. And ate nails."

"I didn't tell him anything," said Torvin, mildly. "That's the point of not telling people things." He reached the bottom of the column, and wrote, and drew his line. "Doesn't work if you tell them nails."

The brothers laughed. Torvin's wife said something about the soup getting cold, and the table went back to its ordinary noise, and after a while Cael picked up his spoon again.

It stayed with him through the rest of the meal: a polite man with boots from somewhere else, standing at the door under the leaning O. From what Torvin had said, he had asked not for a name but for *the circuit kid*, for what he was, the way Vell's book knew him. *Unrated. Fourteen. Vouched.*

It sat beside the fish man's nod and the errand boys' half-marks and Marrow's eyes coming up from his slate, and beside Lira on the low wall saying *the boy who's dangerous, and nobody knows why*.

It was probably nothing. Yeni was probably right, and it was somebody who had stood at the rope on Sunday and wanted to tell his friends where the boy lived.

He wrote it down that night anyway, in the front of the Log, in the margin with no column, under the fish man.

*Torvin: a man asked at the door after "the circuit kid". Polite. Not local, by his boots (Torvin, h). No name asked, none given. Torvin told him nothing.*

He looked at it for a while, and did not know what the ruling was, and left the third column empty.

---

The front of the book came first. That was the rule, and tonight he kept it even though his hands wanted to go straight to the back.

He wrote the morning's drill as an entry, because it had been a bout of a kind. *Lira, the step. Third morning on it.* He wrote the two touches and the step and his own counter, back and out, and was honest about the numbers: his counter had not worked once that morning.

Then he came to the drop, and tried to put it in the columns.

*Claim: the first time she used the step this morning, I dropped under the backhand.*

It was true. He went on.

*Evidence: Lira saw it. I felt the strike go over. Left foot left a mark in the dirt.*

That was true too. He had looked at the mark himself, on the way out through the gate, a long curved scuff across the grey half, already blurring.

*Ruling:*

He held the pencil over the third column for a long time.

There was nothing to put there. A ruling was what he would do next time, and he did not know what he would do next time, because he had not done anything this time. He had neither decided to drop nor known he was going to, and when he reached for it twice afterward, in front of the same strike, it had not come.

A ruling that said *drop under the backhand* would be a lie, because he could not drop under the backhand. He had tried.

It was the third exchange against Renn all over again. The columns would not take it. He had a claim and a witness and a mark in the dirt, and nothing he could do with any of it.

He did not tear the page out this time. He drew one line through it, neatly, so that the words could still be read underneath, and beside it in the margin he wrote *back*.

Then he closed the front of the book and turned it over.

He had been carrying two things in a corner for weeks, and he let himself look at them now, which he had not done before. He had known they were there the way you know a stone is in your boot without stopping to take it out, and he had walked a long way on them.

The first was the cart.

It was written down, in the back of the old notebook, the third one from Denvash, on a page near the end, in pencil, in the dark. *I told the carter I heard it coming. I don't think I did.* He had written it the night of Lira's question at the board, and had not drawn any arrow from it, and had not counted it.

It was a fact by itself on a page, and he had shut the book on it.

The second was Renn.

That one was not written down at all. It was in Vell's book, in her hand, in whatever she had written in those eight extra lines; it was in Lira's head as *tonight's answer*, and in Renn's as *it doesn't miss, it missed*; but it was in no book of his own.

He had tried to write it once, at the pump, and had torn the page out and folded it, and the folded page was still in the pocket of his coat, never unfolded since.

And now there was this.

He sat on the end of his cot with the Log open on his knee at its last page, and Yeni's lamp turned low, and the wheezing man whistling by the door, and he thought very clearly: *there are three.*

He did not like the thought. It went down into him and stayed there, cold.

Three was not one. One was a thing that happened. One could be the wheels he had not noticed hearing, or the watching that had soaked into him without his knowing, or a figure that had come off its lever wrong by luck.

Two was harder, but two could still be a coincidence if you wanted it to be badly enough, and he had wanted it so badly that he had not let himself count to two.

Three was a column.

He could count all three, now, tonight. He could turn to the back of the old notebook and copy the cart into this one, unfold the page from his coat and write the whole of the third exchange as honestly as he could, then write this morning under it and number them. *One, two, three.* That would be honest arithmetic, and Hesk would have done it that way.

Hesk would have said that you did not get to choose where a column started, that it started with the first figure, and if you left the first figure out because it frightened you, then every total under it would be wrong.

He knew that, and he sat with it for a long time, and then he decided, with the pencil in his hand, that he would not.

Not yet. He would not count the cart, because the cart had been on a road with nobody to see it, and he still could not be sure, not all the way, that it was not his own ears.

He would not count Renn, because Renn had been in the middle of a bout, and bouts were full of things, and he had been frightened and fast and watching harder than he had ever watched anything. He could still tell himself, if he tried, that the watching had done it. He did not believe it any more, but he could still tell himself.

This one he could not tell himself anything about.

This one had happened in daylight, in the grey half of a yard he knew, against a strike he had seen thirty times, with somebody standing in front of him who knew exactly how he moved. It had not been hearing, and it had not been watching, because he had been watching, and building his counter, and it had taken the counter out of his hands.

Lira had seen it and said so, and there was a mark in the dirt.

This one he would count.

He wrote, at the top of the clean back page:

*First instance.*

Then he sat and looked at the two words, which he had known were not true before he wrote them.

It was not the first. It was the first he was counting, which was a different thing, and he knew exactly how different, and he was doing it anyway.

A column had to start somewhere. He had found that he could not yet make himself start it further back, and he would rather start it here, on purpose, knowing what he had left out, than not start it at all.

He was not proud of that. He wrote under it anyway.

*Morning, Cinder House yard, the grey half. Lira's step, the two touches and the backhand. I was going back and out, my own counter. I didn't. I dropped. Low, sideways, left leg out behind, right knee under, turned from the hip. I have never been taught that. I have never done it. It was exactly on time. The backhand went over the back of my head.*

*Afterward I was standing wrong. Feet wide, hips too far round, arms low. As if I had put on somebody else's coat and the sleeves were in the wrong place.*

*I tried to find it again twice. Nothing there. It doesn't come when I want it to. It doesn't come because the same strike comes again.*

*She asked me how I knew. I don't know.*

He stopped, because his hand was cold, and then went on.

*Hesk wrote: your body is going to learn before your mind does. On the wall by the road I thought he meant that I would do things before I understood them. Like a reflex you grow into. I don't think that's what this was. My body didn't learn this. It didn't have it to learn. It wasn't mine.*

He read that back, and his stomach turned over, and he nearly crossed it out. Instead he drew a small box round *It wasn't mine*, which was not a thing he had ever done in a notebook before. He did it so that he would know when he came back to it that he had meant it, and been frightened by it, and left it there.

---

He got as far as taking a clean sheet out of the back of the old notebook to write to Hesk. He sat with it on his knee and the pencil over it, as he had sat at the waystation table and the kitchen table, and thought about what he would say.

*Something happened today.*

It would be a week getting there. Hesk would read it at the kitchen table in the twenty minutes of sideways light, and then he would have nothing to do about it but sit in the workshop with a guild review on the shelf and his grandson's fear in a letter.

And whatever Hesk wrote back would come a week after that. By then the morning in the grey half would be two weeks old, and whatever was happening to him would have gone on happening, or stopped, and the answer would be to a question he had stopped asking.

That was true, and it was a good reason, and he knew it was not the whole one. The whole one was smaller, and he was less proud of it: he wanted to understand this himself first.

Everything else about the word had come to him from somebody else. There were the four lines in the summary; the green book in the archive and its three entries ending in weeks; Pellin's form from the back of a drawer; the notice; Hesk's book, with its *I don't know* in faded ink and its fifth entry he had read three times.

All of it had been handed to him, already written, by people who had got there first and were warning him. Even Hesk, who loved him, had been living alongside the word for years before Cael had ever heard it.

This was the first thing about it that had happened to him and to nobody else, the first that nobody had warned him about or had a page on. He did not know what it was, but it was his, and he wanted, for a little while, to be the one who found out.

He knew it was the same choice, exactly. *Don't make a thing real before it is.* He had been angry at Hesk for making it, in a room with a crooked tree in the window. And here he was making it again, with a cart, and with Renn, and now with this.

He put the clean sheet back in the old notebook.

Then, in the back of the Log, under the box, he wrote one more line, because he had decided that if he was going to keep things from Hesk he was at least going to keep a record of keeping them.

*Didn't write to Hesk about it. That's the third thing I haven't told him. I'll have to answer for all of them at once one day. Know that.*

And under that, smaller:

*It happened in her yard, against her strike. Be careful with that. For her sake.*

He sat there afterward with the Log closed on his knee and found that he had been thinking about ledgers for weeks without noticing it. There was Vell's book, which knew him as what he was and what he had done, without a name.

There was Marrow's slate, which knew him as a number that moved, *one*, *two*, *three*, *four*, chalked up and wiped off with a sleeve. There was the Log on his knee, which knew him better than either of them and was still full of empty third columns.

And there was one more, which everybody else in Ardenmere had. Joren and Alis had it, and Renn and Brenna and Amrit Sole and Petra Voss, and Lira, whose eyes he had watched go somewhere for a moment, now and then, between drills, in a way he had never asked about.

It was the one ledger that was supposed to tell you what you were, tier and rank and Path and the declarations one after another as they came, and you could check it whenever you liked, and believe what it said.

He had not checked his in four weeks.

He made himself say it plainly, in his head. Not four days; not since he had settled in, or found his feet, or been too busy. Four weeks. He had looked for it twice in the side room at Weaver's Row, with the crooked tree in the window, and it had not been there, and he had not looked since.

He had told himself all sorts of things about why: that there was no point, because he knew what he would find; that there was no time, between the yard and the mender and the circle; that he had more important things to measure. Each of them had sounded perfectly reasonable while he was saying it.

He knew what the truth was, and had known for some time, and it was the same thing he had been doing with the cart and with Renn.

He was afraid of the answer.

He was afraid that he would reach for it and nothing would happen, and then he would know. And he was afraid, in a way he had not let himself look at at all, that something would happen, that it would open and show him something he would not understand.

An honest log used every instrument it had; he had made that rule himself, at the pump, with the straightedge. *Never trust a number you can't trace.* And there was one instrument he had not touched once, because it frightened him.

That meant every page in the Log, every claim and every piece of evidence and every ruling, had been written by somebody who was not looking at the one gauge everybody else looked at first.

He got up.

---

It was past midnight. The house had gone quiet round him: the wheezing man, the brothers breathing in their long river-tired rhythm, Yeni turned to the wall. He went down the turning stairs in his socks, carrying his boots, and put them on at the bottom, and let himself out through the kitchen into the yard.

The yard at night was a different place.

In the day it was where the pump was, and the washing, and Torvin's wife's buckets, and once a week somebody boiling sheets over a low fire in the corner. At night it was only a square of packed dirt between high walls, with the pump standing up in the middle of it like a thin dark man and the stunted pear tree crouched wide in its corner by the wall.

There were no lamps. The only light came from one shuttered window high up in the next house, a thin bar of yellow that fell across the top of the far wall and did not reach the ground.

It was cold enough to see his breath. He went to the middle of the yard, to a spot that seemed to him, for no reason at all, to be the centre of something, and stood there.

He called up the circle in Pellin's office, the pale wood worn smooth in two places side by side. He called up the feeling of putting his feet where the wood was worn, and the thing that had gathered behind his breastbone, small, and risen, and come to rest at the edge of his sight, just off to one side. *Every practitioner's Arbiter is their own, dormant since birth.* The procedural summary had said that you reached it by attending to the place behind the breastbone.

He had read that a dozen times at the long table under the window without once having to do it.

He attended to the place behind his breastbone.

Nothing happened.

He tried to remember exactly how it had felt, not the word but before the word: the point of light rising through him slowly, like a held breath. The low note at the bottom of his chest that was less a sound than the memory of one.

He held all of it in his mind, carefully, the way he would hold a measurement he did not want to lose, and he reached for the place where it had been.

Nothing.

He moved his feet, which was foolish. Nothing about the circle had needed a particular stance, and he knew it. He set his feet a little wider anyway and squared his shoulders, as a man leans harder on a stuck door when what it wants is the handle turned the other way. Then he shut his eyes.

Joren had said *it shows up right there, not in your eyes, sort of behind them, like words written on the inside of your eyelids.* He looked at the inside of his eyelids.

They were dark.

He opened his eyes and looked to the edge of his sight, left, where it had hung, steady as a nail, for eleven seconds. Then right. Then left again, quickly, the way you turn your head for a sound you are sure you heard.

The pump. The wall. The bar of yellow light. The pear tree.

He tried again, and again, and he counted, because he had always counted, though he would have given a good deal not to. Four. Five. He held the reach longer each time, past the point where it felt reasonable, the way you hold your breath past comfort to find out how long you can, daring the dark behind his breastbone to prove him wrong. Six. Seven.

It did not prove him wrong.

And it was not silence. He knew that now, standing in the yard, as clearly as he had known it in the side room. Silence would have been something there, choosing not to answer, the way Pellin's brass plate had sat dark for eleven seconds and then lit. He could have waited out a silence. He could have knocked louder.

This was absence. It was not a door that would not open. It was the place where he had been told a door would be, and there was no door there, not even the shape of one, and nothing to knock on.

Eight. Nine.

Somewhere after that he stopped counting.

He noticed the number go, the way you notice a clock stop ticking in another room, and he understood that for him, of all people, that was its own kind of answer, and he stopped reaching.

He stood in the middle of the yard with his hands at his sides.

The cold had got into his fingers and was working its way up his wrists, and the burn on his left forearm, which had been only an itch for days, had begun to ache again. Somewhere a long way off a dog barked twice and was told to be quiet, and was.

The bar of yellow light on the far wall went out, and then the yard was quite dark, and he could see only the shape of the pump and the shape of the tree.

He thought about the people who had it. Joren had read his declaration four hundred times. Alis could touch her mother's arm and know she had a headache.

He thought of the twins coming out of the examination room together with their certificates and their loosened faces, and the glazed boy who had got Force and did not know whether he wanted it, and of Lira's eyes going somewhere for a moment between drills and coming back, as though she had glanced at a clock. He had never asked her about that, because he had not wanted to know how easy it was.

Every one of them could reach into the dark behind their breastbone and find a light there, and the light would tell them what they were. Not the whole of a person, but the part the city cared about, the part that decided which side of the bridge you slept on: tier, rank, Path, whether you were getting better.

And he had stood in the circle and been told one word, and then the light had gone out, and he had been standing in the dark ever since and calling it a classification.

He found that he wanted to cry.

He noticed it the way he noticed everything, from a little way off: a tightness at the back of his throat, a heat behind his eyes, his breath gone uneven. He stood very still in the middle of the yard and looked at it.

He did not cry. He did not think it would have been wrong to; it would have been a perfectly reasonable thing to do in a dark yard at midnight after finding out that there was nothing there.

But he did not, and he noted that he did not, and that he wanted to, and that the wanting was information too, and that he did not yet know what it measured, and he put it away with the rest.

Then he went back across the yard, and let himself in, and took his boots off at the bottom of the stairs, and went up in his socks.

---

He did not write anything, though he had thought he would. He lay on his cot with the Log under his pillow and the pencil in the spine, and listened to the room for a long while in the dark, and at some point he slept, because at some point he dreamed.

It was not much of a dream. He was standing in the circle at Weaver's Row, with his feet in the worn places, and nothing was happening, the same nothing as in the yard. But in the dream the nothing had a texture to it, almost a weight, like standing in a doorway that went somewhere and had decided not to open for him and for nobody else.

He woke once, in the black part of the night, not knowing where he was, until he heard the wheezing man and knew. *Torvin's. The window cot. Ardenmere.* And then he slept again.

When he woke for good the window had gone grey and the brothers were dropping their boots.

He lay still and did what he did most mornings now, taking stock of what he had learned, what he still did not know, and what the day was going to ask of him.

The yard was not an open question any more. He had tried properly, many times, and there had been nothing, and now he knew; it was not the knowing he had wanted, but it was a fact, and you could build on a fact, and he had not had this one before.

And there was one other thing he knew, lying there in the grey light with the boots dropping, and it surprised him by how sure it was.

Standing still with this had not helped. He had stood still with it for four weeks, and in a yard at midnight until he stopped counting.

He would try moving instead.

He got up before the others and dressed under his coat. Then he went down through the kitchen and out of the front door under the leaning O, toward the Cinder House, because Lira would be there, in the grey half, before anybody else was awake. And because there was a question he had never asked her, and he thought that he was going to ask it now.

# Chapter 36 — A Week Behind

Hesk read the letter for the first time standing up, in the workshop doorway, with the runner's footsteps still going away down Fen Street and the cold coming in round his legs.

He read it fast, the way you look at a gauge to see whether the needle is in the red before you look at what number it is pointing to. *I won tonight.* That was the needle. Everything after it was the number, and he could take his time over the number. He shut the door and went to the bench and read it again, with Ressa's oven hinge in the vice in front of him and a fine file in his hand, a stroke of the file for every line. If his hands were busy, his head did not run off in front of the words and decide what they meant before it had got to the end of them.

*A woman called Dessa, Copper Rank 5 in the book here, Stone Path, the best defender in the yard.*

He had never seen Dessa, or the yard. He knew the Cinder House only as two words in a boy's handwriting, and a book kept by a woman whose name he had written on the back of an envelope in case he forgot it. And yet he found that he could see all of it perfectly well: a defender who won by standing still, and a boy on a bench watching her twice, a week apart, with his chin tucked and his eyes very wide, writing down the thing she did not know she did.

*The way you taught me to watch a stance.*

Hesk put the file down for a moment, and then picked it up again, because the hinge did not care what he felt, and that was the great comfort of hinges.

It was on that second reading, slower, with the file going, that he noticed what he had been too glad to notice the first time. *In the third I went into it,* the boy had written, and then, with nothing between, *She said afterward that it was the cleanest read anyone had made on her.* The letter did not say whether the winning had happened in the third or somewhere after it, or how; and a boy who wrote down the angle of a gate guard's thumb on a card would not leave out the exchange in which he had won his first fight. Not unless he meant to.

Hesk had spent thirty years reading load sheets that other men had filled in. He knew what a column looked like when a figure had been left out of it. It did not look empty. It looked very slightly too neat.

He looked for the anger, because he thought he might be owed some, and found instead something older and more tired that had his own face. He had kept a great many things in a great many neat columns for a great many years: Tuesdays, the provisions, the preliminary assessment, and a file he had once seen opened in a room above his own clearance. He had kept them from the boy until the boy found them out for himself and came to him angry, across the workshop bench, on the second day after Kindling. *A few years,* he had said in the side room at Weaver's Row, and he remembered the boy's face. If there was a figure missing from this letter, it was missing for the boy's reason. He would tell it when it was worth telling, or he would not, and either way it was not Hesk's to dig out of a letter with a file.

He answered that night at the kitchen table. He wrote about the bench, because the boy had asked for ordinary things, and about Ressa's hinge, which had stopped squeaking at last, and about the guild giving his review a date to sit. He told the boy not to spend a single night on it, though he knew perfectly well that the boy would spend several. Then he sat for a long time with the pen lifted over the last part of the page, and wrote one line about building, which did not ask about anything or point at any column, and left room beside it for whatever else might be true as well.

He sealed it and propped it on the shelf by the door for the morning, against the guild's letter, which had stood against the clock there since the second morning after the depot with *Routine* written across its corner in somebody else's hand.

---

Joren came three afternoons after that, and he did not walk past.

He had walked past every day for two months, and Hesk had watched him do it from the bench, through the window over the vice. Joren would come along Fen Street from the direction of his family's yard, flat-footed, with his heels set down hard at every step as though he were trying to press them into the cobbles. He would look straight ahead until the last moment, and then turn his head to look in, and then look away again very quickly, as if he had been caught at something. Hesk had never once let him see that he had seen.

This afternoon he stopped in the doorway.

"Afternoon," said Hesk, not looking up from the bench.

"Afternoon." Joren stood there. He had grown since the autumn, or else he was standing differently; he was a big boy, broad like his father, who had always filled doorways, and now he seemed to be trying to fill this one carefully, without leaning on the frame. "Has he written?"

"Every week."

"Is he—" Joren stopped, and seemed to look for a better word, and not to find one. "Is he all right?"

Hesk put down the file and considered how to answer, not because he did not know, but because he wanted the answer to be the true one and the right size for a boy in a doorway, and those were not always the same.

"He's lodging at a house with a sign whose O leans. He pays on time. He works two afternoons a week for a mender who doesn't talk in the mornings. He's got a friend who's teaching him to use his feet." He picked the file up again. "And he won a fight, the week before last."

Joren's whole face changed.

"A *fight*?" It was nearly a shout. "Cael? In a ring? Against somebody?"

"Against a woman who was very good at standing still, I gather. He watched her for two weeks and then found the thing she did when she was tired."

Joren stared at him, and then began to laugh, helplessly, holding on to the doorframe after all.

"That's him. That's exactly him. Two weeks. He'd have had a notebook." He wiped his eyes. "He'd have had a *chart*."

"I expect he did."

Joren stood in the doorway grinning, and then the grin went slowly, as if he had remembered something, and he looked down at his own feet on the step.

There was a word between them. Hesk knew Joren knew it, because the boy had written it in a note and left it in the gap in the wall where they had always left things for each other. Joren knew that Hesk knew he knew it, and neither of them said it. It sat in the doorway between them like a third person who had not been introduced, and Hesk let it sit there, because it was not his to say to anybody, and the one person whose it was had chosen who to say it to.

He did not say *summons* either; the summons was not in any letter yet, and there was nothing yet to say.

"I'm practising," said Joren, to his feet. "Patience. Like you said. Every morning, in the yard behind ours. I stand." He looked up. "With the stance. It's the only thing it does, standing. I thought if that's the only thing it does, I'd better be the best at it there is." He made a face. "It's very boring."

"It's meant to be."

"My father says I'm wasting my mornings."

"Your father," said Hesk, "has never stood still in his life, and has never been able to finish anything that needed it." He got up from the bench. "Come here a minute."

He had a level on the bench for the survey office, the fourth, with its glass set in its frame and the cement not yet cured. It had to be held exactly upright, without the smallest tremor, for the best part of an hour while the cement went off. He had been going to rig a clamp for it. He gave it to Joren instead, and showed him how to hold it, and where to set his feet.

"Don't move it," he said. "Not a hair. If the bubble moves, the cement sets crooked and some young man drops off a roof because his level lied to him."

Joren took it as though it were made of eggshell.

Hesk felt the stance come into the boy before he saw it, a kind of settling in the floorboards, very faint, as if the workshop had become a little heavier on one side. Joren's heels went down, and his knees softened, and his whole body seemed to go quiet from the feet upward, and the little bubble in the glass, which had been trembling, stopped.

Joren held it for fifty minutes without a word. Hesk worked at the bench and did not watch him, and watched him the whole time.

At the end of it Joren set the level down on the bench as gently as a sleeping child, and stood rubbing his arms, which had gone dead, and looked as proud as Hesk had ever seen him look of anything.

"Tell him," he said at the door. "When you write. Tell him my heels are down."

"I'll tell him."

"And tell him—" Joren stopped again. "No. Just that." He went off up Fen Street toward his family's yard, flat-footed, his heels set hard into the cobbles at every step, and he did not look back, because he did not need to look in at anything now.

---

The other letter came two days later, a week to the day after the boy had posted it, and Hesk read the first line and sat down on the step of the workshop in the street.

*Your letter came on the fifty-first day, on the same cart as a man from the Compact.*

He read it all the way through sitting on the step, with people going by in the street and a cart going past close enough to touch, and noticed none of them.

A man from the Compact at Torvin's door, asking for the boy by his whole name. A summons for a compliance evaluation, forty-eight hours, *physical retrieval* if he did not go. The boy had gone down into a reading room under the man's own floor and read the law until he found the place where it did not fit him, and read it aloud four times, the way you read a load figure before you sign it. He had walked into the man's office with an objection on a single sheet, and come out with the summons stamped *closed, pending*, and six weeks, and a senior evaluator to come.

He had done all of that eight days ago, two days' road away. He had done it on his own, or nearly; the letter did not say, but there was the girl, Lira, who told him the truth about his feet, and Hesk found that he hoped very much she had been somewhere near. Hesk had known nothing about any of it; he had been filing a hinge for Ressa's oven door while it happened.

He read the line about the reading room again, and the one about the appendix, and the loose page tucked back in crooked. Then he sat on the step with the letter on his knee and found that he was more frightened than he had been at any moment since Weaver's Row, and that there was nothing at all to be frightened about, because it was over.

That was the post, and it was nobody's fault. A letter took a week, and a week was the price of the boy being somewhere he could not be found by the next man who came looking for him in Denvash. Hesk had agreed to the price and helped to set it, and he had not understood until this moment, sitting on his own step, what it would feel like to pay it. It meant being frightened a week late, all at once, for something that had already been finished by somebody braver than he was.

He went back into the workshop and sat down at the bench and opened the drawer of springs, because he had a job for the survey office that needed one, and because his hands wanted something to do.

He did not look at what he was doing. He put his hand into the drawer the way he had put it into that drawer ten thousand times, and the end of a broken coil spring that should not have been in there at all went straight into the heel of his palm.

He looked at it for a moment as though it were somebody else's hand, and then wrapped it in a clean rag from the bench and went out, and walked down to Merchant Row to the healers'.

---

The healers' house on Merchant Row had a blue door and a bench along the wall inside for people waiting, and a smell of hot water and something green. On the bench there were a carter with a crushed finger and a woman with a coughing child, and Hesk sat down at the end of it with his hand in the rag and waited his turn.

A girl came through from the back with two basins of steaming water, one on each hip, and set them down by the senior healer's table without spilling either. She had a plain grey apron on, too big for her, with the strings wound twice round her waist and her hair pinned up out of the way. When she straightened up and saw him, her face went quite still.

It was Alis Trent.

He had not spoken to her since the morning of the boy's Kindling, when they had passed her two blocks from home on the way back from Weaver's Row. She had been on the other side of the street with a basket, and she had lifted one open hand to the boy without crossing, and the boy had lifted his. He had heard from Cael's letters and from Ressa that she had gone to the healers', and had not thought about what that meant. It meant this: a fourteen-year-old girl carrying basins in an apron too big for her, with her sleeves pushed up past the elbow.

She came over, wiping her hands on the apron.

"Master Hesk." She looked at the rag. "What have you done?"

"Put my hand in a drawer without looking."

"That's what everybody says." She knelt on the floor in front of the bench, and unwound the rag, and looked at the heel of his palm with her head on one side. "It's not deep. It's dirty. She'll want it washed and bound and she'll want to look at it after." She glanced back at the senior healer, who had the carter's finger in both her hands and did not look up. "She'll let me wash it. I'm allowed to wash things."

"Only wash?"

"Wash, and carry, and boil, and fold, and watch." Alis fetched a small basin and a cloth and knelt down again. "Not anything else until the spring. Not anything with the Path. She says I don't know what I'm listening to yet, so I'm not to go poking about in anybody with it till I do." She dipped the cloth. "This'll sting."

It stung. She was careful and quick and not at all frightened of the blood, and she washed the cut out three times, and then held it up to the window and looked into it, and washed it a fourth time because she was not satisfied. She was a good deal more thorough than the carter's finger seemed to be getting.

"Is it true," she said, to his hand, "that he's in Ardenmere?"

"It's true."

"Ressa told my mother." She folded a pad of clean linen. "Is he all right?"

Hesk had been asked that twice now in a week, and had answered it two ways, and found he had a third ready.

"He's lodging at a house where they make him eat his oats. He works two afternoons a week for a mender. He has a friend who's fifteen and very quick and tells him the truth about his feet." He watched her bind the pad on. "And he's learning things. He writes me every week. He's well."

All of it was true, and none of it was the word. Alis had seen the boy's face on the way home that morning and knew it had gone badly, but not how badly, or what it had been called, and she had not asked, and he did not tell her. He did not tell her about the reading room either, or the forty-eight hours, or the summons stamped *closed, pending*, or anything that had been in the letter now folded in the inside pocket of his coat. It was not his to tell, and she was kneeling on the floor of a healers' house in an apron too big for her, learning a trade, and she did not need to carry anything of his down Merchant Row tonight.

"Good," said Alis. She tied off the binding, and it was neat, neater than he could have done it with two good hands. "Tell him I carry basins." She almost smiled. "He'll laugh. He always said I'd be the one who ran a whole ward."

"He'll laugh," Hesk agreed.

She got up off the floor and took the basin away, and the senior healer came over afterward, as Alis had said she would, and looked at the binding and turned his hand in hers and said, "Who did this?" When Hesk said that the girl had, she grunted, and did not undo it, and went back to the carter.

He walked home up Merchant Row in the dusk with his bound hand in his coat pocket and the boy's letter against his chest, less frightened than he had been on the step, though nothing had changed. A girl had knelt on a floor and washed a cut four times when three was not enough. Watching her be careful had steadied him, as the boy had once written that the sacks off Ressa's wagon steadied him at a kitchen table with a notice on it.

---

The review sat on the Friday of the week after, in the small room on the upper floor of the guild hall by the canal, with the shutters half closed against the light.

There were three of them at the table: the reviewing officer from the guild's own office, a thin careful man with a portfolio, whom Hesk had met once when he came to look at the workshop and ask about the drawer of springs. The other two were members chosen by lot, as the guild's rules required. One was a lens-grinder from the Inner District whom Hesk knew only by sight. The other was a woman he had sat near at thirty years of guild suppers without ever once learning her name, which he realized only as he sat down. She was a gauge-maker with a shop on the canal, a small fierce woman with a magnifier on a ribbon round her neck. She looked at his bound hand and raised her eyebrows and said nothing.

The reviewing officer read out the notice in full, in the voice in which such things are read: Statute 14, Section 9; the residence; the standing; the paragraph that said what keeping the person close would cost. He read Hesk's own account after it, the one Hesk had written and sent and been asked for a copy of. *The person named in the registry notice of the second day no longer resides at this address. He left on the morning of the third day for another city, where he is lawfully resident, and he goes with my full support.*

"Is that still the case?"

"It is."

"He has not returned to Denvash?"

"He has not."

"Do you maintain contact with him?"

Hesk had known this question would come, and had thought for a long time on several nights about how to answer it, and had decided in the end that there was only one answer he could give and still be able to look at his own hands afterward.

"I write to him every week. He writes to me. I intend to go on doing both."

The lens-grinder made a small note. The reviewing officer looked at him for a moment over the portfolio.

"The statute does not prohibit correspondence," he said at last, as though he were reading it off a page in his head. "It concerns residence and association. Correspondence is—" He seemed to choose a word. "Not specified."

"Then I'll go on with it," said Hesk, "until somebody specifies it."

The gauge-maker with the magnifier made a noise in her throat that might have been a cough.

They asked him about the workshop, and about his work for the survey office and the canal company, and whether any of it was work in which the guild's good name could be said to rest on his. He told them it was levels and gauges, and that the young men at the survey office dropped them off ladders, and that the canal had not flooded anybody in thirty years on his figures. The lens-grinder nearly smiled. They asked whether he had made any application on the boy's behalf, to any office, for any exemption. He said he had not; there was none to make. They asked him whether he had anything to add.

He thought of a great deal he could add: a reading room under a man's floor, and a loose page tucked back in crooked, and four readings aloud. He thought of a girl he had never met who told the boy the truth about his feet, and a level held for fifty minutes by a boy whose father said he was wasting his mornings.

"No," he said.

They sent him out to wait on the landing, on a hard chair under a portrait of some guild master from before anybody now living had been born. He waited for most of an hour and did not look at the portrait, but at his bound hand, and thought about the cut being washed four times.

When they called him back in, the reviewing officer did not sit down.

"The guild has considered the notice and the member's account," he said. "It finds no present cause to act on the member's standing." He closed the portfolio. "The matter will remain on the book."

"Remain," said Hesk.

"On the book. Open." The reviewing officer looked at him with something that was very nearly apology and was not allowed to be. "If the Compact writes to us again, about this, we will be obliged to look again. If it does not, the book will stay as it is."

So it was not closed, and it was not lost either. It was a door left on the latch, which was, Hesk thought, walking down the stairs with his good coat buttoned, very much the shape of everything else this autumn.

The gauge-maker caught him up at the bottom of the stairs. She walked beside him without saying anything, out into the street along the canal, with her magnifier swinging on its ribbon, until they came to the corner where she would go one way and he the other.

"The drawer of springs," she said. "He asked me about it, that man, when he came to my shop. Whether it was usual." She looked at his hand. "I told him every workshop in Denvash has a drawer of springs, and that a man who doesn't is either a liar or a bad maker." She snorted. "He wrote it down."

Then she went off along the canal toward her shop, and Hesk stood on the corner for some time, a little warmed, and still did not know her name.

---

He answered the boy's letter that night at the kitchen table, with the lamp close and his bound hand stiff on the paper. It took him three sheets, not because he did not know what he wanted to say but because of choosing what to leave out.

He put in the review, plainly: that it had sat, that they had asked whether he wrote to the boy and he had told them he did, every week, and meant to go on, and what *remain on the book* meant. He put in the gauge-maker and the drawer of springs, because the boy would like it. He put in that he had checked the citation as well as he could from Denvash with no handbook, and that he was proud of the reading and of the four times aloud, and he left that short so that it would stay true.

Then he came to the fear, and sat a long time with the pen lifted. He could leave it out. It would do no good, and it could not reach back and change one thing that had happened at that desk upstairs at the post. But they had made a bargain, the two of them, one letter apart: *I'd rather have the telling,* he had written once, and the boy had written back, *as it comes.* So he wrote down the step in the street, and the week-late fear, in six sentences, and did not soften any of them.

He did not put in the hand, or the spring, which mattered to nobody but him and the survey office. At the bottom, because he had promised, he put in Joren's heels and Alis's basins.

He read it over once by the lamp, and found that it was all true, and that it was not all of what was true. He was not sure he had ever written a letter in his life of which both of those things could not be said.

---

"Bronze," said Lira.

She said it as if she were reading the word off the side of a crate, to see whether it was the crate she had ordered.

It was Tuesday morning in Ardenmere, a week and a valley east of Fen Street. Vell had her table out for the afternoon card, and they were standing in front of it because she had sent the pie boy to the low wall to fetch them. Cael was standing more or less straight, which had taken him two days to manage. The back was a band of colour from one shoulder blade to the other, black at the middle and green at the edges, and it pulled whenever he lifted his arms. The thigh had a print on it the width of a blade's flat, a long dark bar he could have laid a ruler along. His left forearm, over the old burn, had swollen up like a loaf and was going down slowly in cold water from the pump.

Lira had not let him do anything on Monday but sit on the low wall in the grey half and watch her drill, and on Tuesday she had let him walk the box at the slowest speed she had. *Efficient,* she had said, when he argued. *I said I'd tell you. I'm telling you.*

"Bronze Rank 1," said Vell. "Blade Path. Her name's Sarel. She fights here a few times a season, when she's got nothing better on, and she's on Sunday because she asked for something on Sunday." She looked at Cael. "You'll have her if you want her. If you don't, I'll give her somebody else, and nobody will think the less of you. Most people in this yard wouldn't take her if I paid them."

"Why me?"

"Not to win." Vell set the cup down. "You've won three of seven in this book now, and two of them inside a week. Every one of them, you watched first, and found the thing, and the thing was there. I'd like to know what you do when it isn't. When there's nothing to find, or you can't find it fast enough to use." She looked at Lira. "She's quick. She's quicker than anything you've stood across from. And she's fair. She won't hurt you for the pleasure of it, and she won't make it easy for the pleasure of it either."

Lira was looking at Vell with her arms folded. "Survivable?"

It was not quite a question to Vell. It was the question Lira had promised to ask, on the first morning of the six weeks, by the post.

Vell considered her for a long moment, and seemed to understand exactly what it was.

"Survivable. If he's sensible. And if you've taught him to fall."

Lira looked at Cael, and he looked back and saw her weigh it, the way she weighed a bout between exchanges, quickly and completely, and not like the answer she came to.

"He'll be sensible," she said. "I'll see to the falling."

Vell picked up her pen.

"She's on this afternoon, as it happens. Third bout." She was writing. "And Thursday. Watch her both times." She did not look up. "I'd watch her with both eyes. I don't think it'll help you much. But I'd like to see what you write."

---

He watched Sarel fight that afternoon at the rope on the shaded side, near the barrel, with the Log open on his knee and the pencil ready, and he did not write much.

She was about thirty, long-legged and lean, with dark hair cut short as a boy's and a long scar down the outside of one forearm, the left, old and white. She worked, somebody at the rope said, on the scaffolds the builders put up round the new houses on the east road. She had the scaffold walker's way of standing, with her weight easy and her arms loose, like somebody who has long since stopped thinking about how high she is. She came over the rope without any hurry at all, and her opponent was a Copper Rank 5, a Force man with a reputation, and he was not happy to be there.

"Begin when you're ready," said Vell.

Sarel's blade came up along her right forearm, pale and straight, no longer than Renn's as far as Cael's eye could tell, and no brighter. Then she moved, and he understood why Vell had said what she had said.

She did not amble, or probe, or throw a figure from her mark; she simply arrived. She was on her mark, and then she was in front of the Force man, inside his reach, and the flat of her blade was already coming down on his shoulder. Cael was sure he had seen her start. He had not seen her cross.

It was not like Lira's step, which folded the ground. This was only a body that was very fast, faster than any body he had seen, so fast that the watching could not keep up with it. By the time his eyes had told him *she's moving*, she had moved, and struck, and was already going somewhere else.

He wrote *closes very fast* and looked up, and she had done two more things while he wrote it.

He stopped writing and watched her hips and her feet and her shoulders, all the places Hesk had taught him, and every place he looked she had already left. He tried to find the thing she did before she went. There had to be a thing; there was always a thing. The heel. The dip. The shimmer. The breath. The lean.

He could not find it, not because it was not there, but because by the time he had looked for it in one place, she had done whatever she did, and he was looking for the next one.

The Force man went down in the third exchange, on his back, with the flat of her blade resting on his collarbone.

"Called," said Vell. "Blade Path."

Cael looked down at the Log. It said *closes very fast*, and under it *left foot?*, crossed out, and *shoulders?*, crossed out, and *something before she goes—*, unfinished.

Lira had come up beside him at some point and was standing very close, with her shoulder against his good one.

"Well?"

"I couldn't write fast enough."

"No." She did not say anything else for a moment. "Nobody could. I couldn't, and I've spent half my life looking at fast people." She looked at the page. "That's the bout, Cael. That's what she's for. Not the page you couldn't fill." She took a breath. "You'll watch her on Thursday. You'll get one more line. Maybe two. And then on Sunday you'll go in with two lines against a woman who doesn't care about lines."

"And then?"

"And then you'll find out what you do when you've got nothing to look up." She bumped his shoulder very gently. "And I'll teach you to fall properly before then. Because you're going to."

On Thursday he watched Sarel again, against a Copper 4 Stone, and got one more line. *She doesn't look at you. She looks at the space next to you, where you're going to be.* He looked at the line for a long time on the low wall by the pump afterward, with the cold coming down, knowing it was true and not knowing what to do with it. Then he turned the page and wrote at the top of a clean one, *Sarel. Bronze Rank 1, Blade.* Under it there was nothing at all, and he left it like that, because nothing at all was the honest entry.

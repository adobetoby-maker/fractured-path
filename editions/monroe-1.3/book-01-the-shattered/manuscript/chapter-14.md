# Chapter 14 — Claim, Evidence, Ruling

He woke because one of the brothers dropped a boot, and for a moment, before he moved, nothing hurt at all.

Then he moved. The right side of his chest reported first, the hot band from under his arm to the bottom of his ribs, and the left shoulder joined in a breath later, stiff and thick, as if somebody had packed the joint with wet sand while he slept. Last of all the scrape down his left forearm stung where the blanket dragged across it, the scrape he still could not account for.

He lay still and let all three finish what they had to say, while the window over his cot went from black to grey and the brother whispered an apology to the boot and both of them went creaking off down the stairs toward the river.

Last night he had stood under Torvin's pump in the dark with his shirt off and counted, because Lira had told him to count, and he had stopped at sixty because sixty was what his ribs would stand. He could not tell whether it had helped. He could breathe, though every breath went carefully round the edge of something on its way in and out, and that would have to do.

He sat up in three stages, like a folding chair.

Yeni was awake on the next cot, lying on her side under her blanket with one eye open, watching him do it.

"Renn," she said.

"Renn."

"How many?"

"Four exchanges."

The eye closed again. "Torvin's wife had you at two," she said into her pillow. "Now she owes the one in the boots a bun."

He had not known that anybody in this house had had him at anything. He sat on the edge of the cot with his shirt in his hands and thought about a bet made over a kitchen table on a boy nobody there had seen fight, and he could not decide whether it made him feel better or worse. Both, probably. He was getting used to things being both.

Putting the shirt on took longer than it should have. He did the right arm first, keeping the elbow tight against his side, and then worked the left shoulder into its sleeve a little at a time, like threading a stiff wire through a hole that was slightly too small. When he had finished, Yeni's eye was open again.

"Left's the shoulder. Right's the ribs."

"Yes."

"Don't sleep on either." She rolled over to face the wall. "And don't let him tell you to rest. He'll tell you to rest. Rest is for people with savings."

She did not say whether she meant Torvin or somebody else.

In the kitchen the range was lit and the oats were on, and Torvin's wife stood over the pot with her wooden spoon. The woman who slept in her boots was at the end of the table eating a sweet bun with great slowness and pleasure, and she raised it to Cael when he came in, as somebody might raise a cup.

"Four," she said. "I said three or better. I'm a generous judge of people."

"You said three because you'd heard about the Wind girl," said Torvin's wife, without turning round. "Sit down before you fall down, boy. There's a bowl."

He sat. The bowl came, and with it a spoon and a hard look at the way he lowered himself onto the bench, and then Torvin's wife went back to her pot and said nothing more about it.

He ate slowly. Turning his head to the left was fine, and turning his body was not, and by the bottom of the bowl he had worked out exactly which movements cost what and put the list away in his head beside the other lists.

Torvin came in from the yard with a bucket and stopped in the doorway.

"Pump handle was wet at midnight."

"I used it."

"Lira tell you to?"

"Yes."

"She's right." Torvin set the bucket down by the range and looked at Cael for a moment the way he looked at coins, slowly, checking his own count. "I said fight in the street."

"It wasn't in your house."

"No." Torvin seemed to consider this from several sides and to find nothing wrong with any of them. "Four, they're saying."

"Four."

Torvin grunted. It was not quite approval, and it was not nothing. Then he picked up the oats pot with a cloth round the handle and set it on the table at Cael's elbow, so that there would be a second bowl if he wanted one, and went out again without another word.

Cael had a second bowl. It seemed rude not to.

The Cinder House yard was half in shadow when he got there. The sun was up somewhere behind the east wall, and the wall threw a long, clean shadow across the packed dirt, so that the yard was split down the middle into a grey half and a bright half with the circle's groove running through both.

Mist lay in the low corner by the drain, and the crows were working last night's leavings along the rope line, where the pie boy had dropped crusts and somebody had dropped a whole half pie and never come back for it.

Lira was at the post in the far corner, in the grey half. She did not stop when she saw him. She watched him walk across the yard to her between one strike and the next, and partway across he understood that she was not watching his face.

"Right side," she said, when he reached her. "And the left shoulder. You walk like a man carrying a tray of glasses."

"You were at the rope. You saw where he hit me."

"I'm not telling you what I saw. I'm telling you what I see." She struck the post, low, and let her hand rest against the canvas. "You're not sparring today."

"I didn't come to spar."

"Then what did you come for?"

"You said you'd see me at the yard."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she turned back to the post so that he could not see her face, which told him more than her face would have, and struck it twice more, a little harder than before.

"All right. Then you can stand there and learn something with one arm. Right arm. Hold it up. No, like this." She took his wrist and set his forearm upright in front of him, elbow down and palm toward his own face, as if she were propping up a fence post. "This part. The outside, from the wrist to the elbow. That's what you catch with."

She showed him the deflection, the one she had shown him at the board on the second day, the one every Wind student learned in their first month: catch, reset, send. She went through it so slowly that it stopped looking like fighting and began to look like a craftsman showing an apprentice how to hold a plane.

Her strike came in toward his face at the speed of somebody reaching for a cup, and his forearm met it on the outside and caught it. That was the first part.

"Now stop. Feel where you are."

He felt where he was. The strike had pushed his arm a little across his own body, and his weight had gone with it, onto the wrong foot.

"That's why there's a middle," said Lira. "Catching it moves you. Reset puts you back, weight in the centre, arm back on its line, and then you're ready to do something with what you caught." She took her hand away and brought it in again, just as slowly. "Catch. Reset. Send."

On *send* she turned his forearm for him, rolling it outward against her wrist, and her strike went away past his shoulder into the empty air as if it had changed its mind about where it wanted to go.

They did it twenty times before the sun reached the post, and then forty. The reset was where his ribs complained, because the reset meant a small turn of the body back to centre and the turn went right through the bruise.

The third time he flinched in it she saw, and after that she brought her strikes in from a slightly different angle so that the reset needed less turn, and she did not say anything about why.

"Why not teach me the other way?" he said, around the fiftieth. "Your way. Without the middle."

"Because you can't skip a step you don't own." Catch. Reset. Send. "When I skipped it, I'd done all three parts ten thousand times. I knew exactly what I was leaving out, and what it cost, and why I could afford it. You'd only be leaving out something you never had." She lowered her hand. "That's not a skip. That's a hole."

He thought about that through the next ten repetitions and found nothing in it to argue with, which was annoying.

He had brought bread. On the way he had stopped at the top of the second row of the market and asked the bread woman for two from underneath, watching her face as the nut boy had told him to. She had given him two from underneath with a small sour look that he took as respect.

He held one out to Lira now. She tore it in half, put one half on top of the post and ate the other in four bites, and then set her feet.

"Breakfast's between. Not before. Before, you're slow. After, you're late." She brought her hand up. "Catch."

So he ate his roll in the gaps, in pieces, between sets of ten. When the sun finally came over the east wall and the shadow began to shrink back toward it, Lira moved their work along with the shadow, a step at a time, keeping them both in the grey half until there was no grey half left.

He noticed it, and he noticed that she did it without looking, as people do things they have done so often that they no longer need to decide them.

He did not ask about it. There was probably a reason, he thought, and if she wanted him to have it she would give it to him, and until then it was hers.

She did not mention the third exchange, not once all morning. He kept waiting for it between the catches and the resets, and it did not come. Somewhere around the hundredth repetition he understood that she was not going to ask today, and that the not asking was something she was doing on purpose, as deliberately as she kept them in the shade.

The third notebook from Denvash had nine pages left in it.

He counted them that afternoon, sitting on the low wall in Torvin's yard beside the pump, under the stunted pear tree in the corner that had given up growing tall and grown wide instead, and then he counted them again, because nine was not many.

The front of the book was full of Ardenmere: the nut boy's map, the board decoded, the rules, six pages on two strangers' bouts and Marrow's numbers. The back held the things he had written from the other end, as he always did, in the smaller hand.

There was the archive entry and the gate entry, and on its own page, near the end, the dip between the hedges and the turnip and *I don't think I did.*

He closed it on that page without reading it again.

The paper stall was at the bottom of the market, near the fruit woman with no name. It was run by a man with ink on his cuffs who sold everything from single sheets to bound ledgers with marbled edges that cost more than a week at Torvin's.

Cael looked at the ledgers. Then he bought the cheapest thing on the table that still had a proper sewn spine: a thick, plain notebook with a grey board cover and paper so thin that the ink of one page would show faintly through the next.

It was a good deal more than he wanted to pay. He paid it, and wrote the figure in the back of the old book under the line he had drawn that morning, and took the new one back to the wall by the pump, where he sat with the pencil over the first page.

He had kept notebooks since he was eight, and every one of them had been the same kind of book. Things went into it as they came, in order, a bearing, a dog, a wall, a word on page seven, and that had always been enough, because the things he wrote down were mostly things that would still be there tomorrow.

This was different, and he could feel the difference in his hands. The bout with Renn was already less clear than it had been last night. Had the second probe, the one across the body, really come off the front foot, or had he only expected it to?

He had been sure on the walk home and was nearly sure now, and by next week he would be sure again in the wrong way, as you become sure of a thing you have told yourself often enough.

He wrote *Log* at the top of the first page and underlined it.

Then he wrote the first entry the way he had always written things, in sentences, and it came out like this:

*Renn. Copper Rank 3, Blade. He opens with two probes, high and across, off the front foot. The true strike loads in his back heel about a quarter of a second first. I read the heel in the second exchange and he answered with the sweep. In the third I got out of the whole figure—*

He stopped.

He read the line back. *In the third I got out of the whole figure.* It was true. He had been standing outside the figure at the end of it, and Vell had seen it, and Renn had seen it, and Lira had seen it from the rope. Yet the sentence sat on the page next to the heel and the probes as though it belonged with them, in the same hand and the same voice.

Anybody reading it a year from now would think he had done the third exchange the way he had done the second, by reading and moving. He had not. He did not know how he had done it, and the sentence did not know either. It simply did not say so.

That was what sentences did, he thought. They ran on. You put a true thing in at one end, and a guess came out of the other in the same coat, and nobody could tell them apart.

He tore the page out, carefully, along the spine, so that the binding would not loosen, and folded it and put it in his pocket. Then he took the straightedge from his tool roll, the little steel one he had made at twelve, and ruled the next page into three columns.

He thought about what to call them. Hesk's slates in the workshop had columns for *load*, *figure* and *result*, which were close but not right, because nobody here was hanging weights on a rig.

What he wanted was something that made each line prove itself before he let it stay. He thought about Vell at her table, and the way the yard went quiet when she spoke, and the one word she said at the end of a bout that made it finished.

He headed the columns *Claim*, *Evidence* and *Ruling*.

A claim was what he thought was true. Evidence was what he had seen with his own eyes that made him think so, and when, and how many times. A ruling was what he would do about it next time, and it was only allowed to exist if the evidence was there.

If somebody had told him a thing and he had not seen it himself, it could still go in. But it went in with a small *h* beside it, for *heard*, so that he would always know it was a figure somebody else had traced and he had not.

*Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.*

He began again.

*1. Renn. Copper R3, Blade Path (Vell's rating). Eleven bouts, eight wins (Vell, h).*

*Claim: the probes carry no weight. Evidence: off the front foot, high then across, every exchange but the last. Ruling: spend nothing on them.*

*Claim: the true strike loads in the back heel a quarter second before the blade. Evidence: first exchange, paid for with the shoulder; second exchange, moved on it and was right. Ruling: move on the heel, not on the blade.*

*Claim: he rolls his shoulders when he changes his plan. Evidence: before the first exchange, after the sweep, before the fourth. Ruling: shoulders mean a new plan. Stop expecting the old one.*

*Claim: the sweep is kept for people who have learned the heel. Evidence: second exchange, once. Ruling: one is not enough. Watch for it again.*

*Claim: the blade reaches eight inches past the fist. Evidence: Lira, h. My eye says less. Ruling: add eight until I have measured it myself.*

It took him most of the afternoon. When he had finished he had a page and a half on a man he had fought for perhaps three minutes in all, and every line on it would hold weight, because he had tried each one and could tell exactly where it came from.

There was nothing on it about the third exchange.

He had not left it out by deciding to. It simply had nowhere to go. He had a claim, *I got out of the figure*, but no evidence, because he had not seen himself do it, and no ruling, because he did not know what he would do next time, and so the columns would not take it.

He looked at the empty place where it would have been and felt something that was partly relief and partly shame, and he could not have said which was larger.

He turned the notebook over and opened it from the back, as he always did, to the last page. This would be the other kind of page; things would go here that did not fit the columns.

Sitting on the wall with the pump dripping beside him, he made himself a rule about it: nothing went in the back until the front was finished for the day. The facts first, then the rest. If he could make himself keep to that order, he thought, the rest might stay a sensible size.

He wrote only one line, at the top of the last page.

*There are things that won't go in the columns. I know where they are. I am not counting them yet.*

Then he closed the Log and sat with it on his knee, listening to the pump drip, and did not open the old notebook to the page near the back, though he knew exactly which page it was.

The pouch said he had until the eleventh day.

He did the sum on the morning of the tenth at the kitchen counter, in small stacks of coins, twice. Torvin's week ran out tomorrow. After that the pouch would pay for three more weeks if he ate the way he had been eating, and four if he ate the way the brothers ate, which was mostly bread and whatever the river gave them, but it would not pay for five.

*Find work,* he had written on the rise. He had written it second, after *find somewhere to sleep*, and he had done the first and not the second, and now the second had come round to find him.

He went down to the river workshops in the late morning with his left arm held close, past the cobbler and the lantern woman and the cooper, to the doorway at the end of the row with no sign over it. The mender was not straightening a mangle today.

He had the clock with no hands open on his bench, its works laid out on a cloth in neat rows like a surgeon's instruments, and he was looking at them through a loupe and did not look up.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE*, said the doorframe.

"Something of yours broken?" said the mender, to the clock.

"Not today. I wanted to ask if you need anybody."

That made the man look up. He took the loupe out of his eye and gave Cael the same checking look he had given him over the mangle frame, and his gaze went to the arm Cael was holding close and came back.

"For what?"

"Sorting. Cleaning. Truing small things." Cael could not find a way to say the next part that would be true and not sound like boasting, so he said it anyway. "My grandfather makes instruments. I've worked at his bench since I was six."

The mender said nothing to that. He reached under the bench, felt about without looking, and came up with a little brass balance of the kind a spice seller uses, with a beam no longer than a hand. The beam was bent, though not much.

It dipped a hair to the left at rest, so that anything weighed on it would weigh light in one pan and heavy in the other, and you would have to hold it up to the light and squint along it to see why. He put it on the bench, laid his straightedge beside it, and went back to his clock.

Cael did not touch the balance at first. He looked at it.

Then he did what he had watched the mender do with the mangle, and what Hesk had done at the rig for as long as he could remember: he did not guess. He laid the straightedge along the beam and found the bend, which was not in the middle, as he had expected, but a little way out along the left arm, where somebody had probably caught it on the edge of a stall.

He clamped the beam lightly in the bench vice with a fold of leather between the jaws and pressed, not much, and then took it out and laid the straightedge along it again.

It was better, and not right. He pressed again, less, and looked, and found he had gone over very slightly, so he turned it and pressed back the other way with the pad of his thumb and no vice at all.

The fourth time he laid the straightedge along it, he could see no light underneath. He hung the beam on its pivot and let it settle, and the pointer swung, slowed, and came to rest dead on the mark.

"There," he said.

The mender put down his loupe and came round the bench to look. Without a word he took two identical brass weights out of a drawer and set one in each pan, and the beam dipped and rose and came back to the mark and stayed there. He took the weights off and looked at Cael's left arm again.

"Two afternoons. Not mornings; I don't talk in the mornings. By the piece, not by the hour, because I don't pay for people standing about thinking." He put the weights back in their drawer. "You'll sort what comes in, and sweep, and true the small things. You won't touch the clocks."

"I won't touch the clocks."

"And if you break something you'll pay for it out of what I owe you, and then you'll go." He went back round the bench. "Tomorrow. After noon."

That was all. Cael stood in the doorway a moment longer before he understood that he was being allowed to leave, and left.

On the fish steps below the carriers' hut a barge was unloading sacks of meal onto the landing, and three men were carrying them up to a cart one at a time on their shoulders.

The second man up was Renn. He was in a sleeveless shirt with a sack on his right shoulder, taking the slick steps two at a time as though they were level ground. At the top he swung the sack onto the cart without breaking stride and turned to go down for the next one. Then he saw Cael on the street above and stopped on the top step.

For a moment neither of them said anything. Renn looked at him as he had looked across the circle in the third exchange, briefly and carefully, a man checking a part he had not cut.

"Healing?"

"Slowly."

"Good." He went down a step, then stopped and looked back over his shoulder. "A month," he said, and went on down to the barge.

He went to Vell's table on the fourth morning after the bout, when he could lift his left arm as high as his shoulder without his face doing anything about it.

The yard was empty except for Vell and the crows. She sat at the table in the shade with her ledger squared and her tea at her elbow, exactly as she had on the morning of the terms, and when he stopped in front of her she looked at him without speaking.

"Lift your left arm."

He lifted it to the height of his shoulder.

"Higher."

He lifted it higher and kept his face still. He was nearly sure he kept his face still.

Vell watched the arm, then the face, then the arm again. "Thursday," she said, and picked up her pen. "Not before. Renn's return stays where I put it, at the month. Between now and then you'll fight who I give you."

"Who?"

"Brenna." She wrote a short line. "Copper Rank 2, Shield Path. She lost to Dessa a fortnight ago in three exchanges and she hasn't stopped talking about it. She wants somebody she can finish quickly, so that people will talk about that instead." The pen stopped and Vell looked up. "If you're any use, you'll be somebody she can't finish quickly. That'll be good for both of you, and she won't thank me for it."

He nodded. He was watching the pen.

It had written a line beneath the line from four nights ago. He could see the shape of the old entry, the long one that had run well past two lines and on down the page, though he could not read any of it upside down.

But he could see where a name would go at the start of each entry, and he could see that at the start of his there was no name, only something shorter.

"You haven't written my name."

Vell did not look down at the page. She seemed to have known exactly where he was looking.

"No."

"Is that because I'm unrated?"

"It's because the book doesn't need it yet." She laid the pen across the ledger. "You're in the book as what you are. Unrated. Fourteen. Vouched. Lost to Renn in four exchanges, by the flat, called. That's everything anybody coming to this book will need to know about that night. A name goes in when the yard comes to see it."

"When will that be?"

"For most of them, never. Most of them come and fight and go home, and the book holds what they did, and nobody ever needs to know what they were called. It's no slight. It's what a book's for." She picked up her tea. "When the yard comes to see you, I'll write it. Not before. And not because you asked."

Standing there, he did not mind, though he had thought he would. He was in a book, in somebody else's careful hand, as exactly what he was and what he had done, and every word of it was something Vell had seen herself from this stool. It was a ledger he could check, and it had no word in square brackets in it.

"Thursday," he said.

"After the lamps. Third bout. Don't be late, and don't be early either. Being early makes people think you're frightened."

He went out through the gate in the wall. On his way back down he found the old result still pinned near the bottom of the board at the crossroads, gone pale in the rain: *Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), third exchange, called.* He read it twice, and remembered two men arguing behind him in the triangle on his first afternoon, and one of them saying that Brenna could not stand that it went to three.

He took out the Log, turned to a clean page and wrote *2. Brenna*. Under it, in the first column, he put *wants it over quickly*, and in the second, *two men at the board, h; Vell, h*, and in the third column nothing at all.

The ruling would have to wait until Thursday. He was learning that most of them would.

# Chapter 60 — The Message

Lira came in through the kitchen at dusk with flour on her sleeve, because Yeni had found her at the widow's back door helping carry sacks, and had said four words to her, and Lira had put the sack down where she stood.

She came across Torvin's yard and sat down on the low wall beside him, on the left, without a word, and tore the end off a loaf she had in her bag and held it out, and he took it. They sat under the pear tree with the light going and ate the bread, and she waited.

"There was somebody in the market," he said at last.

He told it from the beginning, as she liked it, without saying what it meant: the apricots, the feeling from the mark, the knife-grinder's bench. A person standing still where everybody moves, with no case, no pin, no book. The look, and how long it lasted, and how it seemed to have been timed, and how they had turned and the market had shut behind them.

Lira did not interrupt, and when he had finished she sat with her chin on her fist and looked at the trough.

"Not Compact. You're sure."

"As sure as I am of anything I've only seen once." He turned the bread over. "Coss carries his work about with him. So did Ilsev. You can see it on them, a sort of hurry with edges, and somebody behind them waiting for a report. Even the man with the pin in the autumn had it, and all he did was walk through a market." He shook his head. "This one had nothing behind them at all. They weren't going to tell anybody what they'd seen. They'd come to see it for themselves."

"That's not better."

"No."

"Is it worse?"

He thought about it honestly.

"I don't know. It's other. It's not the thing I've been afraid of since the autumn. It's a different thing, standing next to it." He looked at the bread in his hand. "And it came after. Two days after. Not in the autumn, when nobody knew my face. Not on Sunday, in the crowd, where it would have been easiest to stand and look. After. When there was a line in Vell's book and my name on the board in Red Cap's writing."

Lira was quiet.

"I made myself loud for one kind of looking. I thought about who else might hear; I said it to you on the steps. But I thought about it the way you think about rain on a day you've already decided to go out." He set the bread down on the wall. "Whoever that was, Coss's drawer may be nothing to them. I could have lit a lamp on Sunday for somebody who was never looking in drawers."

"Or," said Lira, "they'd found you already, and Sunday only made them decide to let you see."

He looked at her.

"You said it felt like the first look you'd been allowed. Not the first look." She shrugged. "I don't know either. I'm only adding. It comes out both ways."

They sat on while the brothers argued on the stairs inside about a shirt, and the wheezing man's window stood open, and somewhere toward the river a dog was asking the lane its one question.

"Can I say a thing, and you not argue with it till I've finished?"

"Yes."

"You saw this at the third hour after noon." She did not look at him. "Yeni says you came in at the fourth and sat on this wall, and didn't move. She came and got me at the end of the sixth. Not you. Yeni." She let that sit. "Two hours, Cael. On a wall. Turning it over on your own till you'd made your mind up what it was, so that you'd have something tidy to bring me."

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"I'm not angry. That's the other thing, the one with Vell, and that's paid, and this isn't that. This is smaller. But it's the same shape." She turned her head at last. "You don't have to have it worked out before you come and get me. You don't have to have anything worked out. You keep forgetting there's two of us. You can bring me a thing in pieces and we can sit on a wall and look at the pieces together. That's what I'm for. It's half of what I'm for."

"What's the other half?"

"Telling you you're doing it again." She almost smiled. "You're carrying all of it by yourself because you think that's what being careful means. It isn't. Being careful is not doing it alone."

He sat with that, and tested it against two hours on a wall, and against the fourth stair, and against five letters to Hesk with five things left out of them, and it held each time.

"I'll try."

"I know you will. And I'll check." She bumped his knee with hers. "You know I check."

"I know you check."

"Good." She stood up, and dusted the flour off her sleeve, which only spread it. "Eat that. You'll not think any better about strangers on an empty stomach, and you'll not think at all with ribs like that if you don't sleep." She held her hand down to him, on the good side, and he came up off the wall the old man's way, one piece on the next, and she watched him do it and said nothing about it at all.

At the kitchen door she stopped.

"Whoever it is, they looked at both of us on Sunday. I was at the barrel. They'll have seen me too." She considered it. "So it's not yours to carry. Not even half."

Coss opened the district return on the Wednesday afternoon, three days after, at his desk at regional, with the window behind him going grey.

He had not meant to read it. Nobody read the district returns, which came in every week in a canvas sack and stood a month in a rack by the door before they went down to the cellars, counted and never opened. But the Ardenmere return had been put on his desk on Monday of last week with a line under it in red, and since then he had gone to the rack himself for the next one before the clerk could tie it up.

It was in the clerk's careful hand, as always: boundaries, permits, a dispute about a gutter referred back to the parties, and under *Public notices posted in the district*, the card from the Cinder House board, copied word for word as the form required, and then, because the clerk was a careful man and the form said *all matter posted*, the chalk that somebody had written underneath it:

*HESK-WARD. FOURTH. VELL'S CALL.*

Coss read it twice, and then put his thumb to the bridge of his nose and sat with his eyes shut.

He had come back on the noon cart from Ardenmere a week ago with his days all spent and a creased coat and a cold. He had told himself all the way back, through two nights of carters' benches, that he had done what he could, and that it had been nothing, and that the boy would do what the boy had decided. At his desk on the Friday he had not been able to keep from thinking about the date on the card, and at home on the Sunday he had not been able to eat his dinner until the evening had gone far enough that it must be over, so that his daughter had asked him whether he was ill.

It was over, and he had not known how until now: a chalk line in a clerk's copy of a district board, three days late, sent in a canvas sack.

He opened his eyes and drew the file across the desk to make the entry, as he had made ten thousand entries, with the line already in his head: *Subject remains in the district. No compliance action taken. Legal review pending. Public notice (district return, this week) of a circuit result; no compliance bearing.* He uncapped his pen.

He opened the cover, and stopped.

There was a slip pinned inside it.

It was on the heavy grey paper they used on the floors above his, with a printed head along the top, a narrow band of small type and a row of numbers, and he knew the head. He had seen it twice before in eleven years, across the room, on other agents' desks, and both times the agent had closed the file very quickly and looked at nobody for the rest of the afternoon. He had never once seen it on a file of his own.

Below the head there was a code that was not in his register and had no prefix he knew at all, not even the regional one the autumn's sweep had carried. Under the code there was no office, no initials, no reason and no instruction. There was a date.

The date was Monday. The day after.

He sat looking at it.

He was very careful with himself about what it was, because he did not know. In the ordinary way a slip with that head meant that somebody above his floor, and above his section head's, wanted to know when the file moved; who, or why, it did not say. Whether it had anything to do with the mark Ilsev had found in the spring, he could not tell, though this one could at least be traced as far as a floor he had never been allowed to stand on. Whether it had anything to do with a chalk line in a clerk's copy of a board, or a Bronze lying down on raked dirt in front of three hundred people, or nothing at all, he could not tell either. A thing could be dated the day after another thing without being caused by it; he had told a great many people that, over the years, when they had come to him with two dates and a story.

He only knew that it was another place where the file had walked off its road, which made four, and that he could still see the footprints going away into the snow, more of them now and all going the same way, and still could not see who was walking.

The procedure allowed him to send a query upward. He took the form out of his drawer and laid it beside the file, and thought about his last query, the one about the sweep, which had come back in the spring pouch with a single word on it in a hand he did not know. *Noted.* He thought about putting his own name at the bottom of a question about this slip, and whether a man who did that might find, a month on, a slip of grey paper pinned inside his own file in the room at the end of the corridor. He did not think it likely, and he was not a man who called things likely without evidence. But sitting there with the form in front of him, he could not quite say it was not.

He had a daughter of fourteen at midsummer who did her sums at the kitchen table with her tongue between her teeth.

He put the form back in the drawer.

Then he closed the file without making the entry. He did not write *subject remains*, or *legal review pending*, or *public notice of a circuit result*, or anything at all. The pen lay on the desk beside it with its cap off, and the ink dried on the nib.

He took out his working log.

He had kept it for eleven years, in his own hand, in plain black books bought with his own money and shelved at home one after another. Nobody had ever asked him to keep it, or read it, and in it he had written every file he had opened and every file he had closed, and why, and what he had seen. He had written in it on the night of the sweep that the sweep was not standard, and on the night of the boy's objection that the objection was correct. He had written the *Noted* in it, and copied the word exactly, and the date it came. He had never once opened a file and not written down that he had.

He opened the log to the day's page and looked at it for a long time, and then closed it again, with nothing written, and put it in his inside pocket and buttoned his coat over it.

It was the first page he had ever left empty on purpose, and he did not like it. He sat at his desk in the grey light from the window and did not like it at all, with his hand flat on the closed file, and he understood that he had just done the first thing in his working life that he could not have written down and then stood behind.

He did not know who he was keeping it from, and that was the part he would lie awake with: whether it was from them, whoever they were, or from himself, or from a boy on a cot two days east who had asked him across a pie whether he knew what a flag was, and had watched him not move. He had told the boy the truth then; he had not known anything about a flag. He did now. He knew exactly as much as a slip of grey paper and a printed head and a date could tell a man, which was almost nothing, and was more than he had known on any day before this one.

At the end of the day he put the file back in the drawer for things under review, and locked the drawer, and took the key home with him, which was not the procedure either.

His daughter was at the kitchen table with her lessons when he came in. She looked up at him with her pencil in her teeth.

"Was anything interesting?"

He hung up his coat, with the log still buttoned inside it, and stood a moment with his hand on the coat.

"Somebody I know won something."

"Who?"

"Nobody you know." He sat down across from her, and drew her slate toward him, and looked at her sums. "You've carried the seven wrong."

She looked, and saw it, and rubbed it out, and did it again with her tongue between her teeth. He sat and watched her do it, and did not think about anything else for nearly a quarter of an hour, which was the longest he had managed in a month.

---

He wrote to Hesk on the Wednesday night, by the bead of Yeni's lamp, with the Log on his knee for a desk and his arm held against his side.

He told him the fourth exchange as near as he could, which was not near, and the crossing and the straw post and the bucket and the gate, the nod and Feryn's hand, the yard door and the chair, and that Vell had his name at the start of a line. He told him about supper: that there had been none, because he had been asleep, and then an egg, and a bun that Sella had lost on him, and the bread woman's own loaf, which she had kept back for herself every morning for seven months and given away to him, and he did not know her name. He did not tell him about the stranger in the market. Then he sat a while, and he did tell him, in four plain lines at the end, because he had promised to tell the next thing as it came and he had already been told off once this week for sitting on a wall.

Then he turned the Log over to the back.

He read the line he had written on Sunday night, at the bottom of the eighth instance, under the struck-through drawings: the fence at the edge of the marsh.

It did not belong there, he saw now; it was not about one bout or one half second. It was about all of it, every instance and both notices and whatever the next one was, and it ought to be somewhere he would see it every time he turned the book over, as Hesk's letters were somewhere he saw them every time he opened it. So he turned to the inside of the back cover, where there was nothing yet but the board's grain, and copied it there, slowly, in his best hand.

*They're not separate things I switch between. They're one thing with parts I haven't found all of yet.*

The front of the book had three letters in it now, and the back had a fence. He looked at the two ends of the book.

Then he turned to the last written page, under the eighth instance, and wrote the thing he had been turning over since the knife-grinder's bench. He had turned it over on the wall, and again with Lira, and all through the next day. It was short enough that it had to be right.

*Someone else wants me gone. I used to think that meant the Compact. I'm starting to think Coss doesn't know who he was talking about either.*

He read it back once, and did not change it.

Then he closed the Log, with its letters in the front and its fence in the back, and put it on the shelf beside Hesk's leather book, and lay down very carefully on his left side, which was the side that let him, and was asleep before Yeni had finished turning down the lamp.

In the morning he went up to the grey half, because Lira had said he could sit on the bench if he did nothing but sit.

The east wall's shadow lay long across the yard, as it did every morning, so that the Cinder House was split down the middle, a grey half and a bright half, with the groove going through both. The straw post stood in the corner of the grey half with Red Cap's fist still printed in it. Lira had taken the grey canvas off its nail on the wall and hung it back on the post, over the straw, where it had been all winter. The columns were on it in her chalk: the *gone* column, ruled off at forty, and the falls, and the long *W* with its strokes and rings and its small cost words, *same*, *less*, *less*, *same*, and the shorter *P*, with its four rings that said *trough*, and one stroke with no ring that said *both. nothing. arms anyway.*

He sat on the bench with his arm against his side and watched her.

She stood in front of the canvas with the chalk. Then, at the top of a clean stretch of grey beside the *P*, she wrote a *W*, and beside it a *P*, and drew a short line between the two of them, joining them. Under that she made one stroke, and put a ring round it, because it had come. Beside the ring, in her smallest letters, she wrote *ribs*. Then, after a moment, *arms*. Then, after a longer moment, she wrote one more word, and stood back so that he could read it from the bench.

*Sunday.*

She looked at it a while, with her head on one side.

"There. Now it's on the post."

She put the chalk in her pocket and came and sat down on the bench beside him, on the good side, and they watched the girls from the wall come in through the gate in a row, yawning, for their coin's worth of falling over. The sun had come up somewhere behind the east wall. The shadow had begun to shrink back across the dirt toward it, as it did, a little at a time.

When it reached the bench, Lira got up and moved them both along it, to the next bench, and sat down again, and said nothing about it. When it reached that one, she moved them again. She kept them in the grey half for as long as there was any grey half left.

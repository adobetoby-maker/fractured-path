# Chapter 48 — An Ordinary Tuesday

Properly, it turned out, meant a column.

Lira was at the post when he came in through the gate on Thursday with the bread, and she had already chalked it. Beside the *gone* column, which was ruled off at forty, and the falls column, which had stopped at nine, there was a third column now, narrow and new, with nothing at the head of it but a small *W* and nothing under the *W* at all.

"That's yours," she said. "Every time you ask for it, a stroke. Every time it comes, a ring round the stroke. And every time it costs you something, you tell me what, and I'll put a word beside it." She took the loaves off him and tore them and gave him the larger half. "You said on Sunday you'd write it down every time. You haven't."

"I've written it."

"You've written that it costs. That's not the same as writing what." She pointed the chalk at him. "*A little* is a word. *Worse* is a word. *Can't feel my foot* is three words, and if you ever say it to me I'll want to know. But it goes on the post, where I can see it, and not only in the back of your book where I can't."

He looked at the empty column, and then at her.

"Is this the rule?"

"This is the rule." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Eat. Then ask."

So he ate, and then he stood up in the grey half with his left arm held against his chest, because it would not yet hang without hurting, and asked.

The step came slowly, as it always came when it was asked for, as if he were reading it aloud from a page in poor light. His hip dropped and his body turned and his foot went down, and the frozen dirt between where it began and where it landed went short by the width of a hand. Then his breath went, and the old dull ache came into the joint of his left hip, and he stood there with his good hand pressed against it, breathing as if he had run up from the river.

Lira made a stroke on the post and put a ring round it.

"What?"

"Breath. The hip. About the same as Sunday."

"*Same*," she said, and wrote it beside the ring in small letters. "Again."

He did it again. It was worse, as the second always was, and the ground hardly changed under his foot.

"*Worse*," said Lira, and wrote that, and made a ring that was only half a ring, because she said that was fair. Then she looked at the two strokes for a while, chewing, with her head on one side.

"And the other one," she said.

He had known she would ask. He had known it since Tuesday, on the low wall, when she had read the second notice twice with her lips moving and said *two doors* in the voice she kept for things she would come back to. He had thought about the answer on the long walk to the brickworks and back yesterday, and he gave her the one he had found.

"I haven't tried it."

"Why not?"

"Because it doesn't go anywhere in particular." He looked across the yard at her, and at the girls coming along the far wall with their stolen breakfasts. "Feryn's went on everything in front of him. All at once. The Wind's only my feet. If I ask for the other one, it'll go wherever I'm facing." He did not look away. "And you're in front of me."

Lira stood quite still with the bread in her hand.

He thought for a moment that she would laugh, as she had laughed at the eel woman, and he would not have minded. She did not. She looked at him as she looked at a guard she had not seen before, the whole of it at once, and then she nodded, slowly, as if he had told her the right answer to a question she had not quite known she was asking.

"Then not me," she said. "Not the girls on the wall. Not the post, either; it's Vell's." She turned and looked at the empty end of the yard, where the grey half ran out against the drain. "Somewhere with nobody in front of it. When your arm can lift a cup."

"When my arm can lift a cup," he agreed.

"Good." She went to her mark. "Box drill. Slowly. And keep that arm out of it, or I'll tie it to you."

---

He posted the letter to Hesk at noon.

He had finished it the night before at the kitchen table, after everybody had gone up, with his left arm lying on the table beside the paper like something he had brought home from the market and not yet put away. It had not been a long letter to write. He had found, sitting down to it, that he knew exactly what it had to say, because he had promised in the last one that he would tell the next thing as it came, and this was the next thing, and it had come only two days ago.

*Hesk,*

*I said I'd tell you the next thing when it happened, and not five things later. This is the next thing.*

*I lost to the Bronze on Monday. Four exchanges, in the sleet. Vell called it in the fourth, by incapacity, and she was right to; I'd have got up into something worse. My left arm's swollen over the old burn and stiff, and the ribs catch on both sides now, and there's a bruise down my right side from the shoulder to the hip where the ground came up. It'll be a week. Nothing's broken. I did the accounting on my back, the way you check a frame after a drop.*

*He bought me supper afterward. He told me about a goat.*

*That night, after midnight, on my cot, there was another one. I'll copy it exactly, as I did the first.*

He had copied the three lines letter by letter from the back of the Log, and checked them twice against the page, and then once more against the place in the dark behind his breastbone where they had been written first.

*I think it came from him. Not from watching; I'd only watched him for one bout, and the first took three months. In the last exchange he threw me, and his hand stayed on my shoulder after the throw was finished, a second longer than it needed to. That's the only thing that doesn't fit anywhere else. It's one data point, and I've written that in the book where I'll have to look at it.*

*Lira knew first. I told her on Tuesday in the grey half before the light, and she read it twice and said she didn't know what it meant either, and gave me the bread.*

*I'm all right. I'm more than all right, most of the time. I lost and learned more than I've learned from most of the things I've won. I don't know what any of it is yet. I'm going to keep finding out.*

*— Cael*

At the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps, the clerk with the inky fingers read the address out to the hut as he read every address, and weighed the letter on his little brass balance, and looked at Cael over it.

"Thinner than the last," he said.

"Yes."

"Torvin's," said the clerk, as he had said it every week since the autumn, as though it were a small private joke between the two of them, and hung it on the third hook along, under WEST.

Cael stood and looked at it. It was a single folded sheet, and it had in it two plain lines of figures copied from the dark, and in a week a man would sit down with it on a workshop step in Denvash and read it twice and not drink his tea. Cael had decided, at the kitchen table, that this was better than the other thing. He found, standing in the hut with the river going by outside and the barges knocking at the quay, that he had not changed his mind.

He went back up the steps one at a time, with his good hand on the rail.

---

The arm got better, slowly, the way arms did.

By Saturday he could close the left hand round a cup, though he could not yet lift the cup. By Sunday the swelling had gone down enough that he could see the shape of the bone again under the skin, and the bruise had turned from black to a dark wet purple and begun, at its edges, to go the colour of a pear left too long on a sill. The ribs caught at the top of every full breath, both sides, the left more than the right. The right hip, where the ground had come up, stiffened every night and loosened every morning on the long walk up the hill to the grey half, and he learned to leave early enough to let it.

On Monday he went down to the mender's.

The mender was at his bench with his loupe in and a clock on its back in front of him, and he did not look up when Cael came in under the four black-painted words on the doorframe. He did not look up for some time. Then he took the loupe out of his eye and looked at Cael's left arm, which Cael was holding a little away from his side so that it would be seen to be holding itself.

"Still there," said the mender.

"Still here."

The mender considered him. Then he put the loupe back in, and pushed a tin of washers across the bench with one finger, and Cael sat down on the stool at the end and began to sort them by size, slowly, with both hands. It hurt a little to do it with the left. He did it with the left anyway, because he had been told to work it in cold water every morning and he thought that washers were very nearly the same thing.

Nobody said anything for the rest of the afternoon. At the end of it the mender counted the tins with his eyes, the way he always did, and nodded once over his clock, which meant Wednesday.

---

The summons came on Tuesday, which was an ordinary day.

That was what he remembered about it afterward. Nothing had happened that morning to warn him. He had gone up to the grey half and done the box drill slowly and asked for the step twice, *same* and *worse*, and Lira had chalked them. He had gone down to the market and bought two loaves from underneath and a twist of nuts from the boy who never haggled. It had rained a little at noon, a thin spring rain that had not been able to decide whether it was still winter. He had sat on the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard for an hour in the afternoon, working his arm in a bucket of cold water and drawing Corvane's figure in the wet dirt with a stick. Everything in the day had been the same size as everything in the day before it.

He came in through the yard door at dusk with the bucket, and Torvin's wife was at the range with her back to the table, and there was a letter propped against the salt crock.

It was not from Hesk. He saw that from the door. Hesk's letters were a little crooked and soft at the corners from a week in the bag, and they had *ROOMS (the O leans)* on the front in Hesk's square hand. This one was long and white and very straight, and it had a seal on the back of it the colour of dried blood, and in the middle of the seal there was the small hard shape he had seen once already, in the autumn, on a summons Torvin had held out to him at arm's length.

He put the bucket down.

"Boy from the post brought it," said Torvin's wife, to the range. "At noon. I made my mark for it." She stirred something. "He said there was no answer wanted. I said I hadn't asked him."

Cael picked it up. It weighed nothing. He broke the seal with his thumb and opened it standing up, by the light from the range.

*To CAELEN HESK-WARD, registration 41-7843-V, resident at the lodging house known as Torvin's, Unranked District, Ardenmere.*

*You are required to attend a senior evaluation, convened under the District Handbook, Part Six, at the post, Ardenmere, on Thursday next, at the second hour after noon.*

*Under the closed-pending order of the fifty-fifth day, this evaluation discharges the senior authorization then applied for.*

*Ilsev, Assessor, Regional Evaluation.*

*Countersigned: Coss, field agent.*

He read it twice, and then a third time, slowly, holding each word up against its neighbours, as he read everything he could not afford to get wrong. It did not change. It did not say what would happen on Thursday at the second hour after noon, or what an assessor did, or what Part Six was. It said a time and a place and a name, and that was all it needed to say, because the time and the place and the name were the whole of it.

He found that his hands were steady, which surprised him. He had thought for weeks that when it came he would feel it come, like the air leaning in Corvane's yard. Instead it had come against the salt crock on an ordinary Tuesday, between a bucket of cold water and a bowl of soup, and he felt mostly a kind of plain recognition, as you feel when a cart you have heard coming for some time finally turns the corner.

The six weeks had run out on the forty-second day. That had been five weeks ago. He had known it was coming, on every one of those days, and on most of them he had been too busy to look.

"Thursday," said Torvin's wife, who had not turned round.

"Thursday."

She stirred the pot. Then she reached behind her without looking and took a bowl down from the shelf and filled it and put it on the table at his place, and put a spoon in it, and put a heel of bread beside it.

"Eat that," she said. "Then go and tell the girl. Before the lamps, mind. That widow's a terror about the lamps."

---

The widow was asleep in her chair behind the counter with her chin on her chest, among the candles hanging by their wicks, and she woke up as he came in, which she had never done before.

She looked at him over the candles with her round pale face.

"Lamps in an hour," she said. "I'm saying it now."

"Yes."

She shut her eyes again, and he went round the side and up the stair, and the fourth step talked, and he said *I know* to it without thinking.

Lira opened the door before he had finished knocking. She had heard the step, and the voice, and the not-quite-running on the stair, and she stood in the door with her hand on the edge of it and looked at his face and then at the white paper in his hand, and stood aside without a word.

She took it out of his hand and read it standing up, under the sloping ceiling, with her lips moving very slightly. Then she read it again, sitting down on the end of the bed.

"Part Six," she said.

"Yes."

"I've never read Part Six." She looked up. "Have you?"

"No. I read Two and Three and Four. Four was the one that mattered." He sat down on the floor with his back against the shelf, on the worn pale strip, and was careful of his ribs. "Nobody's read Part Six. That's what's wrong with it."

"An assessor." Lira looked at the name at the bottom. "*Ilsev*. That's a woman. That's a woman who's come from regional to sit in that little room and do something Coss couldn't." She turned the paper over, as if there might be more on the back, and there was not. "She'll have read your objection."

"Yes."

"She'll have read it the way Coss did. Three times, with her lips moving. Except she'll have had weeks." Lira put the summons down on the blanket beside her, very carefully, as she would have put down the throat-sweet tin. "So she's coming with an answer to it. She'd be a fool not to, and she isn't a fool, or they wouldn't have sent her."

"I know."

"Then you need her answer before she gets here."

"I know." He looked at the paper on the blanket. "The reading room's mornings. Tomorrow. I'll go at first light and sit there till Halden puts me out."

Lira nodded slowly. He could see her doing the sums, the way she did them at the post: what there was, and what it would cost, and what was left over.

"I can't come," she said. "Wednesday's my hour with the dyers' girls, and it's the rent, and if I miss it they'll go and find somebody cheaper who doesn't know what a foot is for." She frowned at the summons as though it had done that on purpose. "And you read faster when I'm not there asking what words mean."

"You don't ask what words mean."

"I ask what they mean *here*. It's slower." She almost smiled, and then did not. "Thursday morning. Before the post. I'll come to Torvin's step at first light, and you'll read me whatever you've found, out loud, the way you did before, and I'll tell you which bits sound like you're frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"I know you're not. That's not what I said." She stood up, under the slope of the ceiling, and he stood up too. "Go home. The widow's got a candle in her lap that she's going to light the minute your foot's off her bottom step, and she'll count to the top of it, and if you're still on the stair she'll tell the whole lane."

He went. On the fourth step, going down, he heard the door shut above him, and the key turn in it, and then, after a moment, the key turn back again, as though she had locked it from habit and then thought better of it, or worse.

---

Halden looked up from his table when Cael came down the three steps into the reading room, and looked at him for the length of a breath, and then looked at the clock on the wall above the shelves, which said that it was very early indeed for anybody to want the law.

"Halden," said Halden.

"Part Six," said Cael.

Halden did not say anything for a moment. Then he got up from his table, slowly, as he had got up in the autumn, and walked down the long room to the left-hand shelves, and took down the thick district Handbook that Cael had read through twice in a day and most of a night. He did not bring it back to the long table. He turned it over in his hands and opened it from the back, and looked at what he found there, and came back along the room with it held open in both hands like a tray with something on it that might spill.

He set it down under the lamp.

The back of the book was a different paper. It was thicker and whiter and had been sewn in after the rest, and the pages had never been cut. They were still joined at the top and the outer edge, folded sheets, the way they had come from the binder, so that you could look between them as into a row of little paper rooms and see that there was print inside, but not read it. At the head of the first sheet, upside down, it said *PART SIX. THE SENIOR EVALUATION. RESERVED TO ASSESSORS.*

"It's bound in with the district edition," said Halden. "Nobody's ever cut it." He took a paper knife from his own table, a thin bone thing worn yellow, and laid it beside the book. "Reserved to assessors means that an assessor is expected to know it. It doesn't mean anybody else may not. Anybody the registry has a number for may read anything in this room." He looked at the uncut pages. "Nobody has wanted to before."

"Thank you."

"I said I'd find you the next book." Halden went back to his table. Halfway there he stopped, and said over his shoulder, without turning round, "Begin at the beginning. Copy it from the first line. The people who start in the middle are always wrong about something."

So Cael began at the beginning.

He cut the pages one at a time, with the bone knife, carefully, slitting along the folds. The paper made a soft dry tearing sound in the quiet that seemed louder than it was. He read each page as it opened, and then he copied it into the front of the Log in his careful hand, all of it, every line, from *Section 1: Definitions* down. His left arm ached from holding the book flat, and he swapped hands, and it ached the other way.

It was dull, and it was long, and he understood very little of it on the first reading and a good deal more on the second. Section one was definitions: what an assessor was, what a senior evaluation was, what the Handbook meant by a primary classification and a supplementary entry and a temporary non-standard category and a referral. Section two was who could ask for a senior evaluation and on what grounds, and his own case was there, in plain words, the same words he had found in Part Four in the autumn. Section three was how an assessor was to conduct one: the questions, the record, the presence of the referring agent. Section four was a long dry paragraph about practitioners whose classification could not be assessed by ordinary means, and a temporary category they could be entered under, and a facility they could be required to attend. Section five was what happened when none of the rest of it applied.

He copied all five. He copied section four the way he copied the others, line by line, with the lamp going on beside him and feet going past the high windows on the walk outside, boots and clogs and a dog. He read it once and thought, *that's for people the Arbiter couldn't read*, and went on to five.

By the time the light from the high windows had moved all the way across the long table and begun to go up the far wall, his hand had cramped twice and the Log had eleven more pages in it than it had had at dawn.

Halden came down the room at noon, when the reading room shut, to take the book back.

He looked at the cut pages, all of them slit now down their folds, lying open and loose, and then at Cael, and then at the Log.

"All of it?"

"From the first line."

Halden nodded slowly. He closed the book and held it for a moment with his hand flat on the cover, as if feeling whether anything inside had changed.

"Go home and read it again," he said. "Tonight. Slowly. You'll find something you didn't see here." He tucked the book under his arm. "People always do. It's why I tell them to copy it."

---

He read it again that night, at Torvin's kitchen table, after the lamps.

Torvin had said *Lamps* through the floorboards an hour ago. The range had been banked and was ticking as it cooled. Torvin's wife, going up, had looked at him sitting there with the Log open and a candle end in a saucer, and had not said anything, and had left the saucer. He had read the five sections through once, slowly, as Halden had told him to, and then again, more slowly. He had found nothing he had not found in the morning, and he had begun a third time, because he had learned in the autumn that the third reading was where things moved.

Somewhere in the middle of the third reading, two hours before dawn, by a candle end gone down to a stub, section four stood up off the page.

It did not change. The words were the words he had copied in the reading room, every one. *Where the primary classification of a practitioner cannot be assessed by ordinary means, the senior evaluator may enter the practitioner under a temporary non-standard category for the period of assessment.* He had read it that morning and thought, *that's for people the Arbiter couldn't read*. He read it now and heard it in a different voice: in the voice of a woman who had read his objection three times with her lips moving, and had had weeks.

*Cannot be assessed by ordinary means.*

He had said that. He had stood in the room above the reading room seven weeks ago, at the desk that rocked, and handed Coss a sheet of paper that said exactly that, in a citation Coss had checked from memory and found correct. A standard evaluation could not assess his classification. That was the whole of his objection. That was the thing that had bought him six weeks.

And it was the first half of section four.

He sat very still at the kitchen table with the cold going through his shirt.

It was not fear, quite. It was the thing that came in the circle when a real opponent showed him what she meant to do, the cold clean alertness that sat under the ribs and made everything else very quiet. He had built the door himself, in the autumn, and walked through it, and been glad of it. Now he could see, from the other side, that it opened both ways. A careful assessor would only have to read his own objection back to him, and then turn the page.

*Enter the practitioner under a temporary non-standard category. A practitioner so entered shall attend at a regional assessment facility within twenty-eight days, and remain there for the period of assessment.*

Remain there. That meant not here. That meant a room in a building he had never seen, in a city two days west or further, for as long as somebody else decided an assessment took, and Lira and the grey half and the Cinder House and Torvin's and the mender's tins of washers all a long way off on the other side of it. It would all be done by procedure, correctly, with a stamp. Nobody would have to do anything a clerk could be ashamed of.

He made himself breathe, four and four, with the catch at the top.

Then he turned back three pages, to the beginning, because Halden had told him the people who started in the middle were always wrong about something, and because he did not yet know what else to do.

*Section 1: Definitions.*

He read them one at a time, with his finger under the line, the way Hesk checked a load figure before he signed it. *Assessor.* No. *Senior evaluation.* No. *Primary classification: the classification returned by the practitioner's Arbiter at Kindling and entered in the registry, including any entry made in the Supplementary Register.* He stopped. He read it again, and his finger did not move.

Then he read the next one.

*Temporary non-standard category: the category under which a practitioner without primary classification is entered pending assessment.*

He sat back.

The candle end guttered in its saucer and steadied. Somewhere upstairs the wheezing man began his long whistle at the top of every breath, and one of the brothers said something in his sleep about a rope.

*Without primary classification.*

The category was not for anybody whose classification could not be read. It was for people who did not have one. Section four gave an assessor the power to put a practitioner into the category, and section one, three pages earlier, said who could be in it. A boy whose Arbiter had returned nothing would be in it. A boy whose return had been lost, or never recorded, would be in it. And a boy whose Arbiter had taken eleven seconds and then returned a word, a real word, a word with four instances and a statute of its own in the Supplementary Register, and a brass plate that had lit eleven seconds late, had a primary classification. He had more of one than anybody in the district. It simply was not one the standard framework could use.

That was not the same as not having one. Section one said so, in the Compact's own words.

He did not let himself feel anything about it yet. He had learned that in the circle too. A plan that felt good at the mark was a plan you had not yet tested. He took a clean page at the front of the Log, and ruled it, and began to build the answer the way he had built the objection in the autumn, in four steps, as plainly as he could.

*One. Section four lets the assessor put a practitioner into the temporary non-standard category when his classification can't be assessed by ordinary means. Mine can't. I said so myself.*

*Two. Section one, three pages before, says what that category is: it's for a practitioner without primary classification.*

*Three. Section one also says what a primary classification is: what the Arbiter returned, entered in the registry, including the Supplementary Register. Mine was entered there. Statute nineteen. Four instances. It resolved; the plate lit.*

*Four. So I have a primary classification. Section four gives her the power to put people in the category, and section one keeps me out of it. Not being assessable isn't the same as not being classified.*

He read the four steps through. Then he read them from the bottom up, as he had in the autumn, the fourth first, to see whether each one stood on the one below it or only leaned. They stood. He read them aloud, under his breath, the way Hesk read a figure, and they still stood, and he read them aloud again.

Then he copied them clean onto the next page, without the crossings-out, in his best hand, short enough to say and not read. He had learned from the autumn that a thing said from memory across a desk sounded like knowledge and a thing read from a page sounded like a hope.

When he looked up the window over the range had gone from black to grey, and Torvin's wife was standing at the foot of the stairs in her shawl, looking at him. He did not know how long she had been there.

She did not ask him anything. She went to the range and opened it and woke the fire, and put the oats on, and when they were done she put a bowl in front of him and stood over him with the wooden spoon until he had eaten all of it.

# Chapter 49 — Three Pages Earlier

Lira was sitting on Torvin's step under the leaning O when he came out, with her knees drawn up and her canvas bag beside her and the end of a loaf in her lap, and she did not get up.

"Sit," she said. "Say it."

He sat down beside her on the cold stone. The lane was grey and empty, and the gutter down the middle of it was running with the night's rain, and somewhere toward the river a cart was going over the cobbles very slowly, as if the carter were still asleep. He did not take out the Log. He had decided in the kitchen that if he could not say it on the step without the page, he could not say it at the desk.

So he said it. He said the summons, and Part Six, and Halden and the bone knife and the uncut pages. He said section four in its own words, and then what he had heard in it at two in the morning: that his own objection from the autumn was the first half of an assessor's argument, and that the second half was a room in a city he had never seen. Lira's face did not do anything while he said that part. Her hand went still on the bread.

Then he gave her the four steps, from memory, the way he meant to give them across the desk.

When he had finished, she sat for a while looking at the gutter.

"Again," she said. "From *one*."

He said it again. Halfway through the third step she lifted a finger, as she did at the post when his hips went square, and he stopped.

"*If I might draw the assessor's attention.*" She said it in his voice, or near enough, a little higher and a good deal more polite than he would ever have admitted to sounding. "Don't. You're not drawing anything. You're telling her what's on the page."

"It's a courteous way to—"

"It's a way of asking permission. You'll be sitting across a desk from a woman who's come two days in a cart to tell you where you have to live, and you'll be asking her permission to read her own book to her." Lira shook her head. "Just say the section. *Section one says.* That's all. She'll draw her own attention."

He thought about it. She was right, and he could feel that she was right, the way you can feel a boot that is a half size too small before you have taken three steps in it.

"Section one says," he said.

"Better. Go on."

He went on. At the end of the fourth step she lifted the finger again.

"*I hope that's clear.*" She looked at him sideways. "You said that at the end. You didn't say it the first time; you said it the second. You're adding it because you're nervous."

"I'm not nervous."

"Then you won't mind leaving it off." She broke the end of the loaf in half and gave him the larger piece. "Stop when you've finished. Don't hope at her. If it's clear she'll see it's clear, and if it isn't, hoping won't help. Just stop, and look at her, and wait. You're good at waiting. I've watched you wait out Dessa."

He ate the bread and said the four steps a third time, with *section one says* where the drawing of attention had been, and nothing at all after the fourth. It came out shorter, and flatter, and it sounded, when he heard it in the grey air of the lane, like somebody who had read a book and knew what was in it.

Lira listened with her chin on her knees.

"That's it," she said. "That's the one." She stood up and put the bag over her shoulder. "I'll be at the top of the fish steps at the second hour. I'm not coming in. If I came in, she'd want to know who I was, and you'd want to tell her, and that's a second thing in a room where there should only be one." She looked down at him. "And afterward, wherever you go, I'll find you."

The clerk at the post's counter looked up at the clock on the wall behind him when Cael came in, and then down at Cael, and then at the bench.

There was nobody on the bench today. The cooper and the dyer had settled their gutter, or given up on it, and the front room was quiet and smelled of ink and damp stone, and through the small window by the door Cael could see the top of the fish steps and, at the top of them, a figure in a turned-back jacket leaning on the post rail with her arms folded, not looking at the building at all. He sat down on the bench under the board nobody read and put his hands on his knees.

At the second hour exactly the clerk jerked his head at the narrow stair.

Cael went up it sideways.

The room at the top was the room it had been in the autumn: the one window looking down the fish steps to the river, the cot under it with its blanket folded square, the partition of planks on one side stopping short of the ceiling. The desk was where it had been. He saw before anything else that the small grey square of folded paper was no longer lying on the floorboards a hand's width from the left front leg. It was under the leg, where it belonged, folded thicker than before.

When the woman behind the desk leaned forward to set down her pen, the desk did not move at all.

She was about forty, perhaps a little more, with dark hair pulled back plainly and a narrow face that had a great deal of attention in it and almost nothing else. She wore a grey coat of good cloth, not new, and had taken it off and hung it over the back of her own chair rather than on the hook on the door. On the desk in front of her there were three things, set square to its edges and to each other: a thick folder tied with tape, a small book bound in dark cloth, and a single sheet that he recognized at once, because he had written it, in the reading room below, seven weeks ago.

"Caelen Hesk-ward," she said.

"Cael."

"Cael." She said it as if she were writing it down somewhere behind her eyes. "I'm Ilsev. I'm an assessor, from regional. Sit down."

He sat.

She did not pick anything up. She looked at him across the three square things on the desk, steadily and without hurry, and he had the feeling he had had once in Corvane's yard: that he was being read the way he read people, and that she was better at it than most. He made himself sit still under it, and look back, and say nothing until she did.

"Your objection was correct," said Ilsev.

He had not expected that to be first. He kept his face where it was.

"A standard evaluation can't be done on your classification. The framework has no place to put it. Field Agent Coss checked your citation against the regulation and found it sound, and so did I, three times, and so did the regional office." She laid two fingers on the edge of his sheet without moving it. "That's why I'm here. The regulation sends a case like yours to a senior evaluator. I'm not here to argue the autumn again. You won the autumn."

"Thank you."

"I didn't say it to be thanked." She took the two fingers away. "I said it so that we both know where we're standing. Because the same regulation goes on, past the part you used, and I don't think you've read where it goes."

She did not open the little dark book. She did not need to. She gave it to him from memory, the section and the words and the order the words came in, the way Coss had checked his citation without getting up, only more smoothly, as though she had said it a great many times in rooms like this one.

"Part Six. Section four. *Where the primary classification of a practitioner cannot be assessed by ordinary means, the senior evaluator may enter the practitioner under a temporary non-standard category for the period of assessment.*" She paused, very slightly, to let the first sentence settle before she gave him the second. "*A practitioner so entered shall attend at a regional assessment facility within twenty-eight days, and remain there for the period of assessment.*"

She looked at him.

"Your classification cannot be assessed by ordinary means. You told us so yourself. I have the power to enter you under the temporary category, and I'm inclined to use it, because it's the pathway the regulation provides for exactly this. You would come to regional. You'd be housed. You'd be assessed properly, by people who have instruments for it, over as long as it takes. It's not a punishment. It's the procedure." She folded her hands on the desk. "That's the case. If you've anything to say to it, now is the time."

Then she stopped, and waited, with the composed and entirely untroubled patience of a fighter who has just put the last piece of her frame in place and is standing on it.

He watched her wait.

He had watched a great many people stand on their marks and wait. Dessa waited like a wall. Corbin waited like a man reading a newspaper. This woman waited like somebody who had shut every door in a house, one after another, and was now standing in the hall listening to be sure that none of them would open. She had done it well. She had done it in order, with the words in the order the Handbook gave them, and she had not reached for the book once. If he had not sat at Torvin's kitchen table until the window went grey, he would have been sitting in the hall with her now, listening to the doors stay shut.

*Section one says.*

"Section one says what the temporary category is," said Cael. His voice came out level and rather quiet, which he had not been sure it would. "It's three pages before section four. It defines it as *the category under which a practitioner without primary classification is entered pending assessment.*"

Ilsev did not move.

"Section one also says what a primary classification is. *The classification returned by the practitioner's Arbiter at Kindling and entered in the registry, including any entry made in the Supplementary Register.*" He did not hurry. "Mine was returned at Kindling. It took eleven seconds. It's entered in the Supplementary Register, statute nineteen, with an incidence count. I have a primary classification. It isn't one the standard framework can use, which is why section four's first half fits me. But section four lets you enter a practitioner into the category, and section one says who the category is for. It's for practitioners without one. Not being assessable by ordinary means isn't the same as not being classified."

He stopped.

He did not say *I hope that's clear*. He felt it come up behind his teeth and he did not let it out. He sat with his hands on his knees and looked at her, and waited, the way he had been told to on a wet step at dawn.

The silence went on.

Ilsev was looking at him, not at the book. Something had happened in her face, and it took him a moment to see what it was, because there was so little in her face to begin with. It was not irritation. Coss's face had done irritation, in the autumn, before it had done anything else. This was something that came after. It looked, for a moment, like a person who has stepped down onto a stair in the dark and found it was not where her foot expected it to be, and who has stopped, quite still, with her weight not yet on it.

Then she reached for the small dark book.

She opened it without searching, at the front of the part he had cut open in the reading room with a bone knife. Her copy had never been uncut. Its pages were soft at the corners and grey along the edges where a thumb had turned them a great many times, and there was a pencil mark in the margin beside section four. She did not look at section four. She turned back three pages, to the first, and laid the book flat on the desk with her hand holding it open, and read.

He watched her read it. She read it the way he had watched Lira read a notice and Feryn read Vell's ledger, all of it, slowly, line by line, with nothing in her face at all. She read section one to the bottom. Then she went back to the top of it and read the two definitions again, the second one twice, and he saw her lips move very slightly on *without*.

Somewhere under the window a gull came down on the rail of the fish steps and said something harsh, and went away again. He kept his hands on his knees. He thought, absurdly, of Dessa on her mark in the autumn with her count going four in and four out, and of how long the space between two exchanges could be when you were the one standing in it, and of how he had never once in his life been on the other side of a desk while somebody read. He did not like it much. He sat in it anyway, the way he would have sat in a held breath, and waited for it to be over.

Ilsev looked up.

"You've read the definitions," she said.

"I read all five sections. From the first line."

"When?"

"Yesterday morning. In the reading room downstairs." He hesitated, and then told her the rest, because it was true and she had asked. "The pages hadn't been cut. The man who keeps it gave me a knife."

Ilsev looked at him for a long moment.

Then she closed the book, and laid her hand flat on the cover of it, as Halden had laid his hand on the Handbook when it came back to him, as if to feel whether anything inside had changed.

"You're right," she said.

She said it flatly, without any softening and without any grudge in it, as she might have said the time or the weather. Then she said it again, a little differently, as though the first time had been for him and the second was for the record.

"You're right. The temporary category doesn't reach you. I've entered eleven people under section four in fifteen years, and every one of them came to me because their Arbiter returned nothing at all, or returned something nobody wrote down. I have never had a practitioner across this desk with a recorded classification that the framework couldn't use." She was quiet a moment. "The regulation wasn't written by anybody who expected you."

"What happens now?"

"Section five." She did not need the book for that either. "Where neither the standard evaluation nor the temporary category applies, the senior evaluator refers the question to legal review. Not your case: the question. How the regulation is to treat a classification like yours at all." She picked up her pen, and drew the folder toward her, and untied its tape. "Legal review is slow. It's slower than anything else we have, because it's the one part of us that's meant to be right instead of quick. It'll be months. It may be the rest of the year."

"And then?"

"I don't know." She looked at him over the folder. "I'm telling you that plainly because it's true. Nobody has ever asked them this question. I can't tell you what they'll answer." She wrote something on the top sheet, two lines, in a small upright hand. "In the meantime, you're not required to appear anywhere, or to change anything about where you live or what you do. The order of the fifty-fifth day stands: closed, pending. It goes on pending."

He let his breath out, slowly, so that she would not see him do it, and saw that she had seen.

She wrote a third line. Then she stopped with the pen above the paper and looked, not at his face, but at his left arm, which was lying along his thigh with the wrist turned out, and the yellow-and-purple edge of the bruise showing under the cuff of his shirt.

"What happened to your arm?"

He thought about how much to say. He thought about *don't change what you do*, and about Vell's book, which stayed clear of the Compact, and about what Lira would say if he were here and she were on the step.

"A man threw me," he said. "In a ring. A week ago Monday. There's a yard in the district where people fight on Sundays and Thursdays, for a purse, and somebody keeps a book. I fight there." He met her eyes. "I don't practise a Path there. I haven't got one to practise that anybody can register. I just fight."

Ilsev wrote it down. It took her one line.

"Who keeps the book?"

"I'd rather not say."

She considered him with her pen still. Then she nodded, once, as if he had given her a correct answer to a different question from the one she had asked.

"It isn't material," she said, and did not write anything more about it. She put the pen down, and sat back, and for the first time since he had come up the stair she looked at him as if she had nothing to write. "Is there somebody I ought to be grateful to? Somebody who sat you down at a table with a regulation and made you start at the first line?"

He thought about the bench in Fen Street, and the two windows, and a load figure checked aloud before it was signed.

"A man who makes instruments," he said. "In Denvash. He taught me a rule at his bench. Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from." His eyes went to the little dark book under her hand. "I don't think he was thinking about regulations."

"It applies," said Ilsev.

She said it without any warmth at all, and he found that it was the warmest thing anybody from the Compact had ever said to him. Then she stood up, and he stood up because she had. "I need to speak to Field Agent Coss. He's below. Would you wait in the next room? There's a stool. It won't be long." She paused. "And I'll want you again before I go."

The next room was a slot of a place behind the partition, hardly wider than the cot, with shelves of tied bundles going up to the ceiling on one side and a stool on the other, and a little window no bigger than a book that looked out at the wall of the house next door. He sat on the stool with his back against the planks.

The partition stopped a hand's breadth short of the ceiling. He had known that in the autumn. He had not thought about what it meant until he heard the stair creak under a heavier tread than Ilsev's, and a man come up it sideways, and a door shut, and Coss's voice say, quite clearly, as if he were standing at Cael's elbow:

"Legal review," said Ilsev.

There was a silence. Cael sat very still on the stool. He could hear Coss's breath, and the scrape of the second chair, and the small sound of Coss not sitting down in it.

"Legal review," said Coss.

"Section five. The temporary category doesn't apply. He has a primary classification under section one, and section one keeps him out of it. I checked it. It's correct."

"He found section one."

"He found all of it. He cut the pages."

"It was supposed to be *resolved*."

"Then somebody ought to have written Part Six with him in it." Ilsev's voice was perfectly even, and perfectly dry, and under it there was something Cael had not heard in it before, which might in another person have been the beginning of a laugh. "You brought a closing form two days in a cart."

"I did."

"Then you can carry it back two days in a cart." Something was set down on the desk, square, with a small flat sound. "It's light. Nothing's written on the line that matters."

Coss did not answer that. Cael heard him breathe out, a long breath, through his nose. He heard him walk to the window, three steps, and stop there, and he imagined him standing with his thumb pressed to the bridge of his nose looking down the fish steps at the river.

Sitting on the stool in the slot of a room, with his back against the planks, Cael found that he was not glad.

He had thought he would be. He had won, and he knew he had won, the way you know in the circle when a strike has landed clean. But he could hear Coss at the window not saying anything, and he found himself thinking of the desk that no longer rocked, and the folded paper under its leg, and who had put it there. He thought of a man who had checked a boy's citation from memory in the autumn and found it right and stamped it anyway, with more force than the stamp needed, because it was right. That man had promised somebody above him a season. He would have to go back now with months, in his own hand, and whoever was above him would read it.

Cael did not know who that was. He did not think Coss did either. That was the part that stayed with him on the stool.

"I'll take it back with me," said Coss at last, from the window. His voice had gone flat again, and tired. "The referral. I'll carry it to regional myself."

"Thank you."

"Don't." The stair creaked. "I'm only carrying it."

The door opened and shut, and the heavy tread went down the stair sideways, and was gone.

Ilsev called him back in after a while, and he came round the end of the partition and sat down again in the chair across from her.

The folder was tied up. Her coat was still over the back of her chair. She had put the little dark book away somewhere, and on the desk between them now there was only one thing, a sheet from her own file lying face down, which she had set there square to the edge of the desk and then left, as if she had not quite decided whether to turn it over.

"Did you hear all that?" she said.

He thought about saying no. "Most of it."

"I thought you would. The wall doesn't go to the top." She did not seem troubled by it. "Field Agent Coss is a careful man and he's had a bad morning. Don't hold the morning against him."

"I don't."

"Good." She looked at the face-down sheet. Then she looked up at him, and her voice changed. It did not get softer, exactly; Cael did not think it knew how to get softer. But it came down out of the place it had been all afternoon, the place where sections and definitions lived, into somewhere plainer, as if she had taken off a second coat he had not known she was wearing.

"There's another thing," said Ilsev. "It isn't in the regulation, and I don't understand it, and I've thought for two days about whether to tell you. I've decided it's yours."

He sat very still.

"When I put your file into standard procedure at regional," she said, "to start this evaluation, it came back from the docket with a flag on it." She touched the corner of the face-down sheet with one finger, without turning it over. "A mark against the record. I didn't ask for it. I'd looked at the file the day before, when it was given to me, and the record was clean. The flag went on when I opened the procedure. Not before."

"What does it mean?"

"I don't know." She said it as flatly as she had said *you're right*. "I've looked for whose it is. Assessors keep the long registers, the ones a field agent never sees; I went through every one of them, and through the ones they point to. This doesn't come from any office I can find. Not mine. Not Coss's. Not any desk I've ever had a paper back from." She took the finger away from the sheet. "It might be nothing. It might be some old mark that goes on every file with a classification as rare as yours, that nobody ever bothered to write down anywhere I'd see it. I've thought of six things it might be that would be nothing at all."

She stopped, and he waited, because he could see that she had not finished.

"I don't believe any of the six," said Ilsev. "So I've put it in my report. A plain paragraph: that it's there, that I didn't request it, that I can't trace it, and that it went on when standard procedure began. I've drawn no conclusion from it, because I haven't got one to draw." She looked at him steadily. "Leaving it out would have made my report tidier and less true. I won't do that. And I thought that if I wouldn't do it to the regional office, I'd better not do it to you."

Cael did not say anything for a while.

He was putting it where he put things. He could feel himself doing it: picking it up and weighing it, as he would have weighed a tell he had never met before, and setting it down beside the others. There was a sweep in the autumn that Coss had not been able to explain. There was an authorization code that was not in Coss's book. And now there was a mark on his record that had appeared on the morning somebody began to treat him like an ordinary case, and that a woman with more ways to look than Coss could not find the end of. He did not know what any of them were. He did not know whether they were one thing or three.

He found that his hands had gone cold on his knees, and he left them there.

"Thank you for telling me," he said.

Ilsev did not answer that. She stood up, and took her coat off the back of the chair, and put it on, and did up the buttons one at a time from the bottom, without hurrying, as if she were giving him the time it took to do them up.

"It isn't a gift," she said, when the last button was done. "It's a fact about you, and facts about you are yours. You're owed the true thing, even when I can only give you the first half of the sentence."

She picked up the folder and the face-down sheet and put the sheet inside the folder without turning it over, and tucked the folder under her arm.

At the top of the stair she stopped and looked back at him.

"Mind the stair," she said. "It's narrower going down." She considered him a moment longer, with the plain attention that seemed to be the whole of her face. "Read the rest of the Handbook, Cael. All of it. Somebody should."

Then she went down the stair sideways, and he heard her say something to the clerk at the counter, and the street door stick and open and shut, and he was alone in the room over the fish steps with the desk that did not rock.

He sat there until he could feel his hands again. Then he went down.

# Chapter 50 — A Talk Owing

Lira was at the rail at the top of the fish steps where she had said she would be, with her arms folded on it and her face turned to the river, and she turned round when the door of the post stuck and opened and let him out.

She did not ask. She looked at him, the whole of him at once, the way she looked at a fighter coming back to his mark after an exchange, and he watched her read it: that he was walking, and alone, and had no paper in his hand, and that there was something else in his face under the walking that she had not seen there before.

"It held," he said. "Legal review. Months."

She let her breath out. He saw her shoulders come down a finger's width, and then go up again, because she had seen the something else.

"And?"

"Walk with me."

He did not know, when he said it, where he was going. He knew only that he could not stand still at the top of the fish steps with the post at his back and say the rest of it, not yet, not with the door that stuck still swinging behind him. Lira walked beside him the whole way and did not say a word. Once, at the corner of the market, she looked at him sideways as if she were about to, and he saw her pick the question up and look at it and put it down again beside her, as she had learned to do. "Yes."

Lira took one hand off her knees and held it up between them, and put a finger down.

"The sweep in the autumn. The man with the pin, walking the market for you five weeks after you came through the gate, when Torvin's seen four pins in eleven years." She put a second finger down. "The code on the sweep that wasn't in Coss's little black book. He said so. Not in words, but he said so." A third. "And this." She looked at the three fingers. "Three things nobody can find the end of."

"Or one."

"Or one." She folded her hand shut. "The old man in the yard said the pin was only the end of a string. Do you remember? The day after the sweep, at his gate. *Don't wonder whether it was you he was walking for. Wonder what changed.*" She turned her head and looked at him. "I think you've found a bit more of the string."

He thought about that. He had thought it himself on the stool behind the partition, in other words. It was different to hear it said out loud on a roof by somebody who was not inside it.

"I want to go back down to the reading room," he said. "Tomorrow. Ask Halden for whatever's after Part Six."

"And find what?" Lira did not say it unkindly. "There's no shelf for this. Your assessor went through every book she's got, and she's got more books than Halden. You've got a mark you can't see on a paper you'll never be shown, and you can't read it, and you can't fight it, and you can't cite it." She put her chin back on her knees. "Put it in the back of your book. Put it with the washers. You'd keep a broken washer if somebody told you it was evidence. Then leave it there, and look at it every so often, and see if it's moved."

"That isn't very much."

"No. It's what there is." She was quiet a moment. "She told you, though. Your assessor. She didn't have to. She could have written it in her report and gone home on the cart and you'd never have known there was anything to keep." She looked at the core across the river, which was going grey now, window by window. "That's something. I don't know what it is. But it's something."

They sat until the lamps began to come out along the lower bridge.

"You put your name down for Dessa," Lira said, without looking at him. "On the way here. Before you told me any of this."

"Yes."

"You walked out of the post with a flag on your record, and the first thing you did was go and get yourself knocked down by a Stone Copper Five."

"I didn't think about it. My legs went there."

"I know. I watched them go." She bumped his shoulder, gently, on the right side, the side that did not hurt. "I'm not cross. It's what I'd have done. I just want it written down somewhere that I noticed."

He wrote to Hesk that night at the kitchen table, after the lamps.

He wrote it all. He had promised to, and he found that he wanted to, which was not always the same thing. He wrote about Part Six and the bone knife and the uncut pages, because he thought Hesk would like the uncut pages more than anything else in the letter. He wrote section four out, and section one, and the four steps between them, plainly, the way he would have written out a load figure. He wrote *you're right*, twice, and legal review, and months. He wrote Coss at the window. He wrote the flag, exactly as Ilsev had given it to him, with nothing added and nothing taken away.

He wrote that he had a bout with Dessa on Sunday week, and that she had asked for it herself, and that his arm would be an arm by then.

Then he stopped, with the pen over the page.

There was a question he had been carrying since the roof. It had been sitting in him, under everything else, like a stone in the heel of a boot. He did not know how to put it, and he had nobody else to put it to who would know more than he did, and he thought that if anybody in the world did, it might be a man who had worked in the Compact's own offices at twenty-two and left them.

He wrote it as plainly as he could.

*I want to ask you something, and I don't need the answer quickly. People here know me now. Not what I am; most of them don't know that, and the ones who do haven't made much of it. But they know my face. The cook. Vell. The yard. The boys with the bets. Forty-one people stood in the sleet to see me lose. If whoever made that mark wanted me gone, would any of that matter? *I don't know which. I think you might.*

*— Cael*

He read it over twice by the candle end. Then he folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning, and sat for a while at the scrubbed table with the range ticking, and counted, as he did now, what he had not told Hesk.

There was nothing to count. He had told him everything there was. It was only that the everything had got larger.

At noon on Friday the carriers' clerk weighed it on the brass balance and looked at Cael over it.

"Thick one again," he said.

"Yes."

"Torvin's," said the clerk, and hung it on the third hook.

He went to the Sunday card that week to watch, because there was nothing else he knew how to do with a week, and Dessa was not on it.

He had known she would not be. She fought when Vell put her down, and Vell had put her down for him, and Dessa had never in her life wasted a bout on a week she meant to spend getting ready. But he went to the rope on the shaded side anyway with his hand on the iron stake, and watched the first bout, a pair of Coppers from the dyers' sheds, and the second, the kiln boy against a girl from Fenrow with a Tide Path who put out his heat with a slap of water like a bucket emptied off a roof. Halfway through the second bout he looked up across the circle and found Dessa on the third bench.

She was where she had sat for Corbin, at the end of the row against the cold wall, beside an old man in a scarf, with her hands folded in her lap. She was not watching the kiln boy. She was not watching the girl from Fenrow. She was looking straight across the circle and the rope at Cael, at his hand on the stake and the pencil in his other hand, and when he looked up and met her eyes she did not look away, as most people would have. She went on looking. Then, after a while, she looked down at his hand on the stake, deliberately, and back up at his face.

He took his hand off the stake.

He did not know why he did it. He stood at the rope with both hands at his sides like a boy who has been caught reading somebody else's letter, and across the circle Dessa watched him do that too, and then turned her head at last and watched the kiln boy lose.

Lira had seen it from the barrel.

"She's still at it," she said at the gate afterward, falling in beside him. "She's been at it since the autumn. Every bout you've had, she's been on that bench." She glanced at him. "You went into her the first time with four pages on her. How many do you suppose she's got on you?"

"More than four."

"A great many more than four. And she hasn't had to write any of them down." Lira put her hands in her jacket pockets. "You'll go in looking for her foot."

"She'll have fixed the foot. She'll know I found it."

"So what will you look for?"

He did not have an answer. He walked a whole street without one, and Lira let him.

"Ask it the other way," she said at the well with the iron cover. "Not what's she got. What have *you* got, that she's had all winter to watch?" She stopped at the turning for her lane. "Think about it tonight. Tell me in the grey half. Same time tomorrow."

---

He thought about it that night on the cot, and in the morning, coming up the hill in the dark with the bread, he gave her the list.

"I put my hand on the ground at my mark. Everybody knows that now. I go in on a tell, when I've found one; I always have. I count my breath, four and four, the way she does. I stand off for the first exchange and watch. I don't strike much." He gave her the bread. "That's all I can think of."

Lira tore the loaf and gave him the larger half. She did not say anything. She went to her mark, and stood there in the grey half with her hands loose, and looked at him.

Then she nodded.

It was a small nod, very short, a dip of the chin no bigger than a sparrow taking a drink, and it was his. He knew it at once, the way you know your own handwriting on a page you do not remember writing. It was the nod he gave himself on his mark when the plan had come right and he was about to go. She had found it in the autumn, and done it at him then, and he had spent most of the winter trying to wear it away.

"That," said Lira.

"It's nearly gone."

"In the grey half, it's nearly gone. When you're fresh and I'm telling you to watch for it." She dropped her hands. "Against Corbin, in the fifth, it was there. Against the Fenrow man it was there. In the fourth against Feryn it was there, a heartbeat before you went to slip, and if Feryn had been watching your chin instead of putting his hand where you'd be, he'd have had it." She looked at him steadily. "When you're tired it comes back. When you're hit, it comes back. And she's watched you be hit and tired in every bout you've had since the autumn, from that bench, with her hands folded."

He stood with the bread in his hand and did not eat it.

"Doss said—"

"Whatever you do twice, you've done once too often." Lira nodded. "You've done that one about fifty times."

They worked on it all week.

It was not like the step, or the build, or anything he had learned in the grey half before. There was nothing to learn. There was only something to stop doing, and he found that stopping was harder than doing, because the nod did not come from anywhere he could reach. It came out of the very place where he made up his mind; it was the outside of the inside of deciding. Every time he got ready to go in the box drill, his chin went down, a hair, and Lira said *there* and he had to begin again. By Wednesday he could keep it in when he was fresh, nine times in ten. By Wednesday afternoon, after the mender's and two hours of the box drill at full speed, he could keep it in three times in ten, and the other seven Lira said *there*, more and more quietly, until she stopped saying it and only touched her own chin.

"Eight days isn't a season," she said on Thursday, sitting on the low wall. "It didn't go in a season and it won't come out in eight days."

"Then what do I do?"

"I don't know." She gave him the larger half. "Go in knowing it's there. That's more than you had yesterday."

It was the answer to the thin one, the one with the second notice in it, and it was thin too, which he took as a good sign before he had opened it. Hesk wrote long when he was frightened and could not do anything with it. He wrote short when he had decided.

He took it out to the low wall by the pump to read.

*Cael,*

*Two, then. I read your letter on the step and at the bench and at the kitchen table, which makes three, and I'll stop there, because there's a level for the survey office on the bench that won't true itself.*

*I'll tell you what I felt first, since you'd ask. I was frightened for your arm before I was frightened for the other thing. That should tell you what I am before I'm anything else. Work it in cold water. Don't lift with it till it tells you you can.*

*The other thing I can't help you with, and I won't pretend to. I don't know what a hand can carry. I don't know what anything can carry, in this. A man who writes "one" when he'd like to write "two" will be all right.*

*A man who beats you and then buys your supper is a man worth knowing. Keep his account open.*

# Chapter 51 — The Nod

The benches were out, all three rows, and the rope was three deep along the sunny side, and there were boys on the wall who had not paid, which Vell allowed on a Sunday so long as they did not shout.

Cael had not seen the yard this full since Corbin. He had not expected to see it like this again for a bout that was not a Bronze, and he stood at the rope on the shaded side with his coat off and his hand on the iron stake and looked at it, and understood, slowly, that some of these people had come because of the sleet. They had heard about a boy who went four with a Bronze and got up at eight, and they had come to see what he looked like when he was not losing.

Marrow was by the barrels with his slate. On the slate, under the third bout, he had written *D. / unr.* and then, beside it, very small, *even*, and then rubbed out *even* with his thumb and written it again.

Lira was at the barrel with her canvas bag. Renn was on the sunny side with two of his barge friends, both of them already shouting at nothing. Torvin's wife was not there, because she never came, but the boots woman was, three rows back on the middle bench in her laced boots, eating something out of a paper. Dessa was at the rope on the far side with her coat off and her hair tied tight, not looking at anybody.

The lamps had just been lit along the three walls. They were not hot yet. The light in the yard was the going light of a spring afternoon, low and long and yellow over the west wall, with the lamps beginning to show against it like coins held up to a window.

"Third bout," said Vell.

He went over the rope.

He stood on his mark in the groove and crouched, as he did now before every bout, and put his right hand flat on the dirt for one breath. The ground was cool and damp under a dry skin. He felt nothing in it yet but the crowd, a faint general shuffle like the sea a long way off.

Across the circle, Dessa crouched too.

She did it slowly, without any hurry at all, so that everybody along the rope could see her do it. She put her right hand flat on the dirt at her own mark, exactly as he had put his, and held it there for one breath, and looked at him across the circle while she did it. Then she stood up.

The yard made a sound. It was not a laugh, though some of it was laughing. It was the sound people make when they see a thing they did not know they were going to see, and know they will tell somebody about it afterward.

Cael stood up slowly. He kept his face where it was, and inside it he wrote down, as plainly as if he had the Log on his knee: *She's read me. Not the page. Me.*

Dessa set her feet. The loose grit round her boots shivered all at once, the way it always did, as if somebody had struck the ground under her with a hammer, and then lay down flat. When it had lain down, she did not look like a girl of twenty standing in a circle any longer. She looked like something that had been built there.

"Dessa," said Vell. "Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

He went left, as he always went left in the first, to see what she did with it.

She did nothing with it. She turned with him, a quarter turn and then another, never coming off her mark by more than a pace, with her hands low and loose in front of her hips and her eyes on his face. She did not strike. She had never once struck in a first exchange in all the bouts he had watched her fight, and she did not strike now. He came in a half step and went out again, and her frame came round to meet the half step, and settled.

Her back foot came down late.

He saw it at once, because he had spent two Sundays on a bench in the autumn learning to see it. The rest of her frame formed, and for half a beat the back foot was still in the air above where it meant to go, and then it came down and she was set. It was the same half beat, at the same place, that he had found in the autumn and built his whole win on.

He went right. She came round. The foot came down late.

He went left again, a longer step this time, so that she had to turn further. The foot came down late.

He did not go in. He kept moving, round the inside of the groove, with his hands up, and watched the foot do it a fourth time and a fifth, and something about it began to sit wrong in him, the way a figure sits wrong on a page before you have found which number is wrong in it.

In the autumn the foot had come late when she was tired. It had come late in the fourth exchange of a long bout, and in the fifth, and not before. It had come late sometimes by half a beat and sometimes by a little less, and once by more, the way any real thing in a body wanders, because a body is not a clock.

This foot was not tired. This was the first exchange. And it was coming late by exactly the same half beat every time, as regular as the drip from Torvin's pump into its trough.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

He went back to his mark and stood there and breathed, four and four. Across the circle Dessa was counting too; he could see her lips. Four in. Four out. Her eyes were on his face, and not on anything else, and they did not count.

*It keeps time like a clock,* he thought. *Real ones don't.*

But he could not be sure. That was the trouble with it. He had seen the foot with his own eyes five times in the first exchange, and he knew what he had seen, and the only thing against it was a feeling about time. It might be that she had got worse at it over the winter, not better. It might be that she was tired before she started, from something he did not know about. He had come into the circle with a page and a half on Feryn and won nothing with it. He had come into this one with four pages on Dessa from the autumn, and here was the thing on page three, as plain as print, and he did not trust it.

*Test it,* he thought. *Cheaply. Then you'll know.*

"Begin."

He went in a half step at the late foot and stopped. It was a feint, and a small one, the kind that cost nothing if it was wrong. Dessa did not move. She never moved for a feint; feints stopped working on her after three, and this was the first, and it had not worked either. The foot came down late, by its exact half beat, a pace away from him.

He feinted again, from the other side. The foot came down late.

So he went.

He went in on the third, properly, with his weight in it, inside her right arm on the late foot exactly as he had gone in in the autumn. He went in at the place where for half a beat she would not be set.

She was set.

The foot was down. It had been down before he started. The whole of her frame was standing there complete, and his strike came in and met her right forearm as it came up, and the forearm was not a forearm. It was a gatepost. The sound of it went across the yard like a mallet on a stump, and the jar of it went up his arm into his shoulder. Before he could take the arm back, the other half of her was already moving, low, and he saw it come and could not get out of its way: the long reclaiming arc of her sweep. It was the one she used only to take back ground somebody had taken from her, and she had given him this ground on purpose so that she could take it back.

It took his legs.

He went down on his left side, all of it at once, and the ground came up into his left hip with the whole weight of him behind it. It was the hip that ached every night after the step. For a moment there was nothing in the world but that hip, white and huge and ringing, like a bell somebody had hit with an iron bar.

He heard the yard from a long way off. He heard Renn shout something.

He did not stay down. He had learned that from Sarel, and from Lira's falls column, and from eight seconds in the sleet. He got his right hand under him, and then his knee, and then he was up, with the hip ringing and his breath coming short. Vell had not begun to count, because he had not stopped moving. Dessa had gone back to her mark. She was standing on it, counting.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

He walked back to his mark. He did it without limping, because the yard was watching, and then on the mark he let the left leg take less than half his weight, because Dessa was watching too and she had seen him go down on it, and there was no point pretending to her.

He stood there and breathed, and the understanding came up through the hip like cold through a boot.

She had grown it for him.

She had sat on the third bench all winter and watched him find tells. She had watched him go in on Renn's heel, and Talis's hum, and the Fenrow man's knee, and the dip in the dyers' boy's shield. She knew what he did, because it was what he always did: he found the thing that came before the thing, and he went in on it. So she had given him one. She had taken her own old late foot, the one he had beaten her with, and she had made it again on purpose, and put it on a clock, and shown it to him five times in the first exchange so that he would believe it in the second.

The old man at the top of the lane had said it in the winter, in his gate, with his sleeves rolled down. *Now they'll plan for you.*

He had thought that meant somebody else.

*All right,* he thought. *Not the foot. Never the foot. What else?*

"Begin."

Dessa came off her mark.

That was new, and he felt the yard feel it. She did not rush. She did not do anything as foolish as rush. But she stepped toward him, one pace, and then another, with her frame coming round with her and settling at every step, and her eyes on his face. She had made him come to her in the second exchange, and in the third she meant to come to him.

He looked for anything true. He looked at her hips, at her shoulders, at her hands low by her belt, at the grit lying flat round her boots. Everything was set. Everything was where it was meant to be. He saw a place where her right side might open if she came one more pace, and he got ready to go into it.

Before he went, her frame came round and closed it.

He circled. He found another place, low on the left, and got ready.

Before he went, she closed that too.

He could not understand it. She was not reading his feet; his feet had not moved. She was not reading his hands. Twice he had found a way in and got ready to take it, and twice, a hair before he went, she had already been there, set, waiting for him, as though he had sent her a note.

The third time he went anyway. He knew as he went that he ought not to. He went because twice was enough, and he wanted to know, and he was fourteen and his hip was ringing and he was tired of being met.

She met him.

He did not see the forearm come. He saw the place he was going into close, and then there was a flat enormous shock across the right side of his face, under the eye. His head went round with it. The yard went white, and then red, and then came back a little smaller than it had been, and with one side of it full of water.

He did not go down. He did not know afterward how he did not. He found that he was standing a pace back from where he had been, with his guard up and his right eye streaming. The whole cheekbone under it was already beginning to feel too big, like a plum that somebody had pressed into his face.

"End of the exchange," said Vell. Her voice was very level.

He went back to his mark.

On the way there, through the one clear eye, he saw Lira at the barrel. She was not looking at the circle. She was looking straight at him, and as he looked at her she lifted one hand off the top of the rope and touched her own chin with two fingers, once, and put the hand back.

He stood on his mark and the yard roared and he did not hear it.

*The nod.*

Of course it was. He had spent eight days in the grey half trying to keep it in, and been told *there* until Lira stopped saying it. He had been told on Monday, in so many words, that it came back when he was tired and came back when he was hit, and that Dessa had watched him be tired and hit in every bout he had fought since the autumn. Twice in the third exchange he had found his way in and got ready to take it, and both times, a hair before he went, his chin had gone down, because that was what his chin did when he had made up his mind. And Dessa had been watching his chin. She had not needed his feet. She had the thing that came before the thing.

*Whatever you do twice against her,* said Doss, at the back of his head, *you've done once too often.*

He had done it fifty times.

He breathed. Four and four. The catch was in his ribs, and his hip rang every time his weight shifted, and his right eye was closing by itself, slowly, like a door on a weight. Across the circle Dessa stood on her mark and counted, and he could see her lips, and it was not four and four any more. It was six and six. She was tired. Re-forming that frame at every step through the whole of the third exchange had cost her, as it had cost her in the autumn, and as it always cost Stone, because a frame had to be built and building took breath.

He could not take the nod out. Not in a breath on his mark, not after eight days of failing to. It lived in the place where he made up his mind, and he could not stop making up his mind.

He thought about baiting her with it. He could nod and not go, and nod and not go, and then go on the third. He put that down almost as soon as he had picked it up. That was a trick for somebody who would bite. Dessa had never bitten anything in her life. She would watch him nod and not go, and she would wait, and she would go on waiting until he gave her the real one, because she was a patience fighter. A patience fighter could outwait anything except nothing.

*Except nothing.*

He stood quite still on his mark.

The nod came when he made up his mind. So he would make up his mind now. Here, on the mark, before Vell said *begin*, all of it at once, when, where and how, so that there would be nothing left to make up in the exchange itself, nothing to decide and so nothing to nod at. He would decide now and then not decide again.

When, then. Not her foot. The foot was a clock she had built for him. He thought of Corvane in her brick yard, walking slowly, with the stone in the middle of her. *Watch what doesn't move.* Stone did not hold anything heavy and still in the middle of it, as Pressure did. But Stone had to build its frame, as Pressure had to build its weight, and the building took breath. He had watched it take breath since the autumn. Every time Dessa's frame came round and settled, she held her breath while it set. That was what her count was for. It was not a tell, exactly. It was a cost, and she paid it every time she moved, and she could no more hide it than he could hide his hip.

*When her breath stops.*

Where, then. On the left. She favoured the left side when she was tired, and she was tired.

How. The one strike he owned, from the hip, inside the forearm.

That was the whole of it. He held it up and looked at it, and it was small and plain and there was nothing in it to be proud of. He set it down inside himself and shut the lid on it.

Then he let everything else go. He did it as Lira had taught him in the six weeks, at the post, forty times, with a stroke of chalk for each, until the chalk had not been needed any more. Every other plan, every other way in, every other place he might have looked: *gone*. He did not say it aloud. He did not need to now.

He looked at the middle of Dessa, below the ribs and above the belt, where her breath went in and out, and at nothing else.

"Begin."

Dessa waited for him.

She stood on her mark with her frame set and her hands low and her eyes on his chin, and waited for it to go down. He stood on his and looked at her middle, and did not decide anything, because there was nothing left to decide.

Nobody moved.

He felt the yard begin to notice it. It went quieter, and then quieter than that. The boys on the wall stopped kicking their heels against the bricks. Somewhere along the sunny side Renn began to shout something and stopped in the middle of it. It went on. He had never stood this long in a circle without moving in his life, and neither, he thought, had Dessa, because nobody had ever stood in front of her and given her nothing to wait for.

Her eyes went from his chin to his face, and back to his chin.

Her breath went in and out. Six and six.

Then she did what she had done in the third exchange, because it was the only thing left to her if he would not come. She came to him.

She stepped. Her frame came round with her and settled, and as it settled her breath stopped, and he saw it stop: the cloth of her shirt going still for a moment over the middle of her while the frame set. He did not go. It was on the right, and he had decided the left.

She stepped again, the left foot leading, and the frame came round, and her breath stopped.

Her back foot came down late.

It was not the clock. It was the real one. It was a little more than half a beat and a little uneven, and the left side of her came down after the rest of her, tired, the way a body is tired. He did not see it and then decide. There was no deciding left in him to do. The going had been decided on his mark a minute before, and it was already happening. He went in under her left forearm as it began to come round, low, inside it, with his bad hip screaming and his good eye open, and he brought the one strike he owned up from the hip, short and straight, and stopped it a finger's breadth from the hinge of her jaw.

He held it there.

Dessa stood quite still with his fist beside her jaw and her left forearm a hand too late across the place where he had been, and her breath still stopped inside her. Then she let it go, slowly, all of it, and he felt it go past his wrist.

"Called," said Vell.

The small pause.

"Unrated."

---

The yard came back all at once, from every side, so loud that he felt it in his ribs before he heard it. It went on round him, and he stayed where he was, fist still up, right eye shut, and could not, for a moment, remember how to put his arm down.

Dessa put it down for him. She took his wrist in her hand, quite gently, and moved it away from her jaw, and let it go. Then she turned and walked to the water bucket.

He walked to the table, because you walked to the table, and stood in front of it with his left leg taking as little as it could and the yard roaring at his back. Vell was writing. She wrote one line, and blotted it, and did not look up.

"Result stands as called," said Vell. "Unrated, over Dessa, Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Fourth exchange. Called. Witnessed and entered."

There was nothing more in the book. He had not expected there to be.

Dessa was at the water bucket. When he got there she had already filled the tin cup, and she held it out to him first, before she drank, as she gave it to everybody first. He took it with his right hand and drank, and the water was very cold and ran down his chin, and he gave it back, and she filled it again for herself.

"You nod," said Dessa.

"I know. I'm told."

"Twice in the third. I had you both times." She drank. "Not at the end."

"No."

She looked at him over the cup with the small white scar pale through her eyebrow. She did not look like somebody who had lost. She looked like somebody checking another person's joinery and finding a joint she had not seen made before.

"How?"

He thought about not telling her. Then he thought that she had come and asked for this bout herself, on her own clock, and had sat on a bench all winter to earn it, and had put her own hand flat on the dirt in front of the whole yard to tell him so. He thought she was owed it.

"I decided on my mark," he said. "All of it. Before Vell said begin. So when I went there wasn't anything left in me to nod at."

Dessa considered that for a long time, with the cup in her hand.

"That's a thing you can do once," she said.

"I know."

"Next time I'll watch your mark." She tipped the last of the water out on the dirt by the bucket, where it went dark and then pale. "Your eye's going. Put something cold on it before it shuts, or you'll be a week getting it open." She hung the cup on its nail. "Now we've talked."

Then she went to the gate in the wall, and out, without stopping to speak to anybody, as she always did.

Lira was waiting at the barrel. She had a cloth out of the canvas bag, already wet from the flask, and she did not say anything. She put it in his hand and pushed his hand up against his eye and held it there, with her own hand over his, until he had the idea.

"Don't move," she said.

He did not move. He stood by the barrel with the cold cloth on his face and the noise of the yard going on round him, Renn shouting that he had taught that boy everything he knew and Marrow paying out with his clean hands, and he let her look at the rest of him. She looked at the hip without touching it, and at the way he was standing off it, and at the forearm, which was still yellow. Then she looked at his face, the half of it she could see.

"You didn't nod," she said.

"No."

"Not once. In the fourth. I was watching your chin the whole time, and it didn't move." Something went across her face that she did not let get very far. "I've never seen you not do it in a bout. Not since the autumn."

"I decided on the mark."

"I heard. I was close enough." She took her hand away from his and let him hold the cloth himself. "That's a good thing. It's a very good thing, and Dessa's right, it'll work once, and I'll want to talk about it in the grey half until you're sick of it." She looked at the hip again. "But not this week."

"Lira—"

"A week," said Lira. "Nothing. No step, no box, no post. You can come and sit on the wall and eat the bread, and that's all, and if you ask for the step I'll put a stroke on the post and write *idiot* beside it." She picked up his coat from the top of the barrel and held it for him. "I mean it. You went down on that hip like a sack off a cart. I heard it from here."

He put his arms in the coat, carefully. It took two tries.

"A week," he said.

"A week." She did the top button for him, as she had once before. "Same time tomorrow. For the bread."

At the top of the lane of yards, on the way home in the dusk, the old man was standing in his gate.

His sleeves were rolled down against the evening. He was not watching the woman with the shoulder, because she had gone home; the yard behind him was empty, with its post and its square of dirt going blue in the last of the light. He was watching the lane. He watched Cael come up it, slowly, with Lira on his right side and his left leg going carefully and the cloth held against his face, and he did not look away when Cael got near, as he usually did.

"Four," said the old man.

"Yes."

The old man looked at the way he was walking. He looked at it for some time, as he had looked at the woman with the shoulder doing her fourth position thirty times in a morning.

"You've coin now," he said. "From the purses."

"Some."

"Saturday." The old man shifted his weight, very slightly, from one foot to the other, as he did. "When you can come up this lane without holding the wall. Bring a coin for yard time." He looked at Lira, briefly, and back at Cael. "Not her. You."

Then the stillness came down over him like a lid, and he went back to watching the empty lane, and Cael understood that he had been given all of it.

He paid the bout into the Log that night by the bead of Yeni's lamp, with the cloth on his eye and one-eyed, which made the lines go uphill.

*Sixteenth. Dessa, Copper Rank 5, Stone. Won, fourth exchange, called.*

*First: nothing struck. Her late back foot, five times, by exactly the same half beat. Too exact. A real tell wanders. Didn't take it.*

*Second: feinted twice at it. Went on the third. The foot was a clock she'd built for me. Gatepost, sweep. Down on the left hip.*

*Third: she came to me. Twice she was set before I went. Went anyway and took the forearm under the right eye. Lira touched her chin. The nod. Dessa had it all along.*

*Fourth: decided on the mark, all of it. When her breath stops, on the left, the one strike. Then nothing left to decide. No nod. She waited, then came. Her real foot, tired, late. Called.*

He looked at that. Then he wrote the ruling.

*She read me back. My nod is my late foot. She's had all winter to find it and she found it, and I've had all winter to lose it and I didn't. Deciding on the mark worked once. Dessa says once. She's right.*

He turned the book over, to the back, and sat with the pencil over a clean line for some time.

*No instance,* he wrote at last. *The step didn't come. I didn't ask for it. Everything in it was mine, and most of what was mine was wrong until the fourth.*

He looked at that, and then wrote one more line under it, smaller, because it was true and because he did not think he would believe it later unless it was written.

*It's the first time I've won anything that I didn't decide while I was doing it.*

On Tuesday he went down to the market for the bread, because Lira had said he could sit on the wall and eat it, and somebody had to buy it.

He went slowly. The hip had stiffened on Monday into something like a plank and was beginning, very slowly, to soften again. His right eye had shut on Sunday night and opened again on Monday afternoon to a slit, and was open now about halfway, with the whole of the cheek under it gone a dark shining colour like a ripe plum. He had looked at it once in the curved glass of one of the lantern woman's broken lanterns and decided not to look again.

People looked at it. He had expected that. What he had not expected was the way they looked at it, which was not the way people in Denvash had looked at him after Weaver's Row. Nobody stepped back. Nobody's eyes went to the door. A man at the cheese stall with the lantern that was never lit looked at the eye, and then at the way he was walking, and said, "Dessa, was it?" and when Cael said yes, the man nodded as if that settled a small question he had been wondering about since Sunday, and went back to his cheese.

At the top of the second row the bread woman had already taken two loaves out from underneath before he got there. She held them out across her trestle, and he gave her the coin, and she looked at his face.

"That's a good one," she said, with professional interest, as if it were a loaf. "My boy came home with one like that once. Off a cart shaft." She put the coin away. "You'll want a raw onion on it. Not butter."

"Not butter," he agreed.

She said something else then, while a cart went by behind him on the cobbles with a load of empty barrels banging together in its bed. She said it quickly, with her chin up, as people say a thing they have decided to say and want done. He caught the end of it, which was *—and you can stop calling me nothing, you've been coming to this table since the autumn*, and he understood, too late, with the barrels still banging, that the beginning of it had been her name.

He had not heard it.

He stood there with the two loaves and thought about asking her to say it again, and saw that it was already too late. She had said it once, with her chin up, and she was turning to the next person, and asking her now would be telling her he had not been listening. He had been listening. It was the barrels.

So he said *thank you*, as warmly as he could make it come out of half a face, and went on.

At the bottom of the row, by the paper stall, a man he did not know was buying string. He was a carter, by his hands and his boots, and he looked at Cael's eye and then at Cael, with the frank interest of a stranger in a place where everybody else already knew.

"You're the boy," said the carter. "From the Cinder House. They were talking about you at the tollhouse." He weighed the string in his hand. "What Path have you got, then? The way they tell it, you go round people like water."

Cael had been asked before. He had been asked by Dessa, and by Feryn, and by a woman with a stick in a brick yard. He had said the word to the ones who had earned it. This was a man buying string.

"Unclassified," said Cael.

The carter frowned.

"That's not a Path. That's a shrug."

"It's the one they gave me," said Cael, mildly. The carter looked at him a moment longer, as if deciding whether he was being made fun of, and then shrugged himself, and paid for his string, and went off up the row with it over his shoulder.

Cael stood by the paper stall with the loaves under his arm and found that he was very nearly laughing, which hurt the cheek.

*Unclassified.* It was the one word in the whole Handbook that he had sat across a desk from an assessor five days ago and proved, from the definitions, three pages back, that he was not. He had a primary classification. He had more of one than anybody in the district. It was in the Supplementary Register under its own statute, with its incidence count beside it, and he had won the right to keep it on the strength of exactly that.

And it was the word that made a man buying string stop asking.

He thought that Ilsev would have liked that, and then thought that she would not have, and then thought that she would have written it down.

He pointed at the pears.

*Carry what you've got. Don't let it carry you. (The fruit woman, h.)*

He looked at it for a while. Then he put a small box round it, not because it was a guess, but because it was the only way he had of marking a thing as his.

# Chapter 52 — Economy

The first barges of the spring came down on Thursday, all at once, as they did every year, because the ice had gone off the upper river in a single night and every boat that had been frozen in since the autumn had cast off the next morning.

He heard them before he saw them. He was sitting on the post at the top of the fish steps in the middle of the morning with a pear, because his hip had got him that far and he had decided to let it rest before asking it for the rest of the way. The sound came up the river from round the bend above the lower bridge: shouting, and the knock of poles, and the long groan of a hull against stone. Then the first of them came round the bend low in the water, and another behind it, and another, and the long quay below the steps filled up with them in the space of an hour. There were grain barges and wool barges and barges loaded with timber and stone and crates and sacks and a cow, and the quay filled with carters and porters and men with lists, and everybody in the district who was waiting for something that had been stuck up the river all winter came down the steps at once to find out whether it had come.

The errand boys were at the bottom of the steps in the middle of it.

He knew them both. Everybody knew them both. They worked the east lanes and the four lanes, carrying things for people who would rather pay a boy than carry them, and they had hated each other since they were about six, and they had spent the whole winter betting on Cael against each other with money neither of them could afford. The one in the red cap had a fistful of paper tickets, and so did the one whose brother was in the carters'. They were standing toe to toe at the bottom of the steps in a heap of parcels and arguing, while the parcels went on arriving round them.

"You take the dyers'," said Red Cap.

"*You* take the dyers'. I took the dyers' at the turn of the year."

"You took one parcel to the dyers' at the turn of the year."

"It was a heavy one."

A porter put down another sack beside them, and somebody's boy came running down the steps with a list of four more, and a woman with a basket leaned over the rail at the top and called down to ask whether the thread from upriver had come yet, because she had been promised it by Fourthweek. Red Cap looked at the heap and at his tickets and at the other boy, and his face did a thing that Cael recognized. It was the face of somebody who has been given more to carry than two arms will hold and is choosing to be angry about it rather than frightened.

Cael got down off the post.

He went down the steps one at a time with his hand on the rail. By the time he reached the bottom both boys had stopped arguing to watch him come, Red Cap with interest and the other one with deep suspicion.

"Give me the tickets," said Cael.

"Why?"

"Because you've got thirty-one between you, and you've each been to the dyers' twice already this morning, from two different directions, and passed each other in the lane both times." He held out his hand. "I watched you from the post."

The two boys looked at each other.

It was the other one who gave in first, which surprised Cael, because he had thought it would be Red Cap. He held out his fistful of tickets as though he were handing over a dog that had bitten him, and Red Cap, seeing it done, put his own on top.

Cael sat down on the bottom step with the tickets on his knee and sorted them.

He had walked every lane of the district a hundred times since the autumn, the way he walked everything, counting. He knew where the dyers' sheds were and where the four lanes went up from the well and how the cut ran crooked between the cooper's wall and the bucket-maker's back. He had the nut boy's map in his head, and the long walk to the brickworks, and the yard lane and the triangle and the east road out past the scaffolds. He sorted the tickets the way he would have sorted Hesk's springs, by where they had to go, and then into two piles, so that one pile went east and up and round through the four lanes, and the other went west along the river and up through the cut, and the two piles never crossed the same street twice.

It took him a quarter of an hour. When he had done it he gave Red Cap the east pile and the other one the west.

"Do them in that order," he said. "Top to bottom. Don't swap. If you swap you'll meet each other at the well and argue, and you'll lose half an hour."

"We don't argue," said Red Cap.

"You've been arguing since I sat down on the post."

Red Cap considered that. Then he looked at his pile, and at the other boy's pile, and at the heap of parcels, which had grown again.

"What about the little ones?" he said. "There's eleven little ones that go round here. The mender. The lantern woman. Torvin's lane."

"I'll take the little ones."

"You can't walk."

"I can walk slowly. They're little."

So he took the little ones. He put them in his coat and in a sack the cooper lent him, and carried them up the steps and along the river workshops and up Torvin's lane, slowly, with the hip complaining at every step and then, after a while, complaining less. He took a parcel of spectacles to the mender, who looked at them and then at Cael and said *Wednesday* as if Cael had asked him something, and a box of lamp wicks to the lantern woman, and a jar of something that smelled of pine to a house in the lane where a cat watched him from a sill. He took the thread up to the woman who had leaned over the rail, and she was so glad to have it that she gave him an egg.

When he got back to the steps in the afternoon the heap was gone.

The boys came back at dusk, one from the east and one from the west, within a few minutes of each other, which they both noticed and neither mentioned. They sat down on the bottom step on either side of Cael with their pockets full and counted their coins out on the stone between their boots, in two piles, very carefully, each watching the other's.

"Most I ever made in a day," said the other one at last, sounding as though it hurt him to say so.

"Most *I* ever made in a day," said Red Cap.

They looked at each other over the two piles.

"We could do it like that again," said Red Cap. "Tomorrow. With the lists."

"I suppose we could."

"Not because I want to."

"No. Nor me."

Then Red Cap pushed a few coins out of his pile toward Cael, and the other boy, after a moment, did the same from his, exactly the same number, and Cael pushed them both back.

"I didn't carry anything that mattered," he said.

They looked at him as if he had said something in another language. Then they looked at each other again, and got up without a word, and went off together up the steps toward the pie man's red lantern. They came back with a pie, which they gave him, and sat down again on either side of him on the step and watched him eat it.

"We've bet on you again," said Red Cap, when he had finished. "For whenever's next."

"Which way?"

"Same as always. Me for you, him against."

"I'm against on principle," said the other one, with dignity. "Somebody's got to be."

Cael looked at the two of them in the last of the light, sitting on the step a little closer together than people who hated each other had any need to sit, with the empty quay below and the barges riding at their ropes. He thought that they had probably not hated each other since they were about seven. He thought that it would be a very bad idea to tell them so.

At supper that night the boots woman lost her bun.

She had bet Torvin's wife a bun on Dessa. She paid it with great ceremony at the end of the meal, getting up from her place on the bench in her laced boots and walking round the table to the range and presenting it on a plate, with a small bow, as if it were the key to a city. Torvin's wife took it without turning round from the pot and put it on the shelf.

"That's three you've lost on him," said Torvin's wife.

"Four," said the boots woman, sitting down again. "I lost one on the Renn boy in the autumn as well, but you were so pleased with yourself that day you forgot to collect." She looked across the table at Cael's eye, which was open properly now, but still the colour of a plum going over. "I'll bet against you again next time. Somebody in this house ought to."

"Why?"

"So that somebody in this house is happy whichever way it goes." She reached for the bread. "If you lose, I win a bun. If you win, I lose a bun and she's pleased, and I'm pleased she's pleased, because she's easier to live with." She buttered the bread with great care. "I've been doing it all winter. It's the only bet in the district you can't lose."

One of the brothers laughed so suddenly that he choked on his stew, and the other brother hit him on the back, harder than was strictly necessary, and they began to argue about whether he had been choking or only pretending.

"Sella," said Torvin's wife, "get your boots off my bench."

The boots woman took her boots off the bench, where she had put them up when she sat down, and set them on the floor. She did it with the air of somebody who has been asked to do it every night for some years and has never once been asked in a voice that meant it.

Cael looked at her.

He had lived in this house since the autumn. He had eaten at this table perhaps a hundred and fifty times, with her across it in her boots, and had listened to her tell the district he was seven feet tall and ate nails, and had watched her lose buns to Torvin's wife on his account. He had never once heard her name. He had not asked it. It had come to feel, after the first fortnight, too late to ask.

*Sella.*

She saw him looking, and looked back, with her bread halfway to her mouth.

"What?"

"I didn't know your name."

"Nobody does. She's the only one who uses it, and only when my boots are on the bench." Sella ate the bread. "Don't spread it about. I've got a reputation."

"For what?"

"For being the woman who sleeps in her boots." She considered him gravely. "It's a very good reputation. People are polite to you. They think you might have to leave in a hurry."

"Why do you?"

"Sleep in them?" Sella looked down at the boots on the floor beside the bench, laced to the top. "In case."

"In case of what?"

"If I knew that," said Sella, "I'd take them off."

He laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, and it pulled at the cheek under the eye, and he put his hand up to hold the cheek and went on laughing anyway. Across the table Yeni, who never laughed at anything, looked down at her sewing with the corner of her mouth gone in, which was as near as Yeni came.

Torvin had said nothing all meal. He sat at the head of the table and ate his stew in the way he did everything, without hurry, and when the bowl was empty he put the spoon down in it, very precisely, as Doss laid down his pipe. Then he looked down the table at Cael with the look he gave a coin he had already counted.

"Purse on Sunday," he said.

"Yes."

"And the one before Feryn, and the one before that." Torvin did not ask how much. He would have known to the half-mark, Cael thought, from the fish steps and the bread woman and the boots woman, without ever asking anybody. "Most houses, when a boarder starts winning purses, the rent goes up on him. It's only sense. He can pay it."

The table went quiet. Even the brothers stopped.

"Not this house," said Torvin. He picked up his spoon again, and looked at it, and put it down. "Rent's what it was. It stays what it was. Whatever they pay you over there." He looked at the range, where his wife had stopped stirring, and then back down the table. "There. Now you'll not lie awake waiting for it."

Cael did not know what to say. He sat with his hands round his bowl.

"Thank you, Torvin."

Torvin looked at him as though he had been handed a coin with the wrong head on it.

"It's sums," he said. "A boarder who pays on the day and keeps his fighting in the street is worth two who pay more and leave owing." He picked up the bowl and stood, and then stood there a moment longer than he needed to, holding it. "I said to her, in the autumn. The night you came. Boy sat on my step asking what a week came to by the night, as if the answer might go for his throat." He looked at the bowl. "I said, that one'll be gone by the turn of the month." He considered the bowl a while longer. "I was out by a winter."

He took the bowl to the basin and rinsed it and went up the stairs, and after a while *Lamps* came down through the floorboards, a little earlier than usual.

Torvin's wife took the bun off the shelf and cut it in two and put half of it in front of Cael without a word, and gave the other half to Sella.

On Saturday he went up the lane of yards without holding the wall.

It was nearly true. He held it once, near the top, where the lane went steep for a few paces, and he took his hand away again so quickly that he did not think it counted. The woman at the bottom of the lane who kept the chalk board with the yard prices on it watched him go past and did not ask him for anything, because she could see where he was going.

The old man was standing in his gate.

He did not say anything. He stood aside, and Cael went past him into the yard, which he had looked into from the lane perhaps three hundred times since the autumn and had never once stood inside. It was a square of hard dirt, swept, with high walls on three sides and the lane on the fourth, and a thick post in the middle worn smooth and grey to the height of a man's chest. The woman with the shoulder was at the far end in her six positions. She finished the sixth as Cael came in, and held it, and let it go, and looked at the old man. He nodded at the gate. She took her coat off a nail and went out past them without a word, rubbing the shoulder.

Cael took the coin out of his pocket and held it out.

The old man looked at it, and took it, and turned it over once in his fingers and put it on the top of the post, where it sat on the grey wood like a small bright eye.

"Yard time's yard time," he said. "Stand there."

Cael stood there, in the middle, by the post.

The old man came and stood in front of him, a pace away, in his plain stance with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his hands at his sides. He was lean and grey and somewhere past sixty, and close to, his eyes were exactly as they had looked from the lane in the autumn, pale and steady and not at all old.

"Push me," he said.

Cael put both hands on the old man's chest and pushed.

Nothing happened. That was the first thing, and it was not what he had expected. He had expected to push a wall, or a door, or a man who pushed back. The old man was none of those. Cael's hands went in a little, as they would against anybody, and then the old man was simply there, as the post was there, not pushing back at all, not doing anything that Cael could see, and Cael's push went into him and down and was gone.

He pushed harder. The hip woke and said something sharp. He set his feet and put his back into it, and his breath went short, and the old man stood there with his hands at his sides and his eyes on Cael's face and did not move.

"Stop," said the old man.

Cael stopped. He stood with his hands still on the old man's chest, breathing hard.

"What did that cost you?"

"Breath. The hip."

"And me?"

Cael looked at him. The old man was breathing exactly as he had been breathing in the gate.

"Nothing."

"Nothing." The old man nodded. "You pushed me with all of you. Hands, arms, back, legs, the bad hip, the good one, your face. I could see you pushing with your face." He did not smile, but something moved at the corner of his eye. "I stood with what was needed. That's all. Bones on bones. Knees not locked, not bent. Nothing held that didn't have to be held." He shifted his weight, very slightly, from one foot to the other, the way he always did, and Cael saw for the first time what it was for. "It isn't anything. Anybody can stand like this. It takes about thirty years."

He took Cael's hands off his chest and let them go.

"Now I'll push you."

He put one finger on Cael's breastbone. Just one. He leaned on it, not very hard, and Cael went back half a step, and caught himself, and the hip rang.

"Again," said the old man.

He leaned again. Cael set himself against it this time, everything, the way he had set himself against Dessa's frame, and the finger pushed, and he held, and his whole body shook with holding, and the old man took the finger away and Cael nearly fell forward into the space where it had been.

"You see," said the old man.

"I held it."

"You held it with everything you've got, against one finger." The old man looked at him with the flat level look Vell used on a horse. "What's left for the next one?"

Cael did not answer, because he did not have to.

"Sunday," said the old man. "She took your legs in the second. You got up." He nodded at the dirt. "Show me how you got up."

So Cael lay down on his left side in the old man's yard, carefully, on the bad hip, and got up the way he had got up on Sunday, all at once: hand, knee, up, fast, with his breath gone and the hip shouting.

The old man watched it.

"Like a man jumping out of a bed that's on fire," he said. "Every time. I've watched you do it all winter. You think the yard's counting. It isn't. Vell's not counting till you stop moving, and while you're moving you've got time." He crouched down, slowly, by the post, with his forearms on his knees. "You spent half a breath getting up off that dirt on Sunday. Then you spent the rest of the breath on your mark standing on the good leg, so she wouldn't see the bad one. She'd seen it go down. Everybody in the yard had." He looked at Cael. "You paid for it twice. Once to get up fast, and once to hide a thing that was already seen. That's the most expensive kind of thing there is to pay for."

"Then how?"

"Get down."

Cael got down.

"Now get up," said the old man, "and let the ground pay for it."

He showed him. He did not get down himself; he only pointed, with one finger, from where he crouched. Roll off the bad side first, onto the back, so the hip was not under anything. Then onto the good knee, slowly, with the hand flat beside it on the ground, so the ground took the weight and not the arm. Then the other foot under, and then up, not with the legs pushing but with the back straightening on top of them, one piece on the next, the way you stack bricks. It took a great deal longer than the bed on fire. When Cael was standing at the end of it, he found that he still had his breath.

"Again," said the old man.

He did it again, and then again. The fourth time the old man said nothing. The fifth time he said, "Your shoulders are early," and the sixth time he said nothing, and the seventh time he stood up out of his crouch, slowly, with one hand on the post.

"You'll lose an eye's worth again," he said. "And a hip's worth, and worse. You've a long road and it's all uphill." He took the coin off the top of the post and put it in his pocket. "The ones that last on it aren't the ones who've got the most. They're the ones who spend least. Spend nothing you don't have to. Not getting up, not hiding a limp, not holding off one finger." He looked at Cael. "Then when you do have to spend, you'll have it."

Cael stood in the middle of the yard by the post and breathed, and found that it was the first time since Sunday that his hip did not hurt when he stood still.

He did not ask the old man where he had learned it. He did not ask how long thirty years had been, or where they had been spent, or what the man had stood against for them, with his bones on his bones and nothing held. It came to his tongue, and he felt the old man see it come, and he let it go back down. Some people gave you the present and kept everything before it. He had decided a long time ago that this was a fair trade, and he made it again now.

"Thank you," he said.

"I'm not being kind." The old man went back to his corner in the shade of the wall and stood there, and the stillness came down over him. "I'm telling you where your breath's going. Same time next Saturday, if you want it. Bring a coin."

Hesk's answer to the Ilsev letter came on Saturday too, propped against the salt crock when he came in from the yard lane.

It was very short. He read it on the low wall by the pump.

*Cael,*

*I have it. I've read it twice and I'll read it again tonight.*

*Your assessor sounds like the kind who checks her own sums, and then checks them again because she doesn't trust the first check. I'm glad of her. I'm glad of the bone knife. I'd have liked to see the pages come open.*

*Your question I'm keeping. I haven't an answer to it yet that I'd put my name under, and I won't send you one I wouldn't. When I have one, I'll write.*

*Win your Sunday. Eat.*

*— H.*

He showed it to Lira the next evening, on the low wall at the side of the Cinder House yard, after the card, while the pie boy raked the circle and the crows went up off the eaves one at a time into the dusk.

She read it twice, as she read everything of Hesk's that he showed her, and gave it back to him, and made a small cross noise in her throat.

"He's keeping it," she said. "Of course he is. You're as bad as each other. Neither of you will put a thing down on paper till you could sign it in front of a magistrate."

"He doesn't want to send me a wrong answer."

"I'd send you a wrong one. At least you'd have something to argue with." She took the bread out of her bag, the end of the morning's loaf, and tore it, and gave him the larger half. "What was the question? You never said. You said he'd answer it."

He told her. He had not meant to, quite; it had felt like a thing between him and Hesk. But she had asked, and he found that he did not have a reason not to that he would have been willing to say out loud.

Lira did not answer for a while. She sat with the bread in her hand and watched the pie boy rake out the place where the kiln boy had gone down that afternoon, slowly, stroke by stroke, until the dirt was level.

"I don't know," she said at last. "I've been trying to. I've been trying since the roof." She turned the bread over. "Do you want to know what I do at night? Under the slope, with the candle out?"

He waited.

"I try to work out who." She did not look at him. "Who'd put a mark on you. Somebody who's never seen you, I'd think, or not close. Who'd do it in the same hour somebody started treating you like anybody else." She shook her head. "I can't. I haven't got anything to work it out with. It's like trying to read a build in the dark. So I lie there, and I think about my tin."

"Your tin?"

"There's enough in it now." She said it quite flatly. "For a cart. Fenrow, or further. Somewhere nobody's ever heard of the Cinder House, or you, or any of it. I could go tomorrow, and I'd never have to lie awake again trying to see somebody I can't see." She pulled a piece off the bread and did not eat it. "I worked that out about a week ago. I've been carrying it about since."

He sat very still on the wall.

"And then I thought about it properly," said Lira, "the way you'd want me to. Both sides, all the columns. And I found I'd rather not know and be here." She ate the piece of bread. "So I've stopped lying awake about it. Mostly. I've put the tin back in my pocket."

He opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Lira. "Don't say anything. If you say anything I'll have to answer it, and I haven't got an answer, I've only got where I've decided to stand." She looked at the circle. "It's a strange thing. I've never in my life trusted anything I couldn't explain. Not a teacher, not a Path, not a step. At the academy I wouldn't do the middle of the deflection till somebody could tell me why it was there, and nobody could, so I never did." She almost smiled. "And I've got no explanation for you at all. Two notices and a bag of guesses. I'm staying anyway. I noticed I'd decided it before I noticed I was deciding."

He did not say anything, because she had asked him not to, and because he did not think there was anything to say that would be the right size. It went into him slowly, the way the cold water went into his arm in the mornings, and stayed.

"I trust Dessa's water cup," he said at last, which was not at all what he had meant to say.

Lira laughed, short and surprised, and put her hand over her mouth.

"That's it? That's all you've got?"

"It's a good cup."

"It's a tin cup on a nail." But she was still laughing, quietly, with her shoulders. "All right. You trust me and a tin cup and an old man with a coin on a post. That's three. That's a column." She stood up and dusted her trousers and held her hand down to him, on his good side. "It's Sunday. The week's done. Tomorrow you can ask."

He took her hand and came up off the wall the old man's way: the good knee, the back straightening on top of it, one piece on the next. Lira watched him do it, and her eyebrows went up.

"Who taught you that?"

"Somebody with a post."

She looked at him a moment longer, and did not ask, and they walked out through the gate together into the lane.

That night he wrote to Hesk about the district.

He did not write about Sunday, except to say that he had won, in the fourth, and that the eye was going down. He wrote about the barrels banging on the cobbles while the bread woman told him her name, and how he would never now know it, and how she had told him to use an onion. He wrote about the fruit woman and the apricots and what she had said. He wrote about the carter buying string and *Unclassified*, because he thought Hesk would like the joke in it. He wrote about thirty-one tickets on a step, and two boys who had stopped hating each other years ago and would be very angry if anybody told them so. He wrote about Torvin rinsing his bowl, and *out by a winter*, and about Sella's boots and why she slept in them. He wrote about a coin on a post.

*I've put down a great many things this winter that I didn't understand,* he wrote at the end. *This letter's the first one I've written where I understand all of it. It's only that there's a lot.*

---

He tried the other one on Tuesday night, after the lamps, in Torvin's yard, because his arm had been able to lift a cup for a week and he had run out of reasons not to.

He went out by the kitchen door when the house was asleep. The yard was very quiet. There was a half moon up over the back wall and the whitewash on the wall opposite had gone silver with it, and the pear tree in the corner had small hard buds on it that had not been there a fortnight ago. The pump stood in the middle like a thin dark man. Its trough was full and still, with the moon lying in it.

He stood with his back to the house and the trough in front of him, so that there was nothing in front of him but water and whitewash and a wall.

Then he went looking.

He knew the way now, a little. He went into the dark place behind his breastbone and found the Wind there first, the leaning thing, the readiness, the shape the step had left. He went past it, carefully. Beyond it there was the other one, which he had felt sitting beside the first since the night after Feryn and had not touched. It did not lean. It was heavier than that, and lower, and quieter: a sort of weight in the dark that seemed to be waiting to be picked up, as a sack waits on a quay.

He picked it up.

It did not come quickly. That was the first thing he learned. The Wind came slowly when he asked, but it came all at once when it came, the way a step does. This did not come all at once. He had to gather it. He felt it begin low in the middle of him, below the ribs and above the belt, in the very place Corvane had carried her stone, and he felt it go on gathering there. It did not go faster when he wanted it to. It took a breath, a whole long breath in and a whole long breath out, the way Hesk took a breath before he lifted something he knew was heavy, before it felt like anything that might go anywhere.

And while it gathered he saw his own shadow on the silver wall.

The moon was behind him and a little to the left, and his shadow lay long and black across the whitewash. He had moved his hands while he gathered, without meaning to, the way Corvane had moved hers in her ring. The shadow's hands moved with them. But the middle of the shadow did not. The middle of it had gone thick, and still, and heavy-looking, as though somebody standing there had swallowed a stone and was carrying it very carefully while pretending not to. Anybody who could read a build would have read it from the far side of the yard. Corvane would have read it at the gate. Feryn would have read it, and finished his sentence about walls, and been somewhere else long before it was ready.

He let it go.

He did not know how to let it go, and it went anyway, forward, the way he was facing. It did not go far, and it did not go hard. It was nothing like Feryn's sky, or even Corvane's hand. But the moon in the trough broke. The whole still face of the water went flat for a moment, pressed down all at once, and then shivered up into small rings that ran out to the edges and came back. Beyond the trough the buds on the pear tree leaned, every twig, away from him, and stood up again. A bucket by the wall moved a finger's width on the flags with a small scraping sound, loud in the quiet.

Then it was gone, and the yard was still, and the moon came back together in the trough.

His arms went heavy.

It came after, not during: a heaviness that came into both arms from the shoulder down and went on coming. It was not pain. It was more as if the arms had been filled with wet sand while he was not looking, all the way to the fingertips. He tried to lift his right hand and it came up slowly, a long way behind the rest of him, and he let it down again. He sat on the low wall by the pump with his hands lying in his lap and looked at them.

It was most of an hour before he could have lifted a cup.

He sat there the whole of it, in the cold, with the moon going over. He did not try again. He thought about Corbin's Shield, which he had read at the mark, and Feryn's build, which he had read at the deciding, and he thought about his own, which he had read on a wall in the moonlight from behind himself. A whole breath. A middle like a sack. And then an hour of arms that would not lift. He thought that if he ever did that in Vell's circle, in front of anybody who had stood in front of Pressure, they would have finished the exchange, and gone to the water bucket, and come back, before his arms were his again.

When he could hold a pencil he went in and up the stairs in the dark and sat on the end of the cot and wrote it down in the back of the Log, by the bead of Yeni's lamp, which was turned a hand's width toward him on the shelf as it always was now.

*The other one. Tried it alone in the yard, facing the trough. Nobody in front.*

*It has to be gathered. A full breath, in and out, before it's anything. I could see it in my own shadow on the wall while it gathered: the middle went still and heavy and stayed that way. Anybody who could read Corvane would read me before I was half ready.*

*It went forward. Not far. The water in the trough went flat and shivered. The pear buds leaned. A bucket moved a finger.*

*Afterward both arms like wet sand, most of an hour.*

He looked at it. Then he wrote the ruling, because he made himself write one even when it was short.

*No good in a circle. Not like this. Not with anybody watching who knows what they're looking at. Only ever alone, and in pieces, until I know more.*

In the morning, in the grey half, he told Lira. She listened to all of it with the chalk in her hand. Then she went to the post, and beside the *W* column, at the top of a clean stretch of grey canvas, she drew a small *P*. Under it she made one stroke, and put a ring round it, because it had come. Beside the ring, in her smallest letters, she wrote *arms. an hour.*

She looked at it a while.

"Facing the trough," she said.

"Facing the trough."

"Good." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Keep facing the trough."

# Chapter 53 — Off the Record

The spring came in properly over the next three weeks, the way it did in Ardenmere, all at once and then backward and then all at once again.

The river went down from brown to green. The pear tree in the corner of Torvin's yard opened its small hard buds into flowers, white, with a smell like a clean shirt, and then a frost came one night and turned half of them brown at the edges, and Torvin's wife stood in the yard in the morning and looked at them and said a word Cael had not heard her use before, which was *ungrateful*. The barges came down every day now. The boys with the red cap and without it did the lists together every morning on the bottom step, arguing, and every evening sat down on the same step and counted, and he did not say anything about it to either of them.

His hip came back. It came back the old man's way, a piece at a time, with nothing spent on it that did not need spending. By the end of the first week he could go up the lane of yards without thinking about it, and by the end of the second he could do the box drill at half speed, and by the end of the third Lira let him do it at full, and said *hips* only twice. The eye went from plum to yellow to nothing, and the bread woman stopped looking at it with professional interest and went back to giving him two from underneath with her chin up, and he still did not know her name.

He went up to the old man's yard on both Saturdays with a coin. The coin went on the post. On the first Saturday the old man had him stand for half an hour with a finger on his breastbone and say, every time he felt himself holding with more than he needed, *there*. On the second he had him do nothing at all for an hour but walk round the inside of the yard wall, slowly, while the old man stood in his corner and said, every so often, without explanation, *shoulders*, or *jaw*, or *you're holding your breath again, what for?* Cael walked home both times more tired than after a bout, and he could not have said why.

He did not ask Vell for a card, and she did not offer him one. Lira said that was the first sensible thing either of them had done since the autumn.

The *W* column on Lira's post grew. Most of its strokes had rings round them now, and the words beside the rings had begun, very slowly, to change: *same*, *same*, *less*, *same*, *less*. Under the *P* there were four strokes, all with rings, and all with the same two words beside them, *arms*, *trough*. He had not once faced anything else.

On the third Tuesday, Hesk's letter was against the salt crock.

It was the answer to the district letter, the long one about barrels and apricots and a coin on a post, and it was short, and he read it on the low wall by the pump with the pear flowers coming down round him.

*Cael,*

*You're describing a home.*

*I read it twice, and the second time I read it out loud to the workshop, which is a thing I've never done with a letter, and I stopped twice in the middle because the stove wanted seeing to and I wanted not to be reading it for a minute. I could see Fen Street in it, and the bench, and Ressa putting the loaf in your bag on the last morning. I didn't know whether you'd find another one. You have. I sat by the stove a good while after, glad of it, and missing you, both at once, and I'm not going to apologise for either.*

*Tell the boots woman I'd have bet against you as well, for the same reason.*

*Your question I've still got. I'm close.*

*— H.*

He sat on the wall with it for a long time after the third reading, with the flowers coming down on the page. Then he folded it more carefully than he folded most things, along its own folds, and did not put it in the leather book with the others. He put it in the front of the Log, inside the cover, where he would see it every time he opened it.

---

Coss had read Ilsev's report nine times.

It had lain on the corner of his desk at regional for four weeks, under the window that looked onto the wall of the records building. It sat squared to the edge, as she had squared it when she put it there on the morning after she came back from Ardenmere, and he had read it on the first day twice, and again on the fourth, and then once a week or so since. Each time he had told himself that he was reading it for its procedure, which was very good. Each time he had read the procedure quickly and come to rest on the last paragraph but one, and stayed there.

He read it again now, at the end of the day, with the office empty behind him and the lamp lit.

*In the course of opening the standard procedure on the subject's file, I observed a flag entered against his record. I did not request it. It was not present when I examined the file on the preceding day. I have attempted to trace its origin through every authorization available to me, which includes the regional trail and a portion of the trail above it, and I have not found it. It appears to have been entered at the hour the standard procedure began. I record it here because to omit it would misstate the record. I draw no conclusion from it.*

It was the most careful paragraph he had ever read. He had read reports by assessors for eleven years, and he knew what careful looked like in a report: the plain nouns, the verbs that did not reach further than their evidence, the conclusion stated and then held at arm's length. Ilsev had written a paragraph that stated everything and held it nowhere. *I draw no conclusion from it.* He had sat in a great many offices with a great many people who would have drawn one. He had been one of them, once or twice, early on, before a man he shared a desk with had drawn too many and gone to copy reports on the coast.

He did not draw one now. He sat with the paragraph and let it be what it was.

What it was, he thought, was the third thing.

He knew the first two by heart. A sweep in the autumn of a district that had not been swept in years, on an authorization with the regional prefix whose remainder was not in his register. His query about that code, written in his working log on the first night in Ardenmere and filed on his return, had come back to him in the spring pouch with a single word on it, *Noted*, in a hand he did not know, and nothing else. He had filed queries for eleven years. Every one of them had come back with an answer or the name of the desk that would give one. None had ever come back noted.

And now a flag that an assessor with more ways to look than he had could not find the end of.

He did not know what they were. He did not know whether they were one thing or three. He knew only that each of them was a place where the file had stepped off the procedure, and that the procedure was the one thing he had spent his working life learning to read, and that he could see the steps off it as clearly as a man can see a path through snow where somebody has walked in the night, and could not see anybody at the end of the path.

Two days ago the head of his section had stopped at his desk.

That was a departure too, though a smaller one. The section head did not stop at desks. He sent for people. But he had stopped at this one with his hands behind his back and looked at the report squared on its corner, and asked, as if he were asking about the weather, when the Ardenmere file was likely to close.

"It's with legal," Coss had said. "Months. Assessor Ilsev was clear."

"Legal will take the rest of the year." The section head had looked at the report a while longer. "The boy fights, I hear. In some ring in the Unranked District. A Sunday thing. A book kept by somebody nobody's licensed." He had said it lightly, and it had not been light. "It isn't the law that troubles anybody. The law's with legal, and legal will say what it says. But nobody above this floor wants a classification like that standing up in an unsanctioned ring every Sunday while they're saying it." He had paused. "If the subject were to remove himself to somewhere smaller, of his own accord, the file would close itself. A closure report on voluntary relocation. Nobody would need to wait on legal at all."

"Is that an instruction?"

"It's a view." The section head had looked at him then, very briefly. "Several people hold it."

Then he had gone on down the room with his hands behind his back.

Coss had sat at his desk for a long time afterward, as he was sitting now.

He had never, in eleven years, had a section head suggest to him how a file under legal review should close. It was not done. It was not wrong, quite; there was no rule against a view. But a file under review was a file nobody touched, and that was a rule, and everybody on this floor knew it. And the section head had said *several people* in the voice of a man repeating something he had himself been told, by somebody else, at another desk.

Coss knew only the departures. He had no names for them. He did not have to have names to see that they were all pointing the same way, which was out of Ardenmere.

He took his thumb off the bridge of his nose and looked out of the window at the wall of the records building, which had gone grey in the dusk.

He could do nothing. That was the procedure. The file was with legal; he was its field agent; he had no business in Ardenmere until legal said he had. He could write a memorandum to the section head setting out, accurately, that a file under review was not to be closed on a view. It would go up to a desk above the section head, and be read by whoever sat there, and be *Noted*.

Or he could do the thing nobody on this floor would ever know about.

He thought about his daughter. She would be fourteen at midsummer. She had asked him, at the door in the autumn, whether this one was going to be interesting, and he had said what he always said. He had not told her anything since, because there was nothing in it he could tell a child. But he had found, more than once this winter, that he was sitting at the kitchen table looking at her doing her lessons with her tongue between her teeth and thinking about a boy of her age across a desk in a room over the fish steps, citing the Supplementary Register to him from memory with his hands on his knees. He did not like where that thought went. He thought it anyway. He thought that if it had been her file, thin and odd and stepping off its procedure in three places, he would have wanted somebody to sit down across from her and tell her the truth about it, as far as they had any. Not across a desk with a stamp on it, but plainly, and in their own coat.

He had three days owed him from the winter, which he had never taken. He had never taken any.

He wrote the leave on a slip in the morning and gave it to the clerk at the end of the room, who looked at it in astonishment and stamped it. He did not take the official cart; the official cart went on the regional account and wrote down who rode it. He walked to the carters' yard by the east gate with his own small bag and paid his own fare out of his own pocket, and sat on the bench in the back of the cart with three wool merchants and a woman with a crate of hens.

He was wearing his own coat. It was an old brown one he wore on Sundays, frayed at the cuffs, which nobody at regional had ever seen him in. Somewhere on the first evening, a mile or so past the waystation, he felt for the small pin on his lapel by habit and found it there. He took it off and looked at it in the last of the light, a small hard shape, and put it in his inside pocket with his register, and did up the button over it.

---

The man sat down across from Cael at the narrow table by the pie man's stall on Saturday morning, and Cael knew him by his walk before he knew him by his face.

He had been sitting there a quarter of an hour, after the grey half, with two loaves from underneath and a twist of the fruit woman's apricots and nothing at all in his head, which was a thing he had been learning to do. The market was loud and slow around him. It was the first warm Saturday of the year, and people had come down to it who did not need anything, only to walk about in the sun and look at things and be seen looking. He had been watching them without meaning to. He had been watching the way a man in a brown coat came down the second row between the stalls, unhurried, reading the bread woman's high cracked lantern as if it were a notice, and he had thought, idly, *that man reads things the way they're written*. Then the man had turned out of the row toward the pie stall, and Cael had seen the set of his shoulders, and the brown coat had stopped mattering.

Coss put a pie on the table in front of himself and did not eat it.

He looked older than he had looked in the autumn, or perhaps only more tired. He had no pin on his coat. The coat was old and brown and frayed at the cuffs, and it was not the dark one he had worn up the fish steps, and Cael understood, before a word had been said, that this was a choice and that it had been made carefully.

"This isn't official," said Coss.

"I can see."

"I want that said first. I'm on my own days. I came on my own fare. Nobody at regional knows I'm here, and nobody at the post." He laid his hands flat on the table on either side of the pie. "I'm not here as anything with a stamp. I'm here because there's a thing I think you ought to be told, and it isn't mine to tell you in the room over the fish steps, so I'm telling it to you here."

Cael put his loaves down beside him on the bench.

"All right."

"Legal review will take months. Ilsev told you that, and she was right." Coss looked at the pie. "What she couldn't tell you, because it isn't hers to know, is that it doesn't matter very much what legal decides. The law's one thing. Above the law there's policy, and policy's already been decided, though nobody's written it down. Nobody above my floor wants a classification like yours standing up in an unsanctioned ring every Sunday. That isn't a statute. You can't find it in Part Six with a bone knife. It's a view that several people hold." He looked up. "And a view held by enough people at the right desks doesn't need a statute."

"Policy," said Cael. "Not law."

"Policy. Not law." Coss nodded slowly. "You can win the law. You've won it twice. I watched you do it once and read about the other. You'll very likely win it a third time, when legal finally sits down to it. Winning the law won't touch the policy at all."

Cael sat with that. He turned it over, the way he would have turned over an opponent's opening stance, looking at it from all the sides. He found that he believed it. He found that some part of him had believed it already, on a stool behind a plank partition, listening to a man at a window say *resolved*.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because there's a door," said Coss, "and nobody but me is going to show it to you." He said it plainly, without any softening, as he had stamped the summons in the autumn. "You leave Ardenmere. On your own feet, of your own accord, before legal sits. Somewhere small. Somewhere there's no ring and no book and nothing for anybody to have a view about. You choose where. I write a closure report on voluntary relocation. It ends the file's active status. Legal never needs to sit at all." He looked at Cael across the pie. "I can make it disappear."

"The file."

"The file. The review. All of it. A closed file is a file nobody reads."

Cael looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind him the pie man was calling his prices, and a child was crying because it had dropped something, and the bread woman was laughing at somebody with her chin up.

"Ilsev's flag," he said. "The one she couldn't trace. Do you know what it is?"

Coss went still.

It was not a large stillness. Anybody at the next table would have missed it. But Cael had spent the whole winter watching what did not move, in a brick yard and in the grey half and in the sleet, and he saw it go through the man across the table like a dropped stitch through a sleeve. Coss's hands stayed flat on the table. His face stayed exactly where it was. And the breath under his coat stopped, for one beat, two, the way the cloth of Feryn's shirt had stopped over the middle of him at the moment of deciding, and did not start again until something in him had been looked at and put away.

"I don't know anything about a flag," said Coss.

He said it evenly, and it was true. Cael could hear that it was true; it had the weight of true things, the plain flat weight of a reading on a dial. And he could hear, as clearly, that it was not the whole of what was in the man. In Denvash, a week behind, Hesk opened the timepiece.

It had come in on Thursday from a house on the canal, a merchant's carriage clock in a case of dark wood and brass, very fine, with a face painted with small blue flowers. It had come with a story. It had belonged, the merchant's man said, to three households in eleven years, and each of them had passed it on because it stopped. It stopped at no hour in particular and for no reason anybody could find, and started again the next day as if nothing had happened, and the last household had sent it to a woman who read cards before they would let it in the house again. The card woman had said it was unlucky. So the merchant, who had bought it cheap for that reason and was not a man who believed in cards, had sent it to the instrument-maker on Fen Street, to be told it was not.

Hesk had it open on the bench under the lamp with the back off, and the works lying in their brass frame like a small town seen from above.

He found it in a quarter of an hour. It was a single spring in the going train, the smallest one, seated a hair loose in its post, so that when the clock was jolted, or the room went cold, or nothing at all happened that anybody would ever notice, it slipped a tooth and the whole town stopped. It had been loose for eleven years. Three households and a card woman had told one another stories about it, and not one of them had ever taken the back off.

He sat looking at it. Then he reached behind him without looking for the drawer marked *Springs, fine*, and found the size he wanted by feel, the way he found everything in that drawer. He did it with the left hand, because the right still had the long pink line across the palm where a coil from the next drawer down had gone into it in the winter. He had never mentioned the hand in a letter. He did not think he would.

When the new spring was seated, he set the clock going, and listened to it.

The letter was on the end of the bench, where it had been for four weeks. It had come at the end of the second week of the spring, the one with the bone knife in it, and *you're right*, and Coss at the window, and a mark on a record that a careful woman could not trace. He had answered that letter in four lines within a day, because he had not been able to bear not answering it. He had not answered the question at the end of it. He had read the question every night since. He had answered the district letter, and said *I'm close*, and he had not been close. He had been sitting with the question as he sat with a figure that would not come out. It was a number he could not trace back to where it came from, and he would not sign it until he could.

*If whoever made that mark wanted me gone, would any of that matter?*

He looked at the clock with the small blue flowers on its face, going.

Three households had passed it on. Each of them had told the next that it was cursed, and the next had told the card woman, and the card woman had told the merchant. Because it had been talked about, at every table it went to, for eleven years, it had come at last to a bench where somebody took the back off. A clock that nobody talked about would have stopped in a drawer in a back room. Some spring cleaning it would have gone out with the ashes, and nobody would ever have asked where it went, because nobody would have known it was there.

He thought about a file, thirty-two years ago, opened in a room he had stood in at twenty-two. He thought about how few people had ever known it was opened at all, and he stopped there. He stopped there as he always did, at the edge of it, with the old care, and did not go on. It was not his to go into tonight. Tonight was the boy's.

He took a sheet of paper from the shelf and uncapped the ink and wrote.

*Cael,*

*You asked me a question a month ago and I said I'd answer it when I had an answer I'd sign. I've had a clock on the bench this week that three families called cursed, and the curse was one loose spring the size of a fingernail paring. I'll tell you about it properly another time. It's what's given me the answer, so I'm writing tonight, before I lose it.*

*You asked whether it matters that people there know your face. Whether it's a wall, or only feels like one because you're standing inside it.*

*It isn't a wall. I won't tell you it is. A wall would keep things out, and nothing you've got there will keep anybody out who's set on coming in. But a thing that's known can't be put in a drawer without somebody asking where it went. A thing that's talked about gets brought to a bench, in the end, and somebody takes the back off. The quiet things are the ones that go out with the ashes.*

He stopped, and read it, and found that it was nearly what he meant and not quite. He sat with the pen over the paper while the clock went on beside him, steady, through a whole minute, two.

Then he wrote the rest of it.

*Being seen is not the same as being safe. But it's not nothing.*

*Keep being known. Keep me told. I'm proud of you past anything I've the words for, and I'll say so properly when I've found them.*

*— H.*

He read it twice. He did not read it a third time, because he was afraid that if he did he would start to change it, and it was right.

He folded it and sealed it and wrote on the front of it, in his square hand, *ROOMS (the O leans)*, and the rest of the address, and propped it against the clock on the shelf over the bench, where he propped everything that had to go in the morning. It would go in the east bag at noon. It would be a week on the road. Whatever was happening in Ardenmere tonight, it had already happened by now. It would have happened a week ago by the time the boy broke the seal.

He sat a while longer in the lamplight, listening to the merchant's clock with the blue flowers on it, going, and not stopping, and not stopping.

## Rhythm calibration (measured, 2026-10-01)

Book 1 Movement 1 — the first movement drafted from this brief — overshot the formula in
the short direction: sentence mean 8.8 words (target 14.6), 41% of sentences at five
words or fewer (target ~28%), 0.3% at forty-plus (target ~3.3%), Flesch-Kincaid grade 2.8
(target 6.8), about 106 dialogue tags per 10k words (target ~41). Paragraph median (15)
and scene spacing were close. So, when drafting:

- Let thought, action and description run in full, well-built sentences. Compound and
  complex sentences with a clear hierarchy are the house texture, not the exception.
- Join a run of three or four clipped statements into one sentence when they are one thought.
- Keep short sentences for beats that land — a recognition, a hit, a turn — not as default.
- Give each chapter a few deliberately long, readable sentences (forty words or more)
  where an action or a thought earns the length.
- Drop "he said" / "she said" when the paragraph already makes the speaker clear.
- Keep short paragraphs (median ~18 words) and scene breaks about every 950 words.

## No scripted prose surgery (2026-10-01)

Rhythm and length repairs are done by reading, sentence by sentence, never by a script that
splits sentences or paragraphs at clause or sentence boundaries. Two repairs did that and
each needed a recheck to find the splits that broke a thought. Where the formula and a
character's speech disagree, keep the speech: one-to-three-word dialogue lines and the short
landing beats of a fight are not drift to be repaired.

## Source-reuse gate tightened to 8 words (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

The 10-word gate let distinctive source phrasing through in 6–9-word fragments (the first
movement's opening line, for example, echoed the current edition's). Measured at 8 words, movements
written after authors began self-checking run 1–6 shared runs per 10k; the worst ran ~30. From now
on `ed.sh overlap` and every editorial review use runs of **8 or more** words. Every listed run that
BOOK_MAP does not protect is rewritten — re-composed, not a synonym swapped. Names, Path names,
ranks and plain facts of canon will sometimes share a short run; re-compose the sentence around them.
Movements closed under the 10-word gate get one book-level 8-word sweep at book completion.

## Draft from your own event list, not from the source page (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

Book 3 Movement 6's review found the retold scenes in ch42–44 to be close paraphrase: 23–32% of
sentences followed a source sentence word for word in order, with words varied enough to pass the
8-word gate. Everything the author invented was clean. The cause is drafting with the source open.
So, for every movement:

1. Read the source chapters the packet names, once, for what happens and who these people are.
2. Close them. Write a private event list in your own words — what happens, in what order, who
   wants what, what changes. Keep it in your working notes, not in the manuscript.
3. Draft from that list and the book map. Do not reopen the source chapters while drafting.
   Choose your own entry point into each scene and your own order of beats; the source's scene
   shape is not canon, only its events are.
4. Only protected wording (BOOK_MAP) is copied, exactly, from the map — not from the source page.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

### City Access by Rank

**LOCKED:**

| Rank tier | City district access |
|---|---|
| Unranked / [SHATTERED] | Unranked Districts only; cannot legally enter inner city |
| Copper | Outer districts; limited market access |
| Iron | General city access; guild district access with credentials |
| Bronze | Full city access; administrative district entry |
| Silver | All districts; inter-city travel credentials |
| Gold | Continental access; diplomatic consideration |

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). Diagnostic technique: **the reading** — contact, both hands, the healer's current run through the patient's architecture ("listening with my hands to how you're built"). Two modes, taught before either is load-bearing (B7 Ch7): the *surface* reading (hands on the hurt, seconds) and the *deep* reading (both hands, whole architecture, minutes, patient still and uninjured, at a mending's cost). Costs and limits: contact range only; healing spends the healer's reserve at roughly the rate of the damage repaired — no crowds, no self-healing; a Tide practitioner **cannot heal what she cannot read**; combat use is a *stall* (a hand on a wrist, a breath, the declaration doesn't render) at brutal cost — Oryn has done it three times in her life. Inside quiet ground: nothing.

**Cael's Tide-adjacent fragment (B7 Ch13):** partial; Iron; self and contact; the first fragment that does nothing in a fight; engagement field *clinical*; **the standing rule, in Oryn's hand and his: ask first, every time.** Consistent with B2's "Tide-adjacent" anomaly label and Book 15's "Oryn's Tide Path burns out."

## Continuity Rules

1. **The Compact Registry is the authority on Path classification.** Any scene involving official rank must be consistent with what the Registry would show for that character at that point in the story.

2. **Path declarations follow standard format.** Only Cael's fragment notices deviate. All other practitioners receive standard declarations.

3. **Tier advancement is not instant.** No character advances a tier in a single scene. Advancement is earned across multiple books for major characters.

4. **The Architect's will is not omniscient.** It can detect [SHATTERED] signatures and issue systemic pressure, but it cannot directly perceive or target individuals until Book 11 when it becomes actively deployed.

5. **The Quieting spreads from ancient sites outward.** It does not appear suddenly in cities. It begins at edge-territory ruins and expands. This gives the companions time to investigate before it becomes a continental crisis.

6. **Cael's ability integration has limits.** He cannot integrate an ability he has not witnessed in use. Seeing a written description does not qualify. The ability must be performed in his presence.

7. **The found family is permanent.** No companion exits the series without narrative justification. The loss in Book 12 is a choice, not a death — the companion is still alive, their relationship with Cael changed.

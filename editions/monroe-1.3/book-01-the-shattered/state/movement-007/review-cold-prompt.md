# Chapter 41 — The Back Pages

He made the step again on Tuesday night, in the narrow space between his cot and the wall, after the brothers had stopped arguing and before the wheezing man began to whistle.

It was the fourth night running. He had told himself on the first night that he would do it only to be sure it was still there, and on the second that he would do it to see whether it came more easily, and by the third he had stopped telling himself anything and simply waited for the house to go quiet. Then he stood up in his stockings on the cold boards, with his back to the room so that Yeni would see only a boy standing by a window, and dropped his hip, and turned, and put his foot down where he had watched Lira put hers a thousand times.

The boards went short under him by the width of a hand.

It was no better than it had been on Saturday. It was slow, and stiff, and anybody watching would have thought he had stood up too fast and lost his balance and caught it again. When it was done his breath came short for a dozen breaths, as though he had run up the stairs from the street, and the ache came into the joint of his left hip, deep and dull, a little worse than the night before. He did it once more, because twice was the number he had set himself, and the second time the boards hardly changed at all.

He sat down on the end of the cot with his hand pressed against the hip and waited for his breathing to come back.

It did not get easier, but it did not go away either. On Saturday he had thought it might be a thing that happened once, like the word in Pellin's office, and then never again. Three nights later it was still there, every time he asked, small and clumsy and costing more than it gave. He did not know whether that was the beginning of something or the whole of it.

He took the Log off the shelf and turned it over to the back, past the boxed *Not yet* and the entry about the first night, to a clean page.

He had been putting off this page for three days. Every night he had opened the back section and looked at the white paper under the last entry, and closed it again, because what he wanted to write was not a fact. Everything else in the back of the book was a fact. The cart was a fact; the drop was a fact; the notice, with its plain words in the dark behind his breastbone, was the plainest fact of all. What he wanted to write tonight was a guess, and he had told Lira once, on a step under a lantern, that guessing was how you wrote the wrong thing down and then trusted it because it was written.

But he had been carrying the guess around for three days, and it had got no smaller, and he thought it would be better on paper, where he could at least see the edges of it.

He wrote slowly, in his best hand.

*I think I'm borrowing something.*

He looked at the line for a long time. Then he went on under it, because one line on its own looked too sure of itself.

*Not taking. It isn't all there, and it isn't mine the way Lira's Path is hers. She was declared at Kindling and it's been hers every day since, whole, to use when she likes. What I've got is more like the outline of how she moves, without the inside. It comes properly when something's coming at me and I haven't got time to choose. When I ask for it on purpose, slowly, by the cot, it comes too, but badly. It's like copying out a word in somebody else's handwriting. You can see whose hand it was meant to be. Nobody would mistake it for theirs.*

*I don't know how it got here. I don't know if "borrowing" is the right word. It's the least wrong word I've got.*

He read it over, and found nothing in it that was more than he knew, and nothing that was less, and left it.

The wheezing man had begun his long whistle by the door. Yeni's lamp was down to a bead, and when he looked up she had turned on her side with her face to the wall, which meant either that she was asleep or that she had decided to let him think so. He put the Log back on the shelf beside Hesk's leather book and lay down on his right side, the side that did not catch, and lay awake for some time with the hip aching and the dark place behind his breastbone quiet and a little less dark than it had been.

---

In the grey half on Wednesday morning Lira was teaching him to move without deciding, and he was watching her do it, and he could not stop thinking about the watching.

He had always watched her. That was what the mornings were. She did a thing at the post and he watched it, and then he tried it and she watched him, and between the two of them they found the place where his body and her body disagreed, and worked on it. It had never before seemed like anything but a lesson. This morning, every time she moved, he found himself wondering what he was doing when he looked at her, and whether looking was all it was.

"Hips," said Lira.

"I know."

"You know, and they're still square." She stepped back out of the box and put her hands on her own hips to show him. "You're thinking. I can see you thinking. You're thinking about something that isn't this."

"I'm thinking about this."

"You're thinking about something *near* this." She looked at him with her head on one side, not cross, only interested, the way she looked at a stranger's guard. "Is it the rating? Because I'll tell you now, I'm still proud of it, and you're still past me, and if you want me to say it again I'll say it at the post in front of the girls on the wall."

"It isn't the rating."

"Then it's something you'll tell me when you tell me." She brought her hands up. "Again. Loose. And stop looking at my feet like they owe you money."

He did it again, and his hips were square, and she said *hips*, and he said *I know*.

She did the step at the end, as she always did. It came round with the two empty touches and the ground folding, and the backhand stopping by his ear, and her weight stopped with it, almost; it went on past by perhaps two fingers' width onto her right foot. That was better than three fingers. He told her so at once, as the rule said, and she grinned and said *two* to herself and pushed her hair off her face with her wrist.

He watched her do that too.

He had watched her push her hair back a hundred times, and her step a thousand, and her breathing and her shoulders and the way she leaned on the post with her good arm when she was thinking. He knew her movements the way he knew the steps up to his room at Torvin's, where to put his foot in the dark. And somewhere in all that watching, if the notice meant what he thought it meant, something of hers had come across into him without either of them noticing. He did not know whether it had come across in a day or in a month. He did not know whether it was still coming across now, this morning, while she stood in the grey half pushing her hair out of her eyes.

She did not seem to have noticed anything. She said something about almost everything; she had told him about his nod, and his hips, and the way his chin went up when he was pleased with himself. If she had seen her own step coming out of him, wrongly, in the third exchange against Sarel, she would surely have said.

But then she had seen it, of course. She had told him so. *I did what you do*, he had said, under a lantern by the chalk board, and she had stopped in the street with her face doing something he had never seen it do. She had thought it was learning. So had he, at the time.

He wrote that down that night, on the cot, in the back.

*Does Lira notice that it's hers? I don't think so. She thinks I'm learning it, and I am, partly. Some of what I did against Sarel was me copying her badly, on purpose, and I can feel the difference between that and the other thing. But I'm only guessing that she hasn't noticed, because she hasn't said. She says something about almost everything. That isn't the same as saying something about everything.*

*Not hearing about a thing isn't the same as there being nothing to hear. I want the first answer to be true, because it's easier. That's a reason to be careful with it, not a reason to believe it.*

---

On Thursday the mender sent the lantern woman's boy up to Torvin's to ask whether he would come for an extra afternoon, because of the keys.

They had come out of the same house by the dyers' as the hinges, from the drawers of a desk that had gone for firewood, and the mender wanted to know which of them went to anything. There were a few door keys, long and heavy, and a great many small ones for boxes and drawers and cupboards. There was nothing to try them in. The mender said that did not matter; a key that had been used for twenty years had a shape worn into it by the lock it fitted, and you could see the lock in the key, if you looked.

So Cael sat on the stool at the end of the bench with a loupe in his eye and looked at keys.

The mender was right. A key that had turned in a lock every day for years had soft bright edges on its wards, worn by the lock's own pins, and every one of those worn places was a little record of the lock it had lived in. A key that had never been used was sharp all over. A key that had been used for a while and then put away had both kinds of edge at once, the bright worn ones going dull again. By the end of the afternoon he could pick out the keys that had been in somebody's pocket every day from the keys that had only ever been in a drawer, and he did not think he could have explained to anybody how.

He did not mean to think about Lira while he did it, and he thought about her the whole time.

He thought about the worn edges on the keys, where the lock had shaped them, a little at a time, every day for years, until the key carried the shape of the lock in it whether anybody meant it to or not. The lock had not given the key anything; it had only been turned in, every day, and the key had come away a different shape.

It was a comfortable thought, and he did not trust it, because comfortable thoughts were the ones that let you stop asking questions. He set it on the side of the bench in his head with the other things he did not trust yet and went on looking at keys.

Walking back up the lane of yards at the end of the day, he passed the old man's gate. The old man was standing in it with his sleeves rolled down against the cold, watching the woman with the shoulder go through her six positions in the last of the light, and he did not look round.

"Assessed," said the old man.

Cael stopped. He had not told the old man; he had not told anybody but Lira and Torvin's wife, and Torvin's wife had told the boots woman, which was the same as telling the whole district.

"Yes."

The old man watched the woman reach her fourth position and hold it.

"Now they'll plan for you," he said. "Before, they bet on you. Now they plan." He shifted his weight very slightly from one foot to the other. "Different thing, being planned for."

Then he went back to watching, and Cael understood that he had been given all of it, and walked on up the lane.

That night, on the cot, he turned to the back of the Log and wrote the longest entry he had written there since the drop. His hand went faster as it went, and less careful.

*I need more than one. One source is a story. Lira is one source. If there's anything to this at all, the next question is whether it's Lira, or whether it's anybody. Is it watching somebody a great deal that does it? Or is watching only the first part, and there's something else as well that has to be there, that I haven't found? I can't tell those apart from one example. Nobody could.*

*I need somebody else. A different person, a different Path. Watched as hard as I watched her, and then see whether anything comes, and whether it comes the same way, or a different way, or not at all.*

He stopped with the pencil over the page, because the next thought had arrived while he was writing the last one, and it was not a comfortable thought at all.

If it was anybody, and not only Lira, then it was everybody. Everybody he had ever watched hard enough. Renn's figure. Dessa's patience. The kiln boy's held breath, Sarel's closing, Talis planting where he could not see him go, Corbin reading feet at the mark. The woman with the shoulder doing her six positions in the old man's yard, every evening for three months, while he walked past. Any of them might have left something behind in him, quietly, the way the lock had left its shape in the key, and it might be sitting there now in the dark behind his breastbone with no notice on it yet, waiting for the right moment to come up.

He sat with that for a long time.

On some nights, he thought, it would be the most exciting thing he had ever thought of. On others it would be the most frightening. Tonight, on the cot, with the wheezing man whistling and the hip aching, it was both at once, and he could not have said which was winning.

He wrote under the entry, smaller: *Find out. Don't guess.*

---

He chose Kestrel because he had watched him twice already and had never meant to fight him.

That was the first reason, and the most important. Kestrel was a Copper Rank 5, Force Path, who fought on the Sunday card once or twice a month and had never once been put in against anybody Cael's size. Vell matched fighters carefully, and she would not put an assessed-Copper of fourteen into the circle with a Force man who had been at it for ten seasons. So he could watch Kestrel as hard as he liked without it being homework for a bout, which meant that if anything came of the watching, it would not have come from needing it.

The second reason was that Kestrel was nothing like Lira. He was a big, slow-spoken man of about thirty who worked at the ropewalk along the river, long in the arm and heavy in the shoulder, with tar ground into the creases of his hands that no amount of washing would shift. He did not skip any middles. He fought the way he walked, steadily and straight at you, and his Force did what Force did, which was to put a second, heavier push into you a half beat after his fist had stopped. It was the shove that Petra had taught Cael at Copper 3, and that Dellin had nearly given him at Copper 3, made slower and much bigger. If something of Kestrel's came across, Cael thought, he would know it was not Lira's.

The third reason was that Kestrel trained in the mornings, alone, in the middle yard on the lane where Renn had shown off his false heel, and anybody could stand at the gate and watch.

Kestrel was on the Sunday card, the third bout, against a Copper 4 Blade from Fenrow, and Cael watched him a third time from the rope on the shaded side with his hand on the iron stake.

He did not watch him the way he had watched him in the autumn, for the Log, with the pencil going. He watched him the other way, the grey-half way, the whole of him at once. It was harder than he had expected to stop looking for things; his eyes kept going to the big man's heels, because the heels were where the shove came from, and every time they went there he found that he was building a plan to beat him, out of habit, a plan for a bout that would never happen. He brought them back. He let the plan go. *Gone,* he thought, without saying it, and the word did what it had been made to do.

Kestrel beat the Fenrow Blade in the fourth exchange, slowly, as he did everything, with one shove that sent the Blade across the groove on his back. Then he walked to the water bucket humming.

Lira was beside him at the rope. She had come up while he was watching and had stood there without speaking, which was not like her.

"You weren't writing," she said.

"No."

"You always write." She looked at the circle, where the pie boy was raking out the furrow the Fenrow Blade had made. "You looked at him the way you look at me in the mornings. Not the way you look at the people you're going to fight."

He had not known it showed. He thought, standing there with his hand on the stake, that perhaps he should have known; she had found his nod, and his hips, and the way his chin went up.

"I won't be at the post tomorrow," he said. "Or Thursday, perhaps. I'm going to watch him train. At the middle yard. All morning."

Lira looked at him for a long moment.

He saw the question come into her face, and saw her look at it. He saw her put it down beside her, on the step that was not there, carefully, the way she had put down *borrowed* under the lantern after Dessa, and leave it lying there.

"All right," she said.

It was the voice that meant she was not going to pick the question up. But she had put it down a little further away this time than she usually did, and on the walk to the well she did not say anything at all, and at the well she said *same time Tuesday*, not tomorrow. He walked home to Torvin's with that sitting in his chest like a stone in a boot.

So on Monday he did not go to the grey half. He went up to the middle yard in the dark, before the light, with bread in his pocket, and stood at the gate with his back against the wall where Renn's friends had sat like crows, and waited.

Kestrel came at first light.

He hung a sack of sand from the beam across the far end of the yard, on a rope he brought with him coiled over his shoulder, and stood in front of it for a long time with his hands at his sides, not doing anything. Then he hit it. He did not hit it hard. His fist went in a hand's depth and stopped, and the sack hung there on its rope with the dent of his knuckles in it. Then, a half beat after his fist had stopped, the whole sack went up and back on its rope as though somebody had kicked it from below, and swung out to the end of the rope, and came back.

Kestrel stepped out of its way without looking at it and hit it again.

Cael watched him do that for four hours.

He watched him the way he had watched Lira for three months, which was not the way he watched an opponent. He did not look for a tell. He did not write anything down, though the Log was in his coat. He tried, as well as he could, to look at the whole of the man at once, as you look at a stance when you want to know what it feels like to stand in it, and not where it is going to go. He watched Kestrel's breath, slow and deep, going down into his belly before every blow. He watched the way the man's weight settled very slightly into his heels as his fist stopped, as if he were bracing a door with his back, and how the shove came out of that settling and not out of the fist at all. He watched until his own heels ached from standing, and his eyes stung, and he had stopped being sure whether he was seeing the thing or remembering seeing it.

At the end of the morning Kestrel unhooked the sack and coiled his rope and walked out through the gate past him, close enough to touch, and did not look at him once. He smelled of tar and sweat and rope. He went down the lane toward the river with his coil over his shoulder, humming something tuneless under his breath.

Cael ate his bread standing at the gate. His hands were cold and his feet were cold, and he was more tired than he had been after Sarel, though he had done nothing all morning but stand.

He tried it that night in Torvin's courtyard, after the lamps.

It was the same courtyard where, three months ago, on the twenty-eighth night, he had sat on the cold flags for an hour looking for the Arbiter, and found only the place where a door should have been. The pear tree was bare now. The pump had a skin of ice on its trough that somebody had broken with a stick and that had frozen again round the stick. He sat on the low wall with his coat buttoned to the chin and his hands in his armpits, and when the house had gone quiet behind him, he closed his eyes and went looking.

He knew what he was looking for, this time, or he thought he did. He went to the dark place behind his breastbone, the place that was not quite as dark as it had been, and found the Wind there. He did not find a word or a picture, or anything he could have drawn; it was more a sort of leaning in the dark, a readiness, the shape the step had left when it went through him. It was the thing he reached for by the cot every night. He did not reach for it now. He went past it, carefully, as you go past a sleeping dog, and felt about in the dark beyond it for anything that was not Lira's.

He thought about Kestrel's heels settling, and the sack going up on its rope a half beat late. He thought about the breath going down into the belly. He put the whole morning in front of himself as clearly as he could, the tar on the hands and the slow humming, and he reached for whatever it had left.

There was nothing.

But it was not the nothing he had found on the twenty-eighth night. That had been a closed door, flat and final, a place where something should have been and was not, and where he had known at once, the way you know a room is empty when you walk into it, that nobody was coming. This was different. This was more like reaching for a word you know perfectly well, a word you used yesterday, and finding it right at the edge of your tongue, with its first letter almost there and its shape almost there, and then not there. It was not an absence so much as something unfinished.

He sat on the wall for an hour, and it did not finish.

At the end of the hour he made the Wind step once, on the frozen flags, to be sure that he still could. It came, clumsily, the stones going a hand's width short under his foot, and his breath went, and his hip ached. So it was not that he had lost the knack of asking. He had asked twice tonight, and one thing had answered and the other had not.

He went up the stairs in the dark with his hand on the wall, and wrote it down on the cot by the bead of Yeni's lamp.

*Kestrel, first day. Four hours, at the middle yard, from the gate. Watched him the way I watch Lira, not the way I watch a bout. Tried in the courtyard after the lamps, an hour. Nothing came.*

*But not the Arbiter's kind of nothing. That was a shut door. This felt like something I couldn't quite reach, which might be a real thing or might only be me wanting it. I can't tell those apart yet. I'm writing it down so that I remember I couldn't.*

*Wind still came when I asked. So it isn't the asking.*

He looked at the entry, and at the one before it, *Find out. Don't guess.* Then he wrote one more line at the bottom, because it was true and because it was the kind of line he was most likely to forget later, when he wanted something to be true.

*One morning isn't three months. Don't expect it to be.*

# Chapter 42 — Some of It's Yours

On Thursday he went back to the middle yard, and this time Kestrel looked at him.

It was not at first light, because the frost had come down so hard in the night that the rope of the sack was frozen stiff, and Kestrel had to stand under the beam for a while beating it against his leg before it would take a knot. Cael was already at the gate. He had come up in the dark again with bread in his pocket, and he had missed the grey half again, and Lira had said *all right* again on Wednesday evening at the well in the same voice as before, with the question set down a little further away from her this time. He did not like how far.

Kestrel hung the sack and stood in front of it, and then hit it, and the sack went up on its rope a half beat after his fist had stopped.

Cael watched as he had watched on Monday. He did not write, and he did not look for anything. He let his eyes rest on the whole of the big tar-stained man, the breath going down into the belly and the heels settling and the shove coming out of the settling, and he tried to let it go into him the way the mornings in the grey half had gone into him, without his permission, a little at a time. He found that he did not know how to do that on purpose. Every time he caught himself trying to let something in, he was really only watching harder, and watching harder was what he did for a bout; it was the opposite of what he had done with Lira for three months, which had mostly been standing about in the cold while she showed him things, liking her.

He thought about that, at the gate, for a long time. He had not liked anybody in this way before, in the plain everyday way you like the person whose morning you share. He did not know whether it mattered. He could not see how it could matter. He thought it would be a very foolish thing to write down, and he thought he would probably write it down anyway.

At the end of the second hour, Kestrel stopped hitting the sack and turned round.

He did not do it suddenly. He took his hand off the rope and wiped his face on the shoulder of his shirt and looked down the yard at the gate, slowly, the way he did everything, as though he had known Cael was there all along and had only now got round to it.

"You're the boy from Torvin's," he said. "Went five with Corbin."

"Yes."

"Second morning you've stood there." Kestrel came a few steps down the yard, not toward him exactly, but nearer, rolling his shoulders. Up close he was even bigger than he looked in the circle, and his voice was soft and slow, a voice that did not expect to have to be raised. "Vell put us down for something?"

"No."

"Then what are you watching me for?"

Cael had thought about this, walking up in the dark, and had not found an answer that was both true and short. So he gave the true one.

"I don't know yet."

Kestrel looked at him for a while with no expression on his broad face at all. Then he nodded, as if that were a perfectly ordinary reason for standing at a gate in a frost for two mornings, and turned back to the sack.

"Stand inside the wall, then," he said over his shoulder. "Out of the wind. You'll lose your feet, standing in that."

So Cael stood inside the wall, out of the wind, and watched Kestrel hit the sack for two more hours. At the end of it the big man unhooked his rope and coiled it, and walked out past him humming the same tuneless thing he had hummed on Monday, and said, "Morning," as he went by, as though they had known each other for years.

That night Cael tried again on the low wall by the pump, with the ice on the trough and the pear tree bare over him.

He did everything he had done on Monday, slowly and carefully, the whole morning laid out in front of him. He went past the Wind in the dark, as you go past a sleeping dog, and reached beyond it for anything that was Kestrel's.

The unfinished feeling was there again, at the edge, a word that would not come. It was perhaps a little nearer than it had been on Monday, or perhaps he only wanted it to be. He sat for an hour and it did not come any nearer, and then he went in.

---

On Friday morning he went back to the grey half, because two mornings away from it had been two too many, and because Lira was there.

She did not say anything about the two mornings. She gave him the larger half of the bread and the box drill at full speed, and then the step, over and over, and he moved without deciding as well as he could, which was not very well. He was tired from two dawns at a gate and two nights on a cold wall, and she could see it, and she did not say anything about that either. She only came at him a little harder, as though she meant to find out how tired he was by experiment.

On the ninth time she came at him with the step, something happened.

It did not happen in his feet, where the Wind lived, but lower down and further back, in his heels. She came in with the two empty touches and the ground folding, and as she came his weight went down and back into his heels, both of them, at once, hard, as if he had braced his back against a door that somebody was pushing on from the other side. It was not his. He had never once stood like that in his life. It was not Lira's either; Lira never stood like anything, she was always going somewhere. It lasted for half a heartbeat, perhaps less. Then her backhand stopped beside his ear, and he was standing on his own two feet again in the ordinary way, and his heels were only heels.

He stood quite still.

"Late," said Lira. "You were late. You went somewhere and stopped."

"I know."

"Where did you go?"

"I don't know."

She looked at him for a moment, and he saw her see that he meant it, and saw her put that down on the step beside the other things. Then she went back to her mark.

"Again."

He did it again, and it did not come. He did it a dozen times more before the sun came over the east wall, and his heels stayed his own every time, and by the end he could not have sworn to anybody, not even to himself, that he had felt anything at all.

He wrote it up that night on the cot. He wrote the two days of Kestrel first, and the two failed nights, and the half heartbeat in the grey half, as plainly as he could. Then he sat for a long time looking at what he had written, because none of it would stand still.

*Inconclusive,* he wrote at last.

*I can see three ways to read it. One: two mornings isn't enough. I watched Lira for three months before anything came, and I wasn't even trying, and maybe I've skipped straight to expecting it. Two: Force doesn't come across the way Wind does, or not to me, for reasons I can't guess at. Three: the thing in my heels this morning was real, and was his, and I need a great deal more before I can say so.*

*I can't tell which. I don't like not being able to tell. I think I'm going to have to get used to it, because I think this whole subject is going to give me a great many answers like this one before it gives me a single one I can stand on.*

He read it over. Then he turned back a page, to the boxed *Not yet* from the thirty-ninth night, with *not sure it is honest* written beside the box.

It had been two weeks tomorrow.

He had told himself, walking home from the well on the forty-second day, that he could carry it two weeks if he was careful. He had been careful, and the two weeks were nearly gone, and in all that time he had not found out a single thing that would make it easier to say. He had only found out that he did not know more things than he had thought.

He shut the Log and lay down on his right side and looked at the window for a long time.

---

"You've been somewhere else," said Lira, "for about a fortnight."

It was Saturday morning in the grey half, and she had stopped in the middle of the box drill to say it, with her hands still up. Then she let them down and stood with her weight a little forward and looked at him, quickly and completely, the way she looked at a bout between exchanges.

"Not distracted. You're here. You're doing the drills. But it's like you're having two conversations at once and I'm only getting half of one of them." She tilted her head. "And you've missed two mornings standing at a gate watching a ropewalker hit a sack, and the girls on the wall told me before you did. Which you'll agree is not how it's meant to go."

"I know."

"I know you know." She did not sound angry. She sounded like somebody who has waited outside a door for a long time and has decided to knock once, politely, and see. "I said I wouldn't ask. I'm not asking. I'm telling you that I've noticed, so you know I've noticed. That's all."

He had rehearsed this a dozen times, lying on the cot with his face to the window. In every version it had come out either too small or too strange, and he had stopped rehearsing it on Thursday because rehearsing it had begun to feel like a way of not doing it. Now she was standing in front of him in the grey half with her hands at her sides, and he found that he did not want to say it here, by the post, with the girls on the wall eating their stolen bread and the frost on the dirt.

"Can I come to your room," he said. "This afternoon. Before the lamps." He heard how it sounded and went on. "I want to tell you something, and I want to tell it with the door shut. Your door. Because it's partly yours."

Lira was very still.

"Partly mine," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long time. He saw her pick up the question she had put down, two weeks ago, on the step beside her, and look at it, and not ask it.

"The third hour after noon," she said. "The widow has her nap then, and she won't say anything about visitors." She picked up the bread. "Bring the book."

---

He walked there with the Log inside his coat, against his chest, the way Lira carried her throat-sweet tin.

The lane was empty in the cold. There was a dog asleep on a step, and a sheet frozen stiff on a line across the lane like a board, and candles hanging over the chandler's counter in bunches by their wicks. The widow was asleep in her chair behind them with her chin on her chest, and she did not wake when he went by. The fourth step talked. He went up the rest on the outsides of the treads, where they were quieter, and stood at the top in front of the door that was green gone grey.

He knocked three times, soft.

There was a pause, not a long one this time. Then the latch lifted and the door opened, and Lira stood in it with one hand on the edge. She did not say *yes?* She looked at his face, and whatever she saw there made her not say it, and she only stood aside.

"Mind your head."

He minded it. She shut the door behind him, and the latch dropped with its small click, and she turned the key in it from the inside and left it in the lock. Then she sat down on the end of the bed with her back to the wall and her knees up, and he sat on the floor on the pale worn strip in the middle of the boards, with his back against the shelf, because it was the only other place to sit.

It was very quiet. Two streets away, through the cracked pane with its strip of paper, Torvin's chimney was smoking straight up into a white sky.

He took the Log out of his coat and held it on his knees, closed.

"I'm going to say it badly," he said. "I've tried it every way, and every way's bad. So I'm going to say it in order, the way I'd give you a report, and you can stop me."

"I won't stop you."

"Two weeks ago," he said, "on the thirty-ninth night of the six, at Torvin's, I was going back through the Log on the cot, and something came."

He told her about the quiet before a thought. He told her about the dark place behind his breastbone where the sigil had gathered at Kindling, and had been dark ever since, and how it had still been dark, and how something had been written in it anyway. He opened the Log to the back, to the page where he had copied it, and turned it round on his knee so that she could read it, and she leaned forward on the bed and read it with her lips moving very slightly, the way she read the board.

*FRAGMENT ACQUIRED. [unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial. Tier equivalent: unknown.*

She read it twice. Then she sat back against the wall and looked at him, and did not say anything, and waited.

"The drop," said Cael. "Under your backhand, in the grey half, the first time. Dessa's fourth exchange, going across. Some of Sarel. The step I did by the cot that night, on purpose, badly." He made himself keep looking at her. "I've watched you more than I've watched anybody in my life. Every morning for three months. I don't know how it works. I don't know if watching's even the thing, or only part of the thing. I don't know how much of it there is, or whether it's still coming. But it says Wind, and the only Wind I've ever watched is you."

He stopped, because the next part was the part he had rehearsed most and trusted least, and then he said it plainly, because plain was the only way it came out true.

"Some of it's yours."

---

Lira heard it, and for a moment did not feel anything at all.

That surprised her. She had been sitting on the end of her own bed for a quarter of an hour before he knocked, with her knees up and her back against her own wall, getting ready to feel something, because a boy who said *partly yours* in the grey half with his ears going red was a boy who was going to say a thing that would need feeling about. She had guessed at it, the way you guess at the weight of a sack before you lift it. She had guessed that it was the drop, and Renn's third exchange, and the hand at the board on the first day; she had kept those three in a row in her head for two months. She had guessed, some nights, that the row was all one thing. She had even guessed, once or twice, lying awake, that the one thing might have something to do with her.

But guessing at the weight was not the same as lifting it, and now it was in her arms, and for a breath or two she only held it and could not tell what it weighed.

Then the first thing came, and it was not the thing she wanted.

It was the desk. It came up out of nowhere, as it did sometimes, quick and sour: the long room and the woman behind the desk, and the feeling of something she had done with her own body, in front of witnesses, being turned over in somebody else's hands and made into something that was not hers any more. It lasted about as long as it took her to recognize it. She looked at it, and she thought, *no, that's not this*, and she put it down. She was proud of herself for that afterward; it had not been easy, and nobody saw.

Cael was sitting on her floor with his book open on his knees and his face very carefully not doing anything. She knew that face. It was the face he made at his mark when he had been hit somewhere that mattered and did not want the other fighter to know where. He was waiting to find out where she was going to hit him.

"All right," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she had expected. "I'm going to say this first, so it's said, because I think you're braced for me to pretend it isn't. That's strange, Cael. That is a very strange thing to be told on a Saturday afternoon in your own room. I don't want you thinking I think it's ordinary."

"I don't think it's ordinary."

"Good. Then we agree it's strange." She looked at the book on his knees, at the four plain lines copied out in his best hand. "And it says Wind."

"Yes."

"And you think it's mine."

"Some of it."

She sat with that. She waited for it to feel like something being taken, because that was what it would have felt like once, and it did not. She waited a while longer, to be sure, and it still did not.

What it felt like instead was stranger, and she had to go round it twice before she could find the edges of it. It was like coming home and finding a letter on the table in your own handwriting, which you did not remember writing, telling somebody something kind. It was something of hers, which she had not given, that had gone out of her anyway and done some good somewhere, and she was only finding out now, after the fact, when it was already done and could not be taken back or given again. It was a very odd way to learn you had been generous. She found that she did not mind it at all.

"It isn't theft," she said.

He looked up.

"I thought it might feel like that. Hearing it. I thought, if he ever says what it is, and it's me, it'll feel like the academy." She turned her hands over on her knees and looked at them. "It doesn't. You didn't take anything. You didn't even know you were doing it. You just paid me more attention for three months than anybody's ever paid me in my whole life, and something about you is built to take attention like that and make it into something you can use." She looked up again. "I don't know what that is. I don't think you do either. But it's not stealing. Stealing's when somebody's got less afterward. I haven't got less. I checked." She lifted her chin at the door. "I did the step at the post this morning before you came, twenty times. It's all still there."

Cael did not say anything for a while. He looked at the floor between his boots, and she watched his face try three or four things and give each of them up.

"Thank you," he said at last.

"Don't thank me. I haven't done anything but not be stupid about it, which is a very low wall to climb, and I'm a bit insulted you thought I'd struggle with it." But she was nearly smiling, and she let him see it, because he needed to see something.

---

Then she asked him the question that had come into her head while she was finding the edges of the other thing, and she asked it carefully, because it was not a question she knew how to ask.

"Does it feel like anything? Having it."

He looked up at her.

"I've had mine since I was fourteen," she said. "It was declared. The Arbiter said it, and it was mine, and I've never once had to wonder where it came from or whose it was. It's just the way my legs work. I don't know what it's like to have something arrive the other way." She hesitated. "If you don't want to—"

"No. I want to." He thought about it for a long time, the way he thought about everything, frowning at the floor, and she watched him do it and did not hurry him. "It feels borrowed," he said. "Not in a bad way. You know how a coat that's yours fits differently from one you've borrowed, even when it's the right size? You can always tell. It's like that. Except that this coat fits better every time I put it on, better than it should, for how long I've had it." He looked at his hands. "And I don't know if that's because it's turning into mine, or because I'm only getting better at wearing other people's coats."

Lira looked at him.

"That's either very good," she said, "or very strange."

"Can't it be both?"

"Everything with you is both. I've given up being surprised." She said it with a fondness she did not bother to hide, because it was her room and her door was locked and there was nobody to hide it from. Then she reached down beside the bed and picked up her throat-sweet tin, for something to hold, and turned it over in her hands.

"Now I'll tell you something," she said. "Because you've told me yours, and it would be a poor kind of rule if it only went one way."

She told him about the row of three: the hand at the board on the first day, going to a strap that was not there; the third exchange against Renn; the drop under her backhand, lower than she had ever asked. She told him that she had kept the three in a row in her head with nothing written over them, and had never said so. Then she told him the worst of it, which was the eleven mornings. She had gone into the empty yard three mornings running before he came and tried his drop, her own backhand turned into his drop, eleven times, and had found that it was easy to do and impossible to do in time.

"You can only already be going," she said. "That's what I found out. You can't decide to do it. If you decide, you're late." She turned the tin over. "I kept that for two months. I told myself I was waiting till you had something to tell me."

He was looking at her very hard.

"So you knew," he said.

"I didn't know. I had a row of three and a guess. That's not knowing. You taught me that." She put the tin down on the bed beside her. "But I wasn't surprised, today. Not by the thing. Only by it being me." She looked at the book on his knees. "Can I see the back?"

He held it out without a word, and she took it, and turned the pages slowly on her knees.

She read *First instance* and the drop. She read the four, numbered, in his careful hand, the cart and Renn and the drop and Dessa. She read *What changed: me.* She read the notice again, and then, under it, a box drawn round two lines.

*Not yet. I'm not hiding it. I just can't explain it yet, not even to myself.*

Beside the box, smaller, it said *not sure it is honest*.

She looked at that for a long time.

"It was about half," she said at last. "Honest. If you want to know." She did not say it unkindly. "The half that wasn't, you've just paid for, sitting on my floor." She closed the book and gave it back to him. "That's what the box is for, I suppose. So you'd know which half."

"Yes."

"Good. Keep the box." She got up off the bed and went to the window and stood under the sloping ceiling without hitting her head, looking out at Torvin's chimney. "You can show me the back whenever you like, now. You don't have to. But you can."

---

He went before the lamps, because the widow would be awake by then, and because there was nothing more that needed saying, and they both knew it.

Lira stood in the door and watched him go down the stair, and the fourth step talked under him, and he said *I know* to it out loud, which was what she said to it, and which he could not have known. Then she shut the door, and turned the key, and sat down on the floor in the middle of the worn strip where he had sat, because it was still warm.

She sat there until it was too dark to see the paper on the cracked pane.

She thought about the drop, and about Dessa's fourth, and about the third exchange against Sarel, when she had stood at the barrel with the rope printing itself into her palms and watched him move the way she moved. She had thought then that she was watching her teaching come up out of him. That was true. She thought now that it had not been the only thing coming up, and that she would never be able to sort the two apart, and that she did not want to. She thought that something she had been, every morning, without trying, had gone into a boy from Denvash and come out of him again in front of the whole yard against a Bronze, and that she had stood there and been proud of it before she knew what it was. She was still proud of it, now that she did.

It was the strangest present she had ever been given: she had given it herself, without knowing, and been thanked for it afterward on her own floor.

She thought about what she would do with it, and decided, sitting there in the dark, that she would do the plainest thing. She would go on teaching him. She would go on being exactly what she was every morning in the grey half, as she had been for three months, only now she would know, and he would know she knew. Whatever was coming across, if anything was, would come across between two people with their eyes open. That seemed to her a better way for anything to happen than the way most things happened.

And the next time he said *I don't know* about it, she would say *neither do I*, and mean it, and they would not know together, which was a different thing from not knowing alone.

She got up off the floor at last and lit her candle end in its tin cup, and saw by its light that she had been smiling for some time without knowing it, the way you find you have been humming.

*Some of it's yours.*

She thought she would keep that. Not in a book; she did not keep a book. In the place where she kept the things that were hers.

# Chapter 43 — Three More Weeks

He wrote to Hesk that night, at the kitchen table at Torvin's, after everybody else had gone up.

He had not meant to do it then. He had meant to sleep on it, and write on Sunday, and post on Monday as he always did, with the west bag at noon. But when he came in from the lane in the dark, Torvin's wife was banking the range and the table was empty and scrubbed, and there was a lamp on it, and he sat down at it with the Log and a sheet of paper and found that he could not get up again.

He had told Lira. That had been the promise: when he knew what it was, really knew, she would be the first. He still did not know what it was. But he had found out, sitting on her floor, that not knowing was not a reason to keep a thing, only a reason to say *I don't know* beside it. And she had been the first, and now there was somebody second.

He sat with the pen for a long time. Then he wrote.

*Hesk,*

*There are five things I haven't told you. I've counted them every time I sealed a letter since the end of the second month, so I know the number exactly. I'm going to tell you all five now, in order, as plainly as I can, and then I'm going to tell you why I didn't before, and you can decide what you think of that.*

*The first was on the road, on the third day, before I ever got here. A cart came round a bend behind me and I stepped out of its way before I heard it. I told the carter I'd heard it. I don't think I did.*

*The second was my first bout, against Renn, in the third exchange. He threw a figure at me that should have caught me whatever I did, and I wasn't where it went. I didn't choose where I went. I tore the page out of the book where I'd written it, and kept it in my coat for a month, and didn't read it.*

*The third was in the yard, in the mornings, with Lira. She threw a backhand and I dropped under it, lower than I've ever dropped, in a way that wasn't mine. She asked me how I knew it was coming and I said I didn't know, and that was true.*

*The fourth was the Dessa fight. I told you I built the third exchange, and I did. I didn't tell you about the fourth, because I didn't build it. It happened the way the drop happened. Your line about a win you built sat on the page right over the place where I hadn't written it, and I read it three times.*

*The fifth was two weeks ago, on the thirty-ninth night of the six weeks, on my cot at Torvin's. Something came up where the Arbiter used to be, written in the dark. I'll copy it exactly, because you'd want it exact.*

He copied the four lines from the back of the Log, letter by letter, and checked them twice.

*I think it's Lira's. Some of it. I think it came from watching her every morning for three months, but I don't know that, and I've spent the last fortnight finding out how much I don't know. I can make the step on purpose now, badly. It costs something in the breath and the hip that I can't measure yet. I tried to get something from a second person, a Force fighter, by watching him two whole days. Nothing came that I could stand on.*

*I told Lira today. I'd promised her she'd be the first, before you, before anybody, and she was. She didn't flinch. She said it wasn't theft, and that she'd checked, and she hadn't got less.*

*Why I didn't tell you. At first it was because I didn't know what it was, and I didn't want to send you something that would frighten you a week late, with nothing I could do about it from here. You know what's in the archive better than I do. Then it was because I'd promised Lira first. Those are both true. But the first one is the same reason you gave me at the kitchen table on the second morning, and I was angry with you for it then, and I think I was right to be. So I'm not going to use it any more.*

*You told me once to tell you as it comes. This is how it came. I'm sorry it came all at once.*

*I'm all right. I'm well. I'm not frightened, most of the time. I'll tell you the next thing when it happens, and not five things later.*

*— Cael*

He read it over, twice, by the lamp. It was the longest letter he had ever written, and there was nothing in it that he did not know to be true. When he had finished the second reading, he folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for Monday, and then sat at the scrubbed table with the lamp turned low and counted, as he always did now, what he had not told Hesk.

There was nothing to count.

He sat with that for a long time. It did not feel the way he had thought it would. He had thought it would feel like putting down something heavy, and it did a little, but mostly it felt like having opened a door in winter: colder, and a great deal more air. Somewhere in Denvash, in a week, a man would sit on the step of his workshop with this in his hands, and be frightened, and there was nothing Cael could do about that. He had decided it was better than the other thing anyway.

Torvin said *Lamps* through the floorboards from upstairs, though there was only one lamp left lit in the house, and it was Cael's. He turned it out.

---

On Sunday morning, in the grey half, Lira did the step for him on purpose.

She did not say she was going to. She was at the post when he came in through the gate with the bread, as she always was, striking the canvas low and stepping round it. She took the loaves off him and put them on top of the post and tore them and gave him the larger half, as she always did. Then, while he was still eating, she went to her mark and stood there in the frost with her hands loose at her sides and looked at him.

"Watch," she said.

He stopped chewing.

She did it slowly, slower than she had ever done it for him, slower than she had done it on the first morning she showed it to him, when he had not known what he was looking at. She did the two empty touches, high and light, one from each hand, so slowly that he could see her fingers open at the end of each. Then the drop of the hip, and the turn, the beginning of everything, the part he had watched a hundred times from the inside and never once been able to see. Then the ground folding, which he could see now, from out here, as a kind of shortening in the air between her front foot and where it landed. Then the backhand coming round at the height of his head, slow as a door swinging in no wind, and stopping by his ear.

She stood there with her hand by his ear and looked at him.

"Again?"

"Lira—"

"I'm not doing it so it'll go into you." She dropped her hand. "I don't know if that's how it works. You don't either. It might be watching, it might be something else, it might be the weather. I'm doing it because yesterday I found out something of mine had been going across to you for three months without either of us knowing, and I'd rather it went across with both of us looking." She shrugged, a little stiffly, as though the shrug were new to her. "If it's going to happen anyway, I want to be in the room. That's all."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and watched.

She did it again, slowly. Then at half speed, and then at the speed she used against Brenna, so that the ground went short between one breath and the next and the backhand was by his ear before he had seen it start. She did it ten times. On the tenth her weight went a hair past the end of the backhand onto her right foot, and he said "one finger," at once, and she said "one," to herself, pleased, and pushed her hair off her face with her wrist.

The girls on the wall had come while she was doing it, and were watching with their bread forgotten in their hands. One of them said something to the other, and the other one shook her head.

"They'll be doing it on the wall by tomorrow," said Lira, without looking round. "Badly."

"Like me."

"Worse than you. You've had more practice." She came and sat on the low wall beside him and took her own bread back off the post. "Now you."

"Now me what?"

"You show me what it looks like when you do it. On purpose. The bad one. The one by the cot." She tore her bread. "I've never seen it. I've seen the other one, against Dessa and Sarel, from the rope. I want to see the one you can ask for."

So he stood up in the grey half and showed her.

It was as clumsy as it had been every night. He dropped his hip and turned and put his foot down, slowly, stiffly, and the dirt between his foot and where it landed went short by the width of a hand, and his breath went, and the ache came into the joint of the left hip. He stood there afterward breathing as if he had run up the stairs at Torvin's, with his hand pressed against the hip.

Lira was looking at the place where his foot had landed.

"Again," she said, very quietly.

He did it again. It was worse, as it always was the second time.

She did not say anything for a while. Then she got up and went to the place where his foot had come down, and crouched, and put her palm flat on the frozen dirt there, as she had put it on the iron stake at Talis.

"It's mine," she said. "The shape of it. Badly. The hip's wrong, and you're far too slow, and you stop at the end as if you're waiting to be told you can." She looked up at him from the dirt. "But it's mine. I'd know it anywhere. It's like hearing somebody hum a tune you made up when you were small, a long way off, out of tune."

"Does that bother you?"

She thought about it, crouched there with her palm on the ground.

"No," she said at last. "It ought to. I keep looking for it to, and it doesn't." She stood up and dusted her hand on her trousers. "It costs you, though. I could see it. Your breath went, and you went white round the mouth." She frowned at him. "What does it cost?"

"I don't know. Breath. The hip. More the second time."

"Write it down. Every time. How many, how much, how long till it goes." She went back to her mark. "That's not me telling you it matters. I don't know if it matters. It's me telling you that a thing that costs you something you can't measure is a thing that's going to cost you more than you think one day, at the worst possible time." She brought her hands up. "Box drill. Full speed. And don't you dare do that in it."

---

On Monday morning, before he could post his letter, Vell sent the pie boy to the post in the grey half to say that she wanted them both at her table before the yard opened, and that she did not mean after the bread.

She was sitting square to the back wall with her tea and the ledger closed in front of her, which he had never seen before; the ledger was always open, even when there was nothing in it to write. She did not look up when they came across the frozen dirt, but turned her tea a quarter turn by its handle and waited until they were standing in front of the table.

"There's a man coming through in three weeks," she said. "Feryn. Bronze Rank 2, Pressure Path."

Lira went very still beside him. Cael felt it rather than saw it.

"He's not from here," said Vell. "He's from nowhere in particular, these days. He's touring the circuits before his own evaluation next season, taking one bout in a town and moving on. He likes to test himself against people he hasn't met. He's posted for interesting opponents, any rating so long as it's an assessed one, and a purse that most people in this yard wouldn't see in a season." She looked at the closed ledger. "There's a gate on it as well. He'll fight here once, on a Monday at noon, because that's when he's passing, and then he'll be gone."

"And you're telling us," said Lira slowly.

"I'm telling you because he holds an assessed circuit rating," said Vell, and she nodded at Cael without looking at him, "and so does he. As of three weeks ago, in my hand. I'd rather you heard it from me than from Marrow's slate." She turned the tea again. "I don't put anybody in against Feryn. I tell them he's coming, and what he is, and if they want him they ask me, and I decide. That's all this is."

Cael looked at the closed book.

"What's Pressure like?"

"Heavy." Vell considered. "I've watched him in three cities over six or seven years. The first time he was Copper and nineteen and he lost to a man with one arm on a wet night, and laughed about it all the way to the water bucket. He's a good deal better than that now. It isn't a strike, what he does. It's more like weather. People who've stood in front of it say it's like the air leaning on you." She looked at him then, properly, for the first time. "I don't know anybody in this district who's fought it and been glad they did."

Lira said nothing. He felt her not say it.

He thought about it the way he thought about everything worth thinking about, turning it over and looking at it from the sides he had not looked at yet. He did not think about whether he wanted it, because he could feel that he did, all through him, and that was not a reason. Three weeks was long enough to watch a man, if the man fought anywhere he could be watched. It was not long enough to build anything like what he had built for Dessa, or for Dellin, or for Corbin. And he had never seen Pressure at all.

"Who else is entering," he said, "that I could watch?"

Vell looked at him.

"On his tour," Cael said. "Before he gets here. Anybody he's fought, anywhere near. Anybody who's going to fight him between now and then, that I could get to."

There was a short silence. Then Lira laughed.

It was the short real one, the one that surprised her, and it came out of her into the cold air over Vell's table and made the crows on the eaves shift their feet.

"That's it?" she said. "That's all you've got to say? A Bronze Rank 2 is coming in three weeks, a man who's been fighting since before you could read, and the first thing out of your mouth isn't *am I ready*, or *is it too soon*. It's *who can I study*."

"Is that the wrong question?"

"It's exactly the right question. It's just not the one most people your age would ask." She was still smiling, but something underneath the smile had gone serious and stayed there. "You're fourteen, Cael. I'm not saying it to talk you out of it. I'm saying it because I want you to hear it out loud, once, before you decide anything. You've got a very bad habit of treating how old you are as if it didn't go in the sum. It goes in the sum. It just doesn't have to be the whole sum."

He thought about that. He could see that it was true.

"So I need three more weeks," he said.

Vell made a small sound in her throat that might, on anybody else, have been a laugh.

"Nobody's entering," she said. "He fought two days west a fortnight ago, and he's gone south since. I don't know who he'll fight between here and there, and neither does he. You'll not see him fight before you see him in my circle, if you see him at all." She opened the ledger at last, and found her place, and picked up her pen. "You've got till Friday to tell me whether you want him. I'd think about it longer than you've thought about it so far."

"I'll think about it."

"You'll think about it, and then you'll say yes." Vell did not look up. "I've watched you think. Go away. You're standing in my light."

They walked down the lane together in the frost, and Lira did not say anything for a whole street. Then, at the corner by the board, she stopped and turned round and looked at him.

"Survivable," she said.

"You haven't said that."

"I'm saying it now. I'm going to work out whether it's true, and if it isn't, I'll tell you, and you'll listen." She folded her arms. "That's the rule. It's been the rule since the first morning of the six weeks. It didn't stop being the rule because the six weeks ran out."

"I know."

"Good." She looked at the board, where Sunday's card was already nailed up in Vell's upright pencil on the back of a bill for lamp wicks. "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. Not with momentum. You've been going on momentum since Corbin, and momentum's a very good way to walk into a Bronze with nothing in your hands." She took her chalk out of her pocket and turned it over in her fingers, though there was no post here to make a mark on. "First thing. I want a list. Every fighter within two days' walk who's ever gone three exchanges with a Bronze and come out the other side with something to say about it. Any Bronze, but Pressure if there's one. We find them, we ask them, and then we know something."

"Who do we ask?"

"Everybody." She put the chalk away. "You take Torvin's. I'll take the yards and the eating house. We'll meet at the well tonight with what we've got."

He posted the letter to Hesk at noon on the way, at the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps. The clerk with the inky fingers read the address out to the whole hut, as he read every address, and weighed the letter in his hand and looked at Cael over it. "Thick one," he said, and hung it on the third hook for the west bag. Cael stood and looked at it hanging there among the others for longer than he needed to. In a week a man would sit down on a workshop step in Denvash with it. There was nothing to be done about that now, and he found that he was glad there was nothing, and went back up the steps.

He took Torvin's, and Torvin's was Doss.

He found him at supper, at the far end of the long table where nobody sat, with his bad leg out stiff along the bench and his pipe beside his bowl, unlit. Cael sat down across from him, which nobody did either. The brothers stopped arguing for a moment to watch him do it, and Torvin's wife looked at them both from the range with her spoon in the air.

"Three exchanges with a Bronze," said Cael. "Does anybody in the district know about that?"

Doss looked at him for a long time with his flattened face doing nothing.

"I was one," he said at last. "For a season and a half. I went more than three with a good many people." He picked up his pipe and looked at it. "That's not what you're asking."

"No."

"You're asking who went three with a Bronze and wasn't one." Doss turned the pipe over. "Pressure?"

"Yes."

Doss was quiet again. Then he set the pipe down beside his bowl, very precisely, as if he were laying a tool on a bench.

"There was a boy, few years back, from the river workshops. Went four with a Pressure man at Fenrow, and walked out. He's gone to the coast, I heard. Six months ago, or more." He took up his spoon. "And there's Corvane."

"Who's Corvane?"

"Lives down past the brickworks, at the far end. Trains young ones for coin, when she can get them. She's old now." He ate a mouthful. "She lost to every Pressure fighter on this river for ten years. Then she stopped fighting them and started studying them. She'll know more about it than anybody you'll find this side of the bridge." He looked up from his bowl. "She won't want to talk to you."

"Why not?"

"She doesn't want to talk to anybody," said Doss, and went back to his stew, and that was the end of it.

---

Lira had two names from the yards and one from Amrit's, and one of hers was the same boy who had gone to the coast, which they agreed did not count twice. The other was a woman who sold eels on the fish steps, who had fought a Bronze at the Cinder House eight or nine years ago and lasted, the girls on the wall said, longer than anybody expected.

They went to see her on Tuesday morning after the grey half, because Lira said there was no point keeping a name in your pocket.

She was a broad, red-faced woman in a sacking apron, with her sleeves rolled up past the elbow in the cold and her arms in a tub of eels to the wrist, and she laughed when Lira told her what they wanted. It was a big laugh, and the men unloading the barges at the bottom of the steps looked round at it.

"Three exchanges," she said. "With Hollow-eyed what's-his-name, the Bronze from across the bridge. Yes. I remember." She lifted an eel out of the tub and looked at it and put it back. "I was a Copper 4 and I thought a great deal of myself. He put me down in the first exchange and I got up. He put me down in the second and I got up. In the third I didn't get up for a quarter of an hour, and when I did, I'd forgotten what day it was." She beamed at them. "And that was the last time I ever stood in a circle, and I've never once missed it."

"What did you learn?" said Cael. "From the three."

She thought about it seriously, with her arms in the eels.

"Don't," she said.

There was a pause.

"Don't what?"

"Just don't." She laughed again, so that the tub shook. "That's the whole of it, my love. Three exchanges with a Bronze, and that's what they taught me, and it's the best thing anybody ever taught me. Don't." She lifted an eel and held it out to Lira. "Do you want one of these? They're very fresh. Half price for fighters."

They did not want one. They walked back up the fish steps with the barges going down the river behind them, and Lira's face was perfectly straight for exactly as long as it took them to reach the top, and then it was not.

"Don't," she said, and had to stop walking.

"It's good advice."

"It's the best advice in the district." She leaned on the post at the top of the steps with her shoulders shaking. "We should put it on the board. Under Vell's card. *Bronze, Monday at noon. Don't.*" She wiped her eyes with her wrist. "That's one name used up and one gone to the coast. Which leaves your Doss's."

"He said she won't want to talk to us."

"Everybody wants to talk to somebody." Lira straightened up. "You just have to work out what they want to talk about. Come on."

---

Corvane lived at the far end of the district, past the brickworks, where the kilns made the air taste of soot and the lanes were all built of the bricks the kilns had cracked, and nobody had wanted. It was an hour's walk from the well. Her house was the last in a row of low brick houses with their backs to the river, and beside it, behind a wall, there was a yard. They could hear someone in the yard, through the wall, giving instructions in a dry level voice that did not carry, and somebody young answering *yes* every time.

Lira stopped him at the gate.

"Wait here."

"Why?"

"Because she's an old fighter who doesn't want to talk to anybody, and you're a boy everybody's started talking about, and if you come in with me she'll decide you're a show and shut the gate." She took her jacket off and folded it over her arm, so that she would look like what she was, a fighter come to ask a fighter something. "Let me find out what she wants to talk about."

He waited in the lane by the wall for most of an hour. The voice in the yard stopped, and a young man came out through the gate rubbing his shoulder and walked off without looking at Cael, and then the voice started again, lower, talking to Lira. He could not hear the words, only the shape of the conversation going back and forth across the yard. Lira's voice went up once, sharply, and came down. There was a long silence. Then the dry voice said something short, and Lira laughed, not the surprised laugh but the other one, the one she used on Marrow when she had got the price she wanted.

She came out through the gate with her jacket over her arm and her face pink with cold and something else.

"Thursday," she said. "The afternoon. Two hours. She wants a mark for it, which is too much, and I've paid half already and you'll pay the rest." She put her jacket on. "And she wants to look at you."

"What did you say to her?"

"I asked her what she'd studied for ten years and never once had anybody worth showing it to." Lira did her buttons up one by one. "And then I told her what you do, which I hope you don't mind, because it was the thing that did it. She'd heard. Everybody's heard something. She'd heard *the Denvash boy at Torvin's*, and *past Corbin*, and she'd thought it was talk." She looked at him. "She asked me whether it was true you watched Renn for two months and found his heel. I said yes. She asked whether it was true you'd quit watching in two exchanges against Sarel and done something else. I said yes. And she was quiet for a long time, and then she said that she had never in her life met anybody who could do both, and that she supposed she'd better see one before she died."

"And you laughed."

"She meant it to be funny." Lira started back down the lane toward the kilns. "I think. She's very hard to read. She's harder than Corbin." She glanced back at him. "You'll like her."

That night, on the cot, he wrote at the top of a clean page in the front of the Log: *Feryn. Bronze Rank 2, Pressure. Three weeks.* Under it he wrote: *Pressure: heavy. Like weather. Like the air leaning on you (Vell, h). Don't (the eel woman, h).*

Then he looked at the page for a long time, and wrote under that: *Corvane, Thursday.* There was nothing else he knew, and he had stopped pretending, a long time ago, that a short page meant a lazy one.

On Wednesday morning, before the grey half, he went to Vell's table and told her yes, two days before she had asked him to. She did not look up. She wrote it down.

# Chapter 44 — The Build

Corvane was waiting for them in her gateway on Thursday afternoon with her hands folded on the head of a stick, and she looked at Cael for a long time before she said anything at all.

She was small and spare and very straight, older than the old man at the top of the lane of yards, with grey hair cut close to her head like a boy's and a face all bones and weather. She wore a man's coat buttoned to the throat and a pair of boots that had been mended so many times that there was more mending than boot. Her eyes were pale and steady and did not blink much. When she looked at him, Cael had the feeling he sometimes had in Hesk's workshop, when Hesk held a part up to the window and turned it slowly, looking for the flaw he already knew was there.

"Taller than I was told," she said. "Thinner. Stands like a Denvash boy." She looked at his feet. "Somebody's been at his feet."

"I have," said Lira.

"I can see that. Not badly." Corvane turned and went into the yard, leaning on the stick, and they followed her. "You can stand by the wall, girl. If you talk, you go."

The yard was small and walled in brick on three sides, with the river going by beyond the fourth wall, which was only waist high, and the smell of the kilns coming over it. There was no groove and no circle, only a square of trodden dirt with a ring scratched in it with the point of the stick, and a sack of sand hanging from a bracket on the wall, patched all over. Corvane went to the middle of the dirt and stood there, and Cael went and stood across from her, because it seemed to be what she wanted.

"Your friend tells me you watch," she said.

"Yes."

"Tell me what you watch."

He told her. He told her about Hesk and the community hall, hips and feet, and the three exchanges. He told her what he had found since: Renn's heel and the dip in Brenna's shield, the breath before the heat, Petra's lean, Dessa's late back foot, and Corbin reading feet at the mark. He told her about Talis and the ground. He did not tell it well, because he had never had to say it all at once before, out loud, to somebody who was listening the way she was listening, without any expression at all.

When he had finished, she was quiet for a while.

"And Sarel?"

"I couldn't find anything. She was too fast. So I stopped looking."

"In two exchanges."

"Yes."

Corvane nodded slowly. Then she put the point of her stick into the dirt between them and began to draw.

She drew a figure, a plain stick figure such as a child draws, standing upright. In the middle of its chest she drew a spiral, wound tight, round and round, smaller and smaller, until it was nearly a dot. Beside the figure she made a row of short strokes, one, two, three, four, like a tally.

"That's what you'll be standing in front of," she said. "Pressure. And everything you've just told me is no good to you."

---

She talked for a quarter of an hour without stopping, and it was the most anybody had said to him at one time since Hesk on the night before Kindling.

She did not talk the way Lira talked, quick and sideways and laughing. She talked the way Hesk talked about a load figure, flat and exact, leaving out everything a sensible person could work out for himself and assuming he was a sensible person. Every few sentences she touched the drawing with the point of the stick, the spiral or the strokes, and went on.

"A Blade strikes. A Force man strikes and then shoves. Ember heats. Every one of them has a moment, one moment, where the thing goes, and every one of them has to get ready for that moment somewhere in the body, and that getting ready is what you've been reading. The heel. The breath. The lean." She tapped the figure's feet. "Pressure has no one moment. It has to be gathered. It's built, the way you'd wind a spring, a turn at a time." She touched the spiral. "And while it's being built, the body has to hold it. Somewhere. All of it, the whole time, and it gets heavier with every turn. That holding is the only true thing a Pressure fighter will ever show you. Everything else he does is there to hide it."

"Where does he hold it?"

"That depends on the fighter." She tapped the spiral again, in the middle of the chest. "But it's always low and always in the trunk, never in the hands. The hands are where it goes, at the end. The hands are what everybody watches." She looked at him. "That's the mistake. Everybody who has ever stood in front of Pressure has watched for the moment it lets go. They watch the hands, or the shoulders, or whatever moves last. And by the time they see it go, they're already inside it." She drew a line from the figure's hands outward, a long one, across the dirt almost to his boots. "It doesn't come at you like a fist, from one place. It comes like the air leaning, all across the front of you at once. You can't step out of the way of it once it's come. There isn't room."

She tapped the row of strokes.

"So you don't watch it go. You watch it being built. A fighter who's building stands differently from a fighter who's only standing, and it's slow to see, and it's very small, and he'll be covering it with every other part of himself the whole time. But it gives you warning. Not much. If you see it early enough." She looked at the strokes. "That's heartbeats. Four, for me, when I was young. I'm Copper, and I was never a good Copper. A Bronze will build in fewer. A good Bronze, in one." She lifted the stick out of the dirt. "Now stand there, and I'll show you, and you can tell me what you see."

She stepped back to the far side of the scratched ring and stood.

It was a plain stance, feet a little apart, hands loose at her sides, the stick leaning against her leg. She did not seem to do anything. Then she began to walk, slowly, round the inside of the ring, and as she walked her hands moved: up a little, and out, and down again, like somebody explaining something with no words. Her shoulders went up and down with the walking. Her head turned to follow him.

"Tell me," she said.

"Your hands are—"

The air leaned on him.

He had not seen anything go. There was no flash, no shimmer, nothing in her hands or her face. There was only, all at once, the air across the front of him going heavy from his knees to the top of his head, and pushing, as though somebody had laid a broad flat hand on his chest and arms and face at the same time, from a pace away, and leaned on it. It was not hard. He did not fall. He went back a full step with his heels sliding in the dirt, and the air let go of him as quickly as it had come, and he stood there with his breath gone short and his hands up in front of him, and Corvane was standing where she had been, with her hands loose at her sides.

By the wall, Lira had put her hand flat on the bricks.

"You watched my hands," said Corvane.

"Yes."

"Everybody does. Again."

She walked. Her hands moved. He made himself look away from them, at her shoulders, and the air leaned on him again, and he went back a step. She walked again, and he watched her feet, and the air leaned on him, and he went back a step.

"Again."

He did not know how many times she did it. Eight. Ten. He looked at everything he knew how to look at, the hips and the feet, the shoulders, the breath, her face. Every time he was looking at something, the air came and put him back a step, and every time he could not have said, afterward, when it had begun. By the eighth time his chest ached from being leaned on and his heels had dug two shallow troughs in her dirt, and he understood why the eel woman had laughed.

"What do you see?" said Corvane.

"Nothing."

"What did you look at?"

He told her: hands, shoulders, feet, breath, face.

"Everything that moves," said Corvane. "And what doesn't move?"

He stood there.

"I'll do it slowly," she said. "Watch what doesn't move."

---

She walked the ring once more, as slowly as an old woman walking to market, with her hands moving and her shoulders going up and down and her head turning. He did not watch any of it. He watched, as well as he could, all the parts of her that were not doing anything; it was harder than he had expected, like trying to listen to the silence between the notes of a tune. His eyes kept going to the hands, because the hands were moving, and he kept bringing them back.

And there, under the moving, low down in her body, below the ribs and above the belt, something was holding still.

It was very small. It was not a stiffness, exactly, nor a stillness you could have pointed at, but a kind of settling, as if the middle of her had gone heavier than the rest and stayed there while the rest of her walked about on top of it. Her shoulders went up and down, and it did not. Her hands drifted and turned, and it did not. It was as though she had swallowed a stone and were carrying it very carefully while pretending not to.

"There," he said.

The air leaned on him, and he went back a step, but he had said it first.

Corvane stopped walking.

She stood in her ring and looked at him with her pale eyes, and for the first time since the gate her face did something: not a smile, but a small shift at one corner of the mouth, as if a smile had been considered and put away for a better occasion.

"Where?"

He put his hand on his own body, below the ribs and above the belt.

"Something stays still," he said. "When the rest of you's moving. It's like you're carrying it."

"How long before I let go?"

He thought back. "Two breaths. Perhaps two and a half."

"I built for four." She leaned on her stick. "So you saw it at two. Halfway." She looked at him for a long moment. "Once is nothing, boy. Once is luck, or it's me walking slow for you. Again."

She did it again, and he saw it at two. She did it again, faster, and he saw it at one and a half. She did it again, faster still, and he saw nothing until the air was on him. She did it six more times, and he saw it three times and missed it three, and by the end he could feel his eyes aching in their sockets from looking at a thing that was not there, and he was more tired than Sarel had made him.

Then she stopped and sat down on an upturned bucket by the wall with both hands folded on the head of the stick, breathing harder than she wanted him to see.

"That's what you'll be doing," she said. "Against him. Only he won't walk slow for you, and he won't build for four. He'll build in one, or less, and he'll hide it under a great deal more than an old woman waving her hands about." She looked at the scratched figure in the dirt, which their feet had half rubbed out. "You'll read it late. Every time. If you're very good, and lucky, you'll read it a little less late each time, because that's what you are. I can see that's what you are." She looked up. "And it won't be enough. Not at Bronze. Not in three weeks. I want you to know that before you go in, not after."

"Then why read it at all?"

"Because a little less late is the difference between being knocked down and being knocked back." She nodded at the troughs his heels had dug. "And because there's nothing else, at Copper, against Pressure, but reading it. I looked for ten years. There isn't."

---

Lira had not said a word. When Corvane stood up and went into her house without saying goodbye, she came away from the wall and put Cael's coat round his shoulders, because he had forgotten he was not wearing it, and they let themselves out through the gate.

Corvane came back to the door before they had gone three houses down the lane. She stood in it with her stick, and called after them in her dry voice, not loudly.

"Boy."

He turned round.

"What are you? Your friend wouldn't say. She said that was yours to tell."

He had said it out loud to very few people since Weaver's Row: to Lira on the first day, at the board, and to a man from the Compact across a desk at the post. It came out of him a little more easily each time, and it still never came out easily.

"[SHATTERED]."

Corvane stood in her doorway and considered it.

"Never heard of it," she said. "Good. I've heard of nearly everything." She looked at him a moment longer. "Come back and tell me what he does. Afterward. If you can walk." Then she shut the door.

They walked back through the kilns in the early dark, with the soot in the air and the river going by on their left. Lira did not say anything for a long time. Then, at the corner of the brickworks, where the lane turned up toward the district, she said, "Ten years."

"What?"

"She lost to them for ten years, and then she studied them for ten more, and in all that time she never found anybody who could see the thing she'd found." Lira had her hands deep in her jacket pockets. "She told me that on Tuesday. Not like that. Sideways. She said she'd had forty students and none of them could ever see it, and she'd stopped showing it to anybody." She looked at the dark lane ahead. "Then you saw it at two, and she sat down on a bucket."

He had not thought of it like that.

"I can't do it," said Lira. "Build. I'm Wind; I haven't got anything to build with. But I can hold. I can stand there and get ready to step and not step, and hide it under something else, and you can try to see me getting ready." She glanced at him. "It's not the same. It's nowhere near the same. But it's the right shape, and it's what we've got, and we've got three weeks less two days."

"Every morning?"

"Every morning. And you'll draw that figure in the dirt with your eyes shut till you can see it standing up."

Supper at Torvin's was the stew with real vegetables in it, though nobody in the house had fought, because Torvin's wife had heard about Monday from the boots woman, who had heard it from the fish steps, and had decided that it counted in advance.

The whole table knew. The brothers had stopped arguing about the river to argue about the Bronze instead. The elder one held that Pressure was only Force made bigger, and the younger that it was a different thing altogether, like weather, because a man on the barges had told him so. Neither of them had ever seen it. They appealed to Cael, who said he had seen it that afternoon from an old woman with a stick and that it was not like Force at all. The younger brother was so pleased that he ate the elder brother's bread to celebrate.

Torvin sat at the head of the table and ate and said nothing until the bowls were nearly empty. Then he put his spoon down.

"Monday," he said.

"Yes."

Torvin looked at him for a while with the look he gave a coin he had already counted twice.

"Rent's paid to Sunday week," he said. "I looked." He picked his spoon up again. "And if you're carried home, they're to bring you in by the yard door. Not the front. People talk."

Torvin's wife hit him on the arm with her wooden spoon, not gently, and he went on eating as if he had not noticed.

At the far end of the table, where nobody sat, Doss had his bad leg out along the bench and his pipe beside his bowl. He did not look up until the brothers had gone up the stairs still arguing. Then he did.

"Corvane," he said.

"She showed me how it's built."

"She showed you." Doss turned his pipe over in his scarred fingers. "Did she sit down?"

Cael looked at him.

"Afterward. On a bucket." He did not know how Doss knew to ask it. "She was out of breath. She didn't want me to see."

Doss was quiet for a long time. He put the pipe down beside his bowl with great care, as he always did, as though it were a tool on a bench.

"Then she saw something," he said. "She doesn't sit down for nothing." He looked at the range, where the fire was going down, and not at Cael. "I'll tell you one thing. It's the only thing I know about Bronze that's worth the breath. At Copper, people learn you over a season. At Bronze they learn you in an exchange." He tapped the knee under the table with the stem of the pipe, twice, the way he did. "Whatever you do twice against him, you've done once too often. Remember that when you're standing there."

He got up, which took two movements, and rinsed his bowl at the basin, and went to the low brown door, and the smell of pipe smoke came out from under it a little later.

Cael sat at the table with his empty bowl and turned it over in his head, *once too often*, until Torvin's wife took the bowl away from him and told him he would wear it out.

That night, on the cot, he copied Corvane's figure into the front of the Log, with the spiral and the four strokes, as near as he could remember it. Then he wrote three pages under it, everything she had said, in the order she had said it. When he had finished, he read it through four times, and then once more aloud under his breath, the way he had read the citation in the reading room. By the fifth time it had stopped feeling like something he had been told and started to feel like something he knew. He had begun to understand, since the reading room, that this was how it worked: a thing went into him from outside, as a word or a drawing, and stayed there, and was gone over, and one day it was his.

He thought about that last sentence for a long time before he let himself write it down, because it sounded very like something else, and he did not know yet whether it was.

On Sunday Vell gave him a Copper 4 from Fenrow, a Stone man of about twenty-five who had come across for the afternoon card because he had heard there was a boy at the Cinder House who went five with Corbin, and he wanted to see for himself.

It was not the kind of bout anybody would talk about afterward, and Cael understood that before it began, and found that he did not mind.

The Fenrow man was square and slow, with the grit coming up off him and lying down flat the way it had come up off Dessa, and none of her patience. He rocked a little on his heels at his mark as Talis had, and looked at the crowd, and at Marrow's slate, and at the girls on the wall, and at everything except Cael.

Cael put his hand flat on the dirt at his mark for one breath before he stood.

The ground was cold, and hard under a soft skin of thaw. When the Fenrow man shifted his weight on his mark, something answered under Cael's palm, very faint, a small far-off hum like a glass set down at the other end of a long table. It was there and then it was not. Cael stood up and set his feet and did not let his face do anything.

"Copper Rank 4, Stone Path, of Fenrow," said Vell, and gave the man's name, which Cael heard and did not keep. "Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

In the first exchange he did nothing with it. He let the Fenrow man come, and plant, and strike, and was not where the strike went. He did not try to feel the hum through his boots; boots were not a cheek on the dirt, and he did not trust them. He watched instead, and found in the first exchange what he had half expected: the man's front knee bent a little before every step he meant to plant on, a small soft dip, as if the leg were testing the ground before it trusted it. It came a half beat before the plant, exactly where the hum had come under his hand.

*Two of them,* he thought, on his mark. *The ground and the knee. Saying the same thing.* He was very glad there were two. One tell was a guess. Two tells telling the same truth were a floor you could stand on.

In the second exchange he made sure. "Result stands as called," she said. "Unrated, over a Copper Rank 4, Stone Path, of Fenrow. Third exchange. Called. Witnessed and entered."

"That's what it's meant to look like," she said instead, quietly, as she held the coat for him to put his arms in. "Most of the time. When you've done the work. Nobody remembers it. It's not a story. It's just a bout you won." She did the top button of his coat up for him, which she had never done before, as if he were somebody's small brother. "You'll want more stories. Don't. A great many people in this district have been ruined by wanting a story every time."

At the top of the lane of yards, on the way home, the old man was standing in his gate in the dusk with his sleeves rolled down. He looked at Cael walking, at the way he was walking, which was the ordinary way, with nothing held against his chest and no limp.

He said nothing at all. He only nodded, once, very slightly, and went back to watching the woman with the shoulder.

Cael thought, walking on down toward Torvin's, that it was the best thing the old man had ever said to him.

Lira took him up on the cooper's roof that evening, because she said there were some things she did not want to say anywhere a wall could hear them.

They went up the ladder at the back of the cooper's shed in the last of the light and sat on the flat part of the roof with their backs against the chimney stack, which was warm from the cooper's fire below. "I've been doing a thing," she said at last. "In the mornings. Since you told me. I don't know if I should."

He waited.

"I've been doing things slower." She did not look at him. "Not all the time. Just now and then. The step, mostly. I catch myself doing it a little slower than I need to, so you'll see it better. And then I catch myself catching myself, and I don't know whether to stop." She pulled her knees up and put her arms round them. "Because if it's watching, then I'm helping. He thought about that seriously, because she had said it seriously.

"Hesk wrote me something once," he said. "When the man with the pin was walking about. *Don't change what you do.* Because changing it is the loudest thing you can do, and you never know who's listening." He looked at the bridge. "I don't know if that's right here. He stopped, because he had not known he thought that until he said it. "I'd rather you just did what you do. The way you did it before you knew. Fast, when it ought to be fast."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"That's what I'd decided too," she said. "On the floor, on Saturday, after you'd gone. I decided I'd go on being exactly what I was. "I know."

She turned her head and looked at him then, and he saw her see that he did know, better than anybody she had ever met, and saw her put that down carefully somewhere she would keep it.

# Chapter 45 — Word Ahead

Lira could not build, but she could hold, and for three weeks she held.

She stood at her mark in the grey half every morning with the frost on the dirt and the post at her back, and got ready to step, and did not step. She kept the readiness in her for a breath, or two, or three, while she did everything she could think of on top of it to hide it: she walked, and rolled her shoulders, and talked to him about the bread, and pushed her hair off her face with her wrist, and once, to make the girls on the wall laugh, sang the first two lines of a song about a miller's daughter. Then, when she had held it as long as she meant to, she went, with the two empty touches and the ground folding, and her palm came across his ribs or his shoulder or the back of his neck, and she said, "When?"

"Your second breath."

"I started on the first."

"I didn't see it on the first."

"Then that's when you saw it." She went back to her mark. "Again."

It was not Pressure, and they both knew it. A Wind fighter getting ready to step did not hold anything heavy in the middle of her body the way Corvane had; she held a lightness instead, a sort of tilt, as if the whole of her had decided to fall forward and was only waiting to be allowed. It was a different thing to look for, in a different place. But it was a thing that did not move under things that did, and it had to be found the same way, by watching what was not happening, and that was the part he had to learn.

He learned it slowly, the way he had learned the moving.

On the first morning he saw her readiness twice in thirty. By the fourth morning he was seeing it nine times in thirty, and by the end of the first week he was seeing it more often than not, and she had begun to change how she hid it. She stopped walking and stood still instead, and talked, and he found that a person standing still and talking was much harder to read than a person walking, because there was so little to tell the holding from. Then he learned that too, a little, and she changed again.

"You're getting better," she said on the eighth morning, breathing hard. "And I'm getting better at hiding it. So it looks the same. That's what it'll be like in there. Remember that. He'll be getting better at hiding it while you're getting better at seeing it, and it'll feel like you're standing still."

In the afternoons, when she went to her sparring hour across the district, he drew Corvane's figure.

He drew it in the dirt of Torvin's yard with a stick, by the pump, and in the margins of the Log, and on the back of a bill from the mender. Then he drew it with his eyes shut, on the inside of his eyelids, the stick figure and the spiral wound tight and the row of four strokes, until he could see it standing up and walking about on top of the stone it was carrying. Yeni watched him draw it once by the pump, from the kitchen door, and said nothing, and the next day there was a stub of red chalk on the low wall where he sat, which he had not put there.

He did not tell anybody about the step.

He practised it at the post when Lira was late, and by the cot at night, on purpose, as he had done since the thirty-ninth night. It was still clumsy. It was a little less clumsy than it had been, or he thought it was; the boards went short under him by the width of a hand, and once, on a night near the end of the first week, by perhaps a hand and a half. It still cost his breath and his hip, a little less each time, or a little more, depending on how tired he was before he began. It still never came clean when he called it.

Once, in the second week, it came without his calling it at all, in the box drill at full speed. Lira came at him from the corner he had not called, and the ground went short under him and he was out of her way before he had decided anything. She stopped dead, with her palm still out, and looked at him.

"That," she said.

"I know."

"Do it again."

He could not. That night he wrote it in the back of the Log as the fifth instance, the first since the notice, and the first in the grey half since the drop. But in the yard he tried for the rest of the morning and it did not come once, not when she came at him from the corner he had not called and not when she came at him from the one he had. By the end he was so tired from trying that his hips had gone square again. She stopped at last and sat on the low wall with her elbows on her knees, and he sat down beside her and they ate the bread.

"It's not going to be reliable," she said. "Not yet. You've had it a month. Give it a season before you expect it to come when it's called. My step didn't come when I called it for a year after I was declared, and it was mine from the day it was declared."

"I haven't got a season. I've got eleven days."

"Then on the twelfth day you'll have what you've got, and not a crumb more, and that's true whether you spend the eleven worrying about it or not." She tore her bread and gave him the larger half. "So don't spend them worrying. Spend them on the build. The build's yours. The step's—" She stopped, and looked at the bread in her hand. "The step's whatever it is. Don't stand in a circle in front of a Bronze waiting for it to come. If it comes, it comes. Read the build."

On the last night before the bout he could not sleep, and did not try very hard.

He lay on his right side on the cot with Yeni's lamp down to a bead and the wheezing man whistling by the door, and he did the thing he had done on the night before Kindling and the night before Renn and every night before a bout since: he went through it. Not the fight, which he did not know, but everything round it, the things he could count.

He counted three weeks of mornings in the grey half, twenty-one of them, and one morning in the box drill when the step had come without asking and never again. He counted Corvane's figure, drawn perhaps two hundred times. He counted two bouts won, a Fenrow Stone and a dyers' Shield, neither of which anybody would remember, both of which had been easy because he had done the work. He counted the money for Corvane, and the money for the gate, which Vell had said he would not have to pay because he was fighting in it, and Lira had said that was the only sensible thing anybody had said about the whole affair.

He counted what he knew about Feryn, which was what Vell had said, and what Renn had said, and what Corvane had said and shown him in a brick yard by the river. It came to a page and a half. He had gone into his first bout with Renn knowing nothing at all. He had gone into Dessa knowing everything there was to know. This was the first time he had gone in knowing a page and a half about a man he had never seen, and the page and a half was mostly about what he would not be able to do.

He found that he was not frightened. He looked for it, honestly, the way he would have looked for a tell, and it was not where he expected it. There was something else there instead, in the same place, and it was a great deal more like wanting.

He wanted to see it. He wanted to stand in front of the thing the eel woman had laughed about and Renn had lain on his back under, and find out what it was, and what he was, standing in front of it. It was not a sensible thing to want, and he knew it. He wanted it anyway, the way he had wanted to know what the word meant in Pellin's office, before he knew enough to be afraid of it.

He thought that probably said something about him, and did not write it down.

"Some."

"That's not an answer." She fell in beside him. "I'll let you off today."

# Chapter 46 — Bronze

Vell counted the yard once from her table, the way she always counted it, and made it forty-one.

It was a poor number for a Bronze. On a Sunday afternoon in good weather a Bronze in her circle would have had the benches out and the rope four deep, and boys on the wall, and Marrow sweating. But this was a Monday at noon in the last of the winter, with a grey sleet coming in slantwise off the river and melting as it touched the dirt, and a gate on it five times the price of a bench. Everybody who had come had walked out of a day's work to be there, or paid somebody to do it for them, or simply not gone in. So forty-one was a good number, and she wrote it at the top of the page in the small hand she kept for things that were hers: *41, sleet, Monday noon*. Then she looked at who they were.

They were people who knew what they were looking at. The old men from the third row had come without their benches and stood in a knot by the drain with their scarves over their mouths. Dessa was by the gate in the wall with her hair tied tight and her hands in her pockets, alone. Corbin stood beside the water bucket, square, saying nothing to anybody. Sarel had not come, and Vell had not expected her to; the girl Lira was at the barrel, where she always was, with her arms folded on the top of the rope and her face giving away nothing. Renn was on the sunny side, though there was no sun, with a few of his friends from the barges round him, all of them quieter than she had ever known them. And the boy was at the rope on the shaded side, with his coat off and his hand flat on the iron stake at the corner, as though he were listening to the ground.

She knew why he did that. She had watched him find it.

The pie boy came across from the gate with the coin bag, and she weighed it in her hand without counting it and put it under the table. Then Feryn came in through the gate in the wall.

He came in the way she had seen him come into three other yards in three other cities over six or seven years, talking over his shoulder to somebody behind him, easy and loose and pleased with the day. He was taller than most and built for use, not for show, in a coat that had been good once and was now only warm. He had been nineteen and a Copper the first time she saw him, on a wet night in a yard three days south of here, and he had lost to a one-armed man and laughed about it all the way to the water bucket. He was twenty-four now, or near it, and a Bronze Rank 2, and he moved like a man who has been hit by a great many good fighters and has stopped finding it interesting. She had watched him win more bouts than she could have named without the books, and she had liked him every time, which she did not often say about anybody.

He was halfway across the yard when he saw who was standing at the rope with his coat off, and he stopped walking.

He stopped as a man stops when he has put his foot on a stair that is not there. Then he looked at the boy for a long moment, and turned round, and found her table, and came across to it.

"No," he said.

He said it quietly, but the yard was very quiet, and the sleet was not loud, and forty-one people heard him.

"He's a child."

"He's fourteen," said Vell.

She had known it was coming. She had known it on the morning she told the boy that Feryn was coming, and every morning since, and she had decided a long time before this one what she would say. She found that it was harder to say it to his face than she had thought, and she said it anyway.

"You posted for any opponent holding an assessed rating. He holds one. Assessed-Copper, in my hand, three weeks gone. He's won two since, and four of his six before that. He's entered under witness, and the purse is met, and the gate's paid." She turned the ledger round on the table so that he could see the page. "Those are the terms. They're the terms you wrote."

Feryn bent over the book and read the page, all of it, with his finger moving down the lines. She had seen a great many fighters stand at her table and complain about what was in her book. She had seen very few read it first. She marked it to his credit and did not let it show.

"I posted for interesting opponents," he said, still reading. "I didn't post for somebody who's still growing into his boots."

"Then you should have written it better."

He looked up at her.

"I don't decide who stands in my circle," said Vell. "I decide who I put across from whom, and what happens inside the rope, and when it stops. That's all I decide. The consent question is his. It isn't yours to answer, and it isn't mine." She turned the book back round. "If you want to know whether he means it, ask him."

She had not always thought that.

Thirty years ago, when she was new to the book, she had turned a girl away from her rope because the girl was too young and too light and had a look in her eye that Vell did not like, and Vell had been certain she knew best. The girl had gone downriver and fought in a yard where the keeper did not care how old anybody was, or what look they had in their eye, and nobody there had called anything for her. Vell had heard about it a month later, from a carter.

She had thought about it for a long time afterward. In the end she had decided that a keeper who chose for a fighter whether he was fit to stand had stopped being a keeper and become something else, something nearer to an owner. A fighter's word over the rope was the one thing in the circle that was entirely his, and the circle stood on it, as it stood on her word inside the rope. You did not protect people by taking their word away from them. You protected them by being very, very good at the call.

So she would not overrule him. But she had already decided, sitting at her table with the sleet on her shoulders, exactly where the call would be.

She knew Feryn's weights. He had two of them, and she had watched him use both. There was the weight he carried on tour, when he was being polite to a town and did not want to put anybody in the healers' for a gate: loose and strolling and very nearly kind. And there was the other one, which she had seen four times in six or seven years, when somebody had made him take them seriously, and which had ended all four bouts inside an exchange. If the boy made him go to the second weight, she would be on her feet. The first exchange after that which could only hurt the boy, and not teach him anything, she would end. She would not wait for him to stop getting up. He would not stop getting up of his own accord; she had watched him in a dozen bouts now, and only once had he put his hand up, against a cook, when there was nothing left in the bout for him to learn.

Feryn had gone to the rope.

He did not go round it to the boy's side. He stood on his own side with his hands resting on the top of it, and looked across the circle at the boy, and the boy looked back. Vell watched Feryn not ask how old he was, which was what nearly anybody would have asked, and which he already knew.

"Do you know," said Feryn, "what Pressure does to whoever's standing in front of it?"

"Not all of it," said the boy. His voice was perfectly steady and rather quiet. "I've spent three weeks finding out what I could. A woman who lost to it for ten years showed me how it's built. I can tell you where you'll hold it, and about how long, if you're slow." He did not smile. "You won't be slow. That's the part I can't find out from anybody. The benches can't tell me, and neither can she."

"Who talked you into it?"

"Nobody. Vell told me you were coming, and what you were, and I asked her for it." The boy looked at him across the circle. "I'd like to know what three weeks bought me. I can't find that out by watching."

Feryn did not say anything for a long time. He was weighing him, and Vell knew what he was weighing, because she had weighed the same things: whether the answer had been learned by heart, and whether anybody behind the boy was pushing. He looked for it. She saw him look past the boy at the rope behind him, and along it, and find nobody there but the girl at the barrel with her arms folded, who looked back at him as flatly as a shut door.

"All right," said Feryn at last. He said it the way you shut a gate behind you. Then he turned round and looked at Vell. "Note that I objected."

"Noted," said Vell, and wrote it. "So is his answer."

She wrote that too.

Then she stood up behind her table, and the yard went lower, and she said the rules for the witnesses as she always said them: that it went until somebody conceded, or could not go on, or until she called it; that her call stood; and that nobody was killed, by circuit custom, which was older than any law. She had said them a thousand times. Today she listened to herself say them.

"Feryn. Bronze Rank 2, Pressure Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

He set his feet. He did not nod. He looked across the circle at Feryn and watched what did not move.

Feryn came off his mark at a walk.

That was the first thing that was wrong. There was no rush and no probe and nothing from the mark; he simply walked, slowly, along the inside of the groove, with his hands half up and open and his shoulders rolling loose, the way a man loosens them at the start of a day's work. He took a half step to his left that did not seem to go anywhere. He turned his head to follow Cael with a mild, friendly look on his face. He could not find it. That was the second thing. Corvane, walking slowly in her brick yard, had carried her stone so carefully that once he knew where to look he could see it. He stood on his mark and did the arithmetic, because his arm was telling him to.

He was not strolling now. That was the change, and Cael saw it and felt the cold go down his back. "Not here," said Feryn. "You stand like a city. Somewhere with walls." He shifted his weight a hair from one foot to the other. "I've fought in a city with walls. Hated it. Everybody watches you from the top."

He was hiding it under stillness, and under talk. Cael understood it at once, because he had seen it done for three weeks in the grey half by somebody who was much better at it than she would ever let anybody know. *A person standing still and talking is harder to read than a person walking,* he had found out on the eighth morning. *There's so little to tell the holding from.* Feryn had learned in one exchange to do what it had taken Lira a week to find. Then, very early, much too early, before Feryn had finished his sentence about walls, before he had shifted his weight again or done anything at all, the cloth of his shirt stopped moving with his breath.

It was the beginning. It was the decision. Cael saw it, and his body was already going.

Afterward, on the cot, he would try to put what happened into its proper order on the page, and fail. He did not decide to step and then step. The step was already happening, with his whole weight in it, before anything in him that made decisions had been consulted. It was a long, low, slanting glide that his legs did with a sureness he had never put into them, quick the way air goes through a doorway when somebody opens a window at the other end of the house. It was familiar and not his. It did not carry him back from the air. It carried him round it, out past the whole edge of the place where it was going to come.

The air came down behind him on the place where he had been standing. Its edge went past him: a hard drag at his sleeve and down the side of his body, solid, close enough to count as a touch. Then he was standing on the far side of it, at an angle no slip and no dodge should have been able to reach, balanced, with his guard up and Feryn's open flank in front of him an arm's length away.

The yard was silent. It was not the held breath of the second exchange, but a real silence, forty-one people and the sleet and nobody moving. He heard the dirt settle where the air had ploughed it. He heard Vell's pen stop.

He did not take the flank.

By the time his thinking had caught up with where his feet had put him, the opening had already begun to close; Feryn was already turning. And the honest truth, standing there in the sleet, was that he did not know what he had just done, nor whether he could ever do it twice. It had come when the air came. It had not come once in three weeks when he called it, at the post, by the cot, in the box drill. It was not a tool he had picked up. It was something that had used his legs.

Feryn turned to face him.

For a breath he only looked. Then the yard came back all at once, a wall of noise from every side, Renn shouting on the sunny side and the old men by the drain stamping in the wet, and Feryn seemed deaf to all of it. His eyes stayed on Cael, as Cael's own eyes stayed on a fighter on the second Sunday, after he had found the thing: the look you give something that has just crossed out everything you wrote about it.

"End of the exchange," said Vell. Her voice was very level.

Then Feryn settled.

There was no other word for it. The looseness went out of him and so did the strolling ease and the friendly interest. Everything he had been carrying round the yard for three exchanges was set aside like a coat taken off and laid over a chair, and what stood there instead was a Bronze Rank 2 at his working weight, which Cael had never seen and understood at once.

"Last one, then," said Feryn. There was no talk in his voice any more. There was nothing in it but what he meant.

Walking back to his mark, with his forearm burning and his ribs catching and the sleet coming down, Cael found that he had a choice to make, and made it.

The safe thing was to go backward. He could circle, and keep his distance, and make Feryn spend the last exchange chasing him round the groove, and lose long and look well doing it. Lira would not have minded. Vell would not have minded. Nobody in the yard would have said a word. He looked at the safe thing and put it down without any fuss. He had not asked for this bout to look well. He had asked for it so that he would know. There was only one thing still to know: how much his reading could hold against Feryn's whole weight, with nobody keeping anything back on either side of the rope.

He set his feet and looked at the middle of Feryn's shirt.

"Begin."

Feryn came in fast.

He came in fast and straight, and the speed changed everything, as everybody had told him speed changed everything, Lira and Vell and Corvane and the eel woman laughing with her arms in a tub. The build was there all the same, and Cael saw it. He *saw* it, folded small and tight under a man coming at him at a run, the faint settling in the middle of the shirt, as early as he had caught it in the third. But it bought him almost nothing now. Feryn was building as he came and closing as he built, and the half breath Cael had had in the third was a quarter, and the quarter was a sliver, and the sliver was almost nothing at all. His reading had not got worse. Reading was simply not enough any more.

He read a release coming, and he called for the step.

He called for it as he had called for it by the cot, deliberately, with his whole attention, the hip dropping and the turn. It did not come. Whatever had moved his legs in the third exchange stayed wherever it lived and did not answer, because he had asked it to. So he slipped instead, with his own feet, his own trained slip, short and quick and honest, to the left, outside where the air would come.

The air did not come.

Feryn had not let it go. He had built it and held it and not released it, and Cael had slipped round a thing that was not there. And Feryn's hand, which had been at his side, was already where Cael was slipping to. Feryn had learned in the third exchange that the boy went round. He had stopped trying to hit where the boy was, and put his hand where the boy would go. *Once too often,* said Doss, somewhere at the back of Cael's head, a beat too late to be any use.

The hand closed on Cael's right shoulder in the middle of his slip.

There was no blow, and no wrench. Feryn simply agreed with him. Cael's own slipping weight went into the hand, and the hand took it and joined it and turned it, a few degrees, gently, so that all of Cael's speed going left became all of Cael's speed going down. The yard turned over. There was just time to understand that the throw was his own, and that all Feryn had done was choose its direction.

And in the middle of it, falling, past any deciding, he noticed the grip.

The hand on his shoulder did not let go. The turn was finished; his weight was already gone past any recovering; there was nothing left for the hand to do that the ground would not do better. And the hand stayed on him anyway, held, a full pressed beat of it, a whole slow second of grip where a glancing touch would have done, riding him halfway down to the dirt before it opened. It was nothing, a small thing in the middle of a large one. He put it away at the back of his head all the same, because it did not fit, and he kept everything that did not fit. Then the ground hit him along the whole of his right side at once, shoulder and hip and ribs together, and knocked the thought and his breath and the rest of the bout out of him in one blow.

He lay in the wet dirt with the sleet coming down on his face and did the accounting from a long way off. Everything hurt in the general way. His right shoulder and hip, where the ground had come up. His ribs on both sides now, the left catching again under the old place, the right new. His forearm, wrapped all the way round the bone. Nothing hurt in the sharp particular way that meant broken. He gave his body the order to stand, and it sent back an answer: *presently*. He got an elbow under him. The yard tilted and came level and tilted again.

"The match is called," said Vell.

She was very close. She had left her table. Her voice went over the whole yard without seeming to be raised.

"Feryn, Bronze Rank 2, by incapacity. So entered."

Part of him put a protest together: *Count me. I'm getting up. Count me.* The rest of him, the part that kept the book, looked at the protest and threw it out. It took him six more breaths to get his feet under him, and when he had them he was swaying. The margin was gone and would not come back today. The fourth exchange had shown him that at whatever speed Feryn cared to use, reading alone put him in the dirt in four seconds. A fifth exchange would not teach him anything. It would only hurt him, and he would have gone into it, and Vell had known that he would.

Her call was not early. It was exact. She had ended it at the very moment it stopped being able to give him anything but damage, and he understood, standing there swaying in the sleet, that this was the whole art of what she did.

"Good call," he said, because it was true, and because the book should hear it.

"I know," said Vell, and went back to her table.

The yard was loud, and he let it stay far away.

He walked to the table, because you walked to the table, win or lose, and stood in front of it with his left arm held against his chest and his right side aching from the shoulder to the knee. Vell was writing. The sleet had got into the corner of her page and she had blotted it and gone on.

Feryn came to him there.

He came straight across the circle, ahead of Renn and the old men and the people with money in their hands, ahead of everybody, and Cael marked that he did it at once, before anything else, the way you would mark it if a man stopped to pick up something you had dropped. He held out his hand. Cael took it with his right, which hurt less, and Feryn closed his own round it and pulled once, smoothly, so that Cael was standing straight instead of swaying, and then let go. He was careful about it without making any show of being careful.

"What tier are you?" said Feryn.

It was the question. Everybody asked it in the end, in one shape or another, and Cael answered it the way he had decided a long time ago that he would always answer it: first, and plainly, before it could be found out sideways.

"[SHATTERED]."

Behind him he heard Vell's pen stop.

He watched Feryn's face. He had a whole catalogue of the ways people took the word, collected at gates and registry counters and across boarding-house tables: the step back, the careful blankness, the nod that came too fast, the eyes going to the door. He watched to see which one it would be.

Feryn's face did none of those things. His eyebrows lifted, and what came into it was not fear, or even fear well hidden. It was appetite. It was the plain, unguarded hunger of a man who has been doing a thing for a long time, and doing it well, and has just been handed a problem from outside everything he knows.

"Huh," said Feryn.

He looked at Cael for a while longer, at the whole wet, swaying, fourteen-year-old fact of him.

"I sat Bronze panels for a year," he said. "Months of it. Every Path there is, and every way of using one. I've never once seen a thing I couldn't put a name to afterward." He shook his head, slowly, and it was respect, of the plain kind that nobody puts on for a crowd. "The third exchange. I know what I saw. I've no idea what I was fighting."

"That makes two of us," said Cael.

Feryn laughed. It came out of him short and surprised and real, as though it had been knocked loose.

"Eat with me," he said. "Tonight. I'm paying."

At her table Vell looked up from the book with both eyebrows raised, which from Vell was as good as a speech. Feryn saw it.

"What?"

"I've watched you win in three cities," said Vell, going back to her page, "and I've never once seen you buy anybody so much as a pie."

"Nobody's been worth a pie," said Feryn. He looked back at Cael. "Well?"

Lira had come round the rope by then, and Cael found her over Feryn's shoulder, standing by the barrel with her arms folded and her mouth pressed flat over something that was not disappointment and was trying hard not to be pride. When she saw him looking, she gave him one short nod. It meant *go*, and it meant *I want every word of it afterward*, and it meant something else under both of those that he did not have a name for and did not need one.

His ribs hurt. His arm was stiffening by the minute, and he had lost, in four exchanges, in front of forty-one people, in the sleet.

"All right," he said. "I'm hungry."

# Chapter 47 — One Data Point

Amrit's was full by the time they got there, because the sleet had driven everybody indoors who had anywhere to be driven to, and half the people at the two long tables had been at the rope at noon.

They went quiet when Feryn came down the three steps and ducked in under the lintel, and then they went loud again, very carefully, in the way people talk when they want you to know they are not talking about you. Two men from the river workshops shifted along a bench without being asked. Feryn thanked them as if they had given him a horse, and sat down with his back to the little windows and the river going by grey outside them, and Cael sat down across from him, which took two tries and a hand on the table.

The girl who carried the bowls came out. She put down in front of Cael a bowl of the fish stew with twice the bread beside it that she put beside anybody else's, and in front of Feryn a bowl of the same stew with exactly the ordinary amount.

Feryn looked at the two plates of bread, and then at the hatch, where a large man with a shaved head was looking at a pot.

"I see," he said.

"He decided I was his," said Cael. "After I lost to him. In the autumn."

"You lost to the cook."

"He's an Ember Copper 4. He's very good."

"I'm sure he is." Feryn tore his ordinary bread in half with great dignity. "I've lost to worse. I once lost to a man with one arm, on a wet night, three days south of here, when I was nineteen." He dipped the bread. "He used the stump. Nobody had ever told me you could use the stump. I lay on my back in the rain and thought about it for some time."

Cael laughed, and his ribs caught, both sides at once, and he had to put his spoon down and hold the edge of the table.

Feryn watched him do it with professional interest.

"That's the right side as well now," he said. "From the ground. And the arm." Everybody does, the first time they stand in front of it."

He ordered more when the bowl was gone, without asking, and then more again. Cael ate all of it. He had not known he was hungry until he started, and then he could not stop, and Feryn sat across from him with his elbows on the table and told stories.

He told them the way Cael wrote the Log, exactly and without mercy, and they were all at his own expense. There was the one-armed man. There was a yard in a farming town where the circle was shared, on the days there was no card, with a goat; the goat had wandered back into it during his bout. He had refused to step on it, and his opponent had not shared his feelings about the goat, and he had lost. There was his first Bronze evaluation, at nineteen, in a long cold room in front of a panel of three. He had been so frightened that he had built far more than he meant to and let it go all at once, at the wall, as the panel had asked. It had blown every paper off the panel's table and out of the window into a canal.

"They passed me," said Feryn. "I've never known why. I think they were afraid not to."

"What did they write?"

"Nobody knows. It's in the canal." He drank. "I asked, years after. The senior assessor said she remembered the day perfectly and that I had *a great deal of promise and no idea where to put it*. Which was true. It's still true some days."

Cael laughed until his ribs made him stop, and then sat holding the table with his eyes watering, and Feryn waited for him with the patience of a man who has been laughed at in a great many eating houses and has learned to take it as a compliment.

At the hatch, Amrit looked up from his pot, once, at the two of them, and then looked down again.

---

But they had not come to Amrit's for the goat, and neither of them pretended they had. When the second bowl was gone Feryn put his cup down on the table and turned it a quarter turn by its handle. It was the first careful thing he had done all evening.

"Your third exchange," he said. "I'd like to go through it with you. If you'll let me."

"All right."

"I've been letting go on people for six years." He said it plainly, as a fact about his trade. "I know what every kind of getting out of the way looks like. The trained slip. The panic dive. The man who goes down flat because he's decided the ground is safer. I've fought Wind, real Wind, ranked, declared, people who'd been doing it since they were your age." He looked at the cup. "That wasn't any slip I know, and it wasn't fright. And you're no declared Wind, or you'd have said Wind when I asked you, and you didn't." He looked up. "You went before I let go. You didn't go away from it. You went round it, as if you knew where all the edges of it were going to be before I'd built them." He waited. "How?"

So it had come. Lira had asked him nearly the same thing in the grey half, months before, and he answered Feryn as he had answered her. It was the only answer he owned, and Feryn had paid for a true one, not a clever one.

"I don't know yet."

Feryn looked at him. Cael let him look. There was nothing to hold up, and nothing to hide; it was only the truth, lying on the table between their cups, unfinished.

"Yet," said Feryn.

"I keep a book." Cael turned his own cup with his good hand, once. "Everything I can't explain goes in it. Everything in it gets explained in the end, or ruled out. That one's been in the book for a while." He looked at the river going by the window. "It happens when something's coming at me and I haven't time to choose. Asking for it has never once made it come. I asked for it today, in the fourth, when you came in fast." He felt his mouth go up at one corner, without much humour. "You saw how that went."

Feryn did not say anything for a long while.

Cael watched him turn the answer over. He watched him notice that it was not an answer, and weigh it, and decide, quite visibly, not to push at it. That was rare enough that he marked it. Most people pushed at a thing they could not explain, or backed away from it; he had a catalogue for both. Feryn only nodded slowly, like a man who has decided to leave a good bout unfinished on purpose, because the second one will be worth more.

"When you figure it out, I want to know."

He said it the way he had said *all right* in the circle, like a gate shut behind him. It was not a politeness. It was a thing he was putting in his own book, with Cael's name against it.

"You'll be somewhere I can find you?"

"I'm always somewhere," said Feryn.

Cael understood that this was how people who travelled the circuits said *yes*.

He paid, as he had said he would, and counted the coins out onto the table more carefully than he had done anything else all evening. At the top of the three steps, out in the street, the sleet had stopped and the cold had come down behind it, hard and clear, with the lamps lit along the lower bridge and the river loud underneath. Feryn stood for a moment in the doorway of the eating house and looked at Cael once more, the whole bruised, standing catalogue of him, and shook his head again, slowly, in the same plain way as at the table.

"Fourteen," he said, to himself, or to the street.

Then he went off along the river toward wherever touring fighters slept, laughing quietly to himself in the dark.

---

It was a slow walk back to Torvin's. Slow was all his legs would give him.

He did not go to Lira's. It was long past the lamps, and the widow had said what she had said, twice, and Lira's door locked from both sides. He stood for a moment at the corner by the well with the iron cover, where the lane went up toward the chandler's, and looked up it, and then went the other way. He would tell her in the morning, every word, in order. She had asked for it with a nod at the barrel, and she would have it first.

At Torvin's the house was asleep. He went up the stairs with his good hand flat on the wall, one stair at a time, and sat down on the end of his cot. Yeni's lamp was turned down to a bead, but when he sat down a hand came out from the next cot and turned it up a little, toward him, and went back under the blanket.

He took the Log down and opened it on his knee, and paid the bout into it.

It took a long time. His writing arm was the aching arm, and every line cost something, and there was more to pay than he had ever had to pay before. He set down each exchange in his usual way: the event, then the read and its timing, then the price of the read and what it had paid for.

*Fifteenth. Feryn, Bronze Rank 2, Pressure. Lost, fourth exchange, by incapacity. Vell's call.*

*First: didn't read it at all. Everything loose; nothing to find. The roof came down. Flat on my back, up at eight.*

*Second: read the build late, about halfway. Turned the guard edge-on and shed some of it down my left arm. Skidded two paces and stayed up. The arm paid.*

*Third: read the build at the start, before he'd built anything, while he was standing still and talking. Earliest I've ever read anything. And the step came. On its own. Same as always. Round it, not away.*

*Fourth: read it fine. The margin was gone anyway. He'd learned I go round, so he didn't let go at all. He put his hand where I'd go. I called the step and it didn't come. Thrown with my own speed.*

He sat and looked at that for a while. Then he wrote the ruling, which was short.

*Reading is necessary. Reading isn't enough. He didn't beat what Corvane showed me. He was only faster than me at it. The drawing's right. It needs somebody quicker standing in front of it.*

He turned the book over to the back, and wrote, under the last entry, *Sixth instance. Feryn, third exchange. The step. Not called. Came when the air came.* Then he flexed his forearm, carefully, and sat holding the pencil, wondering whether the account was paid, and the notice came.

There was no sound and no light and no direction it came from; there was the quiet first, the quiet before a thought arrives whole, and then the turning over, and then, in the dark place behind his breastbone where nothing had lit since the word, something written.

He sat very still on the end of the cot for a long time.

He read it the way he had read the first one: once, and then again, and then a third time, slowly, each word checked against the one before it. It stayed. It sat in the dark beside the other one, the Wind one, quiet and plain, two lines of figures on a page where there had been one.

*Pressure-adjacent.*

Then he did with it what he did with every new thing: he held it up beside the record to find its place.

The Wind had taken three months. It had taken three months of mornings in the grey half, watching Lira move until something in him had taken the shape of it without being asked; hundreds of hours, and even then the notice had not come until the shape had already come up out of him under pressure, more than once, in the drop and against Dessa and against Sarel. That was the only example he had, and it was a long one.

He had never watched Feryn before today. He had heard about him from Vell and Renn and the eel woman, and he had studied Pressure for three weeks from an old woman with a stick and a drawing in the dirt, and no notice had come from any of that. Corvane had built in front of him for two hours and leaned on him a dozen times, and nothing had come of it. He had watched Feryn fight for exactly one bout, his own, four exchanges, and the last of them had taken four seconds.

If it was the watching, the arithmetic would not come out. It did not come out by any road he could find. Three months of mornings for one, and four exchanges for the other.

So it was not the watching. Or not only the watching.

He went over the four exchanges a second time, more slowly, hunting through them as he hunted through any bout for the place where it had turned. He stopped where some clerk in the back of his head had already put a marker, before the notice had come, before he had known there was anything to mark: the fall, and the hand on his shoulder.

The grip had stayed. The throw had been finished, his weight gone past any getting back, everything the hand was for already done, and the hand had stayed on him anyway. It had been one full held beat, a pressed second of grip where a touch would have done, riding him halfway down before it opened. He had filed it because it did not fit. It might fit now.

He picked up the pencil, and his hand was not entirely steady.

*Second fragment. Pressure-adjacent. From Feryn, I think. Not from watching, or I can't make the watching add up: one bout of him against three months of her. His air touched me three times in the bout, and Corvane's a dozen times in her brick yard, and nothing came from either. What's different is the hand. In the fourth his hand stayed on my shoulder after the throw was done. Held. One second, perhaps less, through my shirt. If whatever this is can come across by touch, one second of a hand might do what three months of looking did.*

He stopped there, and read it, and made himself write the next part before he could stop believing it.

*Might. One data point. I wrote that exact thing about the first notice, that one might be chance, and I'm writing it again now, in the book, so I can't quietly forget to doubt it. I don't know that the touch did this. What I know is that the watching won't come out right, and that the touch is the one thing left over that doesn't fit anywhere else. That isn't the same as knowing.*

---

He sat back against the cold wall under the window, careful of his ribs, and thought about Kestrel.

He turned back through the back pages to the two days at the middle-yard gate, and the two nights on the wall by the pump, and the half heartbeat in his heels in the grey half that he had never been able to trust, and the word he had written under all of it: *Inconclusive.* He had given three reasons it might have come to nothing: two days of watching were not three months; Force did not come across, or not to him; or the thing in his heels had been real and needed more time.

He read them again, and found that there was a fourth now.

*Kestrel, reread,* he wrote, under the rest. *Add a fourth: that watching was never going to be enough with him, on its own, and I spent two mornings knocking at the wrong door. Two days at a gate, and he never once came nearer than an arm's length, except going past me with his rope.*

*I don't know that either. I don't know what matters. Time, touch, the person, the Path, something I haven't got a word for. Questions for the record: does touch always do it? Does it have to be long? Did he have to mean anything by it?*

He looked at the last question, and wrote under it, slowly:

*He didn't mean anything. He was throwing me. If anything passed, it passed through a hand whose only business was putting me on the ground.*

Then he sat and looked at the whole page. It was the most he had ever written in the back of the book in one night. He found that there was one more thing to pay in, and that it had no column, because none of the columns were for how the day had felt.

A professional had beaten him in four exchanges, and had been a professional in every one of them. He had been read and outrun and thrown with his own speed and put down on his side in the wet in front of forty-one people, and every particular hurt of it was still on him: the ribs on both sides now, the forearm wrapped round its bone, the whole right side of him from shoulder to hip where the ground had come up, the six breaths it had taken him to stand.

He turned to a fresh line at the very bottom of the page.

*Loss. Feryn, Bronze Rank 2, Pressure, by incapacity, Vell's call. The call was right. I read him in three of four. Corvane's drawing holds. The step came once, on its own terms, the way it always does. Got: the read, a dinner, an open account with a man who's always somewhere, and perhaps a second door I didn't know was there. Paid: one arm, both sides, a fall. Three weeks bought more than I knew I was buying.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote one more line, because it was true, and because he did not think he would believe it in the morning unless it was written down.

*I've never lost anything and felt less like I'd lost it.*

He closed the book and set it on the shelf beside Hesk's leather one. He lay down very carefully on his back, which was the only way left that did not hurt, and found that it hurt anyway. He lay looking at the beam with its old nail holes in the light of Yeni's lamp, and felt the two things sitting quietly side by side in the dark behind his breastbone, the one that leaned and the one that pressed, and listened to the wheezing man begin to whistle.

---

In the morning he went to the grey half before the light, because he had said he would tell her first, and he meant to.

He walked very slowly. The frost had come down hard behind the sleet, and the lane was white, and his right hip had stiffened in the night so that every step up the hill was a separate conversation. When he came in through the gate in the wall Lira was already at the post, in the dark, striking it low, twice, and stepping round it, and she stopped when she heard him and turned round.

She looked at the way he was walking. She looked at his left arm, which he was carrying against his chest because it would not hang, and at his face.

"Sit down," she said. "Before you fall down and I have to put a mark on the post for it."

He sat down on the low wall. She sat beside him, close, on his right, the side the ground had not had, because it was the side that was left, and did not give him any bread yet.

"Every word," she said. "In order."

He gave it to her in order, the four exchanges and Vell's call and *What tier are you?*, and Amrit's two plates of bread, and the goat, and the canal. She laughed at the canal, short and helpless, and put her hand over her mouth. Then he told her the third exchange again, slower, and she did not laugh. Then he told her the fourth, and the hand on his shoulder that had stayed a beat too long, and what had come after midnight on the cot.

She stopped him there, before he could take the book out.

"In the fourth," she said. "When he went heavy. You didn't go backward."

"No."

"I said you'd do whatever you do." She looked at his arm, lying across his chest. "I'm not sorry I said it. And I'm not sorry you didn't. I've been sitting up half the night trying to work out which of those I am, and I'm both." She shook her head. "Go on. The cot."

He took the Log out of his coat and opened it on his knee and showed her, and she read it in the grey light with her lips moving very slightly, both notices one under the other in his careful hand.

She read it twice.

"His," she said.

"I think so."

"Not watching."

"I don't think so. Not only." He told her the arithmetic, and the grip, and the one data point, and Kestrel at the wrong door. She listened to all of it without saying anything, with her shoulder against his and her breath going up white beside his in the dark.

When he had finished she sat for a long time, looking at the two notices on the page.

"I don't know what that means," she said at last.

"Neither do I."

"Good." She took his good hand and turned it over and put a piece of bread in it, the larger half, though he had not seen her tear it. "Then we don't know together. That's better than you not knowing on your own on a cot with your face to the wall." She looked at the notice again. "Two doors."

"Perhaps."

"Perhaps two doors." She almost smiled. "One of them's mine, and I didn't know about it for three months. The other one's a man you met at noon and had supper with." She shook her head slowly. "I don't think either of us is going to like the next one, whatever it turns out to be."

He ate his bread. It hurt to breathe in while he chewed, so he ate it slowly.

The light came up grey over the east wall, and the frost went pink along the top of it, and the girls came and sat on the far wall with their stolen breakfasts and looked at his arm and then, kindly, looked away. Somewhere down in the district a cart was going over the cobbles toward the lower bridge, and the fish steps would be starting, and the board would be saying *TODAY* to a day that had gone.

He thought about Hesk's letter, and *don't let anyone see you move*, and forty-one people in the sleet holding their breath when the step had come. He would write to Hesk, the next thing as it came, and not five things later.

"Survivable," said Lira beside him, not as a question, looking at his arm.

"Survivable."

"Then tomorrow you're doing nothing. Nothing at all. And the day after—"

"We start on it properly."

"We start on it properly," she said, and stood up, and held her hand down to him to pull him off the wall, on his good side. He took it, and she pulled, and he came up.

On Wednesday the mender looked at his arm across the bench, and at the way he was standing, and said, "No."

"I can sort with one hand."

"You can sort with one hand," the mender agreed, without looking up from his clock. "And you'll drop half of it, and I'll have to sort it again. Monday." He considered. "If it's still there."

So Cael went out under the four words in the black paint and stood in the row of workshops with nothing to do, which had not happened to him since Denvash. Then he turned round and walked the other way, down past the dyers' and along the river, an hour through the cold toward the brickworks, because an old woman had told him to come back and tell her what the man did, if he could walk, and he could walk.

It took him most of the afternoon. When he got to the last house in the row with its back to the river, there was a young man in the yard behind the wall, a different one, being told *again* in a dry level voice. Corvane saw Cael in her gateway, and looked at his arm, and at his face, and said to the young man, "That's enough. Go home." The young man went, rubbing his shoulder, and did not look at Cael on his way out.

Corvane sat down on her bucket by the wall with both hands on the head of her stick.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her. He told it the way he had told Lira, in order, but he left out the dinner and the goat, because she would not want them, and he left out the third exchange, because he did not know how to tell it to her yet. He told her the first, when he had found nothing because nothing was being carried; Feryn had not carried it the way she had, but kept every part of himself as loose as every other part. He told her the second, the build read halfway, under the shirt, below the ribs, and the guard turned edge-on, and the arm. He told her that in the third Feryn had stood still on his mark and talked about cities with walls. He said that he had seen it then, the build, under the stillness, before there was any build: the cloth of the shirt stopping with the breath underneath it at the very moment the man decided.

Corvane did not move at all while he said that last part.

"At the deciding," she said.

"Before he'd built anything. I think. As early as I can say."

She looked at the trodden dirt of her yard, where his heels had dug two troughs three weeks ago and the rain had nearly smoothed them out again. Then she leaned forward on her bucket and put the point of her stick into the dirt and drew her figure, quickly, from memory, the stick body and the spiral in the chest. She did not draw the four strokes beside it this time. She drew one.

She looked at it for a long time.

"I lost to them for ten years," she said. "And I studied them for ten more, and I had forty students, and I never once knew that it showed there. At the start. I thought the start was inside where nobody could see it." She tapped the single stroke with the end of the stick. "And it shows."

"It shows a little. If he's standing still."

"A little is a great deal more than nothing." She sat back. "And the fourth?"

"I read it. It didn't matter. He was too fast, and he'd learned what I'd do, and he put his hand where I was going." He looked at the drawing. "You told me it wouldn't be enough."

"I did." She did not sound pleased to have been right. She looked at his arm for a while. "It'll be enough one day. Against somebody. Not him, not this year. But somebody." She got up off the bucket, slowly, with the stick, and stood looking down at her drawing in the dirt. "You'll come back. When you've seen it again."

It was not a question, and he did not answer it as one.

She went into her house and did not say goodbye, as before. But this time she left the drawing in the dirt, with its one stroke, and did not rub it out with her boot.

He walked home through the brickworks in the early dark with the soot in the air and the kilns glowing red in their doorways, slowly, with his arm against his chest.

He thought about the six weeks, which had run out on the forty-second day and were five weeks behind him now. He thought about a senior man from the Compact with a thin file, who had not come yet and who must, by every count he knew how to make, be on the road by now, or at a desk two days west packing a case. He thought about Coss saying that he could buy time but not an answer, and about how much of the time had already been spent.

He thought about two lines of figures in the dark behind his breastbone where there had been one. There were two doors, perhaps, where he had thought there was only one, and he did not know where either of them went, or which of them he had come through, or whether there were others in the dark he had not found yet. He did not know what Kestrel had been, or what Feryn's hand had been, or whether Lira's three months and Feryn's one second were the same thing at all.

He knew that his ribs hurt on both sides, and that his arm would be stiff for a week, and that he had been read and outrun by a man who wanted to know the answer as much as he did.

At the top of the lane of yards the old man was standing in his gate in the dusk, with his sleeves rolled down against the cold, watching the woman with the shoulder hold her fourth position. He looked round as Cael came up the lane, which he had never done before. He looked at the arm, and at the way Cael was walking, and at his face.

"Bronze," said the old man.

"Yes."

The old man nodded slowly, and turned back to the woman with the shoulder.

"Four," he said.

Cael stood in the lane a moment longer. Then he went on down toward Torvin's, where Torvin's wife would have something on with real vegetables in it, because somebody in the house had fought, and where there was a letter to Denvash to finish, telling the next thing as it came.

# Chapter 28 — The Thin File

Coss read the file for the fourth time with the lamp pulled close and the desk rocking under his elbow every time he turned a sheet.

The rocking came from the left front leg. Somebody had once folded a scrap of paper under it to make it sit level, and the scrap had worked itself out and lay on the floorboards a hand's width from where it was needed, grey with dust, as though it had given up. Coss had noticed it in his first minute in the room and had not picked it up. It was not his desk. The ring-stains on its top were the cups of other men, sent out here before him on other errands and gone again, and one of them had carved a small neat letter into the corner by the inkwell, an *R* or a *B*, and then thought better of it and stopped.

The room was over the front room of the post, up a stair so narrow that he had carried his bag up it sideways. It had one window, which looked down the fish steps to the river, a cot along the wall under the window, a chair, the desk, and a partition of planks between this room and the next one that did not quite reach the ceiling. Through it, earlier, he had heard somebody cough on the other side, and then a door, and then nothing.

He had slept in a great many rooms like it. He did not mind them. A borrowed room had nothing in it to distract you, which was more than could be said for most rooms a man owned.

He laid the six sheets out across the desk in the order he had taken them from the case, as he would have laid out tools on a bench, and looked at them all together.

---

Coss had been a field agent of the Guilds Compact for eleven years, and he was forty-one, and somewhere around his fourth year he had made his peace with not being a brilliant man.

He had shared a desk that year with a man who was. The man saw patterns everywhere: in the dates on a run of permits, in which way a witness looked when she lied, in the order a family hung its washing. About a third of the patterns were real. The other two thirds were the man's own cleverness, reflected back at him off things that meant nothing, and he could not tell the thirds apart, because to him they all shone the same. He had gone on finding them, faster and faster, until a report of his sent four agents into a valley after a smuggling ring that was only three farmers who did not like each other. After that he had been moved to a records office in a town on the coast, where he copied other men's reports into a ledger, and was not unhappy, and wrote a very fine hand.

Coss had watched all of that from the other side of their desk and taken a lesson from it that he had never once had cause to regret. Brilliance found things, but it found false things nearly as easily as true ones. Attention, put down patiently day after day and year after year, found the true things in the end, and very seldom found the false ones, because it was too slow and too dull to be fooled by its own shine.

So he had chosen attention. Most files were exactly as dull as they looked, and the few that were not usually said so early, if you were listening.

This one was not saying anything. That was what kept him at the desk with the lamp.

The first sheet was a copy of the registration. *Caelen Hesk-ward. Registration 41-7843-V.* The certifying office was on a street called Weaver's Row, in Denvash, and the officer was a Warden-Adjunct named Pellin, whose signature was small and quick and gave nothing away. The date of the Kindling was in the autumn, fifty-odd days ago. On the line for the classification there was one word in square brackets, stamped, and no tier, no rank and no Path.

The second sheet was the residential notice under Statute 14, Section 9. He knew the form, though he had never had cause to serve one, and he knew what it had been written to do. It took a residence away from a person in one line and then told whoever held the standing, very politely, what it would cost them to keep that person close.

The third sheet was a gate entry. *Denvash, Outer gate, eastbound, on foot.* It was dated the third day after the Kindling, and it was passive, which meant that a guard had read the card in the ordinary way and the record had marked it and nobody had been asked to do anything about it. Eastbound from Denvash, on foot, was the merchant road, and the merchant road came here in two days, and anybody who knew the road would have guessed this city. That part was not strange.

The fourth sheet was the request for the sweep, routed to Ardenmere about three weeks after the gate entry. It carried an authorization code in the place where a name should have been, and one line of instruction.

*Confirm location, status, and compliance.*

The fifth was the sweep's return, in a clerk's round hand. *Location: a lodging house known as Torvin's, Unranked District. Status: resident. Compliance: nothing observed.* It was signed by the clerk of this post, per the report of the officer who had walked it.

The sixth sheet was Coss's own brief, which told him to follow up the sweep, confirm what it had confirmed, and summon the subject for compliance evaluation as procedure required.

That was all of it. He had carried six sheets of paper two days east on the merchant road in a case built for sixty, and he had known on the first morning out of Denvash that the case was light. He had stopped being surprised about it somewhere around the first milestone.

He was not surprised now. He was doing what he did with any file that bothered him, which was to set down, one at a time, not what it said but what it ought to have said and did not.

A non-standard result at Kindling set a procedure going. He had never handled a [SHATTERED] file himself; he did not know anybody who had. But he had handled three files for results the standard framework had trouble with, and they had all grown the same way, in the same order, as faithfully as a tree grows rings. The certifying district wrote a preliminary risk assessment within the first week, because the certifying district was frightened of being blamed for anything that came after. Then a residential compliance note followed, setting out what the household's standing now was and what it was likely to become. Then there was a cross-reference to the standing-holder's own file, because the residential notice threatened a guild review in so many words. When the Compact notified a guild under that statute, the guild opened its review by its own procedure, and the review had a number, and the number went into the file.

Denvash had a compliance office that produced risk assessments as regularly as a bakery produces bread. There was none here. There was no residential note beyond the notice itself. There was no cross-reference to any review. And at the head of the file, where every case he had ever been handed carried its case number in red ink, there was nothing at all. Nobody had opened a case. There was no handler named, nobody whose desk this sat on between visits.

It was not a case. It was some papers in a case.

He had once, on a training course in his second year, been shown a [SHATTERED] file from the archive, as a curiosity, by an instructor who liked curiosities. It had been very old and very thick. He did not remember anything that was in it. He remembered the thickness, and the way the instructor had had to use both hands.

He sat back. The desk rocked.

Then he took out the register.

---

Every field agent carried one. It was a small black book, newly printed each spring, that listed the authorization codes of every office an agent might lawfully take an instruction from, so that he would know a true order from a forged one. Coss's was this spring's. He had checked that before he left, as he checked it every spring, because a register a season out of date was worse than no register at all.

He laid the request for the sweep beside it and found the code.

The prefix was right. It was the region's own prefix, the one that headed his own brief and a hundred others he had carried. The length was right, and the order of letters and figures was right. It looked, in every particular he could see, like a code he ought to know.

The rest of it was not in the register.

He went down the column twice, slowly, with his thumb under each line. He went down the next column in case it had been misfiled. It had not. He turned to the back of the book, where the closed codes were listed, the ones of offices that had been merged or abolished, and it was not there either.

So somebody had requested a sweep of this district under an authorization his own register did not list. It was not a forgery; he was fairly sure of that. The post had acted on it, and regional had issued his own brief on the back of it. It was real. It came from somewhere. It came from somewhere above the reach of the book in his hand.

He looked at the instruction again. *Confirm location, status, and compliance.*

He had read a great many briefs. Most of them told you, if you read them properly, what the office that wrote them was afraid of: a guild dispute that might turn into a riot, a man who might run, a district that might be hiding something it should declare. This one told him nothing, and he had learned that a brief which told you nothing had usually been written by somebody who did not wish to be quoted.

He did not let himself go further than that.

He had seen what happened to agents who did. They asked a question above their clearance, of the wrong person, in the wrong week, and nothing happened to them at all. Then, a season later, they found themselves posted somewhere that was the same rank and the same pay and a long way from anything that mattered. Speculation was free for men in taverns. For a field agent it was the most expensive thing in the world.

So he took out his own working log, which was a plain book of his own and not the Compact's, and wrote in it what he could stand behind.

*File thin for its classification. No preliminary assessment from the certifying district. No residential note. No cross-reference to the standing-holder's review. No case number. No handler. Sweep authorization not in my register. Query on return.*

He looked at the last three words for some time. Then he left them, because they were true, and blotted the page, and turned the lamp down a little to save the oil.

---

He had come up the fish steps that morning with the case under his arm and his bag in his other hand, off a carrier's cart that had been two days on the road and felt like four.

The boy unloading the post sacks on the landing had pointed him up the steps without being asked twice. The post had been where the boy pointed: a squat brown building at the head of the steps, with a board on its front wall of official notices that had plainly not been read by anybody since they were nailed up, and a door that stuck.

Inside, the clerk had been in the middle of a dispute.

Two men stood in front of his counter, a cooper and a man with blue-stained hands, and between them on the counter was a sheet of paper with two roofs drawn on it in chalk and a gutter running from one to the other. The gutter, as far as Coss could make out, drained the cooper's roof onto the other man's woodpile, and had done so for nine years, and the other man had only now decided that he minded. The clerk had a length of string with knots in it at regular spacings, and he was measuring the chalk gutter with it, very seriously, while both men watched the string as though it might take a side.

"Permits," the clerk said to Coss, without looking up from the string. "Permits and boundaries. They're all anybody in this district will come to me about, because a permit is the one thing I've got that they want." He laid the string along the chalk. "Two knots. It overhangs by two knots."

"It's overhung by two knots for nine years," said the cooper.

"Then it's been wrong for nine years," said the man with the blue hands.

"Then it's been your woodpile for nine years," said the clerk, "and you've had nine years of dry wood under my gutter, and I'm not hearing it." He paused. "His gutter. I'm not hearing it. Go and have a drink about it and come back on Tuesday if you're still cross."

They went, still arguing, and the clerk turned at last to Coss and looked at the case under his arm and sighed in the way of a man who had recognized a kind of morning.

His name was Halvern. He was about ten years younger than Coss, sandy and soft round the middle, with ink on the side of his writing hand and an air of having been put here by somebody who had then forgotten him. He did registrations, he said, and boundaries, and permits for awnings and drains and the keeping of more than six pigs, and now and then, when regional remembered he existed, whatever regional sent.

"Field files travel sealed," Coss said, and set the case on the counter. "I break the seal here, in front of you, and you sign that you saw it whole."

Halvern looked at the seal, which was whole, and at Coss.

"Does that matter? Out here?"

"It matters to the next man who opens it."

Halvern considered this, and seemed to find nothing wrong with it, and got out a book with a cracked spine and wrote in it. *Seal whole. Broken in my presence at the post, Ardenmere.* He signed it, and turned it round for Coss to sign under him, and Coss broke the wax with his thumbnail and took out the six sheets, and Halvern watched him do it and said, in the tone of a man passing remarks about the weather, "That's not much."

"No," said Coss.

---

He went down again at the end of the afternoon, after the third reading, and asked for the log.

Halvern found it without much enthusiasm, licked his thumb, and paged back through it while the light went orange in the window behind him. The front room smelled of ink and damp stone and somebody's lunch.

"Sweep," he said at last, and put his finger on a line. "Came about three weeks back. Not from us."

"Not from Ardenmere."

"Not from anybody on this side of the river, no. It came down from regional, with a code on it where a name would usually go." He turned the book so that Coss could read the line, which was one line long and said no more than the request in Coss's own case. The code was the same. It looked no more familiar written in Halvern's round hand than it had in print. "We get one maybe twice a year. Usually it's something you could guess at without being told. A man who's run from a debt across the river. A guild quarrel that's turned into fists. This one didn't say. We don't ask." He shrugged. "Asking gets you a longer form back and no better answer, and I've got pigs to count."

"Who walked it?"

"One of the constables off the bridge. We borrowed him for an afternoon." Halvern closed the log. "Regional wanted it to look like the Compact, not like the bridge, so we lent him a pin out of the drawer for the day. He wore his own coat and our pin." He said it without any particular feeling. "Which tells you, I'd say, that regional minded what it looked like a good deal more than it minded who was doing the looking."

Coss did not answer that, because it was the kind of remark a clerk could make and an agent could not.

"The return says he found the house."

"He did." Halvern put the log back on its shelf. "Nobody here would answer a lodging list, so he didn't bother asking for one. He asked the carriers which house had letters going west to Denvash. The clerk down at the hut told him. That clerk would tell you what you had for breakfast, if you asked him nicely and he thought it made him look clever." He sat down on his stool. "That's how things get found on this side, mostly. Not by asking the person. By asking whoever stands next to them."

Coss turned that over and did not like it, and did not say so.

"Whatever it's about," Halvern said, and he seemed for a moment almost apologetic, "it wasn't cooked here. We only carry the pot." He picked up a pen. "If you want a bed, the house at the end of the bridge keeps a room for anybody the post sends. They'll charge you a Compact price."

"Which is?"

"Double." Halvern dipped the pen. "There's a cot upstairs. It's yours for nothing, and it's worth about that."

---

He took the cot.

Now, with the fourth reading done and the lamp low, he sat for a while at the window with his coat round his shoulders and looked out.

The district went down to the river below him in a long jumble of roofs and lanes and lamps, untidy and alive, with no wall anywhere in it and no gate at its edge. Across the river the Ranked core went up its hill in rings, each ring with its own wall and its own lamps along the top of it, every lamp the same distance from the next. From here it looked like a ladder somebody had leaned against the dark. He had passed through a good many rings in his life with a card that opened them. He had not often looked at them from the outside.

He thought, as he did every first night away, about his daughter. She would be fourteen at midsummer. She had asked him at the door, with her coat half on, whether this one was going to be interesting, and he had answered honestly that he could not say yet. She had said that was what he always said. He had said that was because it was always true.

He thought now that he would have to tell her he had been right not to know.

He put the six sheets back in the case. It weighed nothing. He lay down on the cot in his shirt with his coat over him and listened to the river, and to somebody singing in the street below and stopping, and slept better than he had expected to.

---

In the morning he went to the lodging house.

He found it without asking, from the return and a map of the district he had bought from Halvern for less than it was worth: two streets in from the east road, down a lane with a gutter in the middle, a narrow stone house with a wooden sign over the door that said ROOMS. The O had faded less than the other letters and leaned harder, so that the word looked as though it had been about to fall over for a very long time.

He knocked.

The man who opened the door was broad and heavy and unshaven, past middle age, with the face of somebody who had opened this door a great many times and expected nothing from it. He looked at Coss's face, and at his coat, and at the case, and back at his face, in that order and without any hurry. Coss had met him before. Not this man, but this man's kind, in a dozen districts in a dozen cities, and he knew before either of them spoke how the conversation was going to go.

"Torvin," the man said.

"I'm told a boy called Caelen Hesk-ward lodges here. Fourteen."

"There's a room paid to the end of the week."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what there is." Torvin did not move from the doorway. He did not block it, exactly. He simply went on standing in it, as a post goes on standing in a road. "Room's paid. Somebody sleeps in it. I don't keep a list of who."

"Is it him?"

"It's paid."

Coss had tried, early in his career, to get past answers like this. He had tried charm first, and then a little pressure, and then the leverage that a Compact pin was supposed to give a man on a doorstep, and he had learned every time that all three produced the same thing: a slightly smaller version of the same answer, and a much longer memory of the man who had pushed. He had stopped pushing years ago. It cost him a little information on doorsteps like this one. It saved him a great deal of goodwill on the next doorstep, and the one after that, because word of how an agent behaved got round a district faster than the agent did.

"Is he in now?"

"No."

"Do you know where he is?"

"Not in."

From somewhere behind Torvin, at the back of the house, a woman's voice called out. "Tell him about the oats."

"He doesn't want to know about the oats," said Torvin, without turning round.

There was a short silence from the back of the house, of a kind Coss recognized from his own kitchen.

He wrote in his book that the address matched the return, and that the lane was Unranked by the district map, and that the householder had answered every question he was legally bound to answer and not one word more. Then he thanked Torvin, who did not seem to know what to do with being thanked, and turned to go.

"He pays on time," Torvin said, behind him.

Coss looked back.

"That's the most I'll say for anybody," said Torvin, "and I've said it." And he shut the door.

---

He spent the rest of the day walking.

He did not ask direct questions. Direct questions made people careful, and careful people told you what they thought you wanted, which was worse than nothing. He walked, and bought things, and stood where people stood, and listened, and now and then asked somebody something small, phrased as idle curiosity, about the weather or the price of fish or which way was the river.

He learned a great deal, and very little of it was about the boy.

He learned that the district settled its own quarrels, mostly in the open, at the well by the four lanes or at the edge of the market, with an old woman or a stallholder or whoever happened to be passing deciding who was right, and both sides abiding by it more often than not. He learned that half the people he passed had Paths and used them at their work without a thought: a porter lifting a barrel by its rim with one hand, a woman frying fish in a pan with no fire under it, a man who stood perfectly still at the end of a plank while two others walked a loaded handcart across it. None of it was anything the Compact counted as practice. It was work. The Compact did not count porters.

He learned that the carriers' clerk at the bottom of the fish steps would, as Halvern had said, tell anybody anything. Coss asked him only which bag went west and when, and the clerk told him, and then told him without being asked that the boy at Torvin's wrote west every week, regular as a clock, to Fen Street, and that he could tell him the day.

Coss found that he did not want to know the day. He found also that he did not like knowing that anybody who asked would be told it, and he walked away from the hut with that sitting in him like a stone in a boot.

He went past the workshops along the river, where every ground floor was opened up to the street and somebody in every doorway was making or mending something. At the end of the row, in a doorway with no sign, a man with a loupe in his eye looked up at him exactly once and then went back to his clock. On the doorframe somebody had cut four words into the wood and filled them with black paint. *IF IT'S STILL HERE.* Coss read them twice and could not decide whether they were the beginning of a sentence or the whole of one.

In the afternoon, at a fish stall, two women were packing up, and one said to the other, "Are you going to the circuit Sunday?" and the other said she might, if her sister would mind the stall, and they went on to talk about the sister.

He filed it where he filed such things. Every district he had ever walked had its Sunday entertainment, dice in a back room or dogs in a yard or a long-running argument at a public house about which barge was fastest, and it was none of the Compact's business and never had been. He did not stop.

At the north edge of the district the ground began to rise, and at the mouth of a lane going up there was a chalked board with prices on it for yard time by the hour, and over the walls of the lane came the flat smack of hands on canvas and somebody shouting at somebody else to keep his elbow in. He stood at the bottom of the lane for as long as it took to read the prices. Boxing yards, he thought, and porters keeping fit, and young men showing off for each other, the same as in every district he had ever known. His brief was about one boy, and the boy's address was down by the river, and his instructions spoke of *practice* in the sense the certification offices used it, a Path declared and demonstrated and assessed. He turned away from the lane and went back down toward the dyers' sheds.

That night he wrote in his working log by the low lamp, with the desk rocking under his wrist.

*Second day. Householder cooperative to the legal minimum. Address confirmed. Nothing observed.*

He held the pen over the page for a time. Then he added a line, because it was true and because he did not know where else to put it.

*The district runs like a town, with its own ways of settling things. My categories do not fit it well. I am not sure what I would be looking at if I saw it.*

He blotted that, and looked at it, and did not cross it out.

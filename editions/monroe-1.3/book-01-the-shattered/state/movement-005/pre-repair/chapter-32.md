# Chapter 32 — Three Flat Sentences

He wrote the first draft at the kitchen table that night, after the others had gone up, and it was a page and a half long, and it was terrible, and he could not see why.

He had the three passages copied out in the careful hand at the top of the Log page, and he had the logic laid out under them in four steps, the way he had read it aloud in the reading room. All he had to do was put the two together on a clean sheet for a man in a dark coat to read. He had thought it would take a quarter of an hour.

It took him until the range had gone cold.

Every sentence he wrote wanted another sentence after it to hold it up. He would write that a standard evaluation assessed a person against a recognized tier and Path, and then find himself writing that he understood this might seem a narrow reading, but that he believed it was the plain sense of the section. He would set down statute 19, and then, without deciding to, add that he did not mean to suggest that the evaluation was unnecessary or improper, only that it could not proceed in the form the summons gave. He wrote that he hoped he would not be thought to be avoiding anything. He wrote that he was willing to be evaluated by whatever procedure the regulation did allow. He wrote, at the bottom, that he was sorry for any trouble it caused.

He read it all back, and it read like a boy standing at a desk with his hands behind his back, explaining.

Torvin's wife came down once, in her shawl, for something she had forgotten. She looked at him, and at the pages, and at the cold range, and she did not say anything. She cut a heel off the loaf and put it beside his elbow with a cup of milk, and went back up.

He crossed out the sentence about trouble. He crossed out the one about not avoiding anything. He read it again and it was better and it was still wrong, and the wrongness was in something he could not put his pencil on, like a bracket that held at working load and bent at twice it and left you no mark to show where.

He slept for perhaps four hours. He dreamed that he was reading the handbook and every note at the foot of every page said *see the note above*, and woke with the window grey and his hand on the Log.

---

The frost was thick again in the grey half. Lira was sitting on the low wall at the side of the yard with her coat buttoned to the chin and her right arm inside it, not in the sleeve, and the bread on her knee.

"Don't ask about the shoulder," she said. "Give it to me."

He gave her the sheet.

She read it the way she read a bout from the barrel, which was the way she read anything she was not immediately sure of: once fast, to have the whole shape, and then again slowly, stopping, going back. She did not say anything for a long time. A crow came down onto the eaves of the public house and looked at them both and went away again.

"The law's right," she said at last.

"I know the law's right. I read it four times."

"I know you did." She tapped a line two-thirds of the way down with one finger of her good hand. "This. You've put down what statute 19 says. It's a fact, and you're stating it like a fact, and that's good. But then there's this whole next part." She ran her finger along it. "*I understand that the summons was issued in good faith, and I do not wish to suggest that any officer has acted improperly, but I believe that on a close reading*." She looked up. "Who's that for?"

"Him."

"No. It's for the argument you think he's going to make. You've written his argument for him, in advance, so that you can answer it." She shook her head. "You don't do that in a bout. You don't stand on your mark before the first exchange and say to Vell, *I know he's going to come in high, and I want everyone to know I've thought about it.* You just stand there. You let him come in high, and then he finds out."

"Somebody's going to argue with it. He's going to argue."

"Then let him find his own argument. He won't, because there isn't one. You read it four times; you know there isn't one. But you won't show him there isn't one by sounding frightened that there might be." She took the pencil out of his hand without asking and drew a line through the whole of the bracing part, neatly, so that the words could still be read under it, the way he did it in the Log. "Cut all that. You want three sentences. Flat. Like things that are already settled."

"Say them."

She thought about it with her head on one side and the pencil against her lip.

"*A standard compliance evaluation can only assess a classification the standard framework recognizes.*" She wrote it at the bottom of the page in her quick slanting hand. "*[SHATTERED] is not recognized within that framework.*" She wrote that. "*The evaluation named in this summons cannot proceed under standard procedure.*" She wrote it, and put the pencil down. "Then the numbers. Then your name. Then stop."

He read the three sentences.

They were better at once, as much better as a bout became the moment you stopped explaining your plan to yourself in the middle of an exchange and simply did the next thing. There was nothing in them to take hold of. There was nothing in them that asked for anything, or hoped for anything, or was sorry. They lay on the page like three flat stones set in a stream, and you could walk across them.

"You want it to read like somebody who knows the law, not somebody who's scared of it," Lira said. "Being sure doesn't change what's true. It just changes whether a man argues with it out of habit."

"I am scared."

"I know you are." She said it plainly. "It doesn't have to be on the page."

She gave him back the sheet. He sat on the wall beside her and wrote it out again clean, from the beginning, on a fresh page torn from the back of the old notebook: the three sentences, then the handbook part and section and note, the register and the statute, the appendix and the place where its page had come loose, then *Cael Hesk-ward, Registration 41-7843-V*, and nothing after. It was eleven lines long. The first draft had been fifty.

Lira read it over his shoulder.

"That's good," she said. "That's very good. I've read Compact paper before, you know, in the life before the academy." She did not say anything more about that life; she never did. "That doesn't read like a boy who found the law yesterday in a cellar with an old man pretending not to watch him. It reads like somebody who's been doing this for years and is a bit bored by it."

"Halden wasn't watching."

"Everybody in a reading room's watching." She broke the bread in half with her good hand and her teeth, and gave him the larger piece. "You'll be all right this afternoon. I'm not saying it to be kind. I've read the page. The page is right."

She would not let him sit on the wall after that. She made him walk the box drill at the slow speed while she called the corners from the wall with her arm inside her coat, because, she said, his hands were shaking and he would be no use at a desk with his hands shaking, and the box would give them something to do. She was right. By the time the sun came down the east wall into the yard and there was no grey half left, his feet were going to the corners by themselves, and his hands had stopped.

---

The post kept its hours by a clock on the wall of the front room, behind the counter, and when he came in out of the cold it said a quarter to the second hour after noon. He had come early, because he was always early, and because the paper said *during the hours the post is open*, and he did not want there to be any argument about which hours those were.

There was one bench, against the wall opposite the counter, under a board of notices exactly as unread as the one outside. He sat on it with the Log on his knee and the folded sheet inside the Log's front cover.

The clerk behind the counter was a sandy, soft man with ink on the side of his hand, and he was in the middle of a dispute.

Two men stood in front of him. One was a cooper; Cael knew him by sight from the lane of workshops, the one beside the man who made only buckets. The other had blue hands to the wrist from the dye sheds. Between them on the counter was a sheet of paper with two roofs drawn on it in chalk and a gutter between them.

"You told us Tuesday," said the man with the blue hands. "It's Tuesday."

"I said if you were still cross," said the clerk.

"I'm still cross."

"So am I," said the cooper.

The clerk looked at the two of them for some time with the expression of a man who has been given a pig he did not order. Then he took a length of knotted string from under the counter and laid it along the chalk gutter, very seriously, while both men watched the string as if it might change sides.

"Still two knots," he said.

Cael watched the string. Everybody in this district, he thought, had a string. Lira had a string, and the clerk at the post had a string, and the old man in the yards probably had a string somewhere, and had never needed it. He found that he wanted, absurdly, to laugh, and that if he did, it would come out as something else, so he did not.

He opened the Log to the careful page and read the three passages one more time, silently, his lips hardly moving.

He had learned in the circle that the moment before an exchange was worse than the exchange. His body knew, somewhere under thinking, that waiting cost more than doing, and would not be talked out of the cost no matter how good the plan. He had done the work. He had read it four times out loud. He knew the citation was right as surely as he knew anything since Weaver's Row. None of that made the bench any softer.

At two, the clerk looked up at the clock and then at him, and jerked his head at a narrow stair at the side of the room.

"Up there," he said. "He's expecting you."

The cooper and the man with the blue hands both turned and looked at him, curiously, as he got up. He went up the stair sideways, because it was too narrow to go up any other way.

---

The room at the top was small and plain, with one window looking down the fish steps to the river, a cot along the wall under it with a blanket folded square, and a partition of planks on one side that did not quite reach the ceiling. There was a chair in front of a desk, and a man behind the desk, and on the floorboards by the desk's left front leg, a hand's width from where it ought to have been, there was a small grey square of folded paper.

The desk rocked when the man leaned forward. *Tock.*

Cael had sat under that sound for most of a day. He found that he knew exactly where to look for it, and that the man had seen him look.

"Caelen Hesk-ward," said the man.

He was about forty, as Torvin had said, with a thin face and close-cut hair going grey at the sides, and a habit, which Cael saw almost at once, of pressing his thumb to the bridge of his nose when he looked at a paper. He had taken his coat off; it hung on the back of the door. He did not stand. He did not seem unfriendly. He looked at Cael the way the mender looked at a frame before he leaned on the lever: thoroughly, without any hurry, to see where it was not true.

"Cael," said Cael.

"The summons says Caelen."

"The summons is right. People say Cael."

The man considered that and seemed to file it. "Coss," he said, and touched the paper on his desk where his name was written at the foot, as if the name needed its evidence too. "Sit down. This is a standard compliance evaluation. I'll ask you some questions about your residence and your practice, and you'll—"

"Before you begin," said Cael, and took the folded sheet out of the front of the Log, and held it out across the desk. "There's this."

Coss looked at the sheet in Cael's hand, and then at Cael. Something moved in his face, the faint irritation of a man whose straightforward afternoon has just stopped being straightforward. He took it.

"What is it?"

"A procedural objection."

---

Coss read it.

Cael watched him read it, because the man's face was information. He watched the eyes go down the eleven lines once, quickly, the way you read a thing to find out what sort of thing it is. He watched them stop at the three flat sentences, and go on, and come to the numbers. Then he watched Coss go back to the top and read it again, much more slowly, and saw the irritation go out of his face and something else come into it. It was surprise first. Under the surprise there was a thing Cael had not expected and took a moment to put a name to.

It looked like respect. It was not happy about being respect. It was there anyway.

Coss put the sheet down on the desk, and the desk rocked, and he looked at Cael properly for the first time since he had come in: not as a name on a summons, Cael thought, but as something new turning up in a file that had been odd from its first page.

Then he picked the sheet up again at once, as though he needed a third look to be sure the second had not been a trick of the light. This time he read it with his lips moving. Cael heard him say the numbers under his breath, *Part Four, section twelve, note three*, and stop, and look at nothing, and go on. *Supplementary Register, nineteen.* Cael understood that he was checking them. He was not checking them against a book. He was checking them against the text of the regulation that he carried in his own head, well enough to test a stranger's citation without getting up from his chair.

"You found this yourself," Coss said. It was not quite a question.

"Yes."

"Where?"

"Downstairs." Cael looked at the floorboards between them, and Coss's eyes went down to them too. "In the reading room. Yesterday."

Coss sat very still for a moment. Then, very slightly, so slightly that Cael would have missed it if he had not spent seven weeks watching people's faces for exactly this, he looked at the floor of his own office as if he had just been told something about it.

"I've done compliance evaluations for eleven years," he said slowly. He set the sheet down and laid his hand flat on it, as though holding the problem still on the desk while he decided what to do with it. "In eleven years nobody has ever once objected to the summons itself. Most people don't know there's anything to object with. The ones who know it exists can't find their way to it. That note's in the smallest print in the book." He tapped the sheet. "You've cited it right. The part, the section, the note. The statute. The appendix. All of it, the way it's written." He looked at Cael. "Do you know how unusual that is?"

"I had a day and a reason to be careful."

"A day." Coss said it after him. "Our regional training office took the better part of a fortnight to put together a study guide that covers about half of what's on this sheet, and that was written by clerks whose whole work is cross-referencing the compliance statutes. You did it in a day. In a cellar. Under my desk."

"And most of a night."

Something went across Coss's face that was very nearly a laugh, and was put away again almost as fast as it came, the way a man puts away a thing he has decided long ago is not safe to carry at work. He did not quite manage to put all of it away.

"Is it wrong?" said Cael, into the pause.

Coss did not answer at once.

He was quiet in the particular way of a man going back over his own certainty before he says something he will not be able to take back, running it again in his head from the beginning. Cael had done the same thing in the reading room the day before, the fourth time, from the bottom of the column up. He recognized it, and sat still, and waited.

"No," Coss said at last. He said it flatly, as a man says the reading on a dial that he would rather have read something else. "It's not wrong. A standard compliance evaluation can't be done on your classification. Which means I can't do the evaluation I was sent out here to do." He let his breath out, and for a moment his composure slipped far enough to show what was under it, and what was under it looked to Cael like frustration, and not at him. It was aimed at something past him, at the window, or further. "This is going to be a very unpopular report."

"Why unpopular? You'd be keeping to the rules."

"Because the people who asked for the sweep that brought me here didn't want a delay. They wanted the file resolved." Coss said it with the candour of somebody who had decided, at some point in the last few minutes, that pretending to this boy would cost him more than it bought. "I don't know what resolved means to them. Closed one way or closed the other. I honestly couldn't tell you. But a boy of fourteen reading me the statute across my own desk and walking off with six weeks isn't going to look like resolved to anybody above me." He looked down at the sheet. "I write accurate reports. That's my work. Today's report is going to say that a boy with a day in a cellar has stopped eleven years of mine dead at the door. I won't enjoy writing that. I'll write it anyway, because it's true, and because the other thing is worse."

"What happens now?"

"Now I stamp the summons closed, pending, and I apply to regional for a senior evaluator, because that's what the regulation asks for in your case." Coss picked up a stamp from the corner of the desk. He held it a moment, longer than stamping a paper required, and Cael understood that he was weighing something that had nothing to do with stamping. Then he brought it down on the summons with a good deal more force than the job needed. The desk rocked. *Tock.* "Senior authorization takes time. Regional has to review the file, find an evaluator who's qualified, and send them out to a district this far from anywhere. Six weeks, as a rule. Longer if the evaluators are busy, and they usually are."

Six weeks.

Cael did the sum at once, the way he timed a window in a bout. Six weeks was forty-two days. It was not an answer. It was room.

"Who asked for the sweep?" he said.

Coss looked at him for a long moment.

"I don't know," he said. "That's true. It's also all I'm going to say about it." He set the stamp back exactly where it had been on the corner of the desk. "Don't ask me again. It wouldn't do either of us any good."

---

"Thank you," said Cael.

He meant it. He understood, saying it, that Coss had not done this as a kindness. He had done it because it was correct, in the same way that he did everything, Cael thought, and doing the job correctly when correct was inconvenient to the people who had sent you was its own kind of honesty, and not a common one. He had not expected to learn that from a man in a Compact office.

Coss was looking at him again, with something in his face that Cael could not read, as though he were deciding whether to say a thing that was not part of the evaluation.

"May I ask you something?" he said. "Not for the file."

"You can ask."

"What are you going to do with six weeks?"

Cael thought about how much to say. He thought about the reading room under this floor, and the three lines in the margin of the Log, and Hesk's *don't change what you do*. He thought that the man had been more honest with him in the last quarter of an hour than his position required. He decided on something close to the whole of it.

"Get stronger. Build a reason for people to notice if I disappear."

Something crossed Coss's face. It was not approval, and it was not alarm, but it lived somewhere near both of them.

"That's the cleverest thing a person in your position could do," he said slowly, "or the most dangerous. Most clever things are both, when you look closely." He shut the folder over the stamped summons and the objection and stood up, which Cael understood meant the meeting was over. "Don't thank me. You've bought six weeks. If I were you I'd use every one of them to work out what you mean to do with the classification you've got. Because whoever put a sweep on this district won't be satisfied with a procedure. They want something settled. I don't know what." He looked at the window. "I can buy you time. I can't buy you the answer to whatever they're actually asking. That part's yours."

Cael stood. He looked, without meaning to, at the folded grey square of paper on the floor by the desk's left front leg.

"It's not the floor," he said. "It's the leg. That's come out from under it."

Coss looked down at the paper. He looked at it for some time, as though it had been lying there for a great many visits by a great many men and nobody had ever once said so.

"So it has," he said.

Cael went down the narrow stair sideways. In the front room, the cooper and the man with the blue hands had gone, and the clerk was rolling up his string. He did not look up.

Cael had reached the door before he noticed what had not been asked. In the whole of the quarter of an hour, Coss had not said one word about the yards.

# Chapter 30 — Local Colour

On his second afternoon in Ardenmere, Coss was standing in a lopsided triangle where three streets met, eating a skewer of grilled mutton, when the roar came over the roofs.

It came from somewhere north and a little uphill, two or three streets away. It was not one voice but a great many at once, going up together and holding, and then breaking into pieces, some of them cheering and some of them groaning and some of them only shouting because everybody else was. Then it settled into a long, low noise like a river over stones. A few people in the triangle looked up toward it, the way people look up at a flight of birds, and went back to what they had been doing.

"That's the Cinder House," said the woman at the skewer cart, turning her meat. She said it to nobody in particular, as if the roar had asked her a question. "Sunday."

"What's on?"

He asked it lightly, the way he asked about the price of fish. The woman looked at him for a moment over her firebox. It was not a long look and it was not a hostile one. It was the look of somebody who has been asked by a stranger in a good coat what the weather is, and has noticed the coat.

"The circuit," she said, and her face went quite flat, pleasantly flat, like a shutter painted to look like a window. "The Sunday one. You'll want another of those? They're better hot."

He bought another, because it would have been rude not to, and because it was what a man who was only curious about the weather would have done.

He knew that face. He had seen it in a dozen districts on a dozen stallholders, on the day a man from the Compact asked about something the district did on its own account and would rather keep. Dogs in a yard, mostly, or cocks, or dice in the back of a public house where the stakes ran higher than the law liked, or a long-running wager on barges. Every district had one. It was none of his business and had never been. He had learned in his first two years that pushing at that face got you nothing but a district that remembered you had pushed. Then the next time you came, and needed something that mattered, the shutters were up before you reached the corner.

The circuit, then, at a public house, on a Sunday. He put it where he had put it the day before, beside the two women packing up their fish, with the dice and the dogs.

In the middle of the triangle there was a post with a board nailed to it, so thickly covered with notices that the bottom ones had gone into the grain of the wood. He did not go near it. A man in a dark coat standing in front of a district's own board, reading, would be a man the district talked about by suppertime, and he had a summons to write tonight in a place where he would need people to talk to him tomorrow. He finished the skewer, wiped his fingers, and went back down toward the river by the long street. Behind him, over the roofs, the noise from the public house rose once more, sharply, and then fell away into clapping.

He did not look round.

---

He wrote the summons that evening at the borrowed desk, with the lamp close and the window gone dark over the fish steps.

He had done what the brief required of him with any exactness. He had confirmed the location. He had confirmed the status. He had walked the district for two days, and had seen nothing he could write down as practice in the sense the certification offices meant it, no Path declared or performed or assessed, and he was honest enough with himself to add that he was no longer sure he would have known it if he had. There remained the one instruction in the brief that was not vague. *Summon the subject for compliance evaluation as procedure requires.*

So he took a summons form out of the case, where three of them lay flat under the six sheets, the only weight the case had carried, and filled it in.

He did it slowly. It was not that he was undecided. He did not suffer much from indecision; he had built a career on gathering what he could and then acting on it, and not standing about afterward wishing he had gathered more. It was something else. It was the care he took with any document that would go on existing after the evening he wrote it in, in a file, in other men's hands, saying what he had said in his name long after he had forgotten saying it.

He wrote the subject's name and registration. He wrote the place: the district office, the post at the head of the fish steps, Ardenmere. He wrote the window for appearance, which was forty-eight hours from delivery, during the hours the post kept, and he signed his name at the foot, *Coss, field agent*, and pressed the seal into the wax with the heel of his hand.

Then he came to the last clause, which was printed on the form already, and which he had only to read and leave standing.

*If the person summoned does not appear within the stated time, a compliance flag will be entered against the registration, and a filing made for physical retrieval.*

He had left that clause standing perhaps a dozen times in eleven years. Every one of those times it had been for an adult: a man who had not come to an evaluation he was owed, a woman who had moved districts without telling anybody and then would not answer letters. *Physical retrieval* had meant, in his own experience, two men at a door and an unpleasant walk, and once a man who had sat down in the road and refused to get up, and had been carried, and had complained about it to everybody in the waiting room. It had never once, in his own experience, meant anyone was hurt.

He had never left it standing for a child.

He sat with the pen over the paper and did what he always did when a document made him uneasy. He went back through the regulation in his head, section by section, the way another man might go through his pockets, looking for a version of this summons without that clause in it that would still satisfy the procedure. He had a good memory for regulation. It was one of the things he was. He went through the evaluation sections and the summons sections and the notes under them.

The clause was standard. There was no form of the summons without it. To strike it out would be a deviation he could not justify in writing. Coss did not, as a rule, do things he could not justify in writing. That was not because he had no feelings about them. It was because a feeling with no written reason behind it was exactly what got a field agent quietly posted to a coast town to copy other men's reports.

He thought about his daughter, who would be fourteen at midsummer, and who had asked him at the door whether this one was going to be interesting.

He left the clause standing.

He signed it again where the form asked for a second signature, under the clause, as an acknowledgment that he had read it, and he blotted it, and he did not enjoy one moment of any of it. He noted, with the part of his mind that noted everything, that he did not enjoy it. That was information too. It was a case that kept producing more discomfort than six sheets of paper had any right to produce.

---

Then he wrote his preliminary report, to go west in the regional pouch on the morning cart.

It was short, and accurate, and recorded what he had done and what he had seen and what he had not. Location confirmed. Status confirmed. Householder cooperative to the legal minimum. No practice observed. Summons issued for compliance evaluation. He read it through and it was a good report, the kind he had been writing for eleven years, the kind that had made him the man regional sent when they wanted a report that did not lie or panic.

He sat for some time with the pen lifted before he added the last paragraph.

*The subject's file lacks documents that standard practice for this classification would require: an early assessment of risk from the district that certified him, a note on his residential compliance, and a reference to the standing-holder's guild review. I recommend that the certifying district's compliance procedure in this case be reviewed.*

He knew, writing it, what it was. It was the kind of sentence that could be read two ways. On one desk it was a careful agent being thorough. On another it was a field agent asking, in writing, why somebody else's paperwork looked the way it did. He did not know whose desk it would land on. He did not know who had written *Confirm location, status, and compliance* above a code his own register did not list. He had no way to know which way the sentence would be read.

He left it in. Whatever was going on above him with this file, he had settled the matter as he settled most things, without any fuss: if a report of his was ever to be wrong, he wanted it wrong for noticing too much, and never for having happened not to notice.

He sealed the report and set it by the door for the pouch.

Then he put on his coat, turned the collar up, and carried the summons through the dark lanes to the house with the leaning O.

Torvin opened the door. He looked at Coss, and at the paper, and at the seal on the paper.

"He's not in."

"Give it to him when he is."

Torvin looked at the seal for a moment longer. His face did not do anything that Coss could have written down. Then he took the paper, and nodded once, and shut the door, and Coss stood in the lane under the sign and found that he had been waiting, without meaning to, for the man to say whether he would.

He walked back to the post. Over the roofs, somewhere north, a few late voices were still going, the tail end of a crowd going home from a public house.

---

"First exchange," said Cael. "You went sideways. You didn't go back. Everybody goes back from her, and you went sideways."

Lira walked beside him down the lane in the dusk with her canvas roll on her back and her hands in her jacket pockets, and did not look at him, and did not interrupt. The lanterns along the lane were coming on one at a time, as somebody went along ahead of them with a taper, so that they walked out of the dark into a little light and out of it again.

"You touched the shield twice with two fingers. You didn't hit it. Your left foot stayed. I watched it every time you moved and it stayed every time."

"Mm."

"Second exchange. She'd fixed the dip. She held the shield up through every step." He heard his own voice go a little tight, and let it. "You went in anyway, because you'd already started. She turned half a turn and caught your right shoulder with the edge. You went down on one hand and came up again. You kept your feet."

"It hurts," said Lira, conversationally. "That's not in your report. I'm putting it in mine."

"Third exchange. You caught the chop and sent it with no middle. Three times. She went after it every time, a quarter-turn too far, and had to put a foot down. The old men in the third row liked it. That's evidence. I heard them."

She laughed, short and real, the one that surprised her.

"Fourth exchange. She got angry and her strikes got big. The dip came back on the fourth step and on the fifth. You went in on her right. She caught you on the side of the head coming out. You worked your jaw afterward." He stopped there for a moment. He had seen one other thing in the fourth exchange, and he had decided at the rope that once was not a pattern, and he did not say it. "Fifth exchange. Two touches. She locked up for them. The step. You were inside on her right while her foot was in the air. She turned and you caught the turn and sent it, and she went round past her own feet and down across the groove." He took a breath. "Your backhand stopped a finger from her ear. Called."

They had come to the bottom of the lane, where it opened out toward the four lanes and the well with the iron cover. Lira stopped, as she always stopped there.

"That's a good report," she said. "You left out what I looked like."

"I didn't see what you looked like. I was watching your feet."

"That's the right answer." She took one hand out of her pocket and looked at it, and at the other, which had a long scrape across the knuckles where it had gone down on the frozen dirt. "No ruling."

"No ruling."

"Good." She looked at the dark beyond the well, toward whichever of the four lanes was hers. "Brenna asked who took the lean off me."

"I heard."

"I told her somebody who watches feet. I didn't tell her who." She looked at him sideways, and the morning brightness was in her face, in the evening, which he had never seen before. "She'll work it out. She's not stupid. She's just angry, mostly." She put the hand back in her pocket. In the pocket something clinked, small and heavy. "Vell paid. It's a good purse on a Sunday when Brenna's friends come. It's a week of door."

"A week of door."

"It goes in a tin," said Lira, "with the other weeks of door." She stepped away toward the well. "Same time tomorrow. And I'm not doing the box. My shoulder's going to be the size of a loaf."

She went off into the dark, and he heard her say, quite clearly, to nobody, "*Three,*" exactly as Brenna had said it, and laugh again.

He walked home slowly, with his hands in his own pockets and the red lines from the rope still in his palms. He was thinking about a tin he had never seen, and about the edge of a shield caught on a forearm and sent on round, and about whether a thing that worked against a gate would work against anything else that had to turn to be strong. He had not thought about the man in the dark coat for nearly two hours. He noticed that when he came to Torvin's door, and was surprised, and a little ashamed, and then mostly glad.

---

Torvin was sitting at the kitchen table on his own, with the lamp lit and his thin ledger shut in front of him, and he was not adding anything.

The range had been banked for the night. The others had gone up. Torvin's wife's spoon lay on the side of the cold pot, and the low brown door at the back of the kitchen was shut, with no smell of pipe smoke under it, because Doss had gone out to the warehouses at dusk. There was nothing on the table but the ledger and the lamp and a folded paper, sealed in dark wax, lying squared to the table's edge where Torvin had put it.

Torvin did not look up when Cael came in. He looked at the paper.

Then he picked it up and held it out, across the table, at the full length of his arm, as though it were a thing that had been left on his step and might still be warm.

"Compact left this," said Torvin. "Didn't like the look of it."

Cael took it. The seal was a hand holding a balance, pressed so cleanly that he could see the lines of the fingers.

Torvin got up. He took the lamp, and then put it back down on the table, because Cael would need it. He went to the foot of the stairs and stopped with his hand on the rail and his back to the kitchen.

"He brought it himself," he said, to the stairs. "Same one. Didn't send a boy." He seemed to think about whether that meant anything, and to decide that it was not his to say. "Lamps," he said, more quietly than he usually said it, and went up.

---

He read it standing, by the range, in the lamplight.

*SUMMONS FOR COMPLIANCE EVALUATION.*

*To the bearer of Registration 41-7843-V, Caelen Hesk-ward, classified [SHATTERED], lodging at the house known as Torvin's, Unranked District, Ardenmere.*

*You are required to present yourself at the district office, the post at the head of the fish steps, Ardenmere, for a standard compliance evaluation, within forty-eight hours of the delivery of this summons, during the hours the post is open.*

*If the person summoned does not appear within the stated time, a compliance flag will be entered against the registration, and a filing made for physical retrieval.*

*Coss, field agent.*

He sat down at the table and read it a second time, because his hands were not steady enough standing, and he wanted to be sure he had every clause and not the blur round it.

He read the last clause a third time, slowly, one word at a time, the way he read a figure that mattered.

*Physical retrieval.*

It was the same voice as the notice in the back of the old notebook. *This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer.* It was the voice that held its meaning at arm's length, in long careful words, so that everything in it was true and nothing in it could be felt. He made himself feel it anyway. It meant that if he did not walk up the fish steps to the post, men would come, and take him there, whether he walked or not.

He thought about Hesk in the side room at Weaver's Row with the crooked tree in the window, saying *there are provisions*. He thought about the green book in the archive in Denvash and its three entries, dead within weeks, and the fourth, unknown, and the phrase *practice-related incident*, and somebody, a long time ago, choosing which kind of true to write down. He thought about a grey coat walking the market slowly from one end to the other, and a dark coat on a doorstep asking for his whole name.

He waited for the fear, the formless one, the one that had sat on his chest in the dark at Fen Street and on the rise above this city and on a great many nights since. It did not come. Something else came instead, and he looked at it with some surprise, because it was nearly calm.

It was a paper. It had a number on it. For seven weeks the thing he had been afraid of had had no edges at all, and now it had come to the door folded and sealed, with a time in it, and a place, and a clause, and a name signed at the bottom. You could work against a thing with edges. You could not work against weather.

He did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was there to be done. Forty-eight hours from delivery. But the post shut at dusk, and it was the middle of Sunday night now, so the hours the post was open ran out at dusk on Tuesday. Forty-three hours, then, and not forty-eight. He had used nearly one of them reading.

He took out the Log, and then the leather book, and laid them on the table side by side in the lamplight.

He almost began. He had the Log open and the pencil in his hand and the old familiar shape of the next hour already laid out in front of him: sit here, alone, at this table, and work it, and find the answer if there was one, and then afterward, when it was done, write to Hesk and tell him. That was how it had always gone. He had solved his own problems quietly at the bench in Denvash and told Hesk at supper, and Hesk had nodded, and that had been a good way to live for fourteen years.

He sat with the pencil above the page and looked at that shape, and saw what it was.

He had promised a girl on a step four nights ago that when he knew a thing, she would be first. He had not said *when I have finished knowing it*. He had not said *when I have solved it and it is safe to tell*.

He shut the Log.

---

Yeni was awake. She was always awake; he had begun to think she did not sleep at all, only lay on her cot with her eyes shut and listened to the room. When he came up the stairs in the dark she said, without moving, "That was a seal."

"Yes."

"Torvin doesn't sit up for anything without a seal." The blanket shifted. "Well?"

"Where does Lira sleep?"

There was a pause in the dark that was a different shape from her usual pauses.

"Now?"

"Now."

He heard her sit up. He could not see her face, only the pale shape of her against the window, and he could feel her looking at him, at the coat he still had on and the paper in his hand, in the way she looked at a tear before she mended it.

"Third lane past the well, going up," said Yeni. "The tall house with the rain barrel that's lost its hoop. She's in the roof, with the two sisters from the dye sheds. Don't knock the front. The woman who owns it is deaf in one ear and cross in both." She lay down again. "Go round the side. There's a window at the bottom of the roof stair. Knock on the glass, three times, soft. The sisters sleep like the dead and Lira doesn't sleep like anything."

"How do you know that?"

"I know everything in this district that's worth knowing." The blanket settled. "Add it to what you owe me."

---

The rain barrel was where she had said, at the corner of a tall narrow house in the third lane up from the well, with its iron hoop gone and its staves leaning outward like the petals of something that had given up being a barrel. He went round the side. There was a small window at the height of his chest, dark, at the bottom of what he could just see was a steep stair going up inside.

He knocked on the glass, three times, soft.

Nothing happened for long enough that he began to think Yeni had been wrong, which he did not believe for a moment. Then a light came down the stair, a candle end in a tin cup, and stopped, and the window opened inward a hand's width, and Lira's face looked out of the gap with her coat pulled on over whatever she slept in and her hair down, which he had never seen.

She took one look at him in the candlelight. She did not say anything about the hour.

"What happened."

He held the paper out through the gap.

She went through it quickly, as she went through everything, standing on the bottom stair with the candle in her other hand, and he watched her face stay perfectly level all the way down until she reached the last clause, and then something in her jaw went tight and stayed tight, and she did not quite manage to stop him seeing it.

"Forty-eight hours."

"Forty-three. The post shuts at dusk."

"Of course you've counted." She read it again. Then she handed it back through the window. "All right. What do you know how to do about this?"

"I don't know yet." He had known on the walk up, somewhere about the well, though he had not put it into words until now. "I know how to read. Everything that's on this paper is on it because something written somewhere says it has to be. The summons comes out of a book. If it comes out of a book, the book has edges."

"The record office."

"There isn't one this side of the river. Only the post."

"There's a reading room under the post." Lira said it slowly, as though she were remembering it from a long way off. "Round the side, on the river side, down three steps. There's a board on the door like the one on the front, that nobody reads. It's the registry's. Anybody the registry has a number for can go in, by law. Nobody does." She looked at him through the gap. "I went in once, in my first week, to see whether they'd let me. An old man sat behind a table and didn't look up. I didn't stay."

He thought about the brown building at the head of the fish steps, with its board that nobody read and its door that was always shut. He thought about a man in a dark coat sitting somewhere inside it tonight at a desk, having written this.

"Under his floor," he said.

"Under his floor." Lira's mouth did something at one corner, not a smile. "It's either very stupid or exactly right. Opens in the morning. Early." She was already thinking; he could see her already sorting, the way she sorted a bout between exchanges. "I'll come."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to." There was no heat in it at all. "Stop that. Every time something gets heavy you hold the door open for me and wait to see whether I'll walk out of it. I'm not going to. You can stop holding it." She pulled the coat tighter round her. "I'm choosing it. That's different from having to, and I'd like it noticed."

He did not have an answer. He did not try to make one.

"All right," he said.

"All right." Something in her shoulders let go a little. "Go home and sleep. Not much. Some. You'll be no use to either of us reading law with a head full of fog." She began to close the window, and then stopped it a finger's width from the frame, and looked at him through the gap, and her face had dropped all of its careful composure at once. "You came here. Tonight. You didn't sit at Torvin's table and work it out on your own and tell me on Tuesday."

"No."

"Good," said Lira. "That's the first thing you've done right since the seal."

The window shut. The candle went back up the stair and was gone.

He stood in the lane by the broken barrel with the summons in his hand and the cold coming up through his boots. Then he took out the Log, there in the dark, and found a clean page by feel near the front, and wrote at the top of it with the edge of his hand to keep the line straight, as he had written in the dark once before, at Torvin's, about a cart.

*Forty-eight hours. Find the seam.*

He put the book away and walked back down past the well with the iron cover, and did not count anything at all, because he was already counting the one thing that mattered, and it was going down.

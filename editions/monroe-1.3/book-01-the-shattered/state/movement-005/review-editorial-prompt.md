# Chapter 28 — The Thin File

Coss read the file for the fourth time with the lamp pulled close and the desk rocking under his elbow every time he turned a sheet.

The rocking came from the left front leg. Somebody had once folded a scrap of paper under it to make it sit level, and the scrap had worked itself out and lay on the floorboards a hand's width from where it was needed, grey with dust, as though it had given up. Coss had noticed it in his first minute in the room and had not picked it up. It was not his desk. The ring-stains on its top were the cups of other men, sent out here before him on other errands and gone again, and one of them had carved a small neat letter into the corner by the inkwell, an *R* or a *B*, and then thought better of it and stopped.

The room was over the front room of the post, up a stair so narrow that he had carried his bag up it sideways. It had one window, which looked down the fish steps to the river, a cot along the wall under the window, a chair, the desk, and a partition of planks between this room and the next one that did not quite reach the ceiling. Through it, earlier, he had heard somebody cough on the other side, and then a door, and then nothing.

He had slept in a great many rooms like it. He did not mind them. A borrowed room had nothing in it to distract you, which was more than could be said for most rooms a man owned.

He laid the six sheets out across the desk in the order he had taken them from the case, as he would have laid out tools on a bench, and looked at them all together.

---

Coss had been a field agent of the Guilds Compact for eleven years, and he was forty-one, and somewhere around his fourth year he had made his peace with not being a brilliant man.

He had shared a desk that year with a man who was. The man saw patterns everywhere: in the dates on a run of permits, in which way a witness looked when she lied, in the order a family hung its washing. About a third of the patterns were real. The other two thirds were the man's own cleverness, reflected back at him off things that meant nothing, and he could not tell the thirds apart, because to him they all shone the same. He had gone on finding them, faster and faster, until a report of his sent four agents into a valley after a smuggling ring that was only three farmers who did not like each other. After that he had been moved to a records office in a town on the coast, where he copied other men's reports into a ledger, and was not unhappy, and wrote a very fine hand.

Coss had watched all of that from the other side of their desk and taken a lesson from it that he had never once had cause to regret. Brilliance found things, but it found false things nearly as easily as true ones. Attention, put down patiently day after day and year after year, found the true things in the end, and very seldom found the false ones, because it was too slow and too dull to be fooled by its own shine.

So he had chosen attention. Most files were exactly as dull as they looked, and the few that were not usually said so early, if you were listening.

This one was not saying anything. That was what kept him at the desk with the lamp.

The first sheet was a copy of the registration. *Caelen Hesk-ward. Registration 41-7843-V.* The certifying office was on a street called Weaver's Row, in Denvash, and the officer was a Warden-Adjunct named Pellin, whose signature was small and quick and gave nothing away. The date of the Kindling was in the autumn, fifty-odd days ago. On the line for the classification there was one word in square brackets, stamped, and no tier, no rank and no Path.

The second sheet was the residential notice under Statute 14, Section 9. He knew the form, though he had never had cause to serve one, and he knew what it had been written to do. It took a residence away from a person in one line and then told whoever held the standing, very politely, what it would cost them to keep that person close.

The third sheet was a gate entry. *Denvash, Outer gate, eastbound, on foot.* It was dated the third day after the Kindling, and it was passive, which meant that a guard had read the card in the ordinary way and the record had marked it and nobody had been asked to do anything about it. Eastbound from Denvash, on foot, was the merchant road, and the merchant road came here in two days, and anybody who knew the road would have guessed this city. That part was not strange.

The fourth sheet was the request for the sweep, routed to Ardenmere about three weeks after the gate entry. It carried an authorization code in the place where a name should have been, and one line of instruction.

*Confirm location, status, and compliance.*

The fifth was the sweep's return, in a clerk's round hand. *Location: a lodging house known as Torvin's, Unranked District. Status: resident. Compliance: nothing observed.* It was signed by the clerk of this post, per the report of the officer who had walked it.

The sixth sheet was Coss's own brief, which told him to follow up the sweep, confirm what it had confirmed, and summon the subject for compliance evaluation as procedure required.

That was all of it. He had carried six sheets of paper two days east on the merchant road in a case built for sixty, and he had known on the first morning out of Denvash that the case was light. He had stopped being surprised about it somewhere around the first milestone.

He was not surprised now. He was doing what he did with any file that bothered him, which was to set down, one at a time, not what it said but what it ought to have said and did not.

A non-standard result at Kindling set a procedure going. He had never handled a [SHATTERED] file himself; he did not know anybody who had. But he had handled three files for results the standard framework had trouble with, and they had all grown the same way, in the same order, as faithfully as a tree grows rings. The certifying district wrote a preliminary risk assessment within the first week, because the certifying district was frightened of being blamed for anything that came after. Then a residential compliance note followed, setting out what the household's standing now was and what it was likely to become. Then there was a cross-reference to the standing-holder's own file, because the residential notice threatened a guild review in so many words. When the Compact notified a guild under that statute, the guild opened its review by its own procedure, and the review had a number, and the number went into the file.

Denvash had a compliance office that produced risk assessments as regularly as a bakery produces bread. There was none here. There was no residential note beyond the notice itself. There was no cross-reference to any review. And at the head of the file, where every case he had ever been handed carried its case number in red ink, there was nothing at all. Nobody had opened a case. There was no handler named, nobody whose desk this sat on between visits.

It was not a case. It was some papers in a case.

He had once, on a training course in his second year, been shown a [SHATTERED] file from the archive, as a curiosity, by an instructor who liked curiosities. It had been very old and very thick. He did not remember anything that was in it. He remembered the thickness, and the way the instructor had had to use both hands.

He sat back. The desk rocked.

Then he took out the register.

---

Every field agent carried one. It was a small black book, newly printed each spring, that listed the authorization codes of every office an agent might lawfully take an instruction from, so that he would know a true order from a forged one. Coss's was this spring's. He had checked that before he left, as he checked it every spring, because a register a season out of date was worse than no register at all.

He laid the request for the sweep beside it and found the code.

The prefix was right. It was the region's own prefix, the one that headed his own brief and a hundred others he had carried. The length was right, and the order of letters and figures was right. It looked, in every particular he could see, like a code he ought to know.

The rest of it was not in the register.

He went down the column twice, slowly, with his thumb under each line. He went down the next column in case it had been misfiled. It had not. He turned to the back of the book, where the closed codes were listed, the ones of offices that had been merged or abolished, and it was not there either.

So somebody had requested a sweep of this district under an authorization his own register did not list. It was not a forgery; he was fairly sure of that. The post had acted on it, and regional had issued his own brief on the back of it. It was real. It came from somewhere. It came from somewhere above the reach of the book in his hand.

He looked at the instruction again. *Confirm location, status, and compliance.*

He had read a great many briefs. Most of them told you, if you read them properly, what the office that wrote them was afraid of: a guild dispute that might turn into a riot, a man who might run, a district that might be hiding something it should declare. This one told him nothing, and he had learned that a brief which told you nothing had usually been written by somebody who did not wish to be quoted.

He did not let himself go further than that.

He had seen what happened to agents who did. They asked a question above their clearance, of the wrong person, in the wrong week, and nothing happened to them at all. Then, a season later, they found themselves posted somewhere that was the same rank and the same pay and a long way from anything that mattered. Speculation was free for men in taverns. For a field agent it was the most expensive thing in the world.

So he took out his own working log, which was a plain book of his own and not the Compact's, and wrote in it what he could stand behind.

*File thin for its classification. No preliminary assessment from the certifying district. No residential note. No cross-reference to the standing-holder's review. No case number. No handler. Sweep authorization not in my register. Query on return.*

He looked at the last three words for some time. Then he left them, because they were true, and blotted the page, and turned the lamp down a little to save the oil.

---

He had come up the fish steps that morning with the case under his arm and his bag in his other hand, off a carrier's cart that had been two days on the road and felt like four.

The boy unloading the post sacks on the landing had pointed him up the steps without being asked twice. The post had been where the boy pointed: a squat brown building at the head of the steps, with a board on its front wall of official notices that had plainly not been read by anybody since they were nailed up, and a door that stuck.

Inside, the clerk had been in the middle of a dispute.

Two men stood in front of his counter, a cooper and a man with blue-stained hands, and between them on the counter was a sheet of paper with two roofs drawn on it in chalk and a gutter running from one to the other. The gutter, as far as Coss could make out, drained the cooper's roof onto the other man's woodpile, and had done so for nine years, and the other man had only now decided that he minded. The clerk had a length of string with knots in it at regular spacings, and he was measuring the chalk gutter with it, very seriously, while both men watched the string as though it might take a side.

"Permits," the clerk said to Coss, without looking up from the string. "Permits and boundaries. They're all anybody in this district will come to me about, because a permit is the one thing I've got that they want." He laid the string along the chalk. "Two knots. It overhangs by two knots."

"It's overhung by two knots for nine years," said the cooper.

"Then it's been wrong for nine years," said the man with the blue hands.

"Then it's been your woodpile for nine years," said the clerk, "and you've had nine years of dry wood under my gutter, and I'm not hearing it." He paused. "His gutter. I'm not hearing it. Go and have a drink about it and come back on Tuesday if you're still cross."

They went, still arguing, and the clerk turned at last to Coss and looked at the case under his arm and sighed in the way of a man who had recognized a kind of morning.

His name was Halvern. He was about ten years younger than Coss, sandy and soft round the middle, with ink on the side of his writing hand and an air of having been put here by somebody who had then forgotten him. He did registrations, he said, and boundaries, and permits for awnings and drains and the keeping of more than six pigs, and now and then, when regional remembered he existed, whatever regional sent.

"Field files travel sealed," Coss said, and set the case on the counter. "I break the seal here, in front of you, and you sign that you saw it whole."

Halvern looked at the seal, which was whole, and at Coss.

"Does that matter? Out here?"

"It matters to the next man who opens it."

Halvern considered this, and seemed to find nothing wrong with it, and got out a book with a cracked spine and wrote in it. *Seal whole. Broken in my presence at the post, Ardenmere.* He signed it, and turned it round for Coss to sign under him, and Coss broke the wax with his thumbnail and took out the six sheets, and Halvern watched him do it and said, in the tone of a man passing remarks about the weather, "That's not much."

"No," said Coss.

---

He went down again at the end of the afternoon, after the third reading, and asked for the log.

Halvern found it without much enthusiasm, licked his thumb, and paged back through it while the light went orange in the window behind him. The front room smelled of ink and damp stone and somebody's lunch.

"Sweep," he said at last, and put his finger on a line. "Came about three weeks back. Not from us."

"Not from Ardenmere."

"Not from anybody on this side of the river, no. It came down from regional, with a code on it where a name would usually go." He turned the book so that Coss could read the line, which was one line long and said no more than the request in Coss's own case. The code was the same. It looked no more familiar written in Halvern's round hand than it had in print. "We get one maybe twice a year. Usually it's something you could guess at without being told. A man who's run from a debt across the river. A guild quarrel that's turned into fists. This one didn't say. We don't ask." He shrugged. "Asking gets you a longer form back and no better answer, and I've got pigs to count."

"Who walked it?"

"One of the constables off the bridge. We borrowed him for an afternoon." Halvern closed the log. "Regional wanted it to look like the Compact, not like the bridge, so we lent him a pin out of the drawer for the day. He wore his own coat and our pin." He said it without any particular feeling. "Which tells you, I'd say, that regional minded what it looked like a good deal more than it minded who was doing the looking."

Coss did not answer that, because it was the kind of remark a clerk could make and an agent could not.

"The return says he found the house."

"He did." Halvern put the log back on its shelf. "Nobody here would answer a lodging list, so he didn't bother asking for one. He asked the carriers which house had letters going west to Denvash. The clerk down at the hut told him. That clerk would tell you what you had for breakfast, if you asked him nicely and he thought it made him look clever." He sat down on his stool. "That's how things get found on this side, mostly. Not by asking the person. By asking whoever stands next to them."

Coss turned that over and did not like it, and did not say so.

"Whatever it's about," Halvern said, and he seemed for a moment almost apologetic, "it wasn't cooked here. We only carry the pot." He picked up a pen. "If you want a bed, the house at the end of the bridge keeps a room for anybody the post sends. They'll charge you a Compact price."

"Which is?"

"Double." Halvern dipped the pen. "There's a cot upstairs. It's yours for nothing, and it's worth about that."

---

He took the cot.

Now, with the fourth reading done and the lamp low, he sat for a while at the window with his coat round his shoulders and looked out.

The district went down to the river below him in a long jumble of roofs and lanes and lamps, untidy and alive, with no wall anywhere in it and no gate at its edge. Across the river the Ranked core went up its hill in rings, each ring with its own wall and its own lamps along the top of it, every lamp the same distance from the next. From here it looked like a ladder somebody had leaned against the dark. He had passed through a good many rings in his life with a card that opened them. He had not often looked at them from the outside.

He thought, as he did every first night away, about his daughter. She would be fourteen at midsummer. She had asked him at the door, with her coat half on, whether this one was going to be interesting, and he had answered honestly that he could not say yet. She had said that was what he always said. He had said that was because it was always true.

He thought now that he would have to tell her he had been right not to know.

He put the six sheets back in the case. It weighed nothing. He lay down on the cot in his shirt with his coat over him and listened to the river, and to somebody singing in the street below and stopping, and slept better than he had expected to.

---

In the morning he went to the lodging house.

He found it without asking, from the return and a map of the district he had bought from Halvern for less than it was worth: two streets in from the east road, down a lane with a gutter in the middle, a narrow stone house with a wooden sign over the door that said ROOMS. The O had faded less than the other letters and leaned harder, so that the word looked as though it had been about to fall over for a very long time.

He knocked.

The man who opened the door was broad and heavy and unshaven, past middle age, with the face of somebody who had opened this door a great many times and expected nothing from it. He looked at Coss's face, and at his coat, and at the case, and back at his face, in that order and without any hurry. Coss had met him before. Not this man, but this man's kind, in a dozen districts in a dozen cities, and he knew before either of them spoke how the conversation was going to go.

"Torvin," the man said.

"I'm told a boy called Caelen Hesk-ward lodges here. Fourteen."

"There's a room paid to the end of the week."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It's what there is." Torvin did not move from the doorway. He did not block it, exactly. He simply went on standing in it, as a post goes on standing in a road. "Room's paid. Somebody sleeps in it. I don't keep a list of who."

"Is it him?"

"It's paid."

Coss had tried, early in his career, to get past answers like this. He had tried charm first, and then a little pressure, and then the leverage that a Compact pin was supposed to give a man on a doorstep, and he had learned every time that all three produced the same thing: a slightly smaller version of the same answer, and a much longer memory of the man who had pushed. He had stopped pushing years ago. It cost him a little information on doorsteps like this one. It saved him a great deal of goodwill on the next doorstep, and the one after that, because word of how an agent behaved got round a district faster than the agent did.

"Is he in now?"

"No."

"Do you know where he is?"

"Not in."

From somewhere behind Torvin, at the back of the house, a woman's voice called out. "Tell him about the oats."

"He doesn't want to know about the oats," said Torvin, without turning round.

There was a short silence from the back of the house, of a kind Coss recognized from his own kitchen.

He wrote in his book that the address matched the return, and that the lane was Unranked by the district map, and that the householder had answered every question he was legally bound to answer and not one word more. Then he thanked Torvin, who did not seem to know what to do with being thanked, and turned to go.

"He pays on time," Torvin said, behind him.

Coss looked back.

"That's the most I'll say for anybody," said Torvin, "and I've said it." And he shut the door.

---

He spent the rest of the day walking.

He did not ask direct questions. Direct questions made people careful, and careful people told you what they thought you wanted, which was worse than nothing. He walked, and bought things, and stood where people stood, and listened, and now and then asked somebody something small, phrased as idle curiosity, about the weather or the price of fish or which way was the river.

He learned a great deal, and very little of it was about the boy.

He learned that the district settled its own quarrels, mostly in the open, at the well by the four lanes or at the edge of the market, with an old woman or a stallholder or whoever happened to be passing deciding who was right, and both sides abiding by it more often than not. He learned that half the people he passed had Paths and used them at their work without a thought: a porter lifting a barrel by its rim with one hand, a woman frying fish in a pan with no fire under it, a man who stood perfectly still at the end of a plank while two others walked a loaded handcart across it. None of it was anything the Compact counted as practice. It was work. The Compact did not count porters.

He learned that the carriers' clerk at the bottom of the fish steps would, as Halvern had said, tell anybody anything. Coss asked him only which bag went west and when, and the clerk told him, and then told him without being asked that the boy at Torvin's wrote west every week, regular as a clock, to Fen Street, and that he could tell him the day.

Coss found that he did not want to know the day. He found also that he did not like knowing that anybody who asked would be told it, and he walked away from the hut with that sitting in him like a stone in a boot.

He went past the workshops along the river, where every ground floor was opened up to the street and somebody in every doorway was making or mending something. At the end of the row, in a doorway with no sign, a man with a loupe in his eye looked up at him exactly once and then went back to his clock. On the doorframe somebody had cut four words into the wood and filled them with black paint. *IF IT'S STILL HERE.* Coss read them twice and could not decide whether they were the beginning of a sentence or the whole of one.

In the afternoon, at a fish stall, two women were packing up, and one said to the other, "Are you going to the circuit Sunday?" and the other said she might, if her sister would mind the stall, and they went on to talk about the sister.

He filed it where he filed such things. Every district he had ever walked had its Sunday entertainment, dice in a back room or dogs in a yard or a long-running argument at a public house about which barge was fastest, and it was none of the Compact's business and never had been. He did not stop.

At the north edge of the district the ground began to rise, and at the mouth of a lane going up there was a chalked board with prices on it for yard time by the hour, and over the walls of the lane came the flat smack of hands on canvas and somebody shouting at somebody else to keep his elbow in. He stood at the bottom of the lane for as long as it took to read the prices. Boxing yards, he thought, and porters keeping fit, and young men showing off for each other, the same as in every district he had ever known. His brief was about one boy, and the boy's address was down by the river, and his instructions spoke of *practice* in the sense the certification offices used it, a Path declared and demonstrated and assessed. He turned away from the lane and went back down toward the dyers' sheds.

That night he wrote in his working log by the low lamp, with the desk rocking under his wrist.

*Second day. Householder cooperative to the legal minimum. Address confirmed. Nothing observed.*

He held the pen over the page for a time. Then he added a line, because it was true and because he did not know where else to put it.

*The district runs like a town, with its own ways of settling things. My categories do not fit it well. I am not sure what I would be looking at if I saw it.*

He blotted that, and looked at it, and did not cross it out.

# Chapter 29 — What's on the Bench

Torvin waited until everybody had a bowl in front of them before he said it, and Cael understood afterward that he had been waiting all day.

It was Saturday, and the brothers were in early because the river had come up again in the night and nobody wanted barges on it. The boots woman was at her end of the table, and the wheezing man was not, and Yeni had a shirt across her knee and a spoon in the hand that was not sewing. Torvin's wife had made the thick brown soup again, the one with barley in it, and she stood at the range with her back to them and the big spoon in her hand, which was where she stood when she meant to hear everything.

Torvin sat down at the end of the table with his own bowl and his thin ledger, opened the ledger, licked his pencil, and said to the first column, "Compact came this morning."

The brothers stopped arguing about the river.

Cael put his spoon down in his bowl, slowly, so that it did not knock against the side.

"Man with a case," said Torvin. "Knocked at the door about the middle of the morning. Asked whether a Caelen Hesk-ward lodges here, fourteen." He said the name carefully, the whole of it, as though he had been practising it, and Cael realized that Torvin had never heard it before, because nobody at this table had. "I told him there's a room paid to the end of the week."

"What did he say?"

"Said that wasn't what he'd asked." Torvin ran his pencil down the column. He seemed entirely content. "I said it was what there was."

"He said that to me twice in the kitchen afterward," said Torvin's wife, to the soup. "*It was what there was.* Very pleased with himself."

"It was a good answer."

"It was a rude answer. I wanted him told the boy eats his oats and scrubs the pot after, which is more than I can say for some people at this table." She did not look at the brothers. The brothers looked at their bowls. "But no. *He doesn't want to know about the oats.*"

"He didn't," said Torvin.

The boots woman laughed into her soup. Yeni's needle went in and out. Cael sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of his bowl and made himself breathe in the ordinary way, in and out, the way he did at his mark.

*Caelen Hesk-ward.* Not *the circuit kid*. Not a lodger with letters going west. His name, the whole of it, said at this door by somebody who had brought it here.

Lira had asked him, on the night of the pin, at Amrit's, with the river going past the little windows: *How long before it's a man at Torvin's door asking for you by your name?* He had not known. Now he did. It had been sixteen days.

"What did he look like?" he said.

Torvin's pencil stopped while he considered the question from its several sides.

"About forty. Thin face. Dark coat, good, but creased, as if he'd slept in it on a cart. Collar turned up." He started down the column again. "Had a flat case under his arm, the kind clerks carry papers in. Held it as if there was nothing in it." He wrote a figure. "That's all. Asked was you here. I said the room's paid. Asked was you in. I said no. Asked where. I said not in. He wrote in a book, and thanked me, which I didn't care for, and went."

"Did he ask about anything else?"

"No."

"The yards?"

Torvin looked up at him. It was the coin look, slow and thorough. "He asked was you here," he said again, "and I told him the room's paid. That's all he asked and that's all he got." He looked back down. "I told him one more thing on the step, because I'd decided to before he knocked. I told him you pay on time. That's all I've ever said for anybody."

Nobody at the table said anything for a moment.

"Well," said Torvin, and turned the page.

---

Cael did not hear much of the rest of supper.

He was standing on the landing at the bottom of the fish steps on the morning after Dessa, with a split knuckle and the sun just out over the river, looking past the clerk's shoulder at the west cart with its tailboard down. A woman with a child had climbed off it, and an old man with a crate that clinked, and a man of about forty in a creased dark coat with the collar turned up against the wind. He had held a flat leather case loosely against his side, the way you hold a thing that has very little in it, as if he had expected it to be heavier and had long since stopped being surprised that it was not. He had looked up the steps at the district as though it were something he would have to learn, and asked the boy on the landing the way to the post.

And Cael had watched him for two breaths, and turned away, and gone up the steps two at a time past the brown building, whose door had been shutting behind somebody as he passed.

He had not written him down.

In seven weeks in Ardenmere he had written down nearly every stranger he had seen. He had written the polite man with the boots, from Torvin's account, and the grey coat walking the market, and the old woman in the shawl, and the Fenrow Iron. That morning he had been thinking about a fight and a girl who would be first, and a man had stepped off a cart with his name in a case, and Cael had looked straight at him and let him go by like weather.

Yeni was looking at him across the table.

"You've gone a colour," she said.

"I'm all right."

"I didn't say you weren't. I said you'd gone a colour." She bit her thread. "Eat your soup. Whatever it is, it'll still be there when you've eaten it, and you'll be less stupid about it."

He ate his soup. He did not taste it. When he took his bowl to the basin, Torvin's wife took it out of his hands before he could rinse it and said, quietly, without turning round from the range, "If you go anywhere, you tell me first. I'll want to give you something to carry."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said thank you, and she said it was nothing, and banged the pot.

---

Upstairs, on his cot under the window, with the brothers asleep early and Yeni's lamp turned low, he opened the Log at the front and wrote the man down at last.

*Fifty-first day, the landing, the morning after Dessa. A man of about forty off the west cart. Dark coat, creased from the road, collar up. A flat case carried as though nearly empty. Looked at the district as something to learn. Asked the way to the post. Went up the steps to it. I watched him for two breaths and did not write him down.*

*Fifty-second day. The same man at Torvin's door (Torvin, h). Asked for Caelen Hesk-ward, fourteen, by name. Asked nothing about the yards. Torvin gave him the room's paid, and that I pay on time.*

He looked at the two entries, and then wrote one more line under them, smaller, because it was true and he wanted it on the page where he would have to see it.

*I had him in front of me and let him go by because I was pleased with myself. Write everybody down. Especially on the good days.*

Then he took Hesk's letter out of the leather book, where he had put it to keep flat, and read it again.

It had come up the evening before, on the fifty-first day, with the runner, as the clerk had said it would. He had read it at once, at the window, while the light went, and then again at the kitchen table, and he had been glad of it in an ordinary way and put it away. He had not thought, then, about where it had been that morning.

He thought about it now. The west cart had come in with its tailboard down and the carter handing the post sacks to the boy on the landing, one after another. Hesk's letter had been in one of those sacks. It had come two days along the merchant road in the bottom of the cart, under the boots of a man with a flat case on his knees, and it had come up the fish steps on the boy's shoulder within a minute of the man himself.

They had arrived together. He found that he did not like that at all, and that he could not have said why, because it meant nothing; the cart carried everything that came from the west. He set it down anyway.

The letter was one sheet, both sides, in the square hand.

*Cael,*

*Your letter came on the forty-fourth day. I've read it three times at the table and once in the workshop, which is where I read the things I want to think about with my hands busy.*

*You asked for something ordinary. So. On the bench there's a level for the survey office, the third I've made them this year, because their young men keep dropping them off ladders and then bringing them back to me as though the ladder were my fault. There's the hinge off Ressa's oven door, which has squeaked since you were nine, and which I have at last got round to. And there's the drawer of springs that the guild man asked about. I've moved it six inches to the left so it stops catching my knee. That's the bench.*

*Now the other thing.*

*A man with a pin walking a market is a man doing a job somebody handed him, with a list in his pocket. Mostly he doesn't know why the list says what it says. Don't spend your nights on him. Your old man in the yard is right: what matters is what changed further up, and you won't find that by watching a coat.*

*One more thing, and I'll say it plainly, because I know a little of how such things are done. Don't change what you do. Go where you go and post where you post and keep your hours. The ones who change their habits when a pin comes through are the ones some clerk remembers.*

*Nothing from the guild. A clerk came for a copy of the account I sent, which they already have. That's the guild's way of saying they're still thinking.*

*Joren walks past the workshop most days, flat-footed. He thinks I don't notice.*

*Tell me what you find, when you find it. Even if it's nothing.*

*— H.*

He read the middle again.

*Don't change what you do.*

Hesk had written that on the forty-fourth day, at the kitchen table, in Denvash, a week after the pin. On the thirty-eighth day, on the cooper's roof, with the tar warm under him and Lira lying with her arm over her eyes, Cael had counted on his fingers and decided the same thing: same hut, same bag, same day, nothing on the outside but what they had already. He had written down that he had decided it. Lira had called it cold.

They had come to the same place by two different roads, a week and a valley apart, without either of them knowing the other was on the way.

He had felt this once before, on the cooper's roof on the morning of Renn, when Lira had said almost word for word a thing about fear that he had read in Hesk's hand on a wall by the merchant road. Two people who had never met had worked out the same figure. It had steadied him then, because a figure two people reached separately was a figure you could trace. It steadied him now, a little, in the same way.

But Hesk had been writing to a boy who had seen one man with a pin and did not know what it meant. Tonight there was a second man, in a dark coat, who had asked for him by name at the door under the leaning O, and Hesk did not know it, and would not for a week after Cael wrote it down for him.

He turned to a clean line in the margin and made a list, because a list was a thing you could look at all at once.

*The man at Torvin's door, about the twenty-eighth day. Polite. Boots not local. Asked for "the circuit kid". Got nothing. (Torvin, h.)*

*The grey coat with the pin, the thirty-sixth day. Walked the market. Asked about lodgers without papers, and letters going west. Got Torvin's from the carriers' clerk. Asked nothing about the yards. (Seen, and the seller, h.)*

*The dark coat off the west cart, the fifty-first day. Went to the post. Came to Torvin's the next morning and asked for me by my whole name. Asked nothing about the yards. (Seen, and Torvin, h.)*

He looked at the three lines for a long time.

Three men. Or two, or one, or any arrangement of them. He could make the grey coat and the dark coat into one man who had changed his coat, but Torvin's dark coat had been creased from two days on a cart, and the grey coat had been brushed, and walked the market on the thirty-sixth day, while the dark coat had been somewhere two days' road away at least. He could make the polite man and the grey coat one, but one had asked for the circuit and the other had not seemed to know there was a circuit at all.

He did not decide. He had no evidence that would let him decide. He drew a short line under the three of them, and under it wrote: *Don't guess. Don't change what I do. Tell Lira.*

Then he blew out his own small piece of the lamp's light, the way he did, by turning his face to the wall, and lay a long time listening to the river.

---

The frost was thick in the grey half in the morning, white along the foot of the east wall and in every scuff of yesterday's feet, and Lira was crouched in the middle of the circle with a length of string.

It was a long piece of brown twine with knots in it at what looked like regular spacings, and she had one end of it pinned to the ground with her heel at the near edge of the groove and was walking the other end round the curve, slowly, bent over, counting under her breath. Her breath went up white. She did not look up when he came through the gate.

"Don't talk," she said. "I'm on forty."

He did not talk. He set the bread on top of the post and watched her go round. She went all the way round the long side, past the drain, and stopped, and looked at the knot under her fingers, and then walked back the short way across the middle, counting again.

"There," she said, and stood up, and her face had the morning brightness in it, all the way. "Seven knots. The long side's seven knots longer, going round by the drain, than the short side going round by the gate. That's a stride and a half. That's two steps, if you're you, and a step and a bit if you're me." She wound the string round her hand. "Five months I've walked on this dirt, and I never knew. Don't tell the old man I measured it."

"I won't."

"He'd only say something about it." She stuffed the string in her pocket and looked at him properly for the first time, and her face changed. "What."

He told her. He told it in order, the way he had written it: the landing, the case, the post door shutting, the soup, Torvin's room's paid and *you pay on time*, the oats. He told her the three lines in the margin and why he could not make any of them into one man. He told her about Hesk's letter, and *don't change what you do*, and the roof.

She did not interrupt. She stood in the frost with her hands in her jacket pockets, and when he got to *Caelen Hesk-ward, fourteen*, said at the door, she took her hands out of her pockets and folded her arms instead, tightly, as though she were cold all at once.

"By your name," she said.

"By my name."

"I said that. At Amrit's. I said *before it's a man at Torvin's door asking for you by your name*." She looked at the groove. "I didn't want to be right about that one."

"I know."

"Your whole name. The long one. I don't even know your whole name. I've never heard it." She said it lightly, and then stopped, and looked at him, and he saw her hear what she had said. "Is that what it is? Caelen?"

"Caelen Hesk-ward. It's on the card."

She tried it under her breath, once, as if testing the weight of something somebody had handed her. Then she shook her head.

"No. You're Cael. That's somebody on a card." She took the bread off the post and tore it and gave him the larger half and did not eat hers. "He didn't ask about the yards."

"Not Torvin. And Torvin would have said. He likes telling the table what he didn't tell people."

"Then he doesn't know about the yards. Or he knows and doesn't care. Or he knows and he's being clever." She chewed on nothing. "You can't tell which."

"No."

"Then do what your grandfather says, and what you'd already decided before he said it." She bit into the bread at last. "Which is lucky, because I want you at the rope this afternoon."

He looked at her.

"I'm on the card," Lira said. "Second bout. Sunday, before the lamps." She was looking at the post, not at him, the way she did when a thing mattered more than she wanted it to look. "I asked Vell on Friday morning, while you were at the mender's being told something worked. She told me last night it was on."

"Who?"

"Brenna."

He stood with the bread in his hand.

"I've fought her twice," Lira said. "I lost twice. The first time I never found the dip at all. The second time I found it and went in on it, and she turned on me like a gate and walked me over the groove the same as she did you." She pulled a crust off the bread and threw it at a crow on the eaves, which ignored it. "I've wanted her a third time since the summer. I wasn't ready. I've been not ready for a long time."

"What changed?"

"You did." She said it flatly, and then made a face, as though she had not meant to give him that much. "Not like that. Your stupid face at Dessa. Two Sundays on a bench, and then going into the gap like walking through a door in the dark, and all of it built, every piece. I sat on the end of that bench and bit my knuckle and thought, *I want one of those.* One I built." She looked at him at last. "And I don't lean any more. You remember? The left foot. The morning you touched my sleeve, you told me my weight went onto my left foot before I struck. I'd been doing it four months. I don't do it now."

He remembered. He remembered writing the ruling that night, *never tell anybody their tell unless I'm sure I don't need it*, and not liking it.

"Do you think she read it? The foot?"

"I think it's why she turned on me the second time before I'd got through." Lira shrugged. "I don't know. I'll find out this afternoon." She finished her bread. "And there's the purse. Vell pays a little, on a Sunday, if there's a crowd, and there'll be a crowd, because Brenna brings her friends and her friends bring theirs." She looked away again. "I'm saving for something."

"What?"

"A door." She said it to the eaves. "One that's mine. That's all. Don't make a face about it."

He did not make a face. He thought about Yeni saying that four months was all the name Lira had, and about a room he had never seen, somewhere up in the four lanes, that she had never once mentioned in seven weeks.

"I'll be at the rope," he said.

"Good." She brought her hands up, out of habit, and then dropped them. "No. Not today. No box, no step. I'm keeping my legs." She looked at him sideways. "And you don't tell me one thing about her. Not one. I don't want to hear what you saw when you fought her. I've fought her twice. I'll do my own."

"And if I see something this afternoon?"

Lira thought about it, with her head on one side.

"Write it down," she said. "Give it me after. Like a report." She almost smiled. "I'll have it in the evidence column, with your mark beside it. And I don't want a ruling."

---

He went down to the market at the middle of the morning, because he went down to the market every morning, and Hesk had said not to change what he did, and he had decided it on a roof before Hesk said it.

It was a Sunday, and slower than the weekdays, and colder. The haze was off and the sky was hard and clear over the awnings, so that the lanterns hanging from the poles looked foolish, burning away in full daylight at their different heights. The nut boy had his drum going. The pie man was calling his prices. Nothing had gone tight.

He came down the second row and bought the pears from the fruit woman by pointing, and she gave him full weight, and he turned to go back up, and there was the man.

He was at the top of the second row, at the bread woman's stall, with his back half turned. He had the collar of the dark coat down today, and Cael could see that there was nothing on the lapel at all, no pin and no mark of one. He had no case. He was buying a loaf.

Cael did not stop walking. He did what he had done on the thirty-sixth day without having to think about it: he found the lanterns. The red one on the pie man's pole hung low; the bread woman's hung high, with the crack across its glass. The man was under the bread woman's.

He was talking to her. That was the first thing Cael saw, and it was different from the grey coat at once. The grey coat had looked at stalls. This man looked at the woman. He asked her something, and then he did not say anything, and waited, and she answered, and he waited a little more, until she said a second thing she had not meant to say. Cael saw her hear herself say it and close her mouth on the rest.

The man thanked her. He paid. She gave him a loaf from the top of the pile.

Cael very nearly laughed out loud, there in the middle of the second row with a twist of pears in his hand. The bread woman had sold the man yesterday's loaf, off the top, where she put the ones she thought nobody would notice. She had not given him one from underneath. She did that for people who asked, and for people the market had decided were not new.

The man walked off up the street toward the river with yesterday's bread under his arm, unhurried, looking at the district as he went, at a doorway and then a roof and then a woman beating a rug from a window, the way you look at a page you mean to read again later.

Cael went the other way, up toward Torvin's, the way he went every morning. He did not double back. He did not count to sixty in any doorway.

He wrote it on the wall by the pump, in the margin, under last night's three lines.

*Fifty-third day, the market. The dark coat, no pin, no case. Buying bread. He looks at people, not stalls. He asks and then waits, and they tell him more than they meant to. Not the grey coat. The bread woman sold him yesterday's loaf off the top.*

He looked at the last sentence for a while, and left it in, because it was evidence. The market had made up its mind about the man in the dark coat, even if Cael had not.

---

The yard was fuller than it had any right to be for a second bout on a cold Sunday afternoon.

Lira had been right about Brenna's friends. They were along the rope on the sunny side in a loud knot, a dozen of them at least, big young women and a few men from the yards, and every one of them seemed to have brought somebody. The benches were out along the shaded wall, and the third row was filling with old men in scarves. The pie boy was doing so well that he had sent somebody back into the public house for a second tray.

Cael did not go to the benches. He went to the rope, on the shaded side, near the corner by the drain where the barrel stood, and put both hands on it.

He had stood at this rope before, for other people's bouts, with the Log open on his knee and a pencil going. He had never stood at it for somebody he knew. He found that it made his hands do something on the rope that he had to make them stop doing.

Marrow came along with his slate. Cael read it without moving his head.

*Brenna*, and a short price. *Lira*, and a longer one, not long, but long enough to say what the yard thought of two losses in a row. And under the names, on its own line, *Over/under: 4 exch.*

Marrow saw him reading it, and smiled the good smile, and wrote something with his chalk and went on.

At the water bucket, Brenna was standing with two of her friends, as she had stood on the night she walked a shield at him, talking with her hands. Her face was already red. She looked along the rope while she talked, and her eyes came to Cael and stopped there. She stopped talking. Then she gave him the nod again, the short one across a street, that she had given him by the gate after Dessa, and she turned back to her friends and said something, and one of them looked at him too.

He nodded back. He did not know what else to do.

Lira came in through the gate in the wall with the canvas roll on her back and her hair tied so tightly that it pulled at the corners of her eyes, like Dessa's. He had never seen her tie it that way. She did not look at the rope. She did not look at him. She walked straight along the shaded wall to the far corner, set down the roll, and stood there with her weight a little forward on the front of her feet and her hands loose at her sides, looking at the circle.

Vell came out of the public house's back door with her stool and her book and sat down behind the table. She squared the ledger to the edge. She looked at the crowd once, end to end, as if she were counting it, and her eyes went past Cael at the rope and paused, and went on.

The first bout was two young men, a Blade and a Force, who had both plainly come to impress Brenna's friends, and who both went down in the third exchange, one after the other, in a way that made the old men in the third row laugh. Cael did not watch it. He watched Lira in her corner, not moving.

Then Vell stood up behind her table.

"Second bout."

The yard went lower. Along the sunny side Brenna's friends began to stamp on the frozen dirt, all together, slowly, like a heavy door being knocked on, and somebody on the shaded side told them to be quiet, and they did not.

"Brenna. Copper Rank 2, Shield Path. Against Lira. Copper Rank 3, Wind Path."

Brenna came over the rope in one long step and walked to her mark with her left arm already up and bent across her body, and her face red, and her mouth set.

Lira came over the rope after her.

She walked to her mark the way Cael had seen her walk to a hundred corners of the box drill, light and quick, with her weight forward. When she reached it she stopped, and set her feet, and only then did she look up, across the circle, not at Brenna's shield, and not at Brenna's face, but at Brenna's feet.

Cael's hands had done the thing on the rope again. He let them.

"Begin when you're ready," said Vell.

Brenna was ready before the word was finished. The air in front of her left forearm thickened into its shine, a hand off the skin and a little wider than her shoulders, and through it her red face wobbled like a face behind old glass, and she came off her mark and walked it at Lira.

Lira did not go back.

That was the first thing, and Cael saw half the rope see it and not understand it. Everybody went back from Brenna. He had gone back from her for four exchanges along the long curve of the groove until his heel found the edge. Lira went sideways. She went the way water goes round a post in a stream, without any hurry and without any fuss, and the edge of the shield came down in its flat chop through the place where she had been standing and found the frozen dirt instead.

Brenna turned after her and chopped again. Lira was not there either.

She was close, that was the strange part. She stayed so close to the shield that she could have touched it, and twice she did touch it, with the tips of two fingers, lightly, on its face, the way you touch a pane of glass to find out whether it is cold. She was not trying to hit anything. She was finding out where the thing ended. Each time her fingers touched the shine, Brenna's arm locked up harder behind it, and each time the chop came, Lira was a stride to one side of where it landed, with her weight forward and her hands loose.

Cael watched her left foot.

It did not go. He watched for it every time she moved, as he had watched for it every morning for a week after he had told her, and it stayed exactly where it should, under her, with nothing on it but its proper share of her. Whatever Brenna had read the last time, she was not going to read it today.

The first exchange ended with nobody touched. Brenna went back to her mark breathing harder than Lira, and along the sunny side her friends stamped again, slowly, and one of them shouted at Lira to stop dancing and fight, and Lira did not look round.

*First exchange,* Cael said in his head, because he had promised a report, and a report was what you saw and nothing else. *You went sideways. You touched the shield twice. Your foot stayed.*

---

In the second exchange Lira looked for the dip, and it was not there.

He saw her looking. He knew exactly where her eyes would go, because it was where his own had gone on the night he fought, down under the shine to Brenna's boots, waiting for the right foot to come up on the step-through and the bottom edge of the shield to sink with it. The foot came up. Brenna stepped through.

The shield did not sink.

It stayed exactly where it was, level, a hand off the forearm, and behind it Cael could see the arm shaking very slightly with the effort of holding it there. Brenna had fixed it. Some time in the three weeks since he had gone in on her dip and been collected and walked over the groove, she had stood somewhere and stepped through, and stepped through, and held the arm up until it stayed up, the way the woman with the shoulder in the top yard had held her fourth position thirty times in a morning. *She doesn't know she does it,* Lira had said. She knew now. He had told her, in the fifth exchange, by going in on it, and she had gone away and taken it out of herself.

He felt that land somewhere under his breastbone and did not have time to look at it.

Lira had seen it too. He watched her see the shield not sink, and watched her go in anyway, because her weight was already committed to going, and Brenna turned. It was not the full gate-swing that had collected him. It was half of one, short and fast. The edge of the shine caught Lira across the top of the right shoulder as she came out, and she skidded on the frost, one hand down on the dirt, and came up again on her feet a long stride back.

The sunny side roared. Brenna's friends were stamping in earnest now.

Lira went back to her mark and rolled the shoulder once, and stood. Her face had nothing on it at all.

*Second exchange,* said Cael, in his head, and found his voice in there was not as steady as it should have been. *She's fixed the dip. You went in anyway. She caught your right shoulder with the edge. You kept your feet.*

He thought something else, too, which was not for any report. Brenna had held that arm up through every step of the exchange, and it had cost her. Her breath was louder than it had been at the end of the first. The huff had come back at the top of every breath, the short hard sound through the nose that he had heard on his own night get louder all the way to the fifth. A shield held level through a step was not free. Nothing that had to be held was free.

---

The third exchange was the one where Lira changed her mind, and he saw the moment she did it.

She came off her mark and went sideways, as in the first, and Brenna walked the shield at her and chopped, and this time Lira did not step out from under the chop. She caught it.

She caught it the way she had shown him at the board on the second day, with the outside of her left forearm, wrist to elbow, meeting the edge of the shine as it came down. She did not reset. There was no middle. Catch went straight into send without any pause in it that Cael could see, so that the chop arrived on her arm and left it again in a single movement, and went on, past her, a good deal further than Brenna had meant it to go.

Brenna went after it. She could not help it. Her arm had been told to go one way with all her weight behind it, and the weight had been handed on instead of stopped, and the whole of her turned with the shield, a quarter-turn too far, so that she had to put a foot down to catch herself. When she did, Lira was already on the other side of her, out of reach again.

The rope made a different noise. It was not the sunny side this time. It was the old men in the third row, a low, pleased murmur, the noise people make in front of a thing done properly that they have not seen done before.

Lira did it again. Catch, send, nothing in the middle, and Brenna's chop went on past her and took Brenna with it, and Brenna stamped a foot down on the frost to stop. Then it happened a third time. Each time the shield went further than its owner had sent it. Each time Brenna had to step to bring it back, with the arm held level, the way she had trained it now, and every one of those steps cost her breath she did not have.

"*Stand,*" said Brenna, through her teeth.

Cael had heard her say it before. He had heard it on his own night, in the third exchange, and he knew what came after it.

Lira did not stand. She was not running, either. She was doing the thing she had told him on the first day at the board that she had been failed for: she was skipping the middle in front of witnesses, and it was working, over and over, and Brenna did not know why her own arm kept taking her somewhere she had not asked to go.

"Stand and *fight.*"

At her mark, between the third exchange and the fourth, Brenna's face had gone from red to the darker red. Cael knew it. He had the ruling written in his own hand in the Log, entry two, the third claim: *if somebody gets angry, they will tell me everything. Let them.* He had told Lira about it walking down the lane that night, and she had said it was one of the truest things there was in that yard.

He looked across at her. She was standing on her mark with her shoulder down and her hands loose, and she was looking at Brenna's feet, and she was letting her.

*Third exchange. You caught it and sent it with no middle. Three times. She went after it every time.* He stopped, and added, because it was true: *The old men liked it.*

---

The fourth exchange was loud and short, and Brenna told the whole yard what she meant to do with every strike a full breath before she did it.

Her chops had gone big. They started up by her ear and came down with everything she had, and they were slower for it, as they had been slower for him, and Lira caught two and sent them and was simply not there for the rest. The huff came before each one like a door banging. Brenna's arm was shaking all the time now, and on the fourth chop, stepping through, the bottom edge of the shine sank, a hand's breadth, toward her hip. Then on the fifth it sank again, further. The dip had come back. She had trained it out of herself for three weeks and it had come back in two exchanges, because she was tired and angry and her arm had been told to stay up for longer than it had ever been asked to.

Lira went in on it.

She went on Brenna's right, low, inside the reach, and Cael saw her go a hair further than she needed to. She went past the place where she could have struck and on into the place beyond it, as if the first half of her had arrived and the second half had not been told to stop. Brenna's cocked right fist came round and caught her a glancing blow on the side of the head as she came out. It was not clean, and it did not put her down. But Lira's head went round with it, and she was back a long stride before Cael had let his breath go.

He saw it. He saw that she had gone too far, and on her right side, and that it had cost her. He did not know whether it was anything more than once. Once was not a pattern. He put it somewhere he would find it later and did not put it in the report.

Vell called the end of the exchange. Brenna's friends were not stamping now. They were shouting, and the shouting had a sharp edge to it that it had not had at the start.

Lira stood on her mark and worked her jaw from side to side, once, as Dessa had done at the end of his own night, and seemed to decide it would keep.

---

"Begin," said Vell.

Lira came off her mark and touched the shield.

Two touches, high, quick and light and empty, on the face of the shine, one with each hand. They were nothing. They were a tap on a door to see who was home. And Brenna's arm, which had spent four exchanges being touched and caught and sent where it did not want to go, locked up behind the shine to meet them, high and hard, the way his own forearms had come up every morning in the grey half without his permission for two touches that were already gone.

Then Lira stepped.

He had seen the step perhaps a hundred times, from the inside, with his arms in the wrong place. He had never seen it from the rope. From the rope it was stranger still. She was two paces from Brenna when she began it and she was inside the shield's reach when it finished, on Brenna's right, at the open side, while Brenna's right foot was still in the air stepping through. He did not see the distance go. It was as though the dirt between them had folded in the middle and let her across.

# Chapter 30 — Local Colour

On his second afternoon in Ardenmere, Coss was standing in a lopsided triangle where three streets met, eating a skewer of grilled mutton, when the roar came over the roofs.

It came from somewhere north and a little uphill, two or three streets away. It was not one voice but a great many at once, going up together and holding, and then breaking into pieces, some of them cheering and some of them groaning and some of them only shouting because everybody else was. Then it settled into a long, low noise like a river over stones. A few people in the triangle looked up toward it, the way people look up at a flight of birds, and went back to what they had been doing.

"That's the Cinder House," said the woman at the skewer cart, turning her meat. She said it to nobody in particular, as if the roar had asked her a question. "Sunday."

"What's on?"

He asked it lightly, the way he asked about the price of fish. The woman looked at him for a moment over her firebox. It was not a long look and it was not a hostile one. It was the look of somebody who has been asked by a stranger in a good coat what the weather is, and has noticed the coat.

"The circuit," she said, and her face went quite flat, pleasantly flat, like a shutter painted to look like a window. "The Sunday one. You'll want another of those? They're better hot."

He bought another, because it would have been rude not to, and because it was what a man who was only curious about the weather would have done.

He knew that face. He had seen it in a dozen districts on a dozen stallholders, on the day a man from the Compact asked about something the district did on its own account and would rather keep. Dogs in a yard, mostly, or cocks, or dice in the back of a public house where the stakes ran higher than the law liked, or a long-running wager on barges. Every district had one. It was none of his business and had never been. He had learned in his first two years that pushing at that face got you nothing but a district that remembered you had pushed. Then the next time you came, and needed something that mattered, the shutters were up before you reached the corner.

The circuit, then, at a public house, on a Sunday. He put it where he had put it the day before, beside the two women packing up their fish, with the dice and the dogs.

In the middle of the triangle there was a post with a board nailed to it, so thickly covered with notices that the bottom ones had gone into the grain of the wood. He did not go near it. A man in a dark coat standing in front of a district's own board, reading, would be a man the district talked about by suppertime, and he had a summons to write tonight in a place where he would need people to talk to him tomorrow. He finished the skewer, wiped his fingers, and went back down toward the river by the long street. Behind him, over the roofs, the noise from the public house rose once more, sharply, and then fell away into clapping.

He did not look round.

---

He wrote the summons that evening at the borrowed desk, with the lamp close and the window gone dark over the fish steps.

He had done what the brief required of him with any exactness. He had confirmed the location. He had confirmed the status. He had walked the district for two days, and had seen nothing he could write down as practice in the sense the certification offices meant it, no Path declared or performed or assessed, and he was honest enough with himself to add that he was no longer sure he would have known it if he had. There remained the one instruction in the brief that was not vague. *Summon the subject for compliance evaluation as procedure requires.*

So he took a summons form out of the case, where three of them lay flat under the six sheets, the only weight the case had carried, and filled it in.

He did it slowly. It was not that he was undecided. He did not suffer much from indecision; he had built a career on gathering what he could and then acting on it, and not standing about afterward wishing he had gathered more. It was something else. It was the care he took with any document that would go on existing after the evening he wrote it in, in a file, in other men's hands, saying what he had said in his name long after he had forgotten saying it.

He wrote the subject's name and registration. He wrote the place: the district office, the post at the head of the fish steps, Ardenmere. He wrote the window for appearance, which was forty-eight hours from delivery, during the hours the post kept, and he signed his name at the foot, *Coss, field agent*, and pressed the seal into the wax with the heel of his hand.

Then he came to the last clause, which was printed on the form already, and which he had only to read and leave standing.

*If the person summoned does not appear within the stated time, a compliance flag will be entered against the registration, and a filing made for physical retrieval.*

He had left that clause standing perhaps a dozen times in eleven years. Every one of those times it had been for an adult: a man who had not come to an evaluation he was owed, a woman who had moved districts without telling anybody and then would not answer letters. *Physical retrieval* had meant, in his own experience, two men at a door and an unpleasant walk, and once a man who had sat down in the road and refused to get up, and had been carried, and had complained about it to everybody in the waiting room. It had never once, in his own experience, meant anyone was hurt.

He had never left it standing for a child.

He sat with the pen over the paper and did what he always did when a document made him uneasy. He went back through the regulation in his head, section by section, the way another man might go through his pockets, looking for a version of this summons without that clause in it that would still satisfy the procedure. He had a good memory for regulation. It was one of the things he was. He went through the evaluation sections and the summons sections and the notes under them.

The clause was standard. There was no form of the summons without it. To strike it out would be a deviation he could not justify in writing. Coss did not, as a rule, do things he could not justify in writing. That was not because he had no feelings about them. It was because a feeling with no written reason behind it was exactly what got a field agent quietly posted to a coast town to copy other men's reports.

He thought about his daughter, who would be fourteen at midsummer, and who had asked him at the door whether this one was going to be interesting.

He left the clause standing.

He signed it again where the form asked for a second signature, under the clause, as an acknowledgment that he had read it, and he blotted it, and he did not enjoy one moment of any of it. He noted, with the part of his mind that noted everything, that he did not enjoy it. That was information too. It was a case that kept producing more discomfort than six sheets of paper had any right to produce.

---

Then he wrote his preliminary report, to go west in the regional pouch on the morning cart.

It was short, and accurate, and recorded what he had done and what he had seen and what he had not. Location confirmed. Status confirmed. Householder cooperative to the legal minimum. No practice observed. Summons issued for compliance evaluation. He read it through and it was a good report, the kind he had been writing for eleven years, the kind that had made him the man regional sent when they wanted a report that did not lie or panic.

He sat for some time with the pen lifted before he added the last paragraph.

*The subject's file lacks documents that standard practice for this classification would require: an early assessment of risk from the district that certified him, a note on his residential compliance, and a reference to the standing-holder's guild review. I recommend that the certifying district's compliance procedure in this case be reviewed.*

He knew, writing it, what it was. It was the kind of sentence that could be read two ways. On one desk it was a careful agent being thorough. On another it was a field agent asking, in writing, why somebody else's paperwork looked the way it did. He did not know whose desk it would land on. He did not know who had written *Confirm location, status, and compliance* above a code his own register did not list. He had no way to know which way the sentence would be read.

He left it in. Whatever was going on above him with this file, he had settled the matter as he settled most things, without any fuss: if a report of his was ever to be wrong, he wanted it wrong for noticing too much, and never for having happened not to notice.

He sealed the report and set it by the door for the pouch.

Then he put on his coat, turned the collar up, and carried the summons through the dark lanes to the house with the leaning O.

Torvin opened the door. He looked at Coss, and at the paper, and at the seal on the paper.

"He's not in."

"Give it to him when he is."

Torvin looked at the seal for a moment longer. His face did not do anything that Coss could have written down. Then he took the paper, and nodded once, and shut the door, and Coss stood in the lane under the sign and found that he had been waiting, without meaning to, for the man to say whether he would.

He walked back to the post. Over the roofs, somewhere north, a few late voices were still going, the tail end of a crowd going home from a public house.

Lira walked beside him down the lane in the dusk with her canvas roll on her back and her hands in her jacket pockets, and did not look at him, and did not interrupt. The lanterns along the lane were coming on one at a time, as somebody went along ahead of them with a taper, so that they walked out of the dark into a little light and out of it again.

"You touched the shield twice with two fingers. You didn't hit it. Your left foot stayed. I watched it every time you moved and it stayed every time."

"Mm."

"Second exchange. She'd fixed the dip. She held the shield up through every step." He heard his own voice go a little tight, and let it. "You went in anyway, because you'd already started. She turned half a turn and caught your right shoulder with the edge. You went down on one hand and came up again. You kept your feet."

"It hurts," said Lira, conversationally. "That's not in your report. I'm putting it in mine."

"Third exchange. You caught the chop and sent it with no middle. Three times. She went after it every time, a quarter-turn too far, and had to put a foot down. The old men in the third row liked it. That's evidence. I heard them."

She laughed, short and real, the one that surprised her.

"Fourth exchange. She got angry and her strikes got big. The dip came back on the fourth step and on the fifth. You went in on her right. She caught you on the side of the head coming out. You worked your jaw afterward." He stopped there for a moment. He had seen one other thing in the fourth exchange, and he had decided at the rope that once was not a pattern, and he did not say it. "Fifth exchange. Two touches. She locked up for them. The step. You were inside on her right while her foot was in the air. She turned and you caught the turn and sent it, and she went round past her own feet and down across the groove." He took a breath. "Your backhand stopped a finger from her ear. Called."

They had come to the bottom of the lane, where it opened out toward the four lanes and the well with the iron cover. Lira stopped, as she always stopped there.

"I heard."

"It goes in a tin," said Lira, "with the other weeks of door." She stepped away toward the well. "Same time tomorrow. And I'm not doing the box. My shoulder's going to be the size of a loaf."

He had not thought about the man in the dark coat for nearly two hours. He noticed that when he came to Torvin's door, and was surprised, and a little ashamed, and then mostly glad.

# Chapter 31 — Footnote, Statute, Appendix

The mender was at his bench before it was properly light, with a lamp lit beside him and a clock open on the cloth, and he did not look up when Cael stopped in the doorway, because it was the morning and he did not talk in the mornings.

She had tied her hair back the ordinary way again, with the knot coming loose on one side. Then he got up, slowly, and walked down the room to the left-hand shelves without saying anything. He took down one volume, and then reached higher for another, and then stooped for a third from a bottom shelf, and brought all three back and set them on the long table under the lamp, side by side.

"Handbook," he said, tapping the first, which was thick and newish and bound in brown. "District edition. Copied fresh four years ago." He tapped the second, which was thin and old and so brittle at the spine that it had been tied round with a tape. "Supplementary register. Non-standard designations. Nobody's copied it in my time, and I've been here nineteen years. Mind the binding." He tapped the third, which was fat and grey. "Index. Tells you where everything is in the other two, and is wrong about a third of it."

He went back to his own table and sat down and did not look at them again.

Cael lit the lamp over the long table. Lira sat down at the end with the index and opened it as if it might bite.

Above their heads, through the boards of the ceiling, somebody walked across a room. Cael heard it quite clearly: four steps, a pause, three more, the scrape of a chair. Then, after a while, a sound began that he could not place at first, a soft regular knock of wood on wood, *tock*, a pause, *tock*, a pause, irregular but steady, like a slow clock that was not keeping time.

He looked up at the ceiling.

"That's him," said Lira, very quietly, without looking up from the index. "Isn't it."

"I don't know."

He did know. Somebody was sitting at a desk directly above them and writing, and the desk had a leg that did not sit flat, so that it knocked on the floor whenever the man leaned on it. He was reading the law that the man had used, in the man's own building, under the man's feet.

He opened the handbook.

---

It was the hardest reading he had ever done.

It was not the words. He knew most of the words. It was that the words had been put together by somebody who did not want them to be found by anybody who did not already know where they were. Every section began by saying what it applied to and then said that this was subject to another section, and the other section was in another part. Sentences went on for half a page before their verbs arrived. A thing would be defined on one page as one thing and then, forty pages later, used as though it meant something slightly different, and the only way to know which was to have read both.

He read it the way he had read Renn's heel and Brenna's dip and Dessa's foot, and the way Hesk had taught him to read a load sheet with its headings torn off. He made a list on a clean page of the Log as he went, a line for every section he passed. He wrote down not what it said but what it was for.

*Part One: who may be summoned. Part Two: how. Part Three: where. Part Four: what the evaluation is. This one says nothing; it points at two others. Either that's lazy or it's hiding something. Follow it.*

He followed it.

The first thing he found that looked like a seam was in Part Two, an hour in, and for a few minutes his heart went fast.

*A person under the age of sixteen years shall be summoned through the holder of the household standing under which that person resides.*

He read it twice, and then a third time, because it was very nearly too good. The summons had not gone to any household standing. It had gone to him, by his own name, to a lodging house. If a person under sixteen had to be summoned through the holder of his standing, then the summons had been served on the wrong person, and was no good, and would have to be done again, and done again through Hesk, in Denvash, two days' road away, a week's post.

He looked for where it was qualified, because nothing in this book was ever allowed to stand on its own. He found the qualification in a note at the foot of the next page, in smaller print.

*Where the household standing of such a person has been revoked under the statute governing non-standard classifications, the person answers in his own person from the age of fourteen years.*

He sat back.

Statute 14, Section 9. The notice in the back of the old notebook, the one that took the house off him in one line and then told Hesk very politely what it would cost to keep him near. He had thought, at the kitchen table with the teapot, that it had only taken a house. It had done more than that. It had made him, before the law, a man of fourteen with nobody standing between him and a summons. Nobody would ever be sent a paper on his behalf again. They would be sent to him.

It was not a seam. It was a wall, and it had been built on the second morning after Kindling, and he had not even known it was there.

He sat with that for longer than he could spare. Then he wrote it down, plainly, in the list, *Part Two: under sixteen goes through the standing-holder, except mine is gone, so it comes to me. Closed*, and turned the page, because there were forty hours left and the wall was not going anywhere.

---

The second false seam took him most of the next hour.

It was in Part Three, where the book said what could be done to a person who did not come. *A filing for physical retrieval shall be countersigned by the registering officer of the district in which the person resides.* He thought for a while that this might be something. A filing that needed a second signature needed a second man willing to sign it. But he found, in the index, after Lira had found the right page of the index for him, sighing, that the registering officer of an Unranked district was whoever kept the district's registrations. On this side of the river that was the clerk who sat at the counter in the front room of this very building, a floor above his head and a few paces to the left.

*Part Three: retrieval needs a countersignature. The countersigner is the clerk upstairs. Closed.*

The third one was not even a false seam. It was the index going in a circle. Section 40 of the handbook said *see the Supplementary Register for designations not provided for here*. The index sent him to the Register. The Register's own front page sent him back to section 40 of the handbook. He went round twice before he was sure he had not missed a turning, and then wrote *the index goes round in a circle at 40* and drew a small circle beside it, and felt, for the first time that morning, close to laughing, which he took as a sign that he was tired.

Upstairs the knock had stopped and started and stopped. Through the high windows, feet went past on the walk: boots, a pair of clogs, a dog's legs, a child's.

At noon, Lira shut the index with both hands.

"I'm no use with this," she said. She said it without any shame at all, as a fact about herself, like *fast, not strong*. "I read too quick. I read the page and I've got it and then I've got it wrong, because the next page says the first page didn't mean what it said. You read slow and you don't trust anything. That's what this book wants." She stood up and pushed her chair in. "I'm going to go and listen."

"To what?"

"To the district." She tipped her head at the ceiling. "He's been here three days. He's asked people things. Somebody's heard every one of them, because somebody always does. I want to know what he asked and where, and especially where he didn't." She picked up the loaf, tore it, put half on the long table beside his elbow and kept the other half. "You eat that. I'll find you at dusk."

She went out. Halden, at his table, watched her go without lifting his head, and then looked back at his book.

Cael ate the bread over the handbook, carefully, so that no crumbs went into the binding, and went back to section 40.

---

He found it a little after the middle of the afternoon, and he very nearly did not.

He had gone back to Part Four, which said what a compliance evaluation was, because everything else had come to walls. He had read it once already, in the first hour, and found nothing in it but a definition. He read it again now, slowly, every word, and when he came to the end of the section he did not turn the page. He looked at the foot of it instead, at the band of very small print below the last line, where the book kept its notes. He had stopped reading the notes some time in the second hour because there were so many of them and they were so small.

*A compliance evaluation shall assess the person summoned against the benchmarks of that person's recognized tier and Path, as these are set down in the standard evaluation framework.*

And under it, in print so small that he had to bring the lamp down on its hook to read it:

*Note 3. For classifications not recognized within the standard framework, see Supplementary Register, statute 19.*

He untied the tape round the brittle volume with great care and opened it. The pages were brown at the edges and soft as cloth, and the print was older and blacker and cramped, and he found statute 19 a third of the way through.

*Designations listed in the Second Appendix to this Register are not recognized within the standard evaluation framework. A person bearing such a designation may be evaluated only under senior evaluation authorization, and shall not be evaluated by standard district procedure.*

He turned to the back of the volume. There were two appendices. The second began on a page that had come loose from the binding and been tucked back in, slightly crooked, by somebody's careful hand. At its head was a subheading he knew, because he had copied it in a green book's handwriting at a table under a north window in Denvash on the second morning after Kindling, and he had not seen it since.

*Deprecated and Non-Standard Designations, Pre-Compact Era Holdovers.*

And under it, among the cramped black lines, his word, in its square brackets, with the same cold short sentence about practitioner safety risk that he could have recited in his sleep.

He did not move for some time.

Then he made himself lay it out in order, as Hesk laid out a calculation before he let anybody hang a weight on it.

The summons ordered a standard compliance evaluation. A standard compliance evaluation assessed a person against the benchmarks of their recognized tier and Path, as the standard framework set them down. [SHATTERED] was not recognized within the standard framework; statute 19 said so, and the Second Appendix listed it by name. A person bearing a designation in the Second Appendix could be evaluated only under senior authorization, and not by standard district procedure.

So the evaluation the summons ordered could not be done. Not by this man, at this desk, in this district, under this procedure. The regulation said so in its own words, if you followed it from a note at the foot of a page to a brittle book that nobody had copied in nineteen years, to an appendix that had come loose from its binding.

He did not let himself feel anything about it yet.

He had been wrong before about smaller things. He had been sure of Renn's figure right up until the third cut came up the lane. He had read a supplier's printed number and trusted it, and watched a bracket fail on the rig at less than twice its working load. A thing could look like a seam and be a wall with a crack painted on it.

So he did what Hesk did. Hesk read every load figure aloud before he signed the sheet, under his breath, because a figure said aloud had to get past your ears as well as your eyes, and ears were harder to fool.

He read the three passages aloud, in order, under his breath, in the hush of the half-buried room, with the lamp swinging a little on its hook above him. Part Four, note 3, statute 19, the appendix. Then the logic, step by step, out loud.

It held.

He read it aloud a second time, slower. It held. He read it a third time, starting from the other end, from the appendix back up to the summons, the way you check a column of figures by adding it from the bottom. It held. He read it a fourth time, the slowest of all, trying with every line to find the place where it would give, the way he had leaned on the bracket with his thumb on the bench at Fen Street, feeling for the bend.

It held on the fourth reading exactly as it had on the first. He understood, sitting there, that this was the only difference between a real find and a hopeful one that he would ever be able to trust: not how it felt when you found it, but whether it was still standing after you had done your best to knock it down.

Something in his chest let go, very slightly. It was not relief, not yet. It was the small, plain satisfaction of a figure that had been traced back to where it came from. He let himself have it for exactly one breath. Then he took out the Log.

He copied all three passages word for word, with the section and note and statute and appendix, and the shelf each volume had come from, and the note about the loose page. He did it fast the first time, just to have it down, in case the lamp failed or the room shut or the building fell in. Then he turned the page and did it again, slowly, in the careful hand he kept for things that mattered, with every number checked against the book as he wrote it. He had a feeling, sitting there, that of all the pages in the Log this one would be read by more people than any other, and that some of them would be looking for a mistake.

Upstairs the knock had stopped. As he blotted the second copy he heard a chair go back above him, and footsteps cross the floor, and go down a stair somewhere inside the building, and a door open and shut. Through the high window at the end of the room he saw a pair of dark trouser legs and good plain boots go past along the walk toward the river, unhurried, and out of sight.

---

Halden came down the room at dusk to take the volumes back to their shelves.

He picked up the handbook and looked at the place where Cael's slip of paper still marked Part Four. He picked up the brittle register and tied its tape round it again, with old, careful fingers, and settled the loose page of the appendix back into the binding where it belonged. He looked at the boy.

"Find what you came for?"

"I think so."

Halden nodded, slowly, and stacked the three books in the crook of his arm.

"Most of the people who walk out of here looking that pleased," he said, "are wrong about something." He did not say it unkindly. He said it as a carpenter might mention that a chisel was sharper than it looked. "If it turns out you're one of them, come back. I'll find you the next book."

"Thank you."

"I haven't done anything," said Halden, and went back down the room with the books, and Cael climbed the three steps into the evening.

The sky over the river had gone green and gold behind the core's walls, and the lanterns were coming on along the fish steps. Lira was sitting on the river wall at the top of the steps with her bad arm in her lap and her heels drumming against the stone. She jumped down when she saw him, and looked at his face, and he watched her read it.

"You found something."

"I found something." He gave her the Log, open at the careful copy, and she read it by the last of the light, fast and then slow, and then fast again, and then she looked at him over the top of it.

"It says he can't."

"It says he can't do it like that. Not here. Not him." He took the book back. "It doesn't make anything go away. It moves it. Somebody more senior has to come. That's all it does."

"That's not *all*." She looked up at the post, at the dark window on its upper floor. "It's time. That's not all."

They walked along the river toward Torvin's, the long way, because it was the way he went, and she told him what the district had told her.

The man in the dark coat had eaten at Amrit's on his first night and his third, at the end of the long table by the window, alone, and Amrit had given him a bowl of the fish stew and exactly the ordinary amount of bread. He had asked at the carriers' hut which bag went west and when, and the clerk had told him a good deal more than that. He had stood at the foot of the lane of yards on Saturday afternoon for about as long as it took to read the board of yard prices, and then gone away again down toward the dyers'. On Sunday he had bought two skewers at the cart in the triangle while the yard was shouting for Brenna going down, and asked the skewer woman what was on, and been told the circuit, and asked nothing more.

"Nobody in the yards was asked anything," Lira said. "Not one person. Not about you, not about fighters, not about the Cinder House. I asked the girls on the wall. They'd have told me. They tell me everything, mostly to annoy me." She looked at him sideways. "He doesn't know. Or he doesn't think it matters. Either way he walked right past it."

Cael thought about the margin of the Log, and three lines in it, and a polite man at Torvin's door a month ago asking for *the circuit kid*.

He did not say anything yet. He did not have it in a shape he could say. But he turned it over all the rest of the way home along the river in the dusk, and it did not get any lighter.

# Chapter 32 — Three Flat Sentences

He wrote the first draft at the kitchen table that night, after the others had gone up, and it was a page and a half long, and it was terrible, and he could not see why.

He had the three passages copied out in the careful hand at the top of the Log page, and he had the logic laid out under them in four steps, the way he had read it aloud in the reading room. All he had to do was put the two together on a clean sheet for a man in a dark coat to read. He had thought it would take a quarter of an hour.

It took him until the range had gone cold.

Every sentence he wrote wanted another sentence after it to hold it up. He would write that a standard evaluation assessed a person against a recognized tier and Path, and then find himself writing that he understood this might seem a narrow reading, but that he believed it was the plain sense of the section. He would set down statute 19, and then, without deciding to, add that he did not mean to suggest that the evaluation was unnecessary or improper, only that it could not proceed in the form the summons gave. He wrote that he hoped he would not be thought to be avoiding anything. He wrote that he was willing to be evaluated by whatever procedure the regulation did allow. He wrote, at the bottom, that he was sorry for any trouble it caused.

He read it all back, and it read like a boy standing at a desk with his hands behind his back, explaining.

Torvin's wife came down once, in her shawl, for something she had forgotten. She looked at him, and at the pages, and at the cold range, and she did not say anything. She cut a heel off the loaf and put it beside his elbow with a cup of milk, and went back up.

He crossed out the sentence about trouble. He crossed out the one about not avoiding anything. He read it again and it was better and it was still wrong, and the wrongness was in something he could not put his pencil on, like a bracket that held at working load and bent at twice it and left you no mark to show where.

He slept for perhaps four hours. He dreamed that he was reading the handbook and every note at the foot of every page said *see the note above*, and woke with the window grey and his hand on the Log.

---

The frost was thick again in the grey half. Lira was sitting on the low wall at the side of the yard with her coat buttoned to the chin and her right arm inside it, not in the sleeve, and the bread on her knee.

"Don't ask about the shoulder," she said. "Give it to me."

He gave her the sheet.

She read it the way she read a bout from the barrel, which was the way she read anything she was not immediately sure of: once fast, to have the whole shape, and then again slowly, stopping, going back. She did not say anything for a long time. A crow came down onto the eaves of the public house and looked at them both and went away again.

"The law's right," she said at last.

"I know the law's right. I read it four times."

"I know you did." She tapped a line two-thirds of the way down with one finger of her good hand. "This. You've put down what statute 19 says. It's a fact, and you're stating it like a fact, and that's good. But then there's this whole next part." She ran her finger along it. "*I understand that the summons was issued in good faith, and I do not wish to suggest that any officer has acted improperly, but I believe that on a close reading*." She looked up. "Who's that for?"

"Him."

"No. It's for the argument you think he's going to make. You've written his argument for him, in advance, so that you can answer it." She shook her head. "You don't do that in a bout. You don't stand on your mark before the first exchange and say to Vell, *I know he's going to come in high, and I want everyone to know I've thought about it.* You just stand there. You let him come in high, and then he finds out."

"Somebody's going to argue with it. He's going to argue."

"Then let him find his own argument. He won't, because there isn't one. You read it four times; you know there isn't one. But you won't show him there isn't one by sounding frightened that there might be." She took the pencil out of his hand without asking and drew a line through the whole of the bracing part, neatly, so that the words could still be read under it, the way he did it in the Log. "Cut all that. You want three sentences. Flat. Like things that are already settled."

"Say them."

She thought about it with her head on one side and the pencil against her lip.

"*A standard compliance evaluation can only assess a classification the standard framework recognizes.*" She wrote it at the bottom of the page in her quick slanting hand. "*[SHATTERED] is not recognized within that framework.*" She wrote that. "*The evaluation named in this summons cannot proceed under standard procedure.*" She wrote it, and put the pencil down. "Then the numbers. Then your name. Then stop."

He read the three sentences.

They were better at once, as much better as a bout became the moment you stopped explaining your plan to yourself in the middle of an exchange and simply did the next thing. There was nothing in them to take hold of. There was nothing in them that asked for anything, or hoped for anything, or was sorry. They lay on the page like three flat stones set in a stream, and you could walk across them.

"You want it to read like somebody who knows the law, not somebody who's scared of it," Lira said. "Being sure doesn't change what's true. It just changes whether a man argues with it out of habit."

"I am scared."

"I know you are." She said it plainly. "It doesn't have to be on the page."

She gave him back the sheet. He sat on the wall beside her and wrote it out again clean, from the beginning, on a fresh page torn from the back of the old notebook: the three sentences, then the handbook part and section and note, the register and the statute, the appendix and the place where its page had come loose, then *Cael Hesk-ward, Registration 41-7843-V*, and nothing after. It was eleven lines long. The first draft had been fifty.

Lira read it over his shoulder.

"That's good," she said. "That's very good. I've read Compact paper before, you know, in the life before the academy." She did not say anything more about that life; she never did. "That doesn't read like a boy who found the law yesterday in a cellar with an old man pretending not to watch him. It reads like somebody who's been doing this for years and is a bit bored by it."

"Halden wasn't watching."

"Everybody in a reading room's watching." She broke the bread in half with her good hand and her teeth, and gave him the larger piece. "You'll be all right this afternoon. I'm not saying it to be kind. I've read the page. The page is right."

A standard compliance evaluation can't be done on your classification. Which means I can't do the evaluation I was sent out here to do." He let his breath out, and for a moment his composure slipped far enough to show what was under it, and what was under it looked to Cael like frustration, and not at him. It was aimed at something past him, at the window, or further. "This is going to be a very unpopular report."

"Why unpopular? You'd be keeping to the rules."

"Because the people who asked for the sweep that brought me here didn't want a delay. They wanted the file resolved." Coss said it with the candour of somebody who had decided, at some point in the last few minutes, that pretending to this boy would cost him more than it bought. "I don't know what resolved means to them. Closed one way or closed the other. I honestly couldn't tell you. But a boy of fourteen reading me the statute across my own desk and walking off with six weeks isn't going to look like resolved to anybody above me." He looked down at the sheet. "I write accurate reports. That's my work. Today's report is going to say that a boy with a day in a cellar has stopped eleven years of mine dead at the door. I won't enjoy writing that. I'll write it anyway, because it's true, and because the other thing is worse."

"What happens now?"

"Now I stamp the summons closed, pending, and I apply to regional for a senior evaluator, because that's what the regulation asks for in your case." Coss picked up a stamp from the corner of the desk. He held it a moment, longer than stamping a paper required, and Cael understood that he was weighing something that had nothing to do with stamping. Then he brought it down on the summons with a good deal more force than the job needed. The desk rocked. *Tock.* "Senior authorization takes time. Regional has to review the file, find an evaluator who's qualified, and send them out to a district this far from anywhere. Six weeks, as a rule. Longer if the evaluators are busy, and they usually are."

Six weeks.

Cael did the sum at once, the way he timed a window in a bout. Six weeks was forty-two days. It was not an answer. It was room.

"Who asked for the sweep?" he said.

Coss looked at him for a long moment.

"I don't know," he said. "That's true. It's also all I'm going to say about it." He set the stamp back exactly where it had been on the corner of the desk. "Don't ask me again. It wouldn't do either of us any good."

---

"Thank you," said Cael.

He meant it. He understood, saying it, that Coss had not done this as a kindness. He had done it because it was correct, in the same way that he did everything, Cael thought, and doing the job correctly when correct was inconvenient to the people who had sent you was its own kind of honesty, and not a common one. He had not expected to learn that from a man in a Compact office.

Coss was looking at him again, with something in his face that Cael could not read, as though he were deciding whether to say a thing that was not part of the evaluation.

"May I ask you something?" he said. "Not for the file."

"You can ask."

"What are you going to do with six weeks?"

Cael thought about how much to say. He thought about the reading room under this floor, and the three lines in the margin of the Log, and Hesk's *don't change what you do*. He thought that the man had been more honest with him in the last quarter of an hour than his position required. He decided on something close to the whole of it.

"Get stronger. Build a reason for people to notice if I disappear."

Something crossed Coss's face. It was not approval, and it was not alarm, but it lived somewhere near both of them.

"That's the cleverest thing a person in your position could do," he said slowly, "or the most dangerous. Most clever things are both, when you look closely." He shut the folder over the stamped summons and the objection and stood up, which Cael understood meant the meeting was over. "Don't thank me. You've bought six weeks. If I were you I'd use every one of them to work out what you mean to do with the classification you've got. Because whoever put a sweep on this district won't be satisfied with a procedure. They want something settled. I don't know what." He looked at the window. "I can buy you time. I can't buy you the answer to whatever they're actually asking. That part's yours."

Cael stood. He looked, without meaning to, at the folded grey square of paper on the floor by the desk's left front leg.

"It's not the floor," he said. "It's the leg. That's come out from under it."

Coss looked down at the paper. He looked at it for some time, as though it had been lying there for a great many visits by a great many men and nobody had ever once said so.

"So it has," he said.

Cael went down the narrow stair sideways. In the front room, the cooper and the man with the blue hands had gone, and the clerk was rolling up his string. He did not look up.

Cael had reached the door before he noticed what had not been asked. In the whole of the quarter of an hour, Coss had not said one word about the yards.

# Chapter 33 — Expensive

He came out of the post into the middle of the afternoon and stood on the step with the door shut behind him.

The street was exactly as it had been when he went in. A cart was going down toward the lower bridge with a load of barrels, and one of the barrels had come loose in its ropes and was knocking against its neighbour at every jolt. A woman was calling fish from the bottom of the steps in a voice like a gull's. Two doors along, somebody laughed at something that had nothing to do with him, a long, easy laugh that went on after the joke was over, and somewhere across the river a bell was ringing the hour for people with cards that opened gates.

None of it knew what had happened in the room upstairs. None of it would have behaved any differently if it had. He stood in the middle of all that ordinary noise and let it settle round him, and found that it steadied him, as the sacks of flour coming off Ressa's wagon had steadied him once at a kitchen table, with a notice between him and Hesk.

Somebody would still bake bread tomorrow. A cooper and a man with blue hands would still be cross about a gutter.

Lira was sitting on the river wall at the top of the fish steps, exactly where she had sat at dusk the evening before, with her right arm inside her coat and her heels against the stone. She was not drumming them. She had the look of somebody who has been happening to pass a particular place for about an hour.

She got down off the wall when she saw him, and did not come toward him, and waited.

"Six weeks," he said.

Lira sat down on the wall again, quite hard, as though her legs had decided it for her.

"Six weeks," she said. "He said yes."

"He said it wasn't wrong. He checked the numbers in his head. He didn't need the book." He sat down beside her. The stone was cold through his trousers. "He stamped it closed, pending. Somebody more senior has to come. Six weeks, he says, as a rule. Longer if they're busy."

"And then?"

"And then somebody more senior comes."

She let out a long breath that went up white in front of her and hung there.

"He was decent," Cael said. He had not known he was going to say it until it was out. "He didn't want to write the summons. I don't think he wanted to write any of it. He said it was going to be an unpopular report, and he was going to write it anyway, because it was true."

"Decent men write summonses all the time," said Lira. "That's what they're for."

"I know."

"I'm not saying he isn't. I'm saying it doesn't change what's on the paper." She looked at him sideways. "What did he ask you about?"

"Nothing. He was going to ask me about my residence and my practice, and I gave him the objection before he could start." He looked down at the river going past the bottom of the steps, brown and quick. "He didn't ask about the yards, Lira. Not once. Not the circuit, not fighting, not Vell. He doesn't know."

She was quiet.

"He asked me what I was going to do with six weeks," Cael said. "At the end. He said it wasn't for the file."

"What did you tell him?"

He told her.

Lira looked at him for a long moment with an expression he could not read at all, and then, slowly, she began to smile. It was not the quick one she allowed herself when she had landed something. It was the slow one that started somewhere behind her eyes and took its time getting out.

"You told a man from the Compact," she said, "to his face, in his own office, that you were going to go and make yourself hard to lose."

"I told him the truth. He'd told me the truth."

"I know." She got down off the wall again and held out her good hand to pull him up. "That's the worst part. He'll have believed you."

He did the front of the Log first, at the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard, under the pear tree that had given up growing tall, because that was the rule.

*Fifty-fifth day. Compliance evaluation at the post, upstairs. Objection given before it began. Coss (field agent) read it three times. Checked the citation from memory. Ruled it right. Summons stamped closed, pending. Senior evaluation authorization to be filed. Six weeks, he says, perhaps more.*

He looked at that for a while. Then he wrote the rest, the parts that were facts and would not go in columns.

*Coss asked nothing about the yards.*

*Coss says he doesn't know who asked for the sweep. I believe him. (Not evidence. A face.)*

*Lira, h: in three days he asked nobody in the yards anything.*

He turned the book over and opened it from the back.

He sat with the pencil for some time before he wrote, because the thing he wanted to write was not a fact yet and he did not want it to put on a fact's coat.

*The man at Torvin's door, about the twenty-eighth day, asked for the circuit kid. He knew there was a circuit, and that I was in it. The grey coat with the pin didn't seem to know. Coss doesn't know. Coss has my whole file, all of it, the thin case, and it can't have the circuit in it, because he'd have asked.*

*So whoever the polite man was, he knew a thing the Compact's own file doesn't.*

*I don't know who he was. I don't know whether he's anything. I'm writing down that two different people have been asking two different questions, and I don't know which of them matters more.*

He drew a short line under it, and then he did the arithmetic, because six weeks was a number and he did not trust any number he had not laid out on the page.

Forty-two days. The Cinder House ran a card every Thursday and every Sunday, and an afternoon card most Tuesdays, and Vell did not put anybody in twice in one week who had come off the dirt looking the way he generally came off it. Renn was due back at the turn of the month, which was perhaps a week away, perhaps two. He could not fight more than six times in forty-two days and still be standing at the end of them. Perhaps not more than five.

Six bouts, then, at most. Six chances to be something more than *unrated (vouched)* on a lamp-wick bill nailed to a board.

He sat on the wall with the Log open on his knee and thought about what it would take for a district to notice that a boy had gone.

It would take people who knew his name and said it. It would take a book that had him in it, not as *unrated* but as a person the yard had come to see, so that if the person stopped coming, somebody would turn back the pages and ask why. It would take Torvin, who held a room and knew to the day when it was paid. It would take the mender, who had said *Wednesday*, and would look up from his clock on Wednesday afternoon at a doorway with nobody in it. It would take a fish man who nodded and a bread woman who gave him the loaf from underneath, and two errand boys, and a girl on a wall who told Lira everything to annoy her. It would take Lira, who would be first.

And Hesk, a week away by the west bag, who would know to the day when a letter that should have come had not.

He thought about the green book in Denvash. Three of them dead within weeks. He did not know what had happened to them. He knew only what had been written down, by somebody who had cared which kind of true got believed. But he thought, sitting under the pear tree, that whatever it had been, it had happened very fast, to people who had not yet had time to belong to anybody but the people they came from.

He wrote that down, in the back, with a line round it, so that he would know it was a guess.

*I don't know that. It's what I think. I don't want to be somewhere nobody would look.*

Then he closed the Log, and went up to the Cinder House, because it was a Tuesday, and there had been an afternoon card, and Vell would still be at her table.

She was. The yard had nearly emptied. The pie boy was raking the circle where the afternoon's last bout had scuffed it, and the crows were along the eaves, and Vell sat in the shade of the back wall on her stool with the ledger open in front of her, finishing an entry. She did not look up until she had blotted it.

"You're not on this week," she said. "I told the Wind girl. Rest the hand."

"The hand's rested." He held it up. The split knuckles had healed to thin pink lines. "I want to fight. As often as you'll put me in, for the next six weeks."

Vell looked at him. It was the flat look, the thorough one, and it stayed on him for longer than it usually did.

"Why six?"

He had thought, walking up, about how much to tell her, and he had decided on nothing about the post. The book stayed clear of the Compact. Lira had told him that on his first morning in this yard, and Vell had said it to him herself in her own way, with *nothing to register*, and he was not going to be the one who brought the Compact over the rope on a sheet of paper.

"Because I've got six weeks to get better in," he said. "I'd like to spend all of them getting better."

Vell considered that for a while, and seemed to find that it was true as far as it went, and that it did not go all the way, and that the rest of it was not hers.

"Renn's back at the turn of the month," she said. "I'm holding him for you, as I said I would. Before that, I'll put you in with somebody." She picked up her tea. "I'll tell you who when I've decided. Not before. And not because—"

"Because I asked," said Cael. "I know."

Something happened at the corner of Vell's mouth and was gone again before it had become anything.

"There's one more thing," he said.

She waited.

"My name." He made himself say it plainly, the way he had said the terms back to her on the first morning, in his own words, checking each one before he set it down. "You'll write it in the book when the yard comes to see me. You said so. Not before, and not because I asked. I'm not asking you to write it now." He took a breath. "But when you do, it's not Just Cael. It's Cael Hesk-ward. Hesk is my grandfather. I'd like the book to have it right."

Vell did not pick up her pen.

She sat with the tea in her hand and looked at him. He thought that she was looking past his face at something else, at a margin with *nothing to register* in it, perhaps, or at the long row of entries under no name at all, or at a girl standing in front of this table with her chin up saying *I do*. He could not tell. Vell's face did not let you tell.

"Hesk-ward," she said at last, as if trying the weight of it.

"Yes."

"I'll keep it." She set the cup down. "Not in the book. The book has what the yard's seen, and the yard hasn't seen that yet." She touched two fingers, briefly, to the side of her own head. "In here. Where I keep the things that are mine. When it goes in the book, it'll go in right."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me," said Vell. "I've not done anything but listen." She looked down at the ledger, and he understood he had been dismissed, and went. At the gate he looked back. She had not picked up her pen. She was sitting quite still at her table in the last of the light, with her hand flat on the cover of the closed book, looking at the circle.

He wrote to Hesk that night at the kitchen table, after the others had gone up.

He worked out the days first, as he always did now. This would go in the west bag at noon tomorrow, the fifty-sixth day, at the carriers' hut, the same hut and the same bag as always, with nothing on the outside but the address they had already. It would come to Fen Street on about the sixty-third. Hesk would answer it that night, because that was what Hesk did, and the answer would come back up the fish steps on about the seventieth. By then the six weeks would be a third gone.

He could not write the fear. It would be a week stale before Hesk could do anything with it, and he would have to carry it alone at the table for that week. He could write what was true, though, and it would still be true when it got there.

*Hesk,*

*Your letter came on the fifty-first day, on the same cart as a man from the Compact. I didn't know that until the next night. He came to Torvin's door and asked for me by my whole name, and Torvin told him the room's paid and that I pay on time, and nothing else. Torvin's wife wanted him told I eat my oats.*

*I went. But first I went to the registry reading room under the post, which nobody reads the sign of, and read the law until I found where it doesn't fit me. There's a note at the bottom of a page in the handbook, which sends you to a statute in a register nobody's copied in nineteen years, which sends you to an appendix with my word in it. A standard evaluation can only assess a classification the framework recognizes. Mine isn't recognized. So the evaluation they sent can't be done. I read it out loud four times, the way you read a load figure before you sign it. It held.*

*He checked it in his head and said it was right, and stamped it closed, pending. A senior evaluator has to come instead. Six weeks, he says.*

*He was decent about it. I don't know who asked for the sweep. Neither does he, I think.*

*You said don't change what I do. I'd decided the same thing on a roof a week before your letter came. I'm posting this from the same hut, in the same bag.*

*You'd have liked the appendix. Its page had come loose and somebody had tucked it back in crooked.*

*I'm all right. I'm telling you this as it comes, because you asked me to, and because I asked you to first.*

*— Cael*

He read it over. It was true all the way through. It was not all of what was true. The four were still in the back of the Log, numbered, and he had made a promise on a step to somebody else about who would hear them first, and he had not broken it. He counted what he had not told Hesk, as he always did now, and the number had not changed.

He folded the letter and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

Coss wrote his second report that evening at the borrowed desk, with the lamp close and the window gone dark over the fish steps, and it was the least pleasant document he had written in eleven years.

It was not long. He set down that he had attended for the compliance evaluation as summoned, and that before it could begin, the subject had presented a written procedural objection. He gave the citation, in full: the part, the section, the note, the register, the statute, the appendix. He set down that he had checked the citation against the regulation and found it correct. He set down that the standard evaluation could not lawfully proceed, and that he had marked the summons closed, pending, and filed for senior evaluation authorization as the regulation required.

Then he wrote the sentence he had told the boy he would write.

*The objection was found, prepared and presented by the subject himself, aged fourteen, from the public reading room of this post, in the space of one day.*

He read it back. It was accurate. It would sit on a desk above his own, in an office where somebody read authorization codes that his own register did not list, and it would say to whoever sat at that desk that the file they had kept so thin had been read more closely in a day by a boy in a cellar than by anybody who had handled it in Denvash. He did not know how it would be taken. He had a fair idea.

He left it in. He blotted it, and sealed it, and set it by the door for the morning pouch west.

Then he got down on one knee on the floorboards, beside the desk's left front leg, and picked up the small grey square of folded paper that had lain there, a hand's width from where it belonged, for the whole of his stay and for who knew how many men's stays before him. He folded it once more, to make it thicker, and lifted the corner of the desk an inch, and slid the paper under the leg, and set the desk down on it.

He leaned on the desk.

It did not rock.

He stayed there for a moment on one knee, with his hand flat on the desktop, and found that he was very nearly smiling, alone in a borrowed room over a district he still could not read. Then he got up, and turned the lamp down, and lay on the cot with his coat over him.

In the morning, after the post and before the yard, Cael went up to the crossroads.

The triangle was quiet at that hour, with frost along the shaded side of every street and the skewer woman's cart still covered with its oilcloth. The board stood on its post in the middle of it as it always did, with its notices on notices, its tacks and nails and the bent fork, the bottom layers gone into the grain.

The pie boy had nailed up a new card since yesterday. It was in Vell's upright pencil, on the back of a bill for candles, and it was for Thursday and the Sunday after. His own name was not on it. The word *unrated* was not on it either. Vell had said she would tell him who when she had decided, and not before.

He stood and read it twice anyway, from the top.

Under it, near the bottom of the board, gone pale in four days of weather, was Sunday's result. *Lira (Cu 3, Wind) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), fifth exchange, called.* Beside it somebody had drawn three small strokes in charcoal, the way people marked a tally, and somebody else had circled them.

And under that, older still, he found his own: *unrated (vouched) over Dessa (Cu 5, Stone), fourth exchange, conceded.*

He looked at the three results for a while, one above the other.

Forty-two days. Six bouts, if Vell would give them and his body would take them. At the end of the forty-two days a person more senior than Coss would come out along the merchant road, two days from wherever such people were kept, with a file that was too thin and a question that nobody had told Coss. Cael did not know what the question was. He did not know who was asking it. He knew only that a field agent who had been honest with him had said he could buy time and could not buy an answer, and that the time had been bought, and was going down already, one cold morning at a time.

He could not make himself safe in forty-two days. He did not think anybody could have done that.

But he could make himself expensive.

He could make it so that if a boy at Torvin's stopped coming to the yard, a great many people would notice on the same day, and would say so, and would not all say it quietly. He could put his name into enough mouths that it would be awkward to take it out of them. He could stand in that circle often enough, and well enough, that a woman with a book would find one evening that she could not leave him off a card without a dozen people asking her why. He could make it cost something to lose him.

It was not much of a plan. It was a plan a boy could make standing in front of a board in the frost with his hands in his pockets. But it was his, and he had built it out of evidence, and every piece of it was something he could do tomorrow.

Behind him the street that went up toward the yards had begun to fill: carts with sand, a barrow of straw targets, somebody shouting at somebody about a rope. He heard quick feet on the cobbles that he knew without turning round, because he had learned their rhythm every morning in the grey half, with their weight a little forward on the front of the foot.

"You've been reading that board," said Lira, coming up beside him with the bread under her good arm, "for eleven minutes."

"I wasn't counting."

"I know. I was." She looked at the board, at the new card and the three results under it, and then at him. "Well?"

"Six weeks," said Cael.

"Six weeks." She tore the bread and gave him the larger half. "Then come on. You're wasting them standing here."

They went up the street together toward the yards, into the morning, and he did not look back at the board, because he knew exactly what was on it, and he meant, before six weeks were out, to give it something new.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —
  present throughout, not just in set-piece fight chapters.
- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

  progression-vocabulary spikes.
- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

### City Access by Rank

**LOCKED:**

| Rank tier | City district access |
|---|---|
| Unranked / [SHATTERED] | Unranked Districts only; cannot legally enter inner city |
| Copper | Outer districts; limited market access |
| Iron | General city access; guild district access with credentials |
| Bronze | Full city access; administrative district entry |
| Silver | All districts; inter-city travel credentials |
| Gold | Continental access; diplomatic consideration |

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## The Arbiter System

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

## The Quieting

**SECRET:** The Quieting is not a natural phenomenon. It is the Architect's preserved will beginning to prime Valdris for a systemic reset — a reversion of the Path architecture to its original design parameters, which would eliminate all current practitioner classifications and rebuild the system from scratch. The Architect's reset protocol treats current practitioners as acceptable collateral.

**SECRET:** The Quieting cannot affect Cael's Fractured Path because the Fractured Path does not run through the Architect's system. It is prior to it.

### Observable mechanics — PROVISIONAL, on the page from Book 7 (Void Roads), 2026-09-04

## The Architect

**LOCKED (existence by Book 8); SECRET (nature and intent until Book 13-14)**

The entity — or long-dead person's preserved will — who designed the Path system. The Architect built the Arbiter architecture, deployed it across Valdris, and has been dormant in it for centuries. The Architect did not die. They converted themselves into the system's deep-layer governance protocol.

**SECRET:** The Architect's motivation was not malevolent. In the era before the Path system, Fractured Path practitioners — [UNBOUND] — were extraordinarily dangerous. The Architect designed the classification system specifically to prevent Fractured Paths from ever forming again. The system worked. For four centuries.

**SECRET:** The Architect's error was categorical, not motivational. They believed the problem was [UNBOUND] potential. The actual problem was [UNBOUND] potential without any framework for understanding it. The Path system did not solve the problem — it suppressed the symptoms while eliminating anyone who could have addressed the root cause.

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). Diagnostic technique: **the reading** — contact, both hands, the healer's current run through the patient's architecture ("listening with my hands to how you're built"). Two modes, taught before either is load-bearing (B7 Ch7): the *surface* reading (hands on the hurt, seconds) and the *deep* reading (both hands, whole architecture, minutes, patient still and uninjured, at a mending's cost). Costs and limits: contact range only; healing spends the healer's reserve at roughly the rate of the damage repaired — no crowds, no self-healing; a Tide practitioner **cannot heal what she cannot read**; combat use is a *stall* (a hand on a wrist, a breath, the declaration doesn't render) at brutal cost — Oryn has done it three times in her life. Inside quiet ground: nothing.

**Cael's Tide-adjacent fragment (B7 Ch13):** partial; Iron; self and contact; the first fragment that does nothing in a fight; engagement field *clinical*; **the standing rule, in Oryn's hand and his: ask first, every time.** Consistent with B2's "Tide-adjacent" anomaly label and Book 15's "Oryn's Tide Path burns out."

## Continuity Rules

1. **The Compact Registry is the authority on Path classification.** Any scene involving official rank must be consistent with what the Registry would show for that character at that point in the story.

2. **Path declarations follow standard format.** Only Cael's fragment notices deviate. All other practitioners receive standard declarations.

3. **Tier advancement is not instant.** No character advances a tier in a single scene. Advancement is earned across multiple books for major characters.

4. **The Architect's will is not omniscient.** It can detect [SHATTERED] signatures and issue systemic pressure, but it cannot directly perceive or target individuals until Book 11 when it becomes actively deployed.

5. **The Quieting spreads from ancient sites outward.** It does not appear suddenly in cities. It begins at edge-territory ruins and expands. This gives the companions time to investigate before it becomes a continental crisis.

6. **Cael's ability integration has limits.** He cannot integrate an ability he has not witnessed in use. Seeing a written description does not qualify. The ability must be performed in his presence.

7. **The found family is permanent.** No companion exits the series without narrative justification. The loss in Book 12 is a choice, not a death — the companion is still alive, their relationship with Cael changed.

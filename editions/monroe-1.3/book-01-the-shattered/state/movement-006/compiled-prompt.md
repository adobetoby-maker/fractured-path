## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that

  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,

- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —

- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

# Chapter 33 — Expensive

He came out of the post into the middle of the afternoon and stood on the step with the door shut behind him.

The street was exactly as it had been when he went in. A cart was going down toward the lower bridge with a load of barrels, and one of the barrels had come loose in its ropes and was knocking against its neighbour at every jolt. A woman was calling fish from the bottom of the steps in a voice like a gull's. Two doors along, somebody laughed at something that had nothing to do with him, a long, easy laugh that went on after the joke was over, and somewhere across the river a bell was ringing the hour for people with cards that opened gates.

None of it knew what had happened in the room upstairs. None of it would have behaved any differently if it had. He stood in the middle of all that ordinary noise and let it settle round him. It steadied him, as the sacks of flour coming off Ressa's wagon had steadied him once at a kitchen table, with a notice between him and Hesk.

Somebody would still bake bread tomorrow. A cooper and a man with blue hands would still be cross about a gutter.

Lira was sitting on the river wall at the top of the fish steps, exactly where she had sat at dusk the evening before, with her right arm inside her coat and her heels against the stone. She was not drumming them. She had the look of somebody who has been happening to pass a particular place for about an hour.

She got down off the wall when she saw him, and did not come toward him, and waited.

"Six weeks," he said.

Lira sat down on the wall again, quite hard, as though her legs had decided it for her.

"Six weeks," she said. "He said yes."

"He said it wasn't wrong. He checked the numbers in his head. He didn't need the book." He sat down beside her. The stone was cold through his trousers. "He stamped it closed, pending. Somebody more senior has to come. Six weeks, he says, as a rule. Longer if they're busy."

"And then?"

"And then somebody more senior comes."

She let out a long breath that went up white in front of her and hung there.

"He was decent," Cael said. He had not known he was going to say it until it was out. "He didn't want to write the summons. I don't think he wanted to write any of it. He said it was going to be an unpopular report, and he was going to write it anyway, because it was true."

"Decent men write summonses all the time," said Lira. "That's what they're for."

"I know."

"I'm not saying he isn't. I'm saying it doesn't change what's on the paper." She looked at him sideways. "What did he ask you about?"

"Nothing. He was going to ask me about my residence and my practice, and I gave him the objection before he could start." He looked down at the river going past the bottom of the steps, brown and quick. "He didn't ask about the yards, Lira. Not once. Not the circuit, not fighting, not Vell. He doesn't know."

She was quiet.

"He asked me what I was going to do with six weeks," Cael said. "At the end. He said it wasn't for the file."

"What did you tell him?"

He told her.

Lira looked at him for a long moment with an expression he could not read at all, and then, slowly, she began to smile. It was not the quick one she allowed herself when she had landed something. It was the slow one that started somewhere behind her eyes and took its time getting out.

"You told a man from the Compact," she said, "to his face, in his own office, that you were going to go and make yourself hard to lose."

"I told him the truth. He'd told me the truth."

"I know." She got down off the wall again and held out her good hand to pull him up. "That's the worst part. He'll have believed you."

He did the front of the Log first, at the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard, under the pear tree that had given up growing tall, because that was the rule.

*Fifty-fifth day. Objection given before it began. Ruled right. Closed, pending. Six weeks.*

*Coss asked nothing about the yards. Says he doesn't know who asked for the sweep. I believe him. (Not evidence. A face.)*

He turned the book over and opened it from the back.

He sat with the pencil for some time before he wrote, because the thing he wanted to write was not a fact yet and he did not want it to put on a fact's coat.

*The man at Torvin's door, about the twenty-eighth day, asked for the circuit kid. He knew there was a circuit, and that I was in it. The grey coat with the pin didn't seem to know. Coss doesn't know. Coss has my whole file, all of it, the thin case, and it can't have the circuit in it, because he'd have asked.*

*So whoever the polite man was, he knew a thing the Compact's own file doesn't.*

*I don't know who he was. I don't know whether he's anything. I'm writing down that two different people have been asking two different questions, and I don't know which of them matters more.*

He drew a short line under it, and then he did the arithmetic, because six weeks was a number and he did not trust any number he had not laid out on the page.

Forty-two days. The Cinder House ran a card every Thursday and every Sunday, and an afternoon card most Tuesdays. Vell did not put anybody in twice in one week who had come off the dirt looking the way he generally came off it. Renn was due back at the turn of the month, which was perhaps a week away, perhaps two. He could not fight more than six times in forty-two days and still be standing at the end of them. Perhaps not more than five.

Six bouts, then, at most. Six chances to be something more than *unrated (vouched)* on a lamp-wick bill nailed to a board.

He sat on the wall with the Log open on his knee and thought about what it would take for a district to notice that a boy had gone.

It would take people who knew his name and said it. It would take a book that had him in it, not as *unrated* but as a person the yard had come to see, so that if the person stopped coming, somebody would turn back the pages and ask why. It would take Torvin, who held a room and knew to the day when it was paid. It would take the mender, who had said *Wednesday*, and would look up from his clock on Wednesday afternoon at a doorway with nobody in it. It would take a fish man who nodded and a bread woman who gave him the loaf from underneath, and two errand boys, and a girl on a wall who told Lira everything to annoy her. It would take Lira, who would be first.

And Hesk, a week away by the west bag, who would know to the day when a letter that should have come had not.

He thought about the green book in Denvash. Three of them dead within weeks. He did not know what had happened to them. He knew only what had been written down, by somebody who had cared which kind of true got believed. But he thought, sitting under the pear tree, that whatever it had been, it had happened very fast, to people who had not yet had time to belong to anybody but the people they came from.

He wrote that down, in the back, with a line round it, so that he would know it was a guess.

*I don't know that. It's what I think. I don't want to be somewhere nobody would look.*

Then he closed the Log, and went up to the Cinder House, because it was a Tuesday, and there had been an afternoon card, and Vell would still be at her table.

She was. The yard had nearly emptied. The pie boy was raking the circle where the afternoon's last bout had scuffed it, and the crows were along the eaves. Vell sat in the shade of the back wall on her stool with the ledger open in front of her, finishing an entry. She did not look up until she had blotted it.

"You're not on this week," she said. "I told the Wind girl. Rest the hand."

"The hand's rested." He held it up. The split knuckles had healed to thin pink lines. "I want to fight. As often as you'll put me in, for the next six weeks."

Vell looked at him. It was the flat look, the thorough one, and it stayed on him for longer than it usually did.

"Why six?"

He had thought, walking up, about how much to tell her, and he had decided on nothing about the post. The book stayed clear of the Compact. Lira had told him that on his first morning in this yard, and Vell had said it to him herself in her own way, with *nothing to register*. He was not going to be the one who brought the Compact over the rope on a sheet of paper.

"Because I've got six weeks to get better in," he said. "I'd like to spend all of them getting better."

Vell considered that for a while, and seemed to find that it was true as far as it went, and that it did not go all the way, and that the rest of it was not hers.

"Renn's back at the turn of the month," she said. "I'm holding him for you, as I said I would. Before that, I'll put you in with somebody." She picked up her tea. "I'll tell you who when I've decided. Not before. And not because—"

"Because I asked," said Cael. "I know."

Something happened at the corner of Vell's mouth and was gone again before it had become anything.

"There's one more thing," he said.

She waited.

"My name." He made himself say it plainly, the way he had said the terms back to her on the first morning, in his own words, checking each one before he set it down. "You'll write it in the book when the yard comes to see me. You said so. Not before, and not because I asked. I'm not asking you to write it now." He took a breath. "But when you do, it's not Just Cael. It's Cael Hesk-ward. Hesk is my grandfather. I'd like the book to have it right."

Vell did not pick up her pen.

She sat with the tea in her hand and looked at him. He thought that she was looking past his face at something else, perhaps at a margin with *nothing to register* in it, or the long row of entries under no name at all, or a girl standing in front of this table with her chin up saying *I do*. He could not tell. Vell's face did not let you tell.

"Hesk-ward," she said at last, as if trying the weight of it.

"Yes."

"I'll keep it." She set the cup down. "Not in the book. The book has what the yard's seen, and the yard hasn't seen that yet." She touched two fingers, briefly, to the side of her own head. "In here. Where I keep the things that are mine. When it goes in the book, it'll go in right."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me," said Vell. "I've not done anything but listen." She looked down at the ledger, and he understood he had been dismissed, and went. At the gate he looked back. She had not picked up her pen. She was sitting quite still at her table in the last of the light, with her hand flat on the cover of the closed book, looking at the circle.

---

He wrote to Hesk that night at the kitchen table, after the others had gone up.

He worked out the days first, as he always did now. This would go in the west bag at noon tomorrow, the fifty-sixth day, at the carriers' hut, the same hut and the same bag as always, with nothing on the outside but the address they had already. It would come to Fen Street on about the sixty-third. Hesk would answer it that night, because that was what Hesk did, and the answer would come back up the fish steps on about the seventieth. By then the six weeks would be a third gone.

He could not write the fear. It would be a week stale before Hesk could do anything with it, and he would have to carry it alone at the table for that week. He could write what was true, though, and it would still be true when it got there.

*Hesk,*

*Your letter came on the fifty-first day, on the same cart as a man from the Compact. I didn't know that until the next night. He came to Torvin's door and asked for me by my whole name, and Torvin told him the room's paid and that I pay on time, and nothing else. Torvin's wife wanted him told I eat my oats.*

*On Sunday he left a summons at Torvin's for a compliance evaluation, forty-eight hours, with physical retrieval if I didn't go.*

*I went. But first I read the law in the registry's reading room until I found where it doesn't fit me. A standard evaluation can only assess a classification the framework recognizes, and mine isn't one. I read it out loud four times, the way you read a load figure before you sign it. It held.*

*He checked it in his head and said it was right, and stamped it closed, pending. A senior evaluator has to come instead. Six weeks, he says.*

*He was decent about it. I don't know who asked for the sweep. Neither does he, I think.*

*You said don't change what I do. I'd decided the same thing on a roof a week before your letter came. I'm posting this from the same hut, in the same bag.*

*You'd have liked the appendix. Its page had come loose and somebody had tucked it back in crooked.*

*I'm all right. I'm telling you this as it comes, because you asked me to, and because I asked you to first.*

*— Cael*

He read it over. It was true all the way through. It was not all of what was true. The four times something had moved him that he had not chosen were still in the back of the Log, numbered: the cart, Renn, the drop, Dessa. He had made a promise on a step to somebody else about who would hear them first, and he had not broken it. He counted what he had not told Hesk, as he always did now, and the number had not changed.

He folded the letter and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

Coss wrote his second report that evening at the borrowed desk, with the lamp close and the window gone dark over the fish steps, and it was the least pleasant document he had written in eleven years.

It was not long. He set down that he had attended for the compliance evaluation as summoned, and that before it could begin, the subject had presented a written procedural objection. He gave the citation, in full: the part, the section, the note, the register, the statute, the appendix. He set down that he had checked the citation against the regulation and found it correct. He set down that the standard evaluation could not lawfully proceed, and that he had marked the summons closed, pending, and filed for senior evaluation authorization as the regulation required.

Then he wrote the sentence he had known since the afternoon that he would have to write.

*The objection was found, prepared and presented by the subject himself, aged fourteen, from the public reading room of this post, in the space of one day.*

He read it back. It was accurate. It would sit on a desk above his own, in an office where somebody read authorization codes that his own register did not list. It would say to whoever sat at that desk that the file they had kept so thin had been read more closely in a day by a boy in a cellar than by anybody who had handled it in Denvash. He did not know how it would be taken. He had a fair idea.

He left it in. He blotted it, and sealed it, and set it by the door for the morning pouch west.

Then he got down on one knee on the floorboards, beside the desk's left front leg, and picked up the small grey square of folded paper. It had lain there, a hand's width from where it belonged, for the whole of his stay and for who knew how many men's stays before him. He folded it once more, to make it thicker, and lifted the corner of the desk an inch, and slid the paper under the leg, and set the desk down on it.

He leaned on the desk.

It did not rock.

He stayed there for a moment on one knee, with his hand flat on the desktop, and found that he was very nearly smiling, alone in a borrowed room over a district he still could not read. Then he got up, and turned the lamp down, and lay on the cot with his coat over him.

---

In the morning, after the post and before the yard, Cael went up to the crossroads.

The triangle was quiet at that hour, with frost along the shaded side of every street and the skewer woman's cart still covered with its oilcloth. The board stood on its post in the middle of it as it always did, with its notices on notices, its tacks and nails and the bent fork, the bottom layers gone into the grain.

The pie boy had nailed up a new card since yesterday. It was in Vell's upright pencil, on the back of a bill for candles, and it was for Thursday and the Sunday after. His own name was not on it. The word *unrated* was not on it either. Vell had said she would tell him who when she had decided, and not before.

He stood and read it twice anyway, from the top.

Under it, near the bottom of the board, gone pale in three days of weather, was Sunday's result. *Lira (Cu 3, Wind) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), fifth exchange, called.* Beside it somebody had drawn three small strokes in charcoal, the way people marked a tally, and somebody else had circled them.

And under that, older still, he found his own: *unrated (vouched) over Dessa (Cu 5, Stone), fourth exchange, conceded.*

He looked at the card and the two results for a while, one above the other.

Forty-two days. Six bouts, if Vell would give them and his body would take them. At the end of the forty-two days a person more senior than Coss would come out along the merchant road, two days from wherever such people were kept, with a file that was too thin and a question that nobody had told Coss. Cael did not know what the question was, or who was asking it. He knew only that a field agent who had been honest with him had said he could buy time and could not buy an answer. The time had been bought, and it was going down already, one cold morning at a time.

He could not make himself safe in forty-two days. He did not think anybody could have done that.

But he could make himself expensive.

He could make it so that if a boy at Torvin's stopped coming to the yard, a great many people would notice on the same day, and would say so, and would not all say it quietly. He could put his name into enough mouths that it would be awkward to take it out of them. He could stand in that circle often enough, and well enough, that a woman with a book would find one evening that she could not leave him off a card without a dozen people asking her why. He could make it cost something to lose him.

It was not much of a plan. It was a plan a boy could make standing in front of a board in the frost with his hands in his pockets. But it was his, and he had built it out of evidence, and every piece of it was something he could do tomorrow.

Behind him the street that went up toward the yards had begun to fill: carts with sand, a barrow of straw targets, somebody shouting at somebody about a rope. He heard quick feet on the cobbles that he knew without turning round, because he had learned their rhythm every morning in the grey half, with their weight a little forward on the front of the foot.

"You've been reading that board for eleven minutes."

Lira came up beside him with the bread under her good arm.

"I wasn't counting," he said.

"I know. I was." She looked at the board, at the new card and the two results under it, and then at him. "Well?"

"Six weeks," said Cael.

"Six weeks." She tore the bread and gave him the larger half. "Then come on. You're wasting them standing here."

They went up the street together toward the yards, into the morning. He did not look back at the board. He knew exactly what was on it, and he meant, before six weeks were out, to give it something new.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

> **OPEN:** Whether Warden Coss survives the series.

> **RUMOR (source: Compact Registry):** [SHATTERED] classifications indicate dangerous instability.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

## Learning discipline

- One edit is an example, not a universal law.
- Promote a broad instinct only when several accepted edits support the same
  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.
- Do not carry names, setting facts, or plot events from an example into a new
  movement. Learn the difference between WAS and NOW, not the example's subject.

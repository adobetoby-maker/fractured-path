# Chapter 39 — The Plan That Survives

He could not sit on the low wall that night, or on the cot either, without something under the ribs on the left side catching, sharp, every time he breathed in all the way. So he wrote the Talis entry lying on his right side on the cot with the Log propped against the wall and Yeni's lamp turned toward him.

Yeni had turned it toward him herself. She had looked at the way he came up the stairs, one at a time, with his hand flat on the wall, and she had said nothing at all. Then she had got up off her cot and moved the lamp a hand's width along the shelf, and lain down again, and gone back to her sewing in the half dark as if she could do it by feel, which she probably could.

*Tenth. Talis, Copper Rank 4, Stone. Lost, fourth exchange, match.*

*Claim: he goes somewhere I don't see him go, plants, and strikes from where he's planted. Evidence: four exchanges. Every strike came from a place I didn't expect. Shoulders still. Hands low. Nothing in either.*

*Claim: the tell is in the ground. Evidence: I felt it with my face after he'd put me down. A hum in the dirt, a half beat before he plants. Lira felt it through the iron stake at the corner of the rope, in the third. (Lira, h. Mine, after.)*

*Ruling: I lost to a thing I wasn't looking at.*

He lay there with the pencil for a long time after that. The brothers came up the stairs arguing about whether the river would freeze at the edges this year. They went to bed still arguing, and went quiet, and the wheezing man by the door began his long whistle, like a kettle a long way from boiling.

*I've been looking at hands and shoulders since the community hall,* he wrote. *Hesk taught me hips and feet. I kept the hips and feet and added shoulders and faces and breath. Every tell I've ever found has been on the body. A heel, a dip, a shimmer, a breath, a lean, a foot coming down late. All of them were somewhere I already knew to look.*

*Talis's wasn't on the body at all. It was under it.*

*So: I have a method. It works. It has holes in it, and the holes don't tell you where they are. That's what makes them holes. If I'd known to look at the ground, it wouldn't have been a hole, it would have been a place I looked. I didn't lose today because I watched badly. I lost because there was a whole place I'd never once thought to watch.*

*There will be others. I don't know where. That's the point.*

*Ruling, for every one from now on: before I look for the tell, look for where I haven't looked.*

He read it back. Then he did a thing that he did not put in the Log, because it was too foolish to write down.

Yeni had got up to go down to the yard. When she crossed the room in her stockinged feet, past the end of his cot, he put his hand flat on the floorboards beside the cot, palm down, and shut his eyes.

He felt her. He felt each step come into the boards before it landed, very faint, a small give in the wood under his palm, and then the step itself. It was not like Talis. It was only a floor, and a woman walking on it, and boards that had been creaking in this house for longer than Torvin had been deciding about his sign. But it was there, if you put your hand down and listened with it.

"What are you doing?" said Yeni, from the door.

"Nothing."

"You're lying on the floor with your hand flat on the boards and your eyes shut." She did not sound surprised. Very little surprised Yeni. "That's not nothing. That's something I'd want to hear about, if I had the time."

"I'm listening to the floor."

There was a pause.

"Well," said Yeni, "it's got plenty to say," and went down the stairs, and he heard every one of them through his palm.

---

Hesk's letter had come on the Friday, two days before Talis, and he had read it three times that evening and then put it away in the leather book and not read it again. He had not been able to. He took it out now, on the cot, with his side aching, and made himself read it a fourth time.

The review had sat. They had asked Hesk whether he wrote to the boy, and Hesk had told them that he did, every week, and meant to go on doing it. They had found *no present cause*, and the matter would *remain on the book*, which meant that it was not closed and was not lost either. There was a gauge-maker who had told a man from the guild office that every workshop in Denvash had a drawer of springs, and that a man who did not was either a liar or a bad maker, and the man had written it down. Cael smiled at that, on the cot, and the smile made his side catch.

Then the part about the reading room. *I'm proud of the reading, and the four times aloud.* That was all Hesk said about it. It was short, and Cael read it twice, and found that it was enough.

Then the part he had not been able to read again.

*When your letter came, I sat on the workshop step in the street and read it there, and I was more afraid than I have been since the day at Weaver's Row.*

He made himself read it to the end.

*It was all over by then. You had already done it, and done it well, and I knew that by the second line. It made no difference. That's the post, and it's the price of where you are, and I'd pay it again. But you asked me once to tell you as it comes, and that's how it came.*

He lay with the letter on the blanket in front of his face.

He tried to see it. Hesk sitting on the step of the workshop on Fen Street, in the street, where people could see him, with a letter in his hands and his big square face doing something Cael had never once seen it do. He could not see it. He had never seen Hesk afraid. He had seen him prepared, and still, and careful, and sometimes angry, and once, at a kitchen table on the second morning after Kindling, ashamed. He had never seen him afraid. It had happened anyway, a week ago, on a step, where nobody but strangers could see.

And Hesk had written it down and sent it, because they had said they would tell each other as it came.

There were four things on a page at the back of the Log. Cael had not sent any of them.

He knew the number exactly. He had counted it every time he sealed a letter since the night of the drop. He had told himself every time that it was not the same as Hesk's years of silence, because he had written down that he was keeping them, and because he had told one person that he was keeping them and why. That was true.

But on Wednesday, four days ago, he had stood in the grey half and listened to Lira say *you keep everything till you're sure*. He had felt it land, because it was true. She had said it about one thing he had kept from her for three weeks, about her own feet. There were four things he had kept from Hesk for two months, about his.

He lay there with Hesk's fear on the blanket in front of him and thought about that.

Then he read the end of the letter, because he could not lie there for ever.

*Joren says to tell you his heels are down. He held a level for me for fifty minutes without moving it. His father thinks he's wasting his mornings.*

*Alis carries basins. She says you'll laugh.*

He laughed. He could not help it. He laughed on his side on the cot, and his ribs caught, and he went on laughing anyway, quietly, with his hand pressed against his side. Across the room Yeni's needle stopped, and then went on.

He wrote back that night, with the Log for a desk, lying down. He told Hesk about Renn, and the false heel, and the front heel instead. He told him about Sarel, who had crouched down beside him in the dirt and told him *two*. He told him about Talis, and the ground, and Lira's hand on the iron stake. He told him that Lira had taken a room two streets from Torvin's, over a chandler's, with a door that locked from both sides, and that she had made him knock.

He told him about the right foot. He did not tell him it was Lira's; he said only that he had seen a thing in somebody he trained with and kept it three weeks too long, and that he had been told so, and that he had been right to be told. He thought Hesk would understand what he was saying underneath it. He thought Hesk would understand it better than anybody.

He did not tell him the four.

He sealed it, and put it in the leather book to keep flat, and lay looking at the ceiling. The number had not changed. It was still four. But he had written down, in a letter that would be in Denvash in a week, that keeping a thing too long cost something. He knew that Hesk would read it on a step or at a bench and know exactly how much.

---

The last weeks came in hard.

The frost did not go off the yard at all now, not even by the middle of the morning. It lay white along the foot of the east wall all day, and the dirt in the circle was hard as a floor, and the mist in the low corner by the drain froze into a grey crust that the pie boy broke with a rake. At Torvin's the brothers came in every night with their hands like boiled meat and said that the river was going to freeze at the edges. Torvin's wife put a second blanket on every cot without being asked. The wheezing man coughed through the nights now as well as wheezed. Doss's pipe smoke came out from under the low brown door earlier in the mornings, because the warehouses had put on a second watch.

The left side of Cael's ribs got better slowly. For a week he could not breathe in all the way without the catch. After that he could, if he did it carefully, and after that he forgot to be careful and found that he did not need to be. The back went from green to yellow to nothing. The forearm, over the old burn, stopped swelling and went stiff instead, and Lira made him work it in the cold water from the pump every morning until his fingers ached with the cold instead.

She worked on her right side. She did it the way she did everything, by doing it wrong on purpose until she could feel exactly where the wrong lived. She would go through an hour at the post until she was tired and her shoulders were down. Then she would do the step and the backhand, and let her weight go on past the end of it, onto the right foot, and stop there. She would stand open, exactly the shape she had been, and say *there*, to herself, quietly. Then she did it again, and stopped her weight a little sooner. Then sooner again.

"Tell me," she said, every time.

"Still a hand."

"Tell me."

"Less. Three fingers."

"Tell me."

He told her. He told her every time. He told her other things too, now, because the rule was that he told her when he saw them, and he had found that once he started there was a great deal to see. He told her that her left hand dropped a little when she laughed, which made her laugh and drop it. He told her that she always went round the post to the right, never the left, and that if anybody ever wanted to wait for her behind a post they would know which side to wait on. He told her that she pushed her hair off her face with her wrist before she did the step, every time, and that it was nothing, probably. It was probably only hair. But he had been told to say.

"It is only hair," said Lira. "But thank you." And she stopped doing it, and two mornings later she started doing it again, because, she said, it was nice to have one thing that was hers that nobody could use against her, and if anybody ever tried to read her hair she would deserve to lose.

He was working on the other thing, the moving.

It was slow. It was the slowest thing he had ever tried to learn. The deflection had come in days, and the box in a week, and even the strike from the hip had come in a fortnight of a hundred throws a morning. This did not come in any of those ways, because it was not a thing you could do a hundred times. It was the thing that happened when you stopped doing things a hundred times. Every morning Lira came at him at a speed he could not watch, and he moved without deciding. Every morning, for the first few, his hips went square the moment he thought about them, and she would stop and say *hips* and he would say *I know*.

But it came. It came by the width of a hair a morning, and some mornings not at all. On a morning near the end of the fifth week he got through a whole set without her saying *hips* once. He did not notice until she stopped and stood there looking at him.

"What?" he said.

"You didn't say it," said Lira.

"Say what?"

"*Gone*." She had the chalk in her hand. "I came in with the step and you'd started the counter, the back and out, and you saw me already in, and you let it go and went somewhere else. And you didn't say it. You just went."

He had not noticed. He thought back, and found that she was right. He had seen her inside and been somewhere else, all in the same moment, with no word in between, like a hinge that had been oiled.

Lira went to the post. The *gone* column had thirty-nine marks in it, in rows of five, with the last row one short. She made a fortieth mark. Then she drew a line under the whole column, straight across, hard, so that the chalk squeaked on the canvas.

"That's the last one," she said.

"I didn't say it."

"That's why it's the last one." She looked at the column. "That's the whole point of making you say it. You say it till you don't need to say it. Then you've got the thing the word was for, and you can throw the word away." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Forty. I thought it would be more. You're quicker than you think."

"You said I was slow."

"I said you weren't a natural." She came back to her mark. "I never said slow. I'd never say slow. You're the most efficient learner I've ever stood across from. You just do it in the most annoying order." She brought her hands up. "Again. And if you say it, I'm rubbing out the line."

He did not say it. He found, through the rest of that morning, that he could not have said it if he had tried. There was nowhere in the moving for the word to go.

---

He had watched Dellin twice before he knew he was going to fight him.

He found that out on the night after Talis, going back through the Log. The entries were there, a Thursday and a Sunday in the third week, among the bouts he wrote down because he wrote down every bout. *Dellin, Cu 3, Force*, and half a page each time. He had not underlined either. They had been homework, the way all the cards were homework, and he had filed them and turned the page.

Vell gave him Dellin on the Monday after Talis. She said it was Thursday, after the lamps, the third bout. She said Dellin had asked for him.

"Asked?" said Cael.

"He watched Renn. And Sarel." Vell turned her tea. "He says he'd like to see what you'll do with a man who doesn't care whether you see him coming." She looked up. "He's not clever, Dellin. He's never needed to be. He's very strong and he's done the same thing in the same way for three seasons, and it works, and he sees no reason to change it." She looked down again. "I'd like to see whether you can beat a thing that works."

He watched Dellin a third time on Tuesday afternoon, from the rope, near the barrel, with the Log open on his knee.

He watched the ground first.

He made himself do it. He stood at the rope with his hand on the iron stake at the corner, and felt it, and watched Dellin's feet before he looked at anything else. There was nothing in the ground. Dellin was not Stone, and his feet were only feet. Cael put his hand on the stake anyway, every exchange, and wrote *ground: nothing* at the top of the page. He felt a little foolish, and did it anyway.

Then he watched the rest.

Dellin was a big man of about twenty-five, a carter, broad and slow on his feet, with very short hair and a face like a friendly loaf. He fought exactly the way Vell had said. He opened every exchange with his left hand, high, toward the head. It was not a fast punch, and it was not meant to land. It was meant to make you bring your hands up. Then, as your hands came up, his right went to your body, low, under them. It was a short heavy punch to the ribs, and it did not matter whether it hurt, because it was not the punch that put you down.

It was the shove after it.

Cael had felt Force once before, from Petra. Everything she hit had got an extra push at the end, after her hand had stopped, as if somebody standing behind you had kicked you. Dellin's was the same, and three times as heavy. His right hand landed on the Copper 2 Shield's ribs, and stopped, and a half beat later the Shield went sideways across the circle as though he had been hit by a cart. He went down in the groove with his shield still up.

*Left high, every time,* Cael wrote. *To bring the hands up. Right to the body under them. The shove after the right. That's the whole of it.*

He watched the second and third exchanges, and it was the whole of it. Left high, right low, shove. The Shield tried to keep his hands down for the left, and the left landed on his ear, not hard, and his hands came up for the next one anyway, because a fist at your ear is a fist at your ear.

He saw the other thing in the fourth.

The Shield had got clever. He had stopped putting his hands up for the left at all, and had taken it on his shield, and stepped back from the right. For one whole exchange Dellin's right had found nothing. Dellin had stood in the middle of the circle with his friendly loaf of a face getting red, and then he had changed.

He had not changed what he did. He had changed how close he did it. He came in so near that there was no room to step back from the right. He took hold of the Shield's collar with his left hand, and held it, and hit him with the right, low, and the shove came, and the Shield went over and Dellin went down on top of him.

*When it stops working he holds you,* Cael wrote. *Left hand on the collar instead of left hand high. Then the same right.*

He sat at the rope with the Log on his knee and looked at what he had written. It was three entries now, on three days, and they all said the same thing. That was so rare that he did not trust it, and he went back through the two older pages to be sure. They said the same thing. Left high. Right low. Shove. And when that stopped working, the collar.

*He told me,* he wrote. *Three times. He doesn't care that I know. That's what Vell meant.*

---

He built it that night on the low wall by the pump, in the cold, with the pear tree over him. Lira sat beside him with her knees up and her coat round them and argued with every piece of it, because that was what a plan was for.

"Don't block anything," he said. "Anything he touches gets the shove. If I put my arm up for the left, he's touching my arm."

"It's only the left. The left doesn't shove. You said."

"I said the right shoves. I don't know that the left doesn't. I've never seen him land the left hard. I'm not finding out on Thursday." He wrote *block nothing*. "So the left comes high, and I don't bring my hands up for it. I go outside it, to his left, away from the right."

"His right'll follow you."

"His right can't follow me. It's aimed at where my body will be when my hands go up. If my hands don't go up, my body's not there." He wrote it. "That's the first exchange. I make him miss the right. That's all I do in the first."

"And the second?"

"The same. I need to see he'll do it twice. He will. He's done it three seasons."

Lira chewed her lip.

"And the third he takes your collar."

"In the third he takes my collar. When the right stops working, he comes in close and holds. He'll do it in the third, because it's the third time he's missed." He looked at the page. "That's the one. When he comes in for the collar, his left hand's out in front of him and his right's held back, waiting. He's square to me. Both feet. He has to be, to hold and hit. He's the biggest he'll ever be and he's standing still."

"So you hit him."

"So I don't let him get the collar. I go in when his hand comes out for it. Under it. Inside it." He looked at the strike, the one he owned, written at the top of a page long ago. "And I don't hit his body. I hit the hand that's coming for the collar, on the way past, to turn him, and then the one strike, from the hip, on the jaw, while he's turned."

"And if he doesn't take the collar in the third?"

"Then in the fourth."

"And if he doesn't take it at all?"

He looked at her.

"Then I'll say gone," he said, "and do something else."

Lira looked at the page for a long time in the cold.

"That's the first plan you've ever made," she said slowly, "that doesn't need him to be stupid. Only to be what he is." She pulled her coat tighter round her knees. "I like it. I don't trust it. Plans don't survive."

"I know."

"They don't. Not in there. Something always happens."

"I know," said Cael. "I'm going to find out what."

---

Nothing happened.

That was what he wrote afterward, in the Log, at the top of the entry, before anything else. He wrote it in a hand that was not quite steady, on the low wall by the pump, with the lamps lit and his breath going up white. *Nothing happened.* He looked at it for a long time, because it had never been true before.

The yard had been full for a Thursday, three deep on the shaded side, and the lanterns were lit on their hooks along the three walls. Dellin came over the rope as if he were climbing down from his cart, heavy and easy and in no hurry, and stood on his mark and smiled across at Cael. It was the friendly loaf of a smile. He looked like a man who was looking forward to something.

"Dellin. Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Cael put his hand flat on the dirt at his mark for one breath before he stood. He did not know who saw him do it. He felt the ground. There was nothing in it but cold.

Dellin came off his mark, and the left came high, toward his head.

He did not bring his hands up.

It was the hardest thing he had done since the second exchange against Renn, harder than going right into a blade. Every part of him wanted to bring his hands up for a fist coming at his face. He kept them where they were, and went outside, to Dellin's left, a long step, and the left went past his ear. Then the right came in low, under hands that were not there, at a body that was not there, and found air.

Dellin stood a moment, looking at the place where Cael's ribs should have been. Then he laughed, a big pleased laugh, and went back to his mark.

*First exchange,* said Cael to himself. *Left high, right low. I didn't block. He missed.*

The second was the same. Left high. Cael outside it. Right low, into air. Dellin did not laugh this time. He stood in the middle of the circle with his friendly face going a little pink, and rubbed the knuckles of his right hand against his thigh as if they had been cheated of something.

*Second exchange. The same. He's beginning to mind.*

"Begin."

Dellin came off his mark and the left came high, and Cael went outside it, and the right came low, and found nothing. For the third time in three exchanges it found nothing. Then Dellin did exactly what he had done to the Shield on Tuesday afternoon, and to two other men on a Thursday and a Sunday in the third week.

He came in close. His left hand came out, open, for the collar.

Cael went under it.

He went in as the hand came out, the way he had planned it on the wall, under the arm and inside it, into the big square space in front of Dellin's chest. His left forearm met Dellin's reaching wrist on the way past and turned it. It was not a block. It was the outside of the forearm, wrist to elbow, meeting it and sending it on, a quarter turn further than Dellin had meant to reach, so that the big man's shoulder came round after his hand.

And the strike came up from the hip. Back foot driving, shoulder following, fist arriving last. It went into the hinge of Dellin's jaw, under the ear, where Dellin's own turned shoulder had left it open.

It was not a hard strike. He did not have a hard strike. He had a well-timed one.

Dellin's knees went. Not all the way. They went the way a table's legs go when you lean on it at the wrong corner, a sudden dip. He sat down in the dirt, quite gently, with a surprised look on his friendly face, and put one hand down behind him. He sat there looking up at Cael, and at the fist that had come back up and stopped, half an arm from his nose.

"Called," said Vell. "Unrated."

---

For a moment the yard did not seem sure what it had seen.

It had seen three exchanges in which nothing happened and a fourth that was over almost before it began. The noise came up slowly along the rope, uncertain at first and then not, the shaded side first and then the sun. It was not the roar there had been for Renn. It was the other noise, the low pleased one, the noise of people seeing a thing done properly.

Dellin sat in the dirt and worked his jaw from side to side.

"Huh," he said.

Cael held out his hand. Dellin looked at it and took it, and Cael pulled, and it was like pulling at a cart that did not want to go anywhere. Then Dellin got his own feet under him and came up, and stood there swaying a little, and looked down at Cael from a long way up.

"You knew," he said. He did not say it angrily. He said it the way a man says something that has surprised him and that he wants to make sure of. "The left. The right. My hand coming out. You knew what I'd do before I did it."

Cael opened his mouth to say that he had not known. He had not known anything. He had watched three times and written it down three times, and on the third time he had seen that it never changed. He had worked out how likely it was that it would change on Thursday, and found it was so small that he could stand on it. It was not knowing. It was arithmetic.

He shut his mouth again.

It would not have meant anything to Dellin. It would not have meant anything to anybody, standing in the circle under the lanterns with the yard still deciding how loud to be. A man had asked him whether he had known, and the true answer was a column of figures, and there was no room in a circle for a column of figures.

"I watched you," he said instead. "Three times."

Dellin thought about that for a long while, rubbing his jaw.

"Three times," he said. "Huh." He nodded slowly, as if putting it somewhere. "I'll have to start doing something different, then." He seemed faintly worried by the idea. Then he clapped Cael on the shoulder, gently, for a big man. He went off toward the water bucket, saying to himself, quite loudly, "Three times," as if it were a sum he had been set and could not quite make come out.

Cael walked to the table.

"Result stands as called," said Vell. "Unrated, over Dellin, Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Fourth exchange. Called." The pen moved. "Witnessed and entered."

Then the pen moved again, a second line, and a third. She did not read those out. She did not look up. But when he turned to go she said, without lifting her head, "That was a plan."

"Yes."

"It held."

"Yes."

"They don't, as a rule." She blotted the page. "Write down why this one did. That's the part worth keeping."

---

He wrote it down on the wall by the pump, under *Nothing happened*.

*Eleventh. Dellin, Copper Rank 3, Force. Won, fourth exchange, called.*

*Claim: left high to bring the hands up, right low to the body, the shove after the right. When that stops working, the collar. Evidence: three bouts watched. The same every time, in the same order, for three seasons (Vell, h). Ruling: block nothing. Make him miss twice. Go in under the collar in the third.*

*It went exactly the way I wrote it. Every exchange.*

He sat with that for a while.

*Why it held,* he wrote, because Vell had told him to. *Because he never changes, and I didn't ask him to. I didn't build it on a thing he might do. I built it on a thing he always does. Renn had a plan for me. Sarel had no plan and didn't need one. Talis had a thing I'd never looked for. Dellin has the same thing he had last season, and he's proud of it, and I let him keep it right up until it was the one thing in front of me.*

*A plan holds when it's built on what they are, not on what you hope they'll do.*

*And I put my hand on the ground at my mark first. There was nothing there. I want to keep doing that anyway.*

He looked at that last line, and left it in.

Lira came out of the kitchen door with two cups of something hot that Torvin's wife had given her for them, and sat down on the wall beside him, and gave him one. She looked at the page over his shoulder. She did not read it aloud. She read *Nothing happened* at the top, and her mouth went up at one corner.

"Vell gave you Corbin," she said.

He looked at her.

"While you were writing. She came out to the gate to tell me, because she said you looked busy." Lira blew on her cup. "Thursday week. The last week. The last bout of your six." She was quiet for a moment. "Do you know who Corbin is?"

"A Copper 3. I've watched him once. In the second week."

"He's had nearly forty fights in that yard," said Lira. "He's been on Vell's cards since before I came. Since before Dessa, before Renn." She turned the cup in her hands. "He's not the best. He's never been the best. Nobody's afraid of him. But everybody knows exactly how good he is, because he's always exactly that good. Every season. Every bout. He's the one people use."

"Use for what?"

"For measuring." She looked across the yard at the pear tree, which had given up growing tall. "Somebody new comes up through the cards, and nobody knows what they are yet. So people say, *put him in with Corbin*. And they see how long it takes. If you go down in two, you're a Copper 2. If you go five with him, you're a good Copper 3. If you beat him—" She stopped.

"If you beat him?"

"Then you're whatever's past Corbin," said Lira. "And everybody in the district already knows what that means, because they've all been measuring against him for years." She drank. "It's not fair. You beat Renn, and Renn's better than Corbin on his day. Nobody cares about Renn the way they care about Corbin. Renn's a show-off with a good figure. Corbin's a ruler."

He thought about that, looking at the page.

"So if I beat him—"

"If you beat him, it's not a new fact about you," said Lira. "It's a new fact hung on an old one, one everybody already believes. People can carry that kind. They'll carry it down the fish steps and into the market and across the bridge to their cousins." She looked at him. "That's what you said you wanted. On the first morning. Your name in more mouths."

"Yes."

"Then that's the bout." She finished her cup. "Corbin's the one they'll repeat."

---

He watched Corbin on the Sunday, from the rope, with his hand on the iron stake.

There was nothing in the ground. He had expected nothing and wrote it down anyway. There was nothing in the shoulders, or the hands, or the breath, either. He had expected that too, a little, from what Lira had said, and still he watched for an hour and found nothing at all.

Corbin was about thirty-five, and square, and not tall, with a bald patch he did not try to hide and a short brown beard going grey at the chin. He was Shield Path. Cael had not known that from the one bout in the second week, because he had not written it down; Corbin had won that one in the first exchange and Cael had not had time to see. His shield was not like Brenna's. It was not a pane wider than his shoulders, held up to be walked at people. It was small, hardly bigger than a dinner plate, a little patch of shine off his left forearm. He did not hold it up. He let it come and go, there for a strike and gone again between, as if he did not like to spend it on nothing.

He did not chop with it. He did not get angry. He did not dip on the step-through, because, somebody at the rope said, he had trained the dip out of himself in his first season, before Brenna had been born, near enough.

His opponent was a good Copper 4 Ember, a woman, and Corbin gave her three exchanges and then took her apart in the fourth. He did it without any hurry and without anything Cael could find to write down, except that he did it.

Cael watched the whole bout and wrote nine lines, and every one of them was *nothing*.

Then, at the end of the third exchange, as the two of them went back to their marks, he saw it.

It was not in Corbin's body. It was in his eyes.

On his mark, between the exchanges, while the Ember woman breathed and set herself, Corbin looked at her feet. He did not look at her face, or her hands, or the shimmer coming up off her arm. He looked at her feet, carefully, for the whole of the pause, the way a man reads a page he means to answer. When Vell said *begin*, he moved before the Ember woman did, and he was in exactly the place her feet had said she would go.

Cael sat at the rope with the pencil stopped over the page.

*He reads feet,* he wrote. *At the mark. Before the exchange. He watches how you set yourself, and he knows where you're going.*

He looked at that for a long time.

*He's me,* he wrote under it, very small. *He's a watcher. He's been watching for forty fights.*

He did not know what to do with that. He sat on the low wall by the pump that night with it open on his knee and looked at it, and he did not know what to do with it at all. Then, very late, with the cold coming down and the lamp going out in Yeni's window above, he thought about a big young man in a middle yard on the lane with a friend who had been given the job of being Cael. He thought of a heel settling back into the dirt and holding there. Waiting. Saying a thing that was not true.

He began to write very fast.

*Renn taught me this,* he wrote, at the bottom of the page, when he had finished. *He'll take full credit. Let him.*

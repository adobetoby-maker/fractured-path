# Chapter 37 — Nothing to Look Up

"Again," said Lira. "And this time don't put your hand out."

He had put his hand out. He had put it out every time, eleven times that morning, because it was what a hand did when the ground came up at you, and every time she had stopped him and made him get up and do it again.

They were in the low corner of the yard by the drain, where the mist pooled in the mornings and the dirt was softest from the water that ran down off the public house roof. Lira had chosen it for that. She had said, with a perfectly straight face, that she wanted him to learn to fall on soft ground first so that he would have something to be disappointed about later.

"A hand out is a broken wrist," she said. "Or a broken arm, if she's heavy. You land on the meat. The side of your leg, your hip, your back across here." She slapped the back of her own shoulder. "Chin in. Roll. Let the ground have it. Don't argue with the ground. It's bigger than you."

"Is that a Wind saying?"

"It's a Lira saying. I've been knocked down more than anyone in this yard." She brought her hands up. "Again."

She came at him with the step, slowly, at half speed, and her shoulder came into his and took his feet. He went down sideways. He tucked his chin and let his hip take the dirt and rolled, and his hand stayed folded in against his chest, and he came up onto one knee with mud down his whole left side and his back screaming where the bruise was.

"There," said Lira. "That one."

She made a mark on the post with her chalk. It was not a *gone* mark. She had started a second column on the other side of the post for falls, and it had seven in it now, and the *gone* column had thirty-four.

He had stopped noticing the word. That was the strange thing. It was Saturday, the eighteenth morning since the board, and he still said it every time a plan died, as she had made him promise. But it came out of him now without any heat in his ears, and sometimes it came before he had properly decided the plan was dead. It came out, and he heard it, and his body was already doing the next thing while his mind caught up and agreed. It was beginning to feel less like a confession and more like a hinge.

They did the falls until the sun came over the east wall. Then Lira wanted the step at full speed, because she said she had been going slowly for him all week and her legs had forgotten what they were for.

She did it ten times. The two empty touches, high, quick, one from each hand. The ground folding. The backhand coming round at head height and stopping beside his ear. He got his counter half built on the fourth and on the seventh, which was better than one in ten. On the ninth she was breathing hard. She had been up since before light and had already done an hour at the post before he came, and he could see the morning in her shoulders.

On the tenth she went too far.

It was very small. The backhand came round and stopped by his ear, as it always did. But her weight did not stop with it. It went on past the place where the strike had ended, a hand's width further, onto her right foot, the way a door goes on swinging after you have let go of the handle. She had to put the right foot down hard and wide to catch herself. For half a breath she was standing open, with her whole right side turned toward him and nothing between his hands and her ribs.

Then she was back, and loose, and grinning, and pushing her hair off her face with her wrist.

"Again," she said. "No. Enough. Bread."

He had seen it once before. He had seen it in the fourth exchange against Brenna, three weeks ago, when she had gone in on Brenna's right and gone a hair further than she needed to. Brenna's cocked fist had come round and caught her on the side of the head as she came out. He had seen it at the rope, and he had decided at the rope that once was not a pattern, and he had left it out of her report.

That was twice.

He took the larger half of the bread and ate it and did not say anything.

He told himself that he would tell her after Sunday. He told himself that she should not be carrying a thing about her own feet into a week when she would be at the barrel watching him get hit by a Bronze. That was true. It was the kind of true that has its legs sawn short, and he knew it, and he ate his bread.

---

The yard was full on Sunday by the middle of the afternoon, and it was not full for him.

He understood that the moment he came through the gate in the wall. The benches were out and the third row was already crowded with old men in scarves. The rope was three deep on the shaded side. But the noise of the crowd was a different noise from the one there had been for Renn. It was not the noise of people who had come to see what would happen. It was the noise of people who had come to see something they already knew would happen, done well.

They had come for Sarel. She fought here a few times a season, Vell had said, when she had nothing better on. When she did, people came.

Marrow's slate said *Sarel* with no price beside it at all. Under it, *unrated (vouched)*, with a price so long it ran off the edge of the slate and had been chalked round the corner. And under that, *Over/under: 3 exch.*

"Three," said Lira at the barrel. "He thinks you'll last three. That's generous."

"Is it?"

"It's Marrow. It's whatever makes him money." She had her arms folded on the rope and her eyes on the far side of the circle, where Sarel was standing by the water bucket talking to the woman who sold the water, easy, with one hand in her pocket. "Remember the falling."

"I remember the falling."

"Chin in. Land on the meat. Don't argue with the ground." She was not looking at him. "And remember what it's for. It's not for winning. Vell said so and she's right. You find out what you do when there's nothing to look up. That's all."

"And if I don't find anything?"

Lira turned her head and looked at him at last.

"Then you'll have found that," she said. "That's still something. Go on."

The first bout was two young Blades, and it was quick, and nobody remembered it.

Then Vell stood up.

"Second bout." She did not need to wait for the yard to go quiet. It had been quiet since she stood. "Sarel. Bronze Rank 1, Blade Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Sarel came over the rope.

She came over without hurry, as she had on Tuesday and Thursday. She was taller than he had thought from the rope, long in the leg and lean all through, and the scaffold walker's ease was in everything she did. She walked to her mark and stood on it. She did not look at him. He saw her not look at him. She looked at the dirt just beside his left foot, and then at the dirt beside his right, as though she were measuring a floor for a carpet.

The blade came up along her right forearm, pale and straight.

"Begin," said Vell.

He watched her hips.

That was the first thing, and it was a mistake, and he knew it was a mistake even as he made it. He had decided on Thursday night that watching would not work and that he would watch anyway for one exchange, because he had to know, and because he did not have anything else. So he stood on his mark with his hands up and his eyes on her hips, and he waited for the thing she did before she went.

She went.

He saw her start. He was sure of that afterward. He saw her weight go forward, and he had time to think *now*, and to begin to move. Then she was in front of him, inside his reach, and the flat of her blade was coming down across his right shoulder. His mind arrived at that fact a long way behind his body. It landed like a plank dropped from a height, a broad flat slap that spun him and took his feet.

He fell.

He fell the way Lira had taught him for four mornings. He did not put his hand out. He tucked his chin and let his right hip have the ground, and rolled, and came up on one knee with dirt down his whole side. Sarel was already walking back to her mark.

Along the rope somebody laughed, not unkindly.

*First exchange,* he said to himself, in Lira's report voice. *She started and I saw her start. I didn't see her cross. I fell properly.*

That was all there was to report, so he got up.

In the second exchange he tried his one line.

*She doesn't look at you. She looks at the space next to you, where you're going to be.* He had written it on Thursday and looked at it for an hour. It meant that if he went where he would naturally go, she would be there first. So when she came, he would not go where he would naturally go. He would go the other way.

"Begin."

She looked at the dirt beside his left foot. She went. He saw her go, and he went the other way, right, away from where she was looking, as fast as he had ever moved.

She was there.

She had not been looking at the left at all, or she had, and had seen him begin to go right, and had simply gone right as well, faster. He did not know which. He would never know which. The flat came across his ribs on the right side, low, where Renn had put it on the first night, and it lifted him and put him down. He landed on his back across the groove with every bit of breath gone.

He lay there for a moment looking at the sky, which was white and very far away. He had fallen properly, he thought. Chin in. On the meat. He had not argued with the ground.

He got up. It took longer.

He stood on his mark with his ribs on fire and his shoulder throbbing, and he understood something, all at once and quite clearly. It was not the comfortable clarity of a figure coming right. It was the other kind, the kind that comes when you have pushed hard on a bracket and felt it give.

Everything he did was watching. It was the whole of him. He watched, and found the thing, and planned from the thing. It had beaten Dessa and the kiln boy and Renn, and it had kept him standing against everyone else. It was the oldest thing he owned, older than the Log, older than Ardenmere. Hesk had given it to him on a Tuesday in a community hall in Denvash. *The third exchange is where they tell you the truth.*

Sarel was not going to tell him the truth in the third exchange. She was going to tell it in the first, and the second, and every one after it. She was telling it now, and it was simply too fast to read. By the time he had read anything, it was already over.

The watching was too slow for her. Not wrong. Too slow.

"Gone," said Cael, standing on his mark.

He did not say it loudly. But the yard had gone quiet between the exchanges, the way it did for a Bronze, and he heard it carry. Along the rope, on the shaded side, Lira's head came up. Across the circle, for the first time, Sarel looked at him and not at the dirt beside him. Her eyebrows went up a very little.

"Begin," said Vell.

He did not watch her.

He did not know what else to do, so he did what Lira did. It was the thing she had been trying to teach him for two months and that he had nodded at and gone on not doing, morning after morning. He moved. He did not move to anywhere. He did not decide where he would be and then go there. He moved small and quick and all the time, a half step left, a quarter step back, a shift of the weight, a turn of the hip. He did not plan any of it. If he had stopped to plan it, it would have been a plan, and she would have read it in the space next to him before he had finished.

He did not know where he would be in half a breath. That was the whole point. If he did not know, there was nothing in the space next to him for her to read.

She came. He was somewhere else.

Not far. A hand's width, perhaps, no more. The flat of her blade went past his left ear with a hiss, close enough for him to feel the air of it on his cheek. He had not seen it coming. He had not dodged it. He had simply not been where it was going, because he had not known where he was going himself.

She came again, from the other side. He was somewhere else again. The edge of the flat brushed his sleeve, and he felt it, and it did not land.

The third time she did not come. She stood a pace off, with her blade out, and looked at him.

The yard had gone very quiet. Along the rope, in the third row of the benches, an old man said *huh*, out loud, to nobody.

"Called," said Vell. "End of the exchange. Nothing."

He went back to his mark. His legs were trembling. He had moved more in that one exchange than in the two before it together, and his body did not know how to do it. It was like trying to write with the wrong hand. Every step was a step he had to find, and he found them late, and badly. But they had been his, and she had not been able to read them, because there had been nothing in them to read.

*Third exchange,* he said to himself. *I stopped watching. I didn't plan. She didn't land.*

He looked across the circle. Sarel was standing on her mark with her blade out and her head a little on one side. She was looking at him the way Renn had looked at him in the third exchange of the first night. It was a narrow, careful look, the look of somebody who has found a thing on her bench that she did not put there.

Then she nodded, once, very slightly, to herself. It was not his nod. It was the nod of somebody who has solved something.

*She's learned it,* he thought. *She's already learned it.*

"Begin."

She came in, and she did not come in to strike.

She came in and stayed. She came close, very close, inside the reach of her own blade, and she did not try to land anything. She simply moved with him. When he took his half step left, she took hers. When he shifted back, she was there. She did not need to read where he was going. She only had to keep the space around him small, so small that there was nowhere for him to go that she was not already standing next to. She walked him like that, close, round the inside of the groove. She was so near that he could hear her breathing, which was easy, and smell the lime dust from the scaffolds on her shirt, and see the old white scar down her left forearm. She walked him, a step at a time, toward the long side of the circle, where the drain was. He felt the groove come up under his heel.

He had nowhere left to go but down.

He went down. He did not fall. He chose it. He dropped low and rolled away sideways along the groove, chin in, on the meat. It was the first time he had ever used a fall for anything but surviving one. He came up a stride and a half away, on one knee, with the flat of her blade arriving late across his left forearm as he rose. It landed on the old burn, on Renn's swelling, which had only just gone down.

The white came up behind his eyes. He did not let go of anything.

"Called," said Vell. "End of the exchange."

He got to his mark. He did not remember walking there.

*Fourth exchange. She learned it. She stopped trying to find me and made the place small. I went down on purpose.* He stopped. *My arm.*

The arm was bad. He could feel the fingers of his left hand, but he could not make them close. He held it against his chest, and breathed, and looked across at Sarel. She was looking at the arm, and then at his face, and her face did not do anything at all.

In the fifth he tried to hit her.

He did not plan that either. He had only one strike he owned, short and straight from the hip, and he had decided that if she came close again, as she had in the fourth, he would throw it. He would not choose a time. He would throw it the moment she was there, at whatever was in front of him, without stopping to see whether it was a good idea.

She came close. He threw it.

It was slow. He felt how slow it was as it went, the back foot driving and the shoulder following and the fist arriving last, every piece of it in the right order and every piece of it a breath behind her. She was not there when the fist arrived. But she had been there when it started, and his knuckles brushed the cloth of her sleeve on the way past. It was only cloth, the barest touch, the way a moth touches a lamp.

The rope heard it before he did. It made a sound, a short sharp intake all along the shaded side, as though forty people had all burned their fingers at once.

Then her flat came across his back, across the bruise Renn had left, from one shoulder blade to the other. He went down on his hands and stayed there for a moment, looking at the dirt between them, and could not find his breath anywhere.

He found it.

"Called," said Vell. "End of the exchange."

He got up. It took three tries. Lira was standing at the barrel with both hands on the rope, very white, and she was not biting her knuckle. She was saying something. He could see her mouth move. He could not hear it over the noise in his own ears.

He stood on his mark for the sixth exchange, and he knew it was the last, and he knew why. His legs had gone. They had gone in the fifth, somewhere between the strike and the ground, and they were not coming back today. He could still do the small quick moving. He could feel how to do it now. It was there in him, new and clumsy, the way a word is there in a language you have just started to learn. But he could do it only as fast as his legs would let him, and they would hardly let him at all.

"Begin."

Sarel came.

He moved. He moved small and quick and unplanned, as he had in the third, and for one breath, for two, he was somewhere else, and she was not there. On the third breath his legs were a half-step slow. It was a very small half step. It was all she needed.

The flat of her blade came across his ribs on the right side, exactly where she had put it in the second exchange. He was on his back across the groove before he had understood that he had been hit. The blade came down and rested on his collarbone, lightly, the way you would lay a ruler on a page.

"Called," said Vell. "Blade Path."

---

He lay on his back and looked at the white sky and breathed. It was the only thing he could think of to do.

The blade went out along Sarel's arm. Then she did a thing that he did not expect, and that he heard the rope not expect. She crouched down beside him in the dirt, low on her heels, with her forearms across her knees, the way the scaffold people sat to eat their lunch. Her face was level with his.

She looked at him for a while. She did not seem to be in any hurry about it. Up close she was older than he had thought, with lines at the corners of her eyes from the sun on the scaffolds, and a nick out of one ear.

"How old are you?" she said.

"Fourteen."

She nodded, as if that were a number she had suspected and wanted to have confirmed.

"Two," she said.

He did not understand.

"Two exchanges." She held up two fingers. "You tried the thing you know, twice, and it didn't work, and in the third you threw it away." She put the hand back on her knee. "You know what most people do, at your rating, against somebody quicker? They watch harder. They watch the whole bout. They're still watching when they're on their backs. They go home and tell everybody they nearly had it. Six exchanges of watching harder." She shook her head. "You quit in two. You said it out loud. I heard you."

"It wasn't enough. The other thing."

"No," she agreed. "It wasn't. It was the first time you'd ever done it. You can tell." She looked at his left arm, which he was holding on his chest. "It'll be better the tenth time. Or the hundredth. I don't know which. I'm not your teacher." She glanced toward the barrel, where Lira was. "She is, I'd guess."

"Yes."

"Good. She moves like that. You'll want to steal it off her." Sarel stood up, all in one easy movement, the way she did everything. She did not hold out her hand to pull him up. She looked down at him for a moment longer from her full height, and the look was plain and direct and not at all soft.

"That was a good bout," she said. "Don't ask me for another one. You'll want to. Don't."

She went to the table. Cael lay on the dirt a moment longer, because he could. Then he rolled onto his side, chin in, and got his knees under him, and got up.

---

Vell wrote for a long time.

He stood in front of the table and waited, as you waited, with his left arm against his chest and his ribs pulling at every breath. The pen went down one line, and then another, and another. It stopped. It went on. She did not look up.

"Result stands as called," she said at last. "Sarel, Bronze Rank 1, Blade Path, over unrated. Sixth exchange. Called." The pen moved once more. "Witnessed and entered."

Then she looked up.

"Six," said Vell.

It was what she had said after Amrit, and after Petra, every time one more. It was not the same, and he heard that it was not the same. She did not explain it. She went back to her book.

Lira was waiting at the barrel. She did not say anything. She held out his coat, and when he could not get his left arm into the sleeve she put the coat round his shoulders instead. They walked out through the gate in the wall together, slowly, with her on his right side, the rib side, without being asked.

She did not ask for a report until they were two streets away.

"What happened in the second?" she said. "Between the second and the third. On your mark. You said it."

"I said gone."

"I know what you said. I heard it at the barrel. Half the rope heard it." She was looking straight ahead. "What was gone?"

He walked for a while before he answered, because it was hard to say and his ribs did not like it.

"The watching," he said. "All of it. The whole method. Everything I do." He looked at the cobbles. "It's too slow for her. It's not wrong. It's just too slow. By the time I've seen anything, it's over."

Lira did not say anything for a long time.

"And the third?"

"I did what you do."

She stopped walking.

They were at the corner by the chalk board at the bottom of the yard lane, under a lantern somebody had just lit, and she stood there in its light and looked at him. Her face did something he had not seen it do before. It was not the morning brightness, or the quick smile, or the slow one. It was nearer to the look of somebody who has been shouting into a well for two months and has just heard the bottom answer.

"Badly," he said. "I did it badly."

"I know you did it badly. I watched every step of it." Her voice was not steady. "You did it, though."

"For one exchange."

"For one exchange," said Lira. "Against a Bronze. On your first try." She started walking again, quickly, so that he had to hurry, which hurt. "You're an idiot. Tomorrow you're doing nothing at all. Nothing. And the day after, we start on it properly."

---

He wrote it up that night on his cot, by the light of Yeni's lamp, because he could not sit on the low wall by the pump. He could not sit on anything hard. He wrote it at greater length than any entry in the Log, longer than Dessa, longer than either Renn.

*Ninth. Sarel, Bronze Rank 1, Blade. Lost, sixth exchange, called.*

*Claim: I can't find her tell. Evidence: two bouts watched, both eyes. A page with three crossings-out on it. Ruling: there probably is one. I'm too slow to see it.*

*Claim: watching is too slow for her. Evidence: first exchange, I saw her start and didn't see her cross. Second exchange, I went against my own habit and she was there anyway. Ruling: the method is fine against people slower than my eyes. She isn't slower than my eyes.*

*Third exchange, I stopped watching and moved without deciding. She didn't land. Fourth, she learned it and made the space small. Fifth, I touched her sleeve. Sixth, my legs went.*

He stopped there for a long time with the pencil.

*I need a second way,* he wrote. *For people I can't out-watch. Something that doesn't go through my eyes first. Lira has it. She doesn't decide and then move. She moves and the deciding is in it, somehow, all at once. I did a little of it today, badly, for one exchange.*

*I don't know if I can learn it from watching her. That's how I learn everything. But this might be the one thing watching can't teach, because it's the thing that isn't watching.*

*I don't know. Write that down: I don't know.*

He read it back. It was true all the way through. He turned the book over to the back and sat with it open, and he thought about the second way, and about Lira in the grey half, moving without deciding. He thought about the backhand going on past the place it should have stopped, on her right side, when she was tired. He thought about a report given in the dusk with a thing left out of it, and a morning, yesterday, when he had seen it again and eaten his bread.

He did not write any of that down. He told himself he was too tired. He blew out his small piece of the light by turning his face to the wall, and lay a long time with his ribs pulling and his arm on fire, and did not sleep for a while.

---

Lira made him do nothing on Monday, as she had said. He went to the mender in the afternoon and sorted washers one-handed, and the mender looked at his left arm and said nothing at all.

On Tuesday he could lift the arm to his shoulder. On Wednesday morning, in the grey half, she made him stand by the post and watch while she showed him what he had looked like against Sarel.

She did the first exchange. She stood on her mark with her feet too square and her hands up and her eyes very wide, fixed on an imaginary pair of hips, and she nodded to herself. The girls on the wall laughed so hard that one of them nearly went off it backward.

"You nodded," said Lira. "At a Bronze. You stood there and agreed with yourself, and she was already halfway across."

"I know."

"I know you know. I want you to see it." She did the second exchange. She was him going right, very fast, away from where she was looking, with his chin up and a look on his face of tremendous cleverness. Then she was him being hit, and she flung herself down sideways in the dirt so exactly the way he had gone down that he felt the bruise on his ribs throb in sympathy. The girls on the wall applauded.

She got up, filthy, and grinning.

"Now the third," she said, and stopped grinning. "The third I'll do properly. Because you did it properly, mostly, and you need to see what it looks like from out here."

She did the third.

She did his version of her moving, the small quick unplanned half steps. She did it badly on purpose, a little stiff, a little late, exactly as he had done it. Then she did it again, her own way, loose and quick, and the difference between the two was as plain as the difference between a hinge that swung true and one that caught at the halfway. Then she did it a third time, his way and her way at once. He could not have explained how she did it. He saw at last where his stiffness was. It was in the hips. He was moving his feet and keeping his hips square, as if they still had to be ready for a plan.

"There," he said.

"There." She stopped, breathing. "You see it?"

"I see it."

"Good." She put her hands on her knees for a moment. She had been going for most of an hour already, his way and her way and his way again, flinging herself in the dirt, and she was tired. He saw it in her shoulders. "One more. Then bread."

She did the step, for the end of it, as she always did. Two empty touches. The ground folding. The backhand coming round.

And her weight went on past it, onto her right foot, a hand's width too far. She caught herself with the foot down hard and wide, and for half a breath her whole right side was open to him, and then she was back.

Three times.

He did not let himself eat any bread first.

"Lira," he said.

She was already turning toward the post for it. She looked round.

"When you're tired," he said, "you go too far on your right. After the strike. You don't stop where the backhand stops. You go on past it, onto your right foot, a hand's width, maybe more. You have to put the foot down to catch yourself." He made himself keep going. "For half a breath your whole right side is open. Anybody quick could have it."

She stood very still by the post.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her. He did it the way she did it to him, slowly, from outside. He did the touches and the step, badly, and then the backhand, and he let his weight go on past the end of it, onto his right foot, too far. He caught himself with the foot down wide and stood there open, exactly the shape she had been a moment ago.

Lira looked at him standing there. She looked for a long time.

"I lost a bout like that," she said.

She said it quietly, and not to him.

"At the academy. At the end of a season, against a girl who wasn't as good as me. She wasn't. Everybody knew she wasn't." She was looking at the dirt. "It went long. Seven exchanges. In the seventh I went in on her right and she caught me coming out, on the side of the head, and I went down. And I lay there and I didn't know why. I went over it for weeks. I went over every step, and I couldn't find it, and in the end I decided she'd been lucky." Her voice went odd. "She wasn't lucky. Was she? I was tired. And I went too far on my right."

"I don't know. I wasn't there."

"No. But I was." She looked up at him, and her face was doing two things at once, and he watched them both. One was a kind of startled gladness, very bright, like somebody who has found a coin they lost a year ago in a coat they had stopped wearing. "Cael. That's—" She laughed, a short shaky sound. "Nobody's ever—that's *mine*. That's a thing about *me*. All this time I've been walking round with it, and nobody saw it. My instructor didn't see it. I didn't see it." She put her hand flat on her own right hip, as if to feel where it lived. "That's useful. That's really, actually useful. That's the most useful thing anybody's ever told me about my own feet."

He had known she would be glad. He had not known she would be glad like that.

"When I'm tired," said Lira, half to herself, already turning it over. "It's when I'm tired. All right. All right. I can work with tired." Then she looked up again. He saw the other thing come into her face, the second one, slower, the one that had been waiting under the first.

"You said *three*," she said. "Just now. Under your breath. When I did it."

He had not known he had said it out loud.

"How many times have you seen it?"

He thought about lying. He thought about it for the length of one breath, standing in the grey half with the chalk marks on the post beside him. There were thirty-odd *gone*s on one side and seven falls on the other, every one of them a thing he had done wrong and said out loud. He did not lie.

"Three. Saturday. Just now." He made himself finish it. "And once before that. At Brenna. The fourth exchange. You went in on her right and went too far, and she caught you on the side of the head."

Lira was very still.

"That was three weeks ago."

"Yes."

"You gave me a report." Her voice was quite level. "That night. In the lane. You gave me every exchange. You gave me the old men in the third row liking it." She was looking at him now, straight at him, with nothing in her face at all. "You didn't give me that."

"Once isn't a pattern," he said. "I didn't know if it was anything. I thought—"

"You *ruled*." She said it quietly. It was worse than if she had shouted. "I said no ruling. I said it twice. *Give it me after, like a report, I don't want a ruling.* And you stood at that rope and saw a thing about my fighting and decided it didn't count yet, and kept it." She took a breath. "Where did you put it? In your book. Did you write it down?"

He did not answer. He did not have to.

"In the back," said Lira. "You put my fight in the back of your book."

"I didn't write it anywhere. I kept it." He heard how that sounded. "I was going to tell you when I was sure."

"When you were sure." She said it after him, slowly, as if she were reading it off something. "That's what you do, isn't it. You keep it till you're sure. You keep everything till you're sure." She looked down at the post, and at the chalk, and back at him. "And Saturday. You saw it Saturday. That was twice. Twice is a pattern, in your book. I've heard you say it. And you ate your bread."

"I didn't want you carrying it into Sunday."

"That was my choice." Her voice went up, just a little, and she brought it down again at once. "It's my feet. My right side. My bout that I lost at the academy and never knew why. You don't get to decide when I'm ready to know about my own feet, Cael. Nobody does. Somebody decided things like that for me once, at a desk, and that's how I ended up on a wall in this district." She stopped.

He stood there in the grey half with nothing to say. It was all true. He had kept it until he was sure, and he had been sure on Saturday, and he had eaten his bread.

Lira looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the bread off the top of the post. She did not tear it. She held it in her hand as if she had forgotten what it was.

"Thank you for telling me," she said. "I mean that. It's the best thing you've ever given me, and I'm glad of it, and I'm going to use it." She put the bread down on the post again, whole. "I'm going now. I'll see you."

She did not say *same time tomorrow*.

She went out through the gate in the wall without looking back. He stood by the post in the grey half, with the bread beside him and the chalk marks under his hand, and listened to her quick feet go away down the lane until he could not hear them.

The girls on the wall had stopped laughing some time ago. One of them looked at him, and then looked away, as if she had seen something she was not supposed to.

He did not eat the bread. He stood there until the sun came over the east wall and there was no grey half left. He thought about a boy at a kitchen table in Denvash, two months and a lifetime ago, finding out that his grandfather had known for a few years. He thought about what his own face had done when he heard it.

He thought about four things on a page in the back of the Log that Hesk did not know.

Then he picked up the bread and went to the mender's, because it was Wednesday, and he did not know what else to do.

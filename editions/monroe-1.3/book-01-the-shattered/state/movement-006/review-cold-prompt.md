# Chapter 34 — Efficient

The frost had not gone off the yard when they came in through the gate, and the east wall's shadow lay across the dirt in its long grey bar with the post standing up in the middle of it like a man waiting for a cart.

Lira set the rest of the bread on top of the post. She did not take her coat off. Her right arm was still inside it, not in the sleeve, and she had stopped pretending that this was a choice.

"Go on, then," she said. "You've had it in your head since the board. Say it."

He said it. He had worked it out under the pear tree the evening before and again on the walk up, and it came out in order. Forty-two days. The Cinder House ran a card on Thursdays and on Sundays, and most Tuesdays in the afternoon. Vell would not put him in twice in a week, and his body would not let him even if she did. At the very most that made six bouts, and he thought it was more honest to say five. Each one had to count. Each one had to be against somebody the yard would talk about afterward, so that his name went into more mouths than it had been in before.

"So I want to fight," he said. "As often as she'll let me. I told her so."

"I know you did. She told me you did." Lira leaned her good shoulder against the post. "She said you stood in front of her table and asked for six weeks of bouts like a man asking for six weeks of bread."

"What did she say about it?"

"She said *hm*." Lira looked at him for a while, the long look she gave him sometimes even now, after seven weeks. It was not suspicion. It was more like a person reading a contract she has already signed, to see what exactly she has agreed to. "You've got five bouts in the book. One of them's a win. That isn't a foundation, Cael. That's a first course of stones."

"Then help me lay the rest faster."

"Faster." She said the word back to him as if she were weighing it in her hand. "All right. But we do it my way, and you don't argue with my way until it's failed you at least twice." She held up one finger of her good hand. "Faster doesn't mean reckless. It means efficient."

"What's the difference?"

"Reckless is fighting six times because six is the biggest number on the page. Efficient is fighting as many times as you can and still walk into the next one whole." She pushed herself off the post. "You can lose. You've lost four times and you're standing here. Losing's cheap if you learn something. What you can't do is get hurt in a way that costs you the bout after. Every morning from now on is for the next one. Every night the Log turns round before you sleep, not two days later when you've forgotten what your feet did." She looked at the bread on the post and did not pick it up yet. "And you don't go in against anybody I think will break you, whatever Vell says, and whatever you say. I'll tell you. You'll listen."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong carefully." She almost smiled. "That's the other half of efficient. I've watched people come up through that circle in a hurry. Most of them came up fast and went down faster, and then they weren't in the yard any more, and nobody could tell you where they'd gone." She stopped, and he saw her hear what she had just said, and what it sounded like to somebody who had sat under a pear tree last night working out who would notice if he went. "I don't mean like that."

"I know what you meant."

"Good." She took the bread off the post at last and tore it, one-handed, with her teeth, and gave him the larger half. "Eat. Then the deflection, slow, while I watch your left foot. It's still turning out on the back corner."

"I fixed it."

"You fixed it on Tuesday. It's Wednesday."

---

The mender was at his bench when Cael came down the row of workshops after noon, exactly as though nothing had happened on Monday and nobody had ever stood in his doorway apologizing.

The lantern woman next door had all thirteen of her broken lanterns out on a plank in the thin sun, and was warming her soldering iron between her palms and talking to it. The cooper was knocking a hoop down. The mender's own doorway had no sign over it, only the four words cut into the frame at the height of a man's shoulder and filled in with black paint, the way they had been on the first afternoon Cael ever stopped there.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE.*

He had read them perhaps fifty times. He had thought they were a joke at first, and then a warning to people who left things too long, and then simply the mender's way of answering the question every customer asked, *when will it be ready*. He read them again now on his way in, and they meant something else, and he did not stop to look at what.

"Wednesday," said the mender, without looking up.

"Wednesday."

There was a crate on the end of the bench. In it were forty or fifty door hinges, black with old paint and rust, which had come out of a house by the dyers' that was being pulled down. The mender wanted to know which of them still swung true. He did not want them cleaned, or oiled, or mended. He wanted them sorted into two heaps, one that could go into somebody's door tomorrow and one that would only ever be iron again.

Cael sat on the stool at the end of the bench and opened and shut hinges.

It was slow work and he liked it. You could not tell a true hinge by looking at it. Paint hid the pin, and rust hid the knuckle, and a hinge that looked like scrap would sometimes open as sweetly as a new one, while a hinge that looked clean would bind at the halfway and grind and stop. You had to hold it and work it, and feel for the place where it caught. He found after the first dozen that his hands knew before his eyes did. Something in the first quarter-turn told him which heap it was going in, before he had finished turning it, and he made himself finish turning it anyway, because he did not trust that yet.

He thought about the six weeks while his hands worked, because there was nothing else in his head that afternoon to think about.

A hinge that was still true would go into a door. Somebody would hang it and swing it and forget about it for twenty years. A hinge that was not would go on the scrap heap and be carried off to the smith by the bridge, and in a week nobody in the district would remember it had ever been a hinge. He did not like where the thought was going. He turned the next hinge over in his hands and worked it, and it caught at the halfway, and he put it on the scrap heap and did not think about it any more.

At the end of the afternoon there were two heaps on the bench, and the true one was smaller than the other. The mender came down the bench with his loupe pushed up on his forehead and picked a hinge out of each, one after the other, without any hurry. He worked them both. He put them back on their heaps exactly where they had been.

He looked at Cael for a moment. It was not the long look he gave a frame before he leaned on the lever. It was shorter than that, and plainer.

"Monday," he said.

"Monday," said Cael. "And Wednesday. For six weeks, at least."

The mender considered this, as if it had been a measurement.

"And after?"

"I don't know yet."

The mender nodded slowly, as he had nodded at *I don't know yet* once before, over a tray of keys. Then he put his loupe back in his eye and went back to his clock. Cael went out under the four words in the black paint, and he did not read them, and he heard them anyway all the way up the row.

---

Vell was at her table on Thursday morning before the yard had properly woken, with her tea and the ledger and her stool set square to the back wall, and she did not look up when he came across the dirt.

"Tuesday," she said. "The afternoon card."

He stopped in front of the table.

"Who?"

Vell turned the ledger a little toward the light and laid her finger on a line. He could not read it upside down at that distance, and she did not turn it far enough for him to try.

"There's a boy on tonight's card," she said. "Second bout. He's on again Sunday, the first, before the benches fill. Copper Rank 2, Ember Path. He works the kilns down by the brickworks, and he fights the way you'd expect from somebody who stands in front of a kiln all day." She took her finger off the page. "You'll have him on Tuesday if you still want him after you've watched him twice."

"Why him?"

"Because he's on the card twice before Tuesday, and you'll want to watch him." Vell picked up her tea. "And because you've already been taught Ember once. Amrit taught you. I'd like to see whether it stayed."

"Not because I asked."

"No," said Vell. "Not because you asked." She drank. "Go away. You're standing in my light."

He went to the rope that evening on the shaded side, by the barrel near the drain, with the Log open on his knee and the pencil going, and he watched the kiln boy fight.

He was about seventeen, broad through the shoulders and narrow at the hip, and his forearms were burned all over in small shiny patches, old and new together, the way a baker's hands get from the oven door. He came over the rope as if he were angry with it. When Vell said *begin* he went straight at the other fighter, a Force youth a little older than him, and Cael watched the air in front of his right hand go thick and wavering, the way air goes over a road in summer.

Then the boy stopped breathing.

It was not a long stop. It was the length of a swallow. His chest went still, and his mouth shut, and a half-beat later the heat came. Cael felt it from the rope, on his face, like an oven door opened across a kitchen, and the Force youth flinched sideways out of it and lost his footing on the groove.

*Shimmer,* Cael wrote. *Then the breath stops. Then the heat. Same as Amrit.*

He wrote it again after the second exchange, and after the third, and he made a mark beside it each time. Amrit had stopped his breath before the real heat two times in three, and Cael had had to learn to tell the third time apart, and had paid for it with a burned arm. This boy stopped his breath every time. He did not do it two times in three, or four in five. He did it every single time, as surely as a door creaks on the same hinge.

There was a second thing, which he saw in the fourth exchange and nearly did not believe. Before every strike the boy looked at the place he meant to hit. He did not glance there. He looked, the way you look at a nail before you bring the hammer down, and then he hit it.

The Force youth went down in the fourth. The kiln boy stood over him breathing hard, with the shimmer still going off his hand, and the crowd along the rope cheered him in the comfortable way people cheer a thing that has gone as it was meant to.

Cael looked at his page. It said *breath, every time* and *looks where he's going to hit*, and nothing else, because there was nothing else.

He did not like it. He sat with the Log on his knee while the next pair went over the rope, and he did not like it at all, and he could not have said why.

---

"Gone," said Lira.

"What?"

"That's what you say." She stepped back out of the box drill and stood in the grey half with her good hand on her hip. "Every time a plan dies. The moment it dies. You say it out loud, so I can hear it, and then you do something else."

It was Friday. Her shoulder had gone from purple to a colour like old mustard, and she had taken her arm out of her coat that morning for the first time, and she was using it carefully, as if it belonged to somebody who might want it back. He had been building a counter to her step since the end of the month before. This morning he had built it four times, and four times it had been wrong in the same way. He had seen the two touches and started back and out, and found her already inside, and gone on with the counter anyway, back and out, with her backhand stopped beside his ear while his forearm was still on its way up to a place she had left.

"I knew it was wrong," he said.

"I know you knew. I watched you know it." She tapped her own temple. "You saw me inside. You knew the counter was dead. And you finished it anyway, all the way, every step, as though you'd paid for it and you weren't going to waste it." She shook her head. "It's dead, Cael. You don't owe it anything. You don't get to keep a plan because it was a good plan five breaths ago."

"So I say *gone*."

"Out loud. Every time. Before you do the next thing."

"That's ridiculous."

"It's very ridiculous." She brought her hands up. "Do it anyway."

The first time, it came out of him as a mumble, half under his breath, a beat too late to be of any use, and she stopped and made him say it again. The second time he said it clearly, and felt his ears go hot. Along the top of the far wall the two girls who sat there in the mornings to watch the yard and eat whatever they had stolen from their mothers' kitchens put their heads together and laughed.

"Gone," said Cael, a third time, standing in the wrong place with his arm half up.

"*Gone*," said one of the girls on the wall, in a deep solemn voice, and the other one fell against her shoulder.

Lira did not laugh. She took a stub of chalk out of her pocket, made one short line on the side of the post where the canvas ended, and came back to her mark.

"That's three," she said.

By the end of the morning it was nine. He said it every time he finished building something and saw it fall down, which was nearly every time. Some of the times he said it before he had properly understood that the plan was dead, and then stood blinking while his body worked out that he was right. The word did not feel like a tool. It felt like a boy announcing his mistakes to a yard.

She did one more thing before the sun reached the post.

"Watch," she said, and stepped back, and became him.

She did it so exactly that for a moment he did not understand what he was looking at. She stood on her mark with her feet a little too square. She brought her hands up and looked across at an opponent who was not there, with her chin tucked and her eyes very wide and very serious. Then she nodded, slightly, to herself. It was a small private nod, the nod of somebody who has just solved something. Then, only then, a full breath later, she moved.

The girls on the wall had stopped laughing. They were watching her with their mouths open.

"Do I do that?" said Cael.

"The nod?" Lira dropped out of him and was herself again, all at once, loose and quick. "Every time. You see it, you work it out, and you agree with yourself. Then you go." She picked up the last of the bread. "Anybody who's watched you twice could time you by it. I could. Renn will have."

He thought about the private nod. He had not known it was there. He had felt the agreeing, the small click of a figure coming right, and he had not known it showed on the outside, where anybody standing in a circle could see it.

"Gone," he said, to nobody, to the nod.

Lira looked at him. Then she took out the chalk and made a tenth mark on the post.

"That one counts," she said. "That was a good one."

---

Sunday was the first of the new month, and Renn was not in the yard.

Cael had not expected him to be, exactly. He had expected him somewhere, though. He had looked for him at the fish steps that morning, among the men unloading, and at the market, and at the yard gate, the way you look for a coat you know on a hook. The river had come up again in the week. The barges that should have come down from the upriver farms on Friday had not come. Torvin's brothers had been at the table every night saying so, loudly, with their elbows on the boards.

He went to Vell's table before the benches filled.

"It's the turn of the month," he said.

"I know what day it is." She was ruling a fresh page. "He's not back. The river's holding everything above the weir. He could be three days, or ten. I don't keep a man's slot open for ten days on the chance of a river."

"So the hold's gone."

"The hold's gone." She drew the line to the edge of the page and lifted the ruler. "I said I'd hold him to the turn of the month. It's turned."

He stood there a moment longer.

"When he's back, I'd like to ask again."

"Then ask him." Vell blew on the ink. "Not me. I made you a promise once already and the river broke it. If you want Renn, you go and find Renn, and if he says yes, you both come to me, and I'll see." She looked up. "He'll come up the fish steps like everybody else, when it stops raining up the valley."

So he went and stood at the rope on the shaded side, near the barrel, and watched the kiln boy fight for the second time.

It was the first bout. The benches were still half empty and the pie boy had not yet got his tray going. The kiln boy was against a Blade this time, a tall girl of about sixteen with a long reach, and he went at her across the circle the same way he had gone at the Force youth on Thursday, angrily, as if the circle had insulted him. The shimmer came up off his hand, and his breath stopped, and the heat came.

Cael made a mark in the Log.

It happened again and again. The boy stopped his breath before every real heat and looked at every place he meant to hit. The tall girl worked out the second thing in the third exchange and moved her ribs away from where he was looking. The boy noticed nothing. He kept looking and kept hitting where he had looked. In the fifth exchange she slipped one of his looks and put the flat of her blade across the back of his neck, and Vell called it.

Lira had come up the rope while he was writing. She stood beside him with her arms folded on the top of it.

"That's two," she said.

"Every time. Both nights. He holds his breath, and he looks."

"Then he'll do it on Tuesday."

"I know." He looked at the page. "That's what's wrong with it."

She turned her head and looked at him properly.

"There's nothing wrong with it," she said. "He's seventeen. He's rated Copper 2 because that's what he is. Some people have a tell like a fire bell and nobody's ever told them." She looked back at the circle, where the tall girl was helping the kiln boy up and he was letting her, red in the face. "You're not used to easy. That's all. You've never had one."

Marrow came along the rope with his slate. He had not put the Tuesday card on it yet. He saw Cael reading the slate anyway, and smiled the good smile, and said nothing at all, which was not like him.

---

On Tuesday afternoon the yard was thin.

It was a working day, and cold. Along the rope there were perhaps forty people, most of them men from the yards and the river workshops on their way home early, and the girls from the wall, and the errand boys, and two of Brenna's friends, who had come because Lira had told somebody who had told somebody. Lira was at the barrel. She had both arms out of her coat now. She did not wave.

The kiln boy came over the rope as if it had insulted him.

"Copper Rank 2, Ember Path," said Vell, and gave his name, which Cael heard and wrote down later. "Against unrated. Vouched." She looked at her book. "Begin when you're ready."

The shimmer came up off the boy's right hand before he had left his mark. He looked at Cael's left shoulder, hard, the way you look at a nail. Then he came.

Cael watched his chest.

It went still. The mouth shut. Cael went right, away from the shoulder the boy had been looking at, a full step outside the reach of the hand, and the heat went past his left side like a door of air pushed open and slammed. It was hot on his ear. It was not hot on anything else.

The second exchange was the same. The boy looked at his ribs, held his breath, and struck where he had looked, and Cael was a step outside it. He did not strike back. He had planned not to. He wanted to see whether anything in the boy changed when he missed twice.

Nothing did.

The boy's face went darker. His breathing got louder at the top of every breath, but it still stopped dead before every heat, as if a hand had been put over his mouth. On his mark between the exchanges he glared across at Cael and rubbed the burned side of his right hand against his trouser leg.

In the third exchange Cael went in.

The boy looked at his head. His chest went still. Cael stepped left and forward on the stillness, before the heat. The heat went over his right shoulder and singed the air beside his collar, and he was inside it, close enough to smell the brick dust on the boy's shirt. His right hand came up from the hip, short and straight, the one strike he owned, the one Lira had made him throw at the post until his knuckles knew the canvas by heart. He stopped it with his knuckles touching the boy's jaw, just under the ear.

The kiln boy stood there with his breath still held, and the shimmer going out of his hand, and his eyes very wide.

"Called," said Vell. "Unrated."

It had taken perhaps three minutes.

The rope made a pleased noise, the ordinary kind, the noise a crowd makes for a thing that has gone as people thought it might. The kiln boy let his breath go at last, all at once, and looked at Cael's fist by his jaw, and then at Cael. He did not look angry any more. He looked mostly surprised, like somebody who has been told a thing about his own face by a stranger.

"How'd you know where I'd be?"

Cael took his hand away.

"You look," he said. "Before you hit. You look at where you're going to hit."

The boy stared at him.

"Everybody does that."

"No," said Cael. "Not everybody."

He walked to the table and stood in front of it, as you stood, win or lose, until she was finished with you.

"Result stands as called," said Vell, writing. "Unrated, over—" and she gave the kiln boy's name again, and then, "Copper Rank 2, Ember Path. Third exchange. Called." The pen went on for a line, and stopped, and did not go on. "Witnessed and entered."

That was all. There were no extra lines tonight, or none that she wrote while he was standing there. He had been listening for them without meaning to, the way you listen for a second knock, and he noticed that he had been listening and did not like it.

When he came off the dirt the errand boys were arguing about something, and the one in the red cap held his hand out flat, and the other one put something in it with a very bad grace. Marrow had paid out, by the look of him, and had paid out cheerfully, which meant he had taken more than he gave.

Lira met him at the barrel.

"Well," she said.

"It was easy."

"It was easy." She looked at him, and her face did not do anything. "And you don't trust it."

"No."

"Good." She handed him his coat. "Easy happens. Some days you get a boy who holds his breath and looks where he's going to hit, and it goes exactly the way you wrote it. It doesn't mean the hard ones have gone anywhere. They're still out there, waiting their turn. Don't start thinking you've got a method that works on people. You've got a method that worked on him."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway, so it's said." She bumped his good shoulder, which was both of them, today. "That's one."

He wrote it up at the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard before he went in, while the light went. It was the shortest entry in the Log.

*Seventh. The kiln boy, Cu 2, Ember. Claim: holds his breath before every heat; looks at where he means to hit. Evidence: two bouts watched, every time, both nights. Ruling: step outside the look on the stillness; in on the third. Won, third exchange, called.*

He sat for a moment with the pencil. Then he added, under the ruling, where he would see it:

*It worked because he had never been told. That's not the same as it working. Don't learn the wrong thing from this.*

He looked at the line for a long time, and did not cross it out, and went in to supper.

# Chapter 35 — The Second Look

The barges came down on Thursday morning, all of them at once, as though the river had been holding its breath above the weir for a week and had finally let it go.

Cael heard them before he saw them. He was at the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps with his letter for the west bag. A shout went up somewhere along the long quay, and then another, and then the particular racket of a great many men who have been idle for days and have suddenly been handed something heavy. By the time the clerk had read his address out loud to the whole hut, as he read every address, and hung the letter on the third hook, there were eleven barges along the quay and the planks were going down.

He walked along the river wall toward the noise. He told himself that he was walking that way because it was the long way to the yard, and that the long way was the way he went, and that Hesk had said not to change what he did.

Renn was on the fourth barge.

He was standing on the deck in a heavy oiled coat with the collar up, handing sacks down to a man on the quay, and he had a beard. It was not much of one, three weeks' worth and patchy along the jaw, but it made him look older and more tired, and a good deal more like the men around him. He was laughing at something the man on the quay had said. He swung a sack down, and turned for the next one, and saw Cael standing on the wall.

He stopped with the sack in his arms.

Then he grinned, slowly, all the way across his face, and set the sack down on the deck as carefully as if it were full of eggs.

"Third exchange," Renn called, across the water and the planks and three men carrying grain. "Called. A Copper 2 who holds his breath." He put his hand flat on his chest. "I heard it from a woman with a fish basket before we'd got a rope ashore. I want you to know I take full credit."

"You were a week up the river."

"I was there in spirit." Renn came down the plank with a sack on each shoulder, as easily as if they were coats, and dropped them on the stack and straightened up and looked at him. It was the craftsman's look, the one that checked a joint, and it went over Cael from his boots to his face and lingered for a moment on his right hand, where the Dessa knuckles had healed to thin pink lines. "You've grown. No, you haven't. You're standing differently." He frowned. "Who's been at your feet?"

"Lira."

"Course she has." He wiped his hands on his coat. "So. The month's gone."

"The turn of the month's gone. The river kept you. Vell says the hold's lapsed." Cael had worked out what he was going to say on the walk along the wall, and said it. "She says if I want you, I come and ask you, and if you say yes, we both go to her."

Renn looked at him for a long moment.

"You're asking me," he said.

"I'm asking you."

"Not her. Not through Lira. You." Renn seemed to find this interesting in itself, as if it were a part on his bench he had not expected. Then he shrugged his big shoulders. "I said I wanted to come back with something you hadn't seen. I've got it. I worked it out up the river, poling, which is a good place to work things out, because there's nothing else to do between one bend and the next except think and get wet." He picked up another sack from the stack without seeming to notice that he had. "Sunday?"

"If Vell says Sunday."

"She'll say Sunday. She likes it when people come to her table already agreed. It saves her the trouble of thinking we're fools." Renn hitched the sack higher. "Ten o'clock. I'll be at her table with my hair combed." He started back toward the plank, and then turned round, walking backward. "And you'll want to know where I'm training. Everybody'll want to know. I'll tell you now, so you don't have to creep about asking. The middle yard on the lane, tomorrow afternoon. Come and look, if you like. I'll be glad to see you there."

"Why?"

"Because it won't help you," said Renn, and went up the plank.

---

Vell said Sunday. She said it without looking up from the card she was writing, on the back of a bill for lamp oil. She wrote *Renn (Cu 3, Blade)* on the second line and *unrated (vouched)* under it, and blotted it, and held it out without a word to the pie boy, who took it away to the board at a run.

Renn had combed his hair. He had also shaved half his beard, and then stopped, and the effect was so strange that the pie boy looked at it twice on his way past.

"Second bout," said Vell. "Before the lamps. Don't be late."

"And not early either," said Renn, "or people'll think we're frightened."

Vell looked up at him then, for exactly one breath. It was not an unkind look. It was the look of a woman who has heard every joke a fighter could make at her table, and has kept most of them in a book.

"Go and finish your face," she said.

---

The middle yard on the lane was a square of dirt between two high walls, smaller than the Cinder House yard, with a chalk circle on it instead of a groove. The chalk had been walked half away. By the middle of Friday afternoon there were thirty people at its gate.

Cael had not expected that. He had expected a few of Renn's friends from the fish steps, and perhaps a girl from the wall. He had not expected the errand boys sitting on the top of the gate like crows, or Brenna's friends in a knot at one side, or two old men from the third row of the Sunday benches leaning on sticks with their scarves up over their mouths. There was a pie seller who was not the pie boy. There was a man selling hot chestnuts out of a pan. There was Marrow, very clean, at the back, with his slate under his arm and the chalk not out.

"It's because he told everybody," said Lira, at his shoulder. "On the fish steps, yesterday, all afternoon. *Come and see what I've got for the boy.*" She had her arms folded on the top of the wall and her chin on her arms. "He's a show-off. He's always been a show-off. It's half of why people like him."

"He told me it wouldn't help me."

"Then either he's lying, or he's right." She did not turn her head. "Which do you think?"

He did not answer. He took out the Log.

Renn was in the circle with one of his friends from the fish steps, a broad young man as big as he was, who had been given the job of being Cael. Cael worked that out in the first minute, and he felt his face go hot, and then he made himself stop minding and watch. The friend stood on his mark with his hands up and his chin tucked and his eyes very wide, looking down at Renn's feet. Somebody along the gate laughed and said *that's him to the life*, and the friend nodded to himself, slightly, solemnly, before he moved. The laugh got louder.

Lira did not laugh. She glanced at Cael sideways, once.

"Your nod," she said quietly. "He's put your nod in."

"I know."

"I didn't tell anybody about the nod."

"You didn't have to. He watched me for four exchanges." Cael wrote it down. *He has the nod.* "He watches too."

Renn did not use his blade. He fought the friend open-handed, with the flat of his palm where the flat of the blade would be, and he did the same thing over and over for most of an hour.

The first thing he did was come straight off his mark, with no amble and no probes. The friend had barely got his hands up before the first fold of the figure was on him, high, and the second came out of the end of it, low and across. It was the fourth-exchange figure from the first night, the one with no handshake in it at all, the one that had put Cael in the dirt. Renn was throwing it from the first breath. Every time, he threw it from the mark.

The second thing was the heel.

Renn came off the mark slower, the third time, and settled his weight into his back heel exactly the way he had settled it in the lamplight two months before. It was the spring pressing down, the frame loading. The friend went. He went the instant the heel settled, left and back, exactly as Cael had gone in the second exchange of their first bout. Renn did not strike. He stood there on his loaded heel with his hand still back and watched the friend land, and then he struck, once, flat, across the friend's shoulder as his feet came down.

The gate made a noise. The errand boys hissed through their teeth.

Renn did it again. He settled the heel and held it, and the friend jumped, and Renn waited for him to land and struck. He did it a third time, and the friend, who was not stupid, did not jump, and Renn struck off the heel at once, for real, so that the friend got the flat across his ribs instead.

"Either way," said Renn to the gate, breathing hard and grinning. "That's the beauty of it. If he goes when the heel goes, I get him landing. If he waits, I get him waiting." He wiped his face with his forearm. "And there won't be a third exchange this time. I've had his third exchange once. I don't want it again."

Somebody laughed. Somebody else called out to ask whether that meant Renn was frightened of the third, and Renn laughed too and said yes, terrified, and the friend punched him in the shoulder.

Cael wrote until his hand ached.

He wrote *from the mark, every time*. He wrote *the false heel* and drew a box round it. He wrote *wait or go, he takes either*. And then he stopped, with the pencil over the page, because he was watching something Renn did between the drills that Renn did not seem to know he was doing.

Each time Renn finished a drill and went back to his mark to begin another, he rolled his shoulders. He rolled the left and then the right, slowly, settling them. He did it once after the third drill, when he went from the figure to the false heel. He did it again when he went back. Each time he changed what he was going to do next, the shoulders went round first.

Cael had written it down two months ago, in his first entry, on a page he had later torn out and kept in his coat. *Shoulders when he changes his mind.* Renn had found Cael's nod. He had not, apparently, found his own shoulders.

"You've gone quiet," said Lira.

"He's right," said Cael. "It won't help me. Not the way he means."

She turned her head on her arms and looked at him.

"Knowing the heel can lie doesn't tell me when it's lying," he said. "If I watch it, I lose either way. He's built it so that watching the heel is the mistake." He looked at the page. "So I don't watch the heel."

"You've watched that heel for two months."

"I know." He shut the Log. "Gone."

Lira did not take out the chalk. There was no post here. But she looked at him for a long time with her chin on her arms, and the morning brightness came up in her face, there in the middle of a cold afternoon, at a gate full of people who had come to laugh at him.

---

They walked down the lane afterward in the dusk and he told her the rest, because it was not finished until he had said it to somebody who would argue.

"He throws everything from the mark," he said. "So everything starts in the same place and goes the same way, straight at me. The figure's built to go forward. It's very good going forward. So I don't stand where it's going. When Vell says *begin*, I go sideways, before the first fold."

"Which way?"

"Left. His left." He had worked that out at the gate. "The blade's on his right arm. If I go to his left, it has to come all the way across his body to find me. That's longer. And the figure has to turn to follow me, and anything that's built to go one way and has to turn—"

"Has to stop to turn." Lira finished it, and her voice had gone sharp and interested. "Even for half a breath. Even if he's very good."

"He is very good."

"He's very good," she agreed. "He'll turn it the first time. He'll turn it smoothly, and you'll get hit anyway."

"Probably."

"And the heel?"

"I don't look at it. I look at his shoulders." He told her about the roll, and what it had meant on the first night, and what it had meant at the gate. "He'll start with a plan. When it doesn't work, he'll change it. When he changes it, I'll see it, and I'll know something new is coming, even if I don't know what."

Lira walked for a while without saying anything.

"And when do you hit him?"

"Not till he's tired. He said there wouldn't be a third exchange. He's built a plan that ends early." Cael looked at his hands. "A plan that's built to end in two costs a great deal to keep going for five."

"And if he isn't tired? If it's the fifth and he's still fresh and you've got nothing?"

He had thought about that too, at the gate, and had not got an answer that he liked. He said the only one he had.

"Then I'll say gone, and do something else."

Lira laughed, short and real, the one that surprised her.

"That's not a plan. That's a promise to have another plan." She stopped at the well with the iron cover, where she always stopped, and looked at him in the last of the light. "It's not bad, though. The sideways is good. You stole that from me."

"I watched it on Sunday."

"I know. Every bit of it." She looked down at the four lanes. "Steal the rest of it too, if it's any use to you. That's what it's for." She turned to go, and then turned back. "And Cael. If he catches you on the turn, it's going to be across the back. Don't be surprised by that. Be ready for it."

---

On Saturday, in the grey half, she was Renn.

She did it so well that the girls on the wall stopped eating to watch. She had his stroll and his rolled shoulders and his big careless hands, and a way of sticking her chest out that was not quite Renn but was so exactly what it felt like to stand across from Renn that Cael laughed out loud. Then she came straight off her mark at him with no amble and no probes, open-handed, and he went sideways, left, and her palm caught him across the back of the neck as he went.

"Turned it," she said. "Again."

He went sideways again. She turned it again, and the palm landed across his shoulder blades.

"You're going too late. You're waiting to see me start." She went back to her mark and rolled her shoulders, Renn's way. "Don't wait. He's not giving you anything to wait for. Go on the word."

They did it thirty times. By the twentieth he was going on Vell's word, said by Lira in Vell's voice, which was nearly as good as her Renn. By the twenty-fifth her palm was landing on his arm instead of his back, because the turn was costing her. By the thirtieth she was breathing hard and he was not, and she noticed it, and stopped.

"There," she said. "That."

She did the false heel for him twice, settling into her back foot with her hand drawn back and holding it. Both times he felt his whole body lean toward going, the old way, the way he had learned in the lamplight, and both times he made himself look at her shoulders and not her feet. The second time, while he was looking at her shoulders, her palm came across his ribs.

"Gone," he said.

She made a mark on the post with her chalk. There were twenty-six now, in rows of five.

"The heel's in you," she said. "Deep. That's what two months of watching it does. He knows that. It's why he built the whole thing on it." She wiped her face on her sleeve. "You'll feel it go tomorrow. Let it go. Don't fight it. Just don't let your feet follow it."

The old man was standing in his gate at the top of the lane when Cael walked up past the yards at noon. He was sleeves rolled, in the stance that looked like nothing else in the lane, watching the woman with the shoulder work through her six positions. He did not look round. He did not need to.

"Tomorrow?" he said.

"Renn."

The old man said nothing for a while. The woman with the shoulder reached her fourth position and held it, and the old man watched her hold it, and nodded once.

"He's been up the river," he said. "Three weeks. Poling." He shifted his weight from one foot to the other, very slightly. "That's arms and back. It isn't legs."

Then he went back to watching the woman with the shoulder, and Cael understood that he had been given everything that was coming.

---

There was a letter for him at Torvin's when he came in, propped against the salt crock on the kitchen table where Torvin's wife put things she considered important. It was Hesk's square hand on the outside. It was the answer to the Dessa letter, which meant that it had been written a week ago, at the table at Fen Street, on the night the Dessa letter arrived.

He read it on the low wall by the pump, before supper, in the last of the light.

Most of it was the bench, and Ressa's hinge, which had stopped squeaking at last, and the survey office's young men, who had dropped another level. Some of it was the guild. The review had been given a date to sit, *the second week of next month*, which was this week, or the one after, depending on which day Hesk had counted from. He wrote that he would go in his good coat and answer what they asked, and that Cael was not to spend a single night on it.

Then there was a line on its own near the bottom, set a little apart from the rest.

*You told me how you built it, one piece at a time. Keep that. A win you built is the only kind that teaches you anything. The other kind only teaches you to want another.*

He read that three times. Hesk could not know that there had been another kind on that night too, in the fourth exchange, going across. Cael had not written it. But the line sat on the page exactly over the place where he had not written it, the way a level's bubble sits over the one low corner of a bench, and he could not stop seeing it there. Then he folded the letter and put it in the leather book and sat on the wall in the dusk with the pear tree over him. He thought about a man in a good coat walking into a guild hall in Denvash this week, or next, to answer questions about a boy who had gone east. He could do nothing about it at all from here, and it would be a week before he knew how it had gone.

"Supper," called Torvin's wife from the door. "And the boots woman says she's got a bun on Renn, and I've got a bun on you, so you'd better eat something."

---

On Sunday the yard was full by the middle of the afternoon.

The benches were out along the shaded wall and the third row was full of old men in scarves. The rope was three deep on the shaded side and two deep in the sun. Renn's friends from the fish steps had come in a crowd and taken the sunny side by the gate, where they made the noise of men who had spent three weeks on a barge and had been paid on Thursday. The man who had been Cael in the middle yard was among them, with a bruise on his shoulder that he showed to people.

Marrow's slate said *Renn* with a short price and *unrated (vouched)* with a long one, and under them, *Over/under: 3 exch.*

"He's taken Renn at his word," said Lira, at the barrel. "No third exchange." She had her arms folded on the top of the rope and her hair tied the ordinary way, with the knot coming loose. "Everybody's heard him say it."

"So has Renn."

"So has Renn," she agreed. "And now he's got to do it in front of all of them." She looked across the circle at the sunny side, where Renn was standing among his friends with his coat off and his sleeves pushed up, laughing at something and rolling his shoulders. "That's the trouble with showing off. You've said it. Now it has to be true."

The first bout was two Force fighters, women of about twenty, and it went to the fourth exchange and was won by the smaller of them, and the old men in the third row approved of it. Cael did not watch it. He stood at the rope and breathed the way he breathed at his mark, in and out, four and four, the way he had seen Dessa do it, and found that it helped.

Then Vell stood up behind her table.

"Second bout."

The yard went lower. Along the sunny side, Renn's friends began to bang on the top of the rope with their open hands.

"Renn. Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

He went over the rope.

Renn came over the other side of it, and as he walked to his mark the blade came up out of nothing along his right forearm, pale and steady and straight, with its thin bright line along one edge. Cael's eye told him it stopped a hand past the knuckles. He told himself *plus eight* and saw it then, the real length, a long way further out than his eye wanted to believe.

Renn stood on his mark and rolled his shoulders, left and right.

Cael watched them go round, and did not look at the heel at all.

"Begin."

Renn came off the mark with the figure, and Cael was already going.

He went on the word, as Lira had made him go thirty times in the grey half, before the first fold had started. He went left along the inside of the groove, toward Renn's left side, away from the blade arm. The first fold came high through the place where he had been standing and cut nothing. The second came out of the end of it, low and across, and it was aimed at where he had been too. Then Renn turned it.

He turned it smoothly, as Lira had said he would. The figure bent round after Cael like a river bending round a stone, and the third fold came across low and late. Cael saw it coming and could not get all of himself out of its way, and the flat caught him across the outside of the left forearm, over the old burn, with a crack like a strap brought down on a table.

It was not the shoulder this time. It was the arm. The burn under it lit up white from wrist to elbow, and he heard the sunny side roar, and he kept his feet and kept going, left, round the curve, until Vell called the break.

He went back to his mark with his left arm hanging and the fingers of that hand not quite closing.

*First exchange,* he said to himself, in Lira's report voice. *No probes. All from the mark. He turned it. He turned it well.*

And under that, which was not for any report: *The turn cost him something. His feet were wide at the end. He had to put the right one down hard to come round.*

Across the circle Renn was breathing easily. He grinned at Cael, and lifted his blade arm an inch, the way a man lifts a cup to somebody across a room.

"Begin."

Renn came off the mark slower.

Cael felt the change in it before he saw it, and his eyes went where two months had trained them to go, down to the back foot. The heel went back. It settled. It was the spring pressing down, the frame gathering, exactly as it had been in the lamplight.

And his whole body leaned to go.

He felt it happen. It was not a decision. It was older than a decision, a groove worn so deep in him that his weight was already tipping before his mind had finished seeing the heel, left and back, the way he had gone in the second exchange of the first night. His left foot came up off the dirt to carry him.

*He's built it so that watching the heel is the mistake.*

"Gone," said Cael, out loud.

He put the foot down where it had been.

It was not graceful. It was a stumble that did not quite happen, half a step begun and taken back, and it left him standing flat-footed and off balance in the middle of the circle with Renn's weight still sitting on the heel, waiting for him to land.

He had not jumped, so there was nothing to land. Renn struck anyway, off the heel, because a loaded heel has to go somewhere. It was a real strike now and not a waiting one, and Cael was flat-footed under it. He got his right arm up, and the flat came down across the meat of his right thigh instead, hard. His leg buckled and he went down on one knee in the dirt, and came up again before he had properly understood that he had gone down.

The sunny side roared again.

Renn did not follow it up. He stood a pace and a half away with his blade still out and looked at Cael, and his face had a puzzled crease between the eyes. He had heard the word. Cael saw him hear it and not know what to do with it, as you would not know what to do if a man you were fighting stopped to tell you the time.

"Called," said Vell. "End of the exchange."

Cael limped to his mark. The thigh was a deep hot ache, as if somebody had driven a post into it, and the forearm was worse. He stood on his mark and made himself breathe, four and four.

The heel was still in him. He could feel it, like a stone in a shoe. But he had felt it go, and let it go, and his feet had not gone with it, and he was standing.

Renn stood on his own mark for a long moment, looking at him.

Then he rolled his shoulders, left and right.

*He's changing it,* said Cael, to himself. *Something new.*

"Begin."

Renn settled the heel again. Cael did not move. He watched Renn's shoulders, and the shoulders did nothing, and the heel sat there loaded, and the yard went quiet. Nobody struck. Two seconds went by, then three. In the third row an old man coughed.

Then Renn dropped.

It was the sweep. It was the answer he kept for readers, the whole heavy body going low and swinging round, leg first, with the blade arm flung out high as a counterweight and the pale edge drawing a line up into the air. It was meant for a boy who had learned not to jump for the heel, and who would be standing still, flat-footed, his ankles exactly where the leg went looking for them.

Cael had seen the shoulders go round. He did not know what was coming, but he knew something was, and he had decided at the gate on Friday that whatever it was, he would not be standing where it expected him to stand. He did not jump. He went back. He took two quick steps straight back toward his own mark, out of the whole reach of the swing, and the leg went through the empty place where his ankles had been and found nothing.

Renn came up out of the sweep in one smooth motion and stood there, breathing.

He was breathing harder now. Cael saw it in his chest. It was nothing yet, only a little more, a breath that came from lower down. But the sweep was a heavy thing to throw at nothing, and it was the third heavy thing he had thrown in three exchanges, and it had not been the plan.

The sunny side had gone a little quiet. Somebody along the rope said *three*, and somebody else told him to be quiet.

"Called," said Vell. "End of the exchange. Nothing."

That was the third exchange. Cael went back to his mark and did not look at Marrow's slate, and did not need to. He could hear Marrow's chalk squeak somewhere behind the crowd.

Renn stood on his mark with his blade out and his hand on his hip, and looked at Cael, and the grin had gone. It had not gone into anything angry. It had gone into the narrow, careful attention of the first night, the third exchange, the tradesman holding up a part off his own lathe that was not the shape he had cut.

He did not roll his shoulders this time. He set his feet. Cael saw him look, not at Cael, but at the place on Cael's left where Cael had gone in the first exchange.

*He's learned the sideways,* Cael thought. *He's going to throw it where I'll be.*

"Begin."

Renn came off the mark with the figure. He did not throw it at Cael. He threw it at the left, at the empty place on the inside of the groove where a boy who went sideways would be in half a breath. It was very well done. If Cael had gone left on the word, he would have run straight into the first fold.

He did not go left. He went right, toward the blade.

It was the wrong way and he knew it was the wrong way. It was the side of the arm with the eight inches on it, and the only reason to go there was that nobody would. The first fold went past him on the left, into the place where he was not, and Renn's whole weight went with it. The figure had been thrown to go one way with everything behind it. To come back for Cael it had to stop, and turn the other way, against itself, across his own body.

Renn stopped it. He turned it. He was very strong in the arms, and he dragged the whole figure back round by main force. The second fold came at Cael late and high, and caught him, as Lira had said it would, across the back. The flat went from one shoulder blade to the other with a sound like a door slammed in a stone passage.

He went forward onto his hands. The dirt came up into his face. He felt the whole yard shout above him at once, Renn's friends and Brenna's friends and the old men, and for a moment he could not find his breath anywhere.

He found it. He got his feet under him and stood.

"Called," said Vell, very level. "End of the exchange."

He did not know how he got back to his mark. He was there, and the yard was very loud, and his back was a sheet of heat from one side to the other. His thigh was a post and his forearm was a bar of white fire. Across the circle Lira was standing at the barrel with both hands on the rope and her knuckles pale, and she was not biting them. She was looking straight at him with no expression at all.

He looked at Renn.

Renn was breathing hard now. It was not a little harder. It was hard, through the mouth, with his shoulders going up and down. Cael watched him and saw what dragging a figure back against itself had cost him, the whole heavy weight of it hauled round by the arms. It was the arms and back that three weeks of poling had made, and the legs that it had not. Renn's right foot, the front foot, was set wide on the dirt, and it was carrying everything. He had put it down there to haul the turn round, and he had not brought his weight back off it yet, and his heel was a finger off the ground.

The front heel. Not the back one. The front.

He stood on his mark with his back on fire and looked at that heel, a finger off the dirt with all of Renn on the ball of the foot above it, and he understood that this was the thing. It was not a tell, or a lie, or a plan. It was only what four exchanges of hauling had done to a big man's legs, and it was there because Renn had built a plan to end in two, and was in his fifth.

"Begin," said Vell.

Renn rolled his shoulders.

Cael saw them go round, and knew that something new was coming, and did not wait to find out what.

He went straight in.

He did not go left, or right. He went forward, on the word, straight at the middle of Renn, into the one place no sensible person went against a Blade. A blade was only dangerous at its length. Inside its length, close in, it was a forearm with something pale stuck to it, and a forearm was only a forearm.

Renn's new thing was the figure again, from the mark, thrown high and across at the place where Cael would have been if he had stood. Cael was not standing. He was under it, inside it, so close that the first fold went over his head and behind him. The blade did not touch him. The forearm under it came down across the top of his left shoulder, heavy, a bone on a bone. He felt it shove him down and kept going.

He was inside. He was chest to chest with Renn, close enough to smell the river on his shirt, and Renn's whole weight was still up on the ball of his front foot, where the figure had thrown it.

Cael put his left foot behind Renn's front heel.

He did not kick it. He set his foot there, on the dirt, behind the heel that was a finger off the ground, as you might set a wedge behind a wheel. In the same breath his right hand came up from the hip, short and straight, with the back foot driving and the shoulder following and the fist arriving last. It was the one strike he owned. He did not throw it at Renn's face, or his ribs. He threw it at the middle of his chest, flat, and not hard, because it did not need to be hard.

Renn's weight was going forward, into the figure. The strike pushed it back. It went back onto a heel that could not come down because there was a foot behind it.

He went over.

He went the way a tall stack of sacks goes when you take the bottom one out, slowly at first and then all at once. His arms flew out. The blade went up into the air with its bright edge flashing, and he came down flat on his back across the groove with a sound that went out of him in one great *hah*. Cael went down with him, onto one knee beside him, because there was nowhere else for his legs to go, and his right fist went up over Renn's face, and stopped there.

Renn lay on his back in the dirt and looked up at the fist.

The blade went out along his forearm, like a breath let go.

"Called," said Vell. "Unrated."

---

For a moment the whole yard was quiet.

Then the old men in the third row began to stamp their feet on the boards, all together, slowly. The shaded side took it up with its hands, and then, after a long breath, the sunny side, because Renn's friends were river people and they had seen what they had seen. Up on the wall one of the girls was standing with both arms in the air. The errand boys were shouting at each other.

Cael took his fist away from Renn's face. He found that he could not get up. He stayed on one knee with his hand on the ground, breathing.

Renn lay there.

He lay on his back across the groove with his arms out and his chest going up and down, looking at the sky. Then he began to laugh. It started low, somewhere in his ribs, and came up through him until he was shaking with it, flat in the dirt in front of the whole yard, and he did not seem to mind any of it.

"The front," he said, when he could. "You took the front one."

"You were standing on it."

"I know I was standing on it." Renn wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist. "Five exchanges. I said there wouldn't be a third." He lay there a moment longer. "I let you have that, you know."

"You didn't."

"I know I didn't." He rolled his head on the dirt and looked at Cael, and his face was perfectly serious, and his eyes were not. "Tell everybody I did. Tell them I took one look at your poor face in the second exchange and my heart went soft." He held up his hand. "Pull me up. My legs have stopped."

Cael got to his feet. It took two tries. Then he got hold of Renn's hand and pulled, and Renn came up off the dirt, which took both of them, and stood swaying a little, with his free hand on his thigh. They touched fists. Renn's hand was shaking. So was Cael's.

"I came back for a second look," said Renn, quietly, under the noise. "I wanted to see the third exchange again. What you did that night." He shook his head. "I had a plan for everything you did. Every single thing. I had a plan for the heel and a plan for the sideways and a plan for you standing still." He looked down at his own front foot. "I didn't have a plan for you having a plan."

"You told everybody yours."

"I did, didn't I." Renn laughed again, shorter. "I was so pleased with it." He looked at Cael for a long moment, the craftsman's look, the one that checked a joint. "You said *gone*. In the second. What's gone?"

"The heel."

Renn thought about that.

"I'm keeping that," he said. "I don't know what it means, but I'm keeping it." He clapped Cael on the shoulder, on the left one, the one the forearm had come down on, and Cael's knees nearly went, and Renn saw it and said, "Sorry. Sorry. Everything hurts, doesn't it? Good." And he walked off toward his friends on the sunny side with his arms up, calling out to them before he was halfway there, "I *let* him. I want that understood. I let him."

His friends shouted him down. One of them threw a hat.

Cael walked to the table.

He stood in front of it and waited, as you waited, while Vell wrote. The pen went on for one line, and then a second, and did not stop.

"Result stands as called," said Vell. "Unrated, over Renn, Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Fifth exchange. Called." The pen went on. "Witnessed and entered."

She did not look up until she had finished. When she did, it was only for a breath.

"You said a word in the second exchange," she said.

"Yes."

"Out loud. In my circle."

"Yes."

# Chapter 36 — A Week Behind

Hesk read the letter for the first time standing up, in the workshop doorway, with the runner's footsteps still going away down Fen Street and the cold coming in round his legs.

He read it fast, the way you look at a gauge to see whether the needle is in the red before you look at what number it is pointing to. *I won tonight.* That was the needle. Everything after it was the number, and he could take his time over the number, so he shut the door and went to the bench and did.

He read it the second time sitting at the bench with Ressa's oven hinge in the vice in front of him and a fine file in his right hand. It was how he read the things he wanted to think about. If his hands were busy, his head did not run off in front of the words and decide what they meant before it had got to the end of them. He filed a stroke and read a line. He filed another stroke and read the next.

*A woman called Dessa, Copper Rank 5 in the book here, Stone Path, the best defender in the yard.*

He had never heard of Dessa. He had never heard of the yard. He knew the Cinder House only as two words in a boy's handwriting, and a book kept by a woman whose name he had been told once and had written down on the back of an envelope in case he forgot it. He had never seen any of it. And yet he found that he could see it perfectly well. He could see a defender who won by standing still. He could see a boy sitting on a bench watching her twice, a week apart, with a pencil going. The boy would have his chin tucked and his eyes very wide, and he would be writing down the thing she did not know she did.

*The way you taught me to watch a stance.*

Hesk put the file down for a moment.

Then he picked it up again, because the hinge did not care what he felt, and that was the great comfort of hinges.

He read it a third time, slowly, at the kitchen table, with the lamp lit and his supper going cold beside it. And on the third reading he noticed what he had not noticed on the first two, because he had been too glad.

*In the second exchange tonight I made her set herself so many times, on my count and not hers, that it came early. In the third I went into it.*

And then: *She said afterward that it was the cleanest read anyone had made on her.*

There was nothing between them. The boy had gone into it in the third, and then he had won, and the letter did not say whether the winning had happened in the third or somewhere after it. It did not say how. A boy who wrote down the angle of a gate guard's thumb on a card would not leave out the exchange in which he had won his first fight. Not unless he meant to.

Hesk had spent thirty years reading load sheets that other men had filled in. He knew what a column looked like when a figure had been left out of it. It did not look empty. It looked very slightly too neat.

He sat with that for a while, with the lamp hissing.

He did not get angry, though he looked for the anger, because he thought he might be owed some. What he found instead was something older and more tired, and it had his own face. He had kept a great many things in a great many neat columns for a great many years. He had kept Tuesdays. He had kept the provisions, and the preliminary assessment, and a file he had once seen opened in a room above his own clearance. He had kept them from the boy until the boy found them out for himself and came to him angry, at this table, on the second morning after Kindling. *A few years,* he had said then. He remembered the boy's face.

If there was a figure missing from this letter, it was missing for a reason, and the reason was the boy's. He would tell it when it was worth telling, or he would not. Either way it was not Hesk's to go and dig out of a letter with a file.

He wrote his answer that night, at the same table.

He wrote about the bench, because the boy had asked for ordinary things and ordinary things were what he had. He wrote that Ressa's hinge had stopped squeaking at last, and that she had come round to the workshop specially to tell him so, and then stood in the doorway for some time saying nothing in particular in the way of a person who has come to ask after somebody and does not like to. He wrote about the survey office's young men, who had dropped another level off another ladder. He wrote that the guild had given his review a date to sit, the second week of next month, and that he would go in his good coat and answer what they asked. He told the boy not to spend a single night on it, though he knew perfectly well that the boy would spend several.

Then he sat with the pen lifted for a long time over the last part of the page.

*You told me how you built it, one piece at a time. Keep that. A win you built is the only kind that teaches you anything. The other kind only teaches you to want another.*

He read it back. It did not ask about anything. It did not point at any column. It only said what was true, and left room beside it for whatever else might be true as well.

He signed it *— H.* and sealed it, and put it on the shelf by the door for the morning, propped against the guild's letter, which had stood against the clock on that shelf since the second morning after the depot with *Routine* written across its corner in somebody else's hand.

Joren came three afternoons after that, and he did not walk past.

He had walked past every day for two months. Hesk had seen him do it from the bench, through the window over the vice. Joren came along Fen Street from the direction of his family's yard, flat-footed, with his heels set down hard at every step as though he were trying to press them into the cobbles. He looked straight ahead as he went by the workshop. Then he turned his head at the last moment, every time, and looked in. Then he looked away again, very quickly, as if he had been caught at something. Hesk had never once let him see that he had seen.

This afternoon he stopped in the doorway.

"Afternoon," said Hesk, not looking up from the bench.

"Afternoon." Joren stood there. He had grown since the autumn, or else he was standing differently. He was a big boy, broad like his father, and he had always filled doorways. Now he seemed to be trying to fill this one carefully, without leaning on the frame. "Has he written?"

"Every week."

"Is he—" Joren stopped, and seemed to look for a better word, and not to find one. "Is he all right?"

Hesk put down the file and considered how to answer. It was not that he did not know. It was that he wanted the answer to be the true one and the right size for a boy in a doorway, and those were not always the same.

"He's lodging at a house with a sign whose O leans," he said. "He pays on time. He works two afternoons a week for a mender who doesn't talk in the mornings. He's got a friend who's teaching him to use his feet." He picked the file up again. "And he won a fight, the week before last."

Joren's whole face changed.

"A *fight*?" It was nearly a shout. "Cael? In a ring? Against somebody?"

"Against a woman who was very good at standing still, I gather. He watched her for two weeks and then found the thing she did when she was tired."

Joren stared at him. Then he began to laugh, helplessly, holding on to the doorframe after all.

"That's him," he said. "That's exactly him. Two weeks. He'd have had a notebook." He wiped his eyes. "He'd have had a *chart*."

"I expect he did."

Joren stood in the doorway grinning, and then the grin went slowly, as if he had remembered something, and he looked down at his own feet on the step.

There was a word between them. Hesk knew Joren knew it; the boy had written it in a note and put the note in the gap in the wall where they had always left things for each other, the morning he left. Joren knew Hesk knew he knew it. Neither of them said it. It sat in the doorway between them like a third person who had not been introduced, and Hesk let it sit there, because it was not his to say to anybody, and the one person whose it was had chosen who to say it to.

He did not say *summons* either. The summons was not in any letter yet. There was nothing yet to say.

"I'm practising," said Joren, to his feet. "Patience. Like you said. Every morning, in the yard behind ours. I stand." He looked up. "With the stance. It's the only thing it does, standing. I thought if that's the only thing it does, I'd better be the best at it there is." He made a face. "It's very boring."

"It's meant to be."

"My father says I'm wasting my mornings."

"Your father," said Hesk, "has never stood still in his life, and has never been able to finish anything that needed it." He got up from the bench. "Come here a minute."

He had a level on the bench for the survey office, the fourth, with its glass set in its frame and the cement not yet cured. It had to be held exactly upright, without the smallest tremor, while the cement went off, and that took the best part of an hour. He had been going to rig a clamp for it. He gave it to Joren instead, and showed him how to hold it, and where to set his feet.

"Don't move it," he said. "Not a hair. If the bubble moves, the cement sets crooked and some young man drops off a roof because his level lied to him."

Joren took it as though it were made of eggshell.

Hesk felt the stance come into the boy before he saw it. There was a kind of settling in the floorboards, very faint, as if the workshop had become a little heavier on one side. Joren's heels went down. His knees softened. His whole body seemed to go quiet from the feet upward, and the little bubble in the glass, which had been trembling, stopped.

Joren held it for fifty minutes without a word. Hesk worked at the bench and did not watch him, and watched him the whole time.

At the end of it Joren set the level down on the bench as gently as a sleeping child, and stood rubbing his arms, which had gone dead, and looked as proud as Hesk had ever seen him look of anything.

"Tell him," he said at the door. "When you write. Tell him my heels are down."

"I'll tell him."

"And tell him—" Joren stopped again. "No. Just that." He went off up Fen Street toward his family's yard, flat-footed, his heels set hard into the cobbles at every step. He did not look back, because he did not need to look in at anything now.

He answered the boy's letter that night, at the kitchen table, with the lamp close and his bound hand stiff on the paper.

It was the hardest letter he had written since the first one. He knew what he wanted to say. It was choosing what to leave out that took him three sheets.

He put in the review. He put it in plainly: that it had sat, that they had asked whether he wrote to the boy and he had told them he did, every week, and meant to go on. He put in that they had found no present cause, and that the matter would remain on the book, and what *on the book* meant. He put in the gauge-maker and the drawer of springs, because the boy would like it.

He put in that he had read the citation three times and checked it as well as he could from Denvash with no handbook, and that it held, as far as he could see, as it had for the boy. He put in that he was proud of the reading, and of the four times aloud. That was the truest thing in the letter, and he left it short so it would stay true.

Then he came to the fear.

He sat for a long time with the pen lifted. He could leave it out. The boy had enough to carry without a letter from Denvash telling him that his grandfather had sat on a step in the street and been more frightened than at any time since Weaver's Row, a week after there was anything left to be frightened of. It would do no good. It could not reach back and change one thing that had happened at that desk upstairs at the post.

But they had made a bargain, the two of them, one letter apart. *I'd rather have the telling.* He had written that himself. *As it comes.* The boy had written that back.

He wrote it down.

*When your letter came, I sat on the workshop step in the street and read it there, and I was more afraid than I have been since the day at Weaver's Row. It was all over by then. You had already done it, and done it well, and I knew that by the second line. It made no difference. That's the post, and it's the price of where you are, and I'd pay it again. He did not put in the hand. He did not put in the spring. That was not leaving out what mattered. It was leaving out a broken spring in a drawer, which mattered to nobody but him and the survey office.

At the bottom, because he had promised, he wrote: *Joren says to tell you his heels are down. He held a level for me for fifty minutes without moving it. His father thinks he's wasting his mornings.*

And then, because it was also true: *Alis carries basins. She says you'll laugh.*

He signed it *— H.* He read it over once by the lamp, and found that it was all true, and that it was not all of what was true, and that he was not sure he had ever written a letter in his life of which both of those things could not be said.

"Bronze," said Lira.

She said it as if she were reading the word off the side of a crate, to see whether it was the crate she had ordered.

He watched Sarel fight that afternoon, at the rope on the shaded side, near the barrel, with the Log open on his knee and the pencil ready.

He did not write much.

She was about thirty, long-legged and lean, with dark hair cut short as a boy's and a long scar down the outside of one forearm, the left, old and white. She worked, somebody at the rope said, on the scaffolds the builders put up round the new houses on the east road. She had the scaffold walker's way of standing, with her weight easy and her arms loose, like somebody who has long since stopped thinking about how high she is. She came over the rope without any hurry at all. Her opponent was a Copper Rank 5, a Force man with a reputation, and he was not happy to be there.

"Begin when you're ready," said Vell.

Sarel's blade came up along her right forearm, pale and straight. It was no longer than Renn's, as far as Cael's eye could tell, and no brighter. Then she moved, and he understood why Vell had said what she had said.

She did not amble, or probe, or throw a figure from her mark. She simply arrived. She was on her mark, and then she was in front of the Force man, inside his reach, and the flat of her blade was already coming down on his shoulder. Cael had seen her start. He was sure he had seen her start. He had not seen her cross.

In the seventh I went in on her right and she caught me coming out, on the side of the head, and I went down. And I lay there and I didn't know why. I went over it for weeks. I went over every step, and I couldn't find it, and in the end I decided she'd been lucky." Her voice went odd. "She wasn't lucky. Was she? I was tired. And I went too far on my right."

"I don't know. I wasn't there."

"No. But I was." She looked up at him, and her face was doing two things at once, and he watched them both. One was a kind of startled gladness, very bright, like somebody who has found a coin they lost a year ago in a coat they had stopped wearing. "Cael. That's—" She laughed, a short shaky sound. "Nobody's ever—that's *mine*. That's a thing about *me*. All this time I've been walking round with it, and nobody saw it. My instructor didn't see it. I didn't see it." She put her hand flat on her own right hip, as if to feel where it lived. "That's useful. That's really, actually useful. That's the most useful thing anybody's ever told me about my own feet."

He had known she would be glad. He had not known she would be glad like that.

"When I'm tired," said Lira, half to herself, already turning it over. "It's when I'm tired. All right. All right. I can work with tired." Then she looked up again. He saw the other thing come into her face, the second one, slower, the one that had been waiting under the first.

"You said *three*," she said. "Just now. Under your breath. When I did it."

He had not known he had said it out loud.

"How many times have you seen it?"

He thought about lying. He thought about it for the length of one breath, standing in the grey half with the chalk marks on the post beside him. There were thirty-odd *gone*s on one side and seven falls on the other, every one of them a thing he had done wrong and said out loud. He did not lie.

"Three. Saturday. Just now." He made himself finish it. "And once before that. At Brenna. The fourth exchange. You went in on her right and went too far, and she caught you on the side of the head."

Lira was very still.

"That was three weeks ago."

"Yes."

"You gave me a report." Her voice was quite level. "That night. In the lane. You gave me every exchange. You gave me the old men in the third row liking it." She was looking at him now, straight at him, with nothing in her face at all. "You didn't give me that."

"Once isn't a pattern," he said. "I didn't know if it was anything. I thought—"

"You *ruled*." She said it quietly. It was worse than if she had shouted. "I said no ruling. I said it twice. *Give it me after, like a report, I don't want a ruling.* And you stood at that rope and saw a thing about my fighting and decided it didn't count yet, and kept it." She took a breath. "Where did you put it? In your book. Did you write it down?"

He did not answer. He did not have to.

"In the back," said Lira. "You put my fight in the back of your book."

"I didn't write it anywhere. I kept it." He heard how that sounded. "I was going to tell you when I was sure."

"When you were sure." She said it after him, slowly, as if she were reading it off something. "That's what you do, isn't it. You keep it till you're sure. You keep everything till you're sure." She looked down at the post, and at the chalk, and back at him. "And Saturday. You saw it Saturday. That was twice. Twice is a pattern, in your book. I've heard you say it. And you ate your bread."

"I didn't want you carrying it into Sunday."

"That was my choice." Her voice went up, just a little, and she brought it down again at once. "It's my feet. My right side. My bout that I lost at the academy and never knew why. You don't get to decide when I'm ready to know about my own feet, Cael. Nobody does. Somebody decided things like that for me once, at a desk, and that's how I ended up on a wall in this district." She stopped.

He stood there in the grey half with nothing to say. It was all true. He had kept it until he was sure, and he had been sure on Saturday, and he had eaten his bread.

Lira looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the bread off the top of the post. She did not tear it. She held it in her hand as if she had forgotten what it was.

"Thank you for telling me," she said. "I mean that. It's the best thing you've ever given me, and I'm glad of it, and I'm going to use it." She put the bread down on the post again, whole. "I'm going now. I'll see you."

She did not say *same time tomorrow*.

She went out through the gate in the wall without looking back. He stood by the post in the grey half, with the bread beside him and the chalk marks under his hand, and listened to her quick feet go away down the lane until he could not hear them.

The girls on the wall had stopped laughing some time ago. One of them looked at him, and then looked away, as if she had seen something she was not supposed to.

He did not eat the bread. He stood there until the sun came over the east wall and there was no grey half left. He thought about a boy at a kitchen table in Denvash, two months and a lifetime ago, finding out that his grandfather had known for a few years. He thought about what his own face had done when he heard it.

He thought about four things on a page in the back of the Log that Hesk did not know.

Then he picked up the bread and went to the mender's, because it was Wednesday, and he did not know what else to do.

# Chapter 38 — A Door

Lira got as far as the board before she had to stop walking.

She had meant to go straight down the lane and through the triangle and on, anywhere, at the pace she used for markets, so that people got out of her way without knowing why. Her feet had other ideas. They took her down the lane quickly enough. Then at the board they simply stopped, as if somebody had put a hand on her chest, and she stood in front of it in the cold with her hands in her jacket pockets and pretended to read it.

There was a new card for Sunday in Vell's upright pencil, on the back of a bill for candles. There was the result from last Sunday, already going pale: *Sarel (Br 1, Blade) over unrated (vouched), sixth exchange, called*. Somebody had drawn a small circle round *sixth* in charcoal. Under that was the Renn result, and under that, nearly gone into the grain, her own. *Lira (Cu 3, Wind) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), fifth exchange, called.* The three charcoal strokes beside it had been rubbed half away by a sleeve.

She looked at her own name for a while.

She was angry. That was the first thing, and it was the biggest, and she let it be big because she had learned a long time ago that if you tried to make anger smaller while you were still standing in it, it only went somewhere you could not see it and waited. So she stood in front of the board and was angry, properly, all the way through, at a boy with a notebook who had stood at the rope two weeks ago, seen a thing about her, and decided she could not have it yet.

*Once isn't a pattern.*

She knew exactly what his face had looked like when he decided that. She could see it as clearly as if she had been standing beside him. He would have had his chin tucked and his eyes very wide, and he would have given the small private nod he did not know he did. *That's settled. Put it somewhere. Not now.* She had watched him make that face a hundred times about other people's feet. She had never once thought he would make it about hers.

And the worst of it was that she would have done the same. That was the thing she did not want to look at, standing in front of the board with her own name going pale on it. If it had been the other way round, and she had seen a thing in his fighting once, in a bout, she might not have said so either. She might have waited to see it again. She would have called it being careful.

But she would not have left it out of a *report*. Not after he had asked her for no ruling. Not after he had given her every other exchange, so carefully, in the dusk, with the old men in the third row put in for evidence. That was what she could not get past. He had made the report into something it was not. It had looked like everything he saw, and it had been everything he saw except one thing, and he had decided which thing.

Somebody had decided things for her once. A woman at a desk had decided that a thing Lira had done in front of witnesses had not counted, because it was not in the book with the diagrams. Lira had stood in front of that desk with her chin up and said *what was the exam actually testing*, and the woman had not answered, and that was the end of the academy. She had not thought about that desk for a long time. She was thinking about it now, and she did not like that a boy who was the opposite of that desk in every way had put her in mind of it.

A cart went by behind her. The skewer woman was setting up at the corner of the widest street, and she looked across at Lira standing still in front of the board, which Lira never did. She looked for a moment and then looked away, which was kind of her.

Lira turned her back on the board and walked on.

The other thing came up under the anger as she walked, slowly, the way the warmth comes up in your feet after you have been standing in snow. She had not let it come up in the yard because she had not wanted him to see it. She let it now.

*When you're tired, you go too far on your right.*

She went over it as she went down through the triangle. She went over the backhand, and the weight going on past the end of it, and her right foot coming down hard and wide to catch her. She had felt that foot come down a thousand times. She had never once thought about it. It was simply what her right foot did at the end of a long morning, like her hair coming loose on one side. You did not think about your hair coming loose. It came loose.

And then the academy. The seventh exchange against a girl who was not as good as her. Lira had gone over that bout for weeks, lying on her bed in the long dormitory with her eyes open, trying every step of it from every side, and she had never found it. She had decided in the end that the girl had been lucky. It was not a good answer. It was the only one left.

It had been her right foot. It had been a hand's width. It had been there the whole time, in plain sight, at the end of every long morning, and nobody had seen it. Her instructor had not. The woman at the desk had not. She had not.

A boy from Denvash had seen it, from a rope, on a cold afternoon, while his own heart was going so hard against his ribs that he had printed the rope into his palms.

She found that she had stopped again, in the middle of the street, and that she was smiling, and that she was still angry, and that both were true at once. A man with a barrow had to go round her. He said something. She did not hear what.

It was the strangest feeling she had ever had about another person, and she stood in the street and made herself look at it straight, because she did not believe in looking at things sideways. Somebody had looked at her and seen her exactly, all the way down to her feet. Nobody had ever done that before, not once, not anybody, not in her whole life. And then he had kept back the one thing he saw that she most needed to know.

Both of those were him. That was the trouble. You could not have the first one without the kind of person who did the second.

She thought, then, because she was honest with herself when nobody was looking, about a row of three things she kept in her own head.

The first was a hand at the board on the first day that had moved before it should have. The second was the third exchange against Renn on the first night. The third was a boy dropping under her backhand in the grey half, lower than she had ever asked him to go, and coming up standing in a way that was not his. She had never told him about the row. And she had never told him that she had gone into the yard alone, three mornings running, before he came, and tried the drop eleven times. She had learned that it was easy to do and impossible to do in time.

She had kept that. She had kept it for a month. She had told herself she was waiting until he had something he wanted to tell her.

It did not make what he had done all right. It took some of the heat out, that was all, the way a bucket of water takes some of the heat out of a forge without putting it out.

She walked on, and she did not go anywhere she had meant to go. She went where her feet had been going all along, two streets over from Torvin's, to a narrow lane she had walked down nine times in the last month on purpose and pretended each time that it was on the way to somewhere else.

---

The chandler's shop was at the end of the lane, with its door open and a smell of tallow coming out of it, and candles hanging in bunches from the beam over the counter by their wicks, like onions.

The widow who kept it was sitting behind the counter dipping tapers. She was a small woman with a face as round and pale as one of her own candle ends, and she had told Lira, the first time Lira came in to ask about the room upstairs, that she did not let to fighters. Then she had looked at Lira's boots, which had been resoled twice, and her jacket cuffs, which were turned back and stitched. And she had said that she supposed fighters had to sleep somewhere, and that she would want two weeks paid first and no visitors after the lamps.

"I'll hold it till Wednesday," she had said, a week ago. "Then I'll let it to whoever asks."

It was Wednesday.

"I've come about the room," said Lira.

The widow looked up from her tapers.

"I said Wednesday," she said, "and you've come on Wednesday." She seemed faintly surprised by this, as if people seldom did. "Two weeks first."

Lira took the tin out of the inside of her jacket.

It was a small flat tin that had once held throat sweets, and the picture on its lid had worn away to a brown smudge. She had carried it ever since she came to Ardenmere, in the inside pocket where it pressed against her ribs when she sat down. She opened it on the counter between the bunches of candles. Inside were the weeks of door, in small worn coins: the purse from Brenna, and the two summer purses before that, and every coin from every week she had eaten at Amrit's for half price because he had decided she was his. There was the money from the sparring hour across the district that she did not tell anybody about. There were three coins she had found in the groove at the Cinder House since the summer, dropped by people at the rope, and had kept because they felt like the yard's own.

She counted out two weeks.

She counted it twice, the way Torvin did, not because she did not trust the coins but to check her own attention. Then she pushed it across the counter. There was not very much left in the tin afterward. She shut the lid on what there was.

The widow counted it once, quickly, and swept it into a drawer, and took a key off a nail behind her.

"Stair's round the side," she said. "Mind the fourth step. It talks."

The room was at the top of a stair so narrow and steep that Lira had to go up it with one hand on the wall, and the fourth step did talk, a long creak like an old man complaining.

There was a door at the top.

It was a plain door, made of four boards with a crosspiece, painted a long time ago in a green that had gone grey. It had a latch, and a keyhole below the latch, and a scuff at the bottom where somebody had kicked it shut for years. She stood on the top step with the key in her hand and looked at it for a long time.

Then she put the key in and turned it. The lock was stiff, and then it was not. She pushed the door open and went in, and shut it behind her, and stood with her back against it.

The room was very small. It was smaller than the roof room in the tall house in the third lane, and she had shared that with two sisters from the dye sheds who talked in their sleep about indigo. There was a bed frame with a straw mattress rolled up on it, and a hook on the back of the door, and a shelf. There was a window, one, looking down into the lane, with a crack across one pane that somebody had stuck over with a strip of paper. The ceiling sloped down on the window side so that she would have to remember not to stand up too fast in the morning. It smelled faintly of tallow from the shop below. The floorboards were worn pale down the middle where somebody had walked from the door to the window, and from the window to the door, a great many times.

She stood there with her back against the door and listened to it be shut.

That was all she had wanted. She had not been able to say it to anybody, not to Yeni, who knew everything anyway, and not to Cael. When he asked what she was saving for, all she had been able to say was *a door*, and then tell him not to make a face about it. It had sounded like nothing. It was nothing. It was four boards and a crosspiece and a lock that stuck. But it was hers, and it shut, and when it was shut there was nobody on the other side of it who had any right to open it.

She had not had that since she left the academy. She had not had it at the academy either, in the long dormitory with its rows of beds. And before the academy—

She did not think about before the academy. She had a rule about that, and she kept it, the way you keep a door shut in a house you have moved out of.

She sat down on the floor, in the middle of the pale worn strip, with her back against the bed frame and her knees drawn up. She sat there for a long time, in the thin light from the cracked window, and let herself think about the six weeks. It was the first time she had sat still long enough to do it.

They were half gone. Twenty-two days of them were gone, and twenty were left. She had counted the days without meaning to, the way he would have.

In twenty-two days he had fought four times. He had beaten a boy who held his breath, and Renn, and nearly nobody had believed it about Renn until they saw it. He had lost to a Bronze in the sixth exchange, and in the third he had done the thing she had been trying to teach him for two months. He had done it badly, stiff in the hips. But he had done it, in front of the whole yard, against a woman the whole yard was afraid of. He had said *gone* thirty-four times at her post with his ears going red. Then he had said it once more at a Bronze with his ears not going red at all, and half the rope had heard him.

She had watched every one of those. She had stood at the barrel with her hands on the rope and watched him get better. It was not the slow, careful getting better of the first weeks, when every morning bought a hand's width and cost a bruise. It was faster now. He had found something, or it had found him, and he was coming up through the circle the way she had said people came up who went down faster, and he was not going down. He was being careful. She had made him careful.

She thought about being his teacher. She had never been anybody's teacher before. She had been somebody's student and failed, and somebody else's student and been right in front of witnesses, and had walked the yards of this district on her own for months, losing. She had not known, when she put her name to a vouch for a boy from Denvash who said a word nobody had ever heard out loud, that it would feel like this. She had not known it would feel like watching something she had planted come up through the ground and turn out to be a different plant from the one on the packet.

And she thought, sitting on the floor, about being seen.

She had told him at the board on the first day that the examiner had failed her for being right in front of witnesses. That had been true. It had not been the whole of it. The whole of it was that she had stood in front of that desk wanting, more than she had ever wanted anything, for somebody who understood to look at what she had done and say *yes, that, that's real*. Nobody had. The examiner had not understood it, or had not wanted to. The witnesses had understood it and said nothing, because it was not their exam.

Then a boy from Denvash had stood at a rope, three weeks ago, and watched her skip the middle against Brenna in front of the whole yard. He had given it back to her in the dusk afterward, exchange by exchange, flat, with nothing added. *You caught the chop and sent it with no middle. Three times. She went after it every time.* He had not said it was good. He had not needed to. He had seen it exactly as it was, and said it exactly as it was, and that had been better than *good*.

And this morning he had seen the other thing, the bad thing, the thing that had cost her a bout and all the time since of not knowing. He had seen it just as exactly.

It was the same eyes. That was what she kept coming back to, on the floor. The eyes that saw the skipped middle were the eyes that saw the right foot. You could not have one without the other. You could only ask the person who had them not to keep half of what they saw in a pocket.

She would ask him. Tomorrow. Not like this morning, with her voice going up and the bread whole on the post and *I'll see you*. She would ask him properly. She would tell him the rule, plainly, the way she told him everything, and let him pick it up, and it would be heavier than what other people gave him, and it would be the right weight.

And then she would show him the door.

She got up off the floor. She went to the window and stood under the sloping ceiling without hitting her head, and looked down into the lane. A dog was asleep on a step. A woman was hanging a sheet out of a window opposite. Two streets away, over the roofs, she could see a corner of Torvin's chimney with the smoke going straight up, and she found that she had chosen this room partly for that. She had not known it until now. She stood at her own window, in her own room, with her own door shut behind her, and let herself be proud, all the way through, without anybody there to see it.

Then she went down the stair, and the fourth step talked, and she said *I know* to it out loud. She went to her sparring hour across the district very late, and was shouted at, and did not mind at all.

He did not know whether she would be there.

He went up to the yard in the dark on Thursday morning anyway, the way he always did, by the lane where the ground began to rise, with two loaves from underneath in his arms. He told himself that it was what he did, and that Hesk had said not to change what he did. That was true. It was not why he went.

He stopped in the gateway.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm not."

"Talis. Copper Rank 4, Stone Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

The grit came up off him and lay down flat, and he came.

Cael did what he had done in the third exchange against Sarel. He moved. He did it small and quick and unplanned, with his hips loose this time, as Lira had shown him on Wednesday. He watched Talis's shoulders while he did it, because Talis was not Sarel and the watching was not too slow for him. The shoulders dipped. The right hand came. Cael was somewhere else.

But Talis was somewhere else too.

Cael had not seen him go there. One breath Talis was in front of him, and the next he was a stride to the left, with his weight already planted. The left forearm came across, heavy and grey with the grit, and hit Cael on the upper arm like a mallet on a stump. Cael went sideways and kept his feet, just, and Talis was already rocking back on his heels, impatient, waiting.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

*First exchange,* said Cael to himself. *The shoulders dipped before the right. I read it. He didn't use the right. He went somewhere I didn't see him go, and planted, and used the left.*

In the second it happened again.

He watched the shoulders. He watched the hands. He moved without deciding. Every time, Talis was suddenly somewhere Cael had not expected, a stride off to one side, planted, as solid as if he had grown there. Every time the strike came from the place where he had planted, with all the weight of the ground behind it. Cael got out of the first one. The second caught him on the hip, a short blunt thud like a door kicked shut, and he felt it go right through him into his back, into Sarel's bruise.

*He knows where he's going to plant before I do,* Cael thought, on his mark, with his hip aching. *Of course he does. It's his foot.* He looked at Talis's shoulders and Talis's hands. *But I should see it. There should be something before it. A dip, a lean, a look. Something.*

There was nothing. He watched for it all through the third exchange, watched until his eyes ached. He got a touch on Talis's arm, short and straight from the hip, the one strike he owned. Talis's face went red, and Cael thought *good, let him*, and nothing else came of it. Talis's temper did not make his shoulders talk, or his hands. It only made him plant harder.

In the fourth, Talis planted on Cael's left, where Cael had not seen him going. The gatepost forearm came round into Cael's side under the ribs, on the left, the side Sarel had not touched. It was the strike he never saw building. He did not see it at all. He was looking at Talis's shoulders, and the shoulders were still, and then he was on the ground.

He went down on his side with his cheek against the dirt and every bit of air gone out of him at once.

"Match," said Vell.

He lay there.

He could not get up yet. He lay with his face on the cold packed earth of the circle, and breathed, or tried to, and listened to the yard shouting above him. He thought about nothing at all for a moment.

Talis walked back to his mark.

Cael felt him go.

He felt it in his cheekbone, where his face lay against the ground. It was a faint, quick buzz, very small, like the hum in a table when somebody sets a heavy glass down at the other end of it. It came up out of the dirt into the bone of his face. Then, a half beat after it, Talis's foot came down, a stride away, and planted.

Talis took another step. The buzz came first. Then the foot.

Cael lay very still with his cheek on the ground.

*Again,* he thought, and Talis took a third step, and it came again: the hum in the dirt, and then, half a beat behind it, the foot. Every time. The ground knew where Talis was going to plant before Talis's foot got there. It said so, if you were listening with the right part of you, and Cael had not been. He had been listening with his eyes, at shoulders and hands, the way he listened to everybody. It had never once occurred to him that a tell could live in the ground.

He got up, eventually. It took a long time. His left side felt as though somebody had driven a wedge into it, and when he breathed in all the way something caught under the ribs, sharp, and let go. He walked to the table. Vell wrote. He did not hear what she said, and then he did.

"Result stands as called. Talis, Copper Rank 4, Stone Path, over unrated. Fourth exchange. Match." The pen moved. "Witnessed and entered."

Lira was waiting at the barrel. She had her hand on the iron stake at the corner of the rope, where it went down into the ground, and she did not take it off when he came up. She was looking at him with an odd expression.

"Did you feel it?" she said.

He stared at her.

"Through the stake," said Lira. She tapped the iron with one finger. "I didn't, at first. I was holding the rope. In the third exchange I put my hand down on the stake to lean, and it was humming. Every time he was going to plant. A half beat before he put his foot down." She looked at him. "I thought you'd have seen it."

"I felt it," said Cael. "After. With my face."

Lira looked at the side of his face, where the dirt was.

"Well," she said, after a moment. "That's one way to find it."

"I wasn't looking at the ground."

"Nobody looks at the ground." She took her hand off the stake at last. "That's why it's such a good place to keep something."

# Chapter 39 — The Plan That Survives

He could not sit on the low wall that night, or on the cot either, without something under the ribs on the left side catching, sharp, every time he breathed in all the way. *Tenth. Talis, Copper Rank 4, Stone. Lost, fourth exchange, match.*

*Claim: he goes somewhere I don't see him go, plants, and strikes from where he's planted. Evidence: four exchanges. Every strike came from a place I didn't expect. Shoulders still. Hands low. Nothing in either.*

*Claim: the tell is in the ground. Evidence: I felt it with my face after he'd put me down. A hum in the dirt, a half beat before he plants. Lira felt it through the iron stake at the corner of the rope, in the third. (Lira, h. Mine, after.)*

*I've been looking at hands and shoulders since the community hall,* he wrote. *Hesk taught me hips and feet. I kept the hips and feet and added shoulders and faces and breath. *Talis's wasn't on the body at all. It was under it.*

*So: I have a method. It works. It has holes in it, and the holes don't tell you where they are. That's what makes them holes. If I'd known to look at the ground, it wouldn't have been a hole, it would have been a place I looked. I didn't lose today because I watched badly. *There will be others. I don't know where. That's the point.*

*Ruling, for every one from now on: before I look for the tell, look for where I haven't looked.*

He read it back. Then he did a thing that he did not put in the Log, because it was too foolish to write down.

Cael had felt Force once before, from Petra. Everything she hit had got an extra push at the end, after her hand had stopped, as if somebody standing behind you had kicked you. "So you hit him."

"Then in the fourth."

Nothing happened.

That was what he wrote afterward, in the Log, at the top of the entry, before anything else. He wrote it in a hand that was not quite steady, on the low wall by the pump, with the lamps lit and his breath going up white. *Nothing happened.* He looked at it for a long time, because it had never been true before.

The yard had been full for a Thursday, three deep on the shaded side, and the lanterns were lit on their hooks along the three walls. Dellin came over the rope as if he were climbing down from his cart, heavy and easy and in no hurry, and stood on his mark and smiled across at Cael. It was the friendly loaf of a smile. "Dellin. Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Dellin came off his mark, and the left came high, toward his head.

His left forearm met Dellin's reaching wrist on the way past and turned it. It was not a block. It was the outside of the forearm, wrist to elbow, meeting it and sending it on, a quarter turn further than Dellin had meant to reach, so that the big man's shoulder came round after his hand.

It was not a hard strike. He did not have a hard strike. He had a well-timed one.

For a moment the yard did not seem sure what it had seen.

It had seen three exchanges in which nothing happened and a fourth that was over almost before it began. The noise came up slowly along the rope, uncertain at first and then not, the shaded side first and then the sun. It was not the roar there had been for Renn. It was the other noise, the low pleased one, the noise of people seeing a thing done properly.

Dellin sat in the dirt and worked his jaw from side to side.

"Huh," he said.

"You knew," he said. He did not say it angrily. He said it the way a man says something that has surprised him and that he wants to make sure of. "The left. The right. My hand coming out. You knew what I'd do before I did it."

Cael opened his mouth to say that he had not known. He had not known anything. He had watched three times and written it down three times, and on the third time he had seen that it never changed. Your name in more mouths."

"Yes."

Cael had not known that from the one bout in the second week, because he had not written it down; Corbin had won that one in the first exchange and Cael had not had time to see. His shield was not like Brenna's. It was not a pane wider than his shoulders, held up to be walked at people. It was small, hardly bigger than a dinner plate, a little patch of shine off his left forearm. He did not hold it up. He let it come and go, there for a strike and gone again between, as if he did not like to spend it on nothing.

He did not chop with it. He did not get angry. He did not dip on the step-through, because, somebody at the rope said, he had trained the dip out of himself in his first season, before Brenna had been born, near enough.

His opponent was a good Copper 4 Ember, a woman, and Corbin gave her three exchanges and then took her apart in the fourth. He did it without any hurry and without anything Cael could find to write down, except that he did it.

Then, at the end of the third exchange, as the two of them went back to their marks, he saw it.

It was not in Corbin's body. It was in his eyes.

On his mark, between the exchanges, while the Ember woman breathed and set herself, Corbin looked at her feet. He did not look at her face, or her hands, or the shimmer coming up off her arm. He looked at her feet, carefully, for the whole of the pause, the way a man reads a page he means to answer. When Vell said *begin*, he moved before the Ember woman did, and he was in exactly the place her feet had said she would go.

Cael sat at the rope with the pencil stopped over the page.

*He reads feet,* he wrote. *At the mark. Before the exchange. He watches how you set yourself, and he knows where you're going.*

He looked at that for a long time.

*He's me,* he wrote under it, very small. *He's a watcher. He's been watching for forty fights.*

He did not know what to do with that. He sat on the low wall by the pump that night with it open on his knee and looked at it, and he did not know what to do with it at all. Then, very late, with the cold coming down and the lamp going out in Yeni's window above, he thought about a big young man in a middle yard on the lane with a friend who had been given the job of being Cael. He thought of a heel settling back into the dirt and holding there. Waiting. Saying a thing that was not true.

He began to write very fast.

*Renn taught me this,* he wrote, at the bottom of the page, when he had finished. *He'll take full credit. Let him.*

Corbin came over the rope like a man stepping over a low wall in his own garden.

Up on the wall the two girls were sitting with their legs hanging down and a bag of roast chestnuts between them, and they were not laughing at anything.

He had seen faces along the rope he knew. Renn was on the sunny side by the gate with two of his river friends, and he had waved, and pointed at his own front foot, and laughed. Dellin was at the back, tall over everybody, rubbing his jaw. Brenna was with her friends and not stamping. Dessa was on the end of the third bench, beside an old man in a scarf, with her hair tied tight and her hands folded in her lap. He had looked at her once and she had looked back, and given him nothing at all, and he had understood that she had come to watch him from the benches, as she had said she would, on her own schedule, and that she would tell him nothing about it.

Marrow's slate said *Corbin*, and a short price, and *unrated (vouched)*, and a price that was not long at all. Under it there was no over/under. Marrow had rubbed it out. Cael could see the ghost of a *5* under the chalk where it had been.

"He doesn't know," said Lira at the barrel. "First time since you came. He couldn't make up his mind."

Corbin stood on his mark across the circle with his arms at his sides. He was square and not tall, with his bald patch catching the lantern light and his short beard going grey at the chin. Cael had known he would. He had written it down on Sunday night in a hand that would not keep still. He looked down at his own feet on his mark, as if checking them, and set them.

He did it the way Renn had settled his heel in the middle yard on the lane, on purpose, for somebody to read. He put his weight a little onto his right foot and turned the left toe out a hair, along the groove, the way a boy stands who means to go sideways to the left on the word. Corbin read his feet.

Cael watched him do it, and let him, and did not move them.

"Begin."

He went left. He went exactly where his feet had said, honestly, because the first exchange was a handshake and he wanted to see what Corbin did with a true read before he gave him a false one.

Corbin was there.

He was there before Cael was. It was not a hard blow. It was a sensible one. It knocked the breath out of him and put him back two steps and told him, as clearly as words, *I was here first*. He read it.

He did not read it with his eyes. He did not know what to call what he read it with. He read it the way he had read the archive entry in the green book at a table in Denvash, under a north window: once, and then again, and then a third time, slowly, checking each word against the one before it, before he let himself believe any of it.

It stayed. It did not go out, the way the word had gone out. It sat there in the dark behind his breastbone, quiet and plain, in the way a figure sits on a page once it has been written down.

*Wind-adjacent.*

He knew where it had come from.

He knew it at once, before he had finished the third reading, with the same certainty that had told him something had changed. It did not come as an argument. It came as a fact, like the river being cold.

It had come from the grey half. It had come from two months of mornings in the shadow of the east wall, at a canvas post, with a girl who would not tell lies to anybody she trained. It had come from watching her step a hundred times from the inside with his arms in the wrong place, and once from the rope. It had come from the drop under her backhand that was lower than she had ever asked him to go. It had come from the fourth exchange against Dessa, going across, into the closing space, in a way that was not his. It had come from the third exchange against Sarel, when he had stopped watching and moved without deciding, the way she moved. That was the thing she had been trying to teach him for two months, and he had thought he was only learning it.

He had been gathering it. He had been gathering it all along, mornings and bouts and the long minutes at the rope, and he had never had any word for what it was he gathered. Now it had a name, or the beginning of one. *Adjacent.* Next to Wind, but not Wind. Lira's, and not Lira's. Partial. Undetermined. Unknown.

It was not an answer. It was a page with more on it than yesterday's.

He sat on the cot for a long time with the notice there in the dark behind his breastbone, and did not write anything.

Then he thought about Lira.

He thought about her in her room two streets over, behind her green door that locked from both sides, with Torvin's chimney in her window. He thought about going there. He could see the whole of it, the way he had seen the whole of it on the night of the summons, when he had shut the Log on the kitchen table and gone up through the dark to the window at the bottom of the roof stair. The habit of telling had begun that night. He had been proud of it. He could go now. He could knock on her green door, three times, soft, and she would open it with her coat on and say *what happened*. He could tell her.

*I think I've got something of yours.*

He tried the sentence in his head and it came out wrong. He tried it another way, and it came out wrong again.

He had promised her on the step on the night of Dessa: *when I know what it is, really know, you'll be the one I tell first.* He did not know what it was. He knew what it was called, now, a little. He knew where it came from. He did not know what it did, or why, or how a thing could be collected by watching, or whether it would answer if he called it. He did not know whether it was a thing he had done or a thing that had been done to him.

And in the grey half, on the morning after he had named her right foot, she had stood in front of him with her hands loose at her sides and said *mine are mine*. *You don't keep my things.*

This was hers. Or some of it was. He did not know how much.

He took the Log up off the blanket and turned it over to the back, and wrote.

*Not yet. I'm not hiding it. I just can't explain it yet, not even to myself. If I tell her now, she'll help me work it out, and afterward I'll never be sure which parts of the working out were mine.*

He read it back.

He was not sure that it was honest.

He read it a second time and was less sure. It had the shape of a reason. He could see the shape quite clearly, and he could not see whether there was anything inside it. It might be true. It might be the kind of true that has its legs sawn short. It might be a boy on a cot who had been told once, plainly, in the grey half, *you keep everything till you're sure*, keeping one more thing till he was sure, and writing down a good reason for it in his best hand.

He did not cross it out. He drew a line round it instead, a box, so that when he came back to it he would know he had not been sure.

---

Then, because he could not sit still with it any longer, he got up.

There was a narrow space between his cot and the wall, perhaps two strides long and one wide, where his bag stood and the cold came in under the window. He stood in it in his stockinged feet on the boards. The brothers were asleep. Yeni was asleep, or was lying with her eyes shut and listening, which with Yeni was the same thing.

He thought about Lira's step.

Not the whole of it, with the touches and the backhand. Only the beginning of it, the part he had watched a hundred times from the inside and never once been able to see. The first drop of the hip, which started everything, a small dip and turn, so slight that from the inside it looked like nothing at all.

He did it.

He did it on purpose, slowly, in the narrow space between his cot and the wall, at half the speed Lira would have done it. He dropped his hip the way he had seen her drop hers. He turned the way he had seen her turn. And he put his foot down where he had seen her put hers.

It worked.

It did not work well. It worked so badly that if anybody had been watching they would have thought he was trying to step over something on the floor and had misjudged it. It was clumsy. It was slow. It was nothing like the drop under her backhand, or the going across against Dessa, which had come out of him whole and perfect and not his. But for a moment, for one step, the boards between the cot and the wall were very slightly less firm about how far apart they were. It was the width of a hand, perhaps less. The space between where his foot had been and where it landed was a little shorter than it should have been, and he felt it go short under him, like a stair that is not quite the height you expected.

He had done it. Not had it done to him. Done it. On purpose. He had chosen to.

He stood there afterward in the narrow space, breathing.

He was breathing much harder than one slow step in his stockings had any right to make him. His hip ached, the left one, deep in the joint, as if he had carried something heavy up a long stair on that side. It cost something. He did not know what, or how much. He only knew that it had cost more than it should have, and that he had paid it, and that he did not know yet what it had bought.

He did it once more, to be sure.

It was worse the second time. It was slower and clumsier, and the boards were only a very little less firm, hardly at all, and the hip ached more after it. He did not do it a third time. He sat down on the end of the cot with his hand pressed to the hip and his breath coming short. He turned the fact over in his mind like a stone he had picked up off a road and could not yet tell the weight of.

Then he picked up the Log and wrote under the box.

*I did it on purpose. Once. Badly, slowly, and it cost something in the hip and the breath that I can't measure yet. Twice, worse. But on purpose.*

*Every time before, it came when I didn't ask for it and wasn't there when I did. Tonight I asked, and it came. Not much. A little. Badly.*

*I think that's the thing that matters. Not that it's there. That it's started to answer when I ask.*

He read that over, and it was true all the way through, and he left it.

---

He wrote to Hesk before he slept, because it was Saturday, and the letter would go in the west bag on Monday and be in Denvash by the Monday after. And because he had found, sitting on the cot with his hip aching, that he wanted very much to write to somebody. There was only one person it could be, and he could not write it to him.

He wrote it short.

*Hesk,*

*Six weeks nearly done. Six bouts in them, four won. The last was a man everybody here uses to measure people by, and he put his hand up in the fifth. People were saying it in the street on the way out. Lira says that's the one they'll repeat.*

*Lira's drawn a line under the forty. I'll tell you about the forty another time; it's a long story and most of it is me saying one word.*

*Tell Joren I'm putting my hand on the ground at my mark before every bout now. He'll know why, or he won't, and either way he'll like it.*

*Tell Alis I laughed.*

*I'm all right. The ribs are mending.*

*— Cael*

He read it over. It was true all the way through. He folded it and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

Then he sat with the leather book closed on his knee and counted, as he always did now, what he had not told Hesk.

The cart. Renn. The drop. Dessa. And tonight.

It was five.

He sat with the number for a long time, in the cold, with the wheezing man whistling by the door. It was not that it had gone up. He had known it would go up the moment he read the notice; he had made a promise on a step about who would hear first. It was that tonight it had gone up and the other number had not gone down. There was one person who was meant to be first. She was two streets away behind a green door, and he had not gone.

He blew out his small piece of the light by turning his face to the wall. He lay a long time with the notice quiet behind his breastbone and his hip aching, and listened to the district's late sounds come up through the window, and did not sleep for a while.

---

On Tuesday the afternoon card went off without him, three bouts, and he stood at the rope and watched all three and wrote them down. Then the yard emptied, and the pie boy began to rake the circle, and the crows came down along the eaves, and he went to Vell's table.

She was finishing an entry. She did not look up until she had blotted it.

"Sit down," she said.

There was nowhere to sit but the dirt and the barrel, so he stood, and she did not seem to mind.

"Assessed-Copper," said Vell.

He waited.

"It's not a real rank. I want that said first, so you don't go thinking it is." She turned her tea a quarter turn by its handle. "It's a word I use. It means the circuit has watched you for long enough to say you fight at about Copper 5, or 6, on a good night, whatever the registry calls you. And the registry calls you nothing." She looked at the ledger. "Take it to Denvash and they'd not know what it meant. Take it across the river and they'd laugh. Nobody outside this district would give you a mark for it."

"But inside it," said Cael.

"Inside it," said Vell, "it's the difference between a boy people bet on for sport and a fighter people plan for." She picked up her pen and wrote. It was a short line, beside the long-standing one at the top of his page. He could not read it upside down, and she did not turn the book. "That's worth something here. It's worth more here than you know yet."

She blotted it.

"Is it worth anything to you?" said Cael.

He had not known he was going to ask it until it was out. Vell looked up at him. Something went across her weathered face, very nearly amusement, and was put away again.

"I don't rate fighters because I like them," she said. "I rate them because an honest book is the only thing that keeps this circuit from tearing itself apart over who's good." She looked at him a moment longer. Then she said, more quietly, in a different voice, the one she kept for things that were hers: "For what it's worth. I do."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." She went back to the book. "You did the fighting."

He went.

Lira was sitting on the low wall at the side of the yard with her knees up and her coat round them, in the last of the light. She had waited. She had not come to the table. She had sat on the wall, as she had sat on the river wall on the day of the six weeks, with the look of somebody who had been happening to pass a particular place for about an hour.

"Well?" she said.

"Assessed-Copper."

She did not jump off the wall. She sat quite still for a moment. Then the slow smile began, the one that started behind her eyes and took its time getting out.

"Copper 5," she said. "Or 6. On a good night." She got down off the wall. "That's higher than mine, you know. My circuit rating. You're past me." She did not sound as if she minded. She sounded as if she had won something herself. "I vouched for you. Three months ago, near enough, with my name on it, for a boy who said a word nobody had ever heard out loud. And now you're past me." She held out her good hand to pull him up off nothing, out of habit, and then laughed and dropped it. "Come on. It's the forty-second day. You've spent them all."

They went out through the gate in the wall together, and down the lane in the dusk toward the well with the iron cover, where she would stop, and go one way, and he the other.

He walked beside her and did not say it.

He had it with him. It was behind his breastbone, quiet and plain, in the dark place that was not quite as dark as it had been. *Wind-adjacent.* It was there every moment. It had been there at Vell's table and on the wall and in the gate, and it was there now in the lane. Every step he took beside her, it was there, and every step he did not say it.

The six weeks were spent. Somewhere on the merchant road, or at a desk two days west, somebody more senior than Coss was being found and sent, with a file that was too thin and a question nobody had told Coss. A girl with a green door was walking beside him in the dusk, pleased about a word that was not a rank. She had drawn a line under forty. She had told him, plainly, *mine are mine*.

At the well she stopped, as she always stopped.

"Same time tomorrow," said Lira.

"Same time tomorrow."

She went off up the lane toward the chandler's, and he stood at the well and watched her go until he could not see her. Then he turned toward Torvin's, two streets the other way.

He did not know how long he could carry it. He knew it would not be long. He thought, walking home with his hip aching, that it might be two weeks, if he was careful, and that he did not want to be careful. He thought that the thing behind his breastbone was partly hers. He thought that every morning he kept it from her in the grey half, it would get heavier, the way a thing does that has been held up too long. Nothing that has to be held is free.

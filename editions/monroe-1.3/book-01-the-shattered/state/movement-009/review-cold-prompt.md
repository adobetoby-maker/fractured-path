# Chapter 54 — Loud

On Monday morning he stood in front of the board at the triangle for a long time and read what was not on it.

It was a good board, as boards went. It had been there longer than anybody could remember, a slab of old door nailed to the side of the cooper's shed where the three lanes met, and everything the district wanted the district to know went up on it sooner or later. There were rooms to let and a dog gone missing and a man in the river workshops who would mend a kettle while you waited. There was a notice about the gutter dispute between the cooper and the dyer, written by the dyer and corrected in charcoal by the cooper. And in the middle, where the light was best, there was Vell's card for Sunday, on the back of a bill for lamp oil, in her small square hand.

He knew every card she had put up since the autumn. He had read them all here, standing where he was standing now. For seven months the line under his bouts had said the same thing: *unrated (vouched)*. It had said it under Renn, twice, and under Dessa, twice, and under Sarel and Talis and Corbin and the rest. When the yard had chalked a joke on the board after Corbin, it had been about Corbin putting his hand up, and not about the boy who had made him do it. The board knew him as a line in brackets.

He had never minded. He had wanted it that way. In the autumn a line in brackets had been the safest thing a person could be.

He took the Log out of his coat and turned to the back, and stood at the board with it open on his forearm, and wrote down every person in Ardenmere who would ask where he had gone if he did not come back.

It did not take long. *Lira.* That one was first and needed nothing beside it. *Torvin. Torvin's wife. Yeni. Sella. Doss.* The house, all of it, even the wheezing man, perhaps, though only because Cael's cot would be empty and the wheezing man would have nobody to complain to about the draught. *The old man.* He would not ask anybody; he would stand in his gate and notice the lane was short of somebody, and that would be all. *The mender. Halden.* The boys, both of them, though they would ask each other and then argue about it. *The bread woman.* He had no name to write for her and wrote *the bread woman*. *The fruit woman*, the same. *Vell*, and then he looked at it and was not sure, and put a small mark beside it, as he did beside a tell he had seen only once.

Fourteen. Five of them without names that he knew.

He looked at the list for some time. Then he thought about Coss's drawer.

*The drawer for things that are finished.* Coss had said it plainly, across a pie he had not eaten, as a kindness. A file closed on voluntary relocation was a file nobody opened again. It was not hidden, or burned. It only went into a drawer with a great many others, and the drawer was shut, and nobody had any reason to pull it out. It was not a bad thing that happened to a file. It was only a quiet one.

Fourteen people could be told a boy had gone home. Most of them would believe it. The ones who did not would have no one to ask.

He turned back a page to where he had written, two nights ago on the cot, that he did not know what he would do instead of leaving. He read it again. *I'm going to find out the way I find out everything.* He had found it out, he thought, standing at a board, by reading what was not on it.

Vell had a custom about her book. She had told it to him in the autumn, in his first week, when he had wanted to know why his name was not in it: a name went into Vell's book when the yard came to see it. It was no slight. It was what a book was for. A fighter the yard did not come for was a fighter the yard would not miss. So she wrote him the way the yard knew him, in brackets, and waited.

Nobody had ever come to the Cinder House to see Cael. They had come to see Renn, or Dessa, or a Bronze in the sleet. He had been the thing on the other side of the rope.

He shut the Log. Across the triangle the cooper was rolling a barrel out of his shed with his foot, very slowly, and the dyer was standing in her doorway with her arms folded, watching him do it.

*Quiet made me easy to put away,* he thought. *So I'll stop being quiet.*

He said it once more in his head, to see whether it held, and it did. Then he went up the lane toward the Cinder House, walking fast, before he could start finding all the reasons it would not.

---

Vell was at her table in the shade of the back wall with her tea and her ledger and her pen. It was a Monday morning and there was nothing in the circle. The pie boy was raking the groove out level after Sunday, stroke by slow stroke, and two crows were walking about on the bench behind him in case he dropped anything.

Cael stood in front of the table and waited until she looked up.

"I want a marquee bout."

Vell did not answer at once. She put her pen down across the page, very straight, and looked at him. She did it the way she looked at a horse somebody wanted to sell her, from the feet up, and took her time over it.

"Say what you mean by that."

"A Sunday. The last bout on the card. Somebody whose name people outside Ardenmere would know." He had been saying it to himself all the way up the lane, and it came out steadier than he had expected. "On every board in the district. Not only yours."

"You've built everything you've got the other way." Vell picked up her tea, and did not drink it. "Quietly. One bout and then the next, in front of people who'd come to see somebody else. It's why the people who watch you trust what they see. Nobody came expecting anything." She put the cup down. "A marquee brings a different crowd. It brings people who've come to see a thing happen. Some of them have money on it, and some of them have only come to say they were there, and some of them have come because they heard a name and they want to know whose it is." She looked at him levelly. "Not all of that is the kind of looking you want."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"I know it's not safe." He had thought about how much to tell her on the way up, and had decided. He would tell her the part that was his, and not Coss's part, which had been given to him across a table by a man in his own coat. "Somebody would like me to go somewhere small. Of my own accord. Quietly, so nobody has to write anything down." He looked at the ledger, not at her. "I've been quiet since the autumn. I was good at it. It's the only thing anybody's got against me, and it's the only thing I've been giving them."

Vell sat very still.

"You've a custom," he said. "A name goes in when the yard comes to see it."

"I know my own custom."

"Then I'd like the yard to come."

It was a long time before she said anything. The pie boy reached the end of the groove and started back along it. The crows went with him.

"Thirty years I've kept this book," said Vell at last. "I've had fighters ask me for a big name to make their money, and for a big name to make their fathers look up from the paper. I had a Fenrow man once who wanted his own brother." She drew the ledger toward her and turned back a page, and then another, not reading. "I've never had one ask for a crowd so he'd be harder to lose."

"Will you do it?"

"I'll think about who." She picked up her pen. "There are three things first, and they aren't for arguing."

He waited.

"I choose who you stand across from. Not you. You'll have opinions and I'll hear them, and then I'll choose." She wrote nothing yet; the pen only hovered. "A marquee wants a name, and a name is easy to find. A name who'd hurt you because the gate's watching is easy to find as well. I won't have that one. I want somebody worth the gate who knows when to stop hitting."

"Yes."

"Second. It goes like a Thursday card, big or not. Same split of the purse. Same rules said to the witnesses. Same call, mine, when I make it." She looked at him over the pen. "No special terms because it's bigger, and no special terms because it's you. This book's only worth something because it's the same book on a big day as on a small one. If I bend it once for a crowd, it's worth nothing to anybody."

"Yes."

"Third." Vell put the pen down again. "The girl's told before anybody signs anything."

He felt it go into him somewhere under the ribs, like cold water.

"Not after," said Vell. "Not on the morning, and not the night before either. Before. Whoever's idea it was." She did not take her eyes off him. "If something goes wrong in my circle she's the one who walks home from it. She'll want to have had a say while it was still a thing you could say something about."

"I'll tell her."

"I didn't ask what you'd do." She turned the ledger round to its own side again and wrote, at last, a single short line at the bottom of the page, and blotted it, and he could not read it upside down. "I'll send to Feryn. He's working the river towns this month, or he was a week ago. He'll know who's touring and who's worth a gate, and who'd rather stop than win." She looked up. "Now go and stand in somebody else's light."

He went back down the lane. He had got what he came for, and it should have felt like more than it did. The cold place under his ribs went down the hill with him, and he did not look at it.

---

Feryn came on Wednesday afternoon. He did not come to the Cinder House, or to Vell's table. He came to Torvin's, and when Cael came in from the mender's he found him in the yard, sitting on the low wall by the pump in his once-good coat, eating an apricot out of a paper and swinging one heel against the stones as if he lived there.

"Vell says you want to be loud," said Feryn.

"Yes."

"I came up on the barge this morning to find out why." Feryn finished the apricot and looked for somewhere to put the stone, and put it in his pocket. "Two days I've been thinking about it on a deck full of cabbages. You know what I thought first, when her note came? I thought, there it is. All winter that boy's been the most careful fighter I've ever stood across from, and he's finally found something stupid to do." Cael opened his mouth, and Feryn waved the apricot at him. "That was the barge talking. The barge is usually wrong. I've lost more money listening to barges than to bookmakers." He shifted along the wall to make room. "Sit down. Convince me."

Cael sat.

He told it the way he would have told a bout, from the beginning, in order. He told it without Coss's name, but with everything else: a sweep through the market in the autumn that nobody could account for, a thin file, a mark on it that a careful woman from regional had found and could not trace. Then a man who had come to tell him, off the record, that there was a way out, if he took it quietly. A drawer for things that were finished. Fourteen names on the back page of a book, five of them without names.

Feryn listened. He did not interrupt, except twice, and both times with a question that went straight to a joint in the thing. *Who told you about the drawer? Somebody who wanted you to have the choice.* And: *What does Vell get out of it? A full yard and a straight book.* Cael answered both and went on, and watched Feryn turn each answer over and fit it in, as he had watched him turn over the third exchange at Amrit's.

When it was done Feryn sat for a while with his elbows on his knees, looking at the pump.

"A boy who goes away quietly is a closed file," he said at last. "A boy three hundred people paid to see is a question somebody has to answer." He rubbed his jaw. "I've heard worse plans. I've made most of them, and I'm still here, which is more than the plans are." He was quiet a moment longer, and then he sat up. "All right. I know a name."

Cael waited.

"Darrow Innes." Feryn said it as though he were setting a weight down on the wall between them. "Bronze Rank 1. Iron Path. He's been doing this for money for eight years, which is longer than me, and he's good, and he's careful. I've never heard of him putting anybody in the healers' who didn't need to be put there." He looked at Cael sideways. "Do you know what Iron does?"

"I've watched two. A Bronze in my second week, and a Copper from Fenrow."

"Then you've watched it take." Feryn nodded slowly. "That's all Iron does, at the bottom of it. You hit it and it takes it, and it doesn't give it back. It goes in and goes somewhere and you never see it again. At Copper it's a thick hide. At Bronze it's a wall that's had eight years to learn where to put things." He turned the paper with the apricots in it round in his hands. "Darrow's touring. Down the river and back up it, the way I did, looking for gates with a name on them before his own evaluation in the autumn. He'll come for a marquee if it's a straight book and a full yard. He likes a straight book."

"Why him?"

"Because he won't kill you," said Feryn simply, "and he won't make it easy either, and either way you'll know something when it's over. And because if you beat him, it'll be said in five towns by the end of the month, and that's what you want." He looked at Cael. "Isn't it?"

"Yes."

Feryn put the paper of apricots down on the wall between them.

"I'll write to him tonight. He's two towns down, at a yard on the coal wharf, till the weekend." He stood, and brushed off his coat, and stood there a moment longer, looking down at Cael on the wall.

"You can ask," he said. "You've been wanting to since I sat down."

"Why are you doing it? You've got your own evaluation. You don't owe me anything."

Feryn did not answer quickly. He sat down again, which Cael had not expected, and looked at the pear tree in the corner, where the frost-browned flowers had all come down and the first small leaves had come out in their place.

"I did Bronze on my own," he said. "Nobody put a name across from mine that meant anything till I'd beaten every name in two circuits that didn't. I sat outside keepers' tables for the best part of two years at Copper, waiting to be worth somebody's afternoon. It took longer than it should have, and it cost more, and the people who could have made it shorter made it shorter for each other." He shrugged. "I always said that when I was somebody I wouldn't do that. Then I was somebody, and I'd forgotten." He picked up an apricot and looked at it. "And it's selfish as well, if you want the whole of it. I'd like to be there. I'd like to be able to say I was."

He put the apricot in his mouth and stood up for good this time.

"Tell Vell I've written," he said, round it. "She'll want to know before I do."

---

He told Vell. He went up to the Cinder House straight away, while Feryn went off down the lane toward the river with his coat flapping, and found her at her table with the Wednesday card half written, and told her: Darrow Innes, Bronze Rank 1, Iron Path, a letter going down the river tonight.

Vell wrote the name on the corner of her blotter and looked at it.

"I've seen him once," she said. "Three summers ago, at Fenrow, against a Blade with a temper. He let the Blade wear the temper out on him for four exchanges, and then he put him down so gently the man thanked him." She tapped the name with her pen. "He doesn't spend anything he doesn't have to. You'd like him." She was quiet for a moment. "Yes. If he'll come, yes."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when he's come." She went back to the card, and then, without looking up, in exactly the same voice: "Has the girl been told?"

It was nearly five o'clock. Lira was across the district at her sparring hour, and had been since two, and he had not seen her since the grey half that morning. In the grey half that morning he had done the box drill and the deflection and forty strokes at the post, and she had chalked a ring in the *W* column and written *less* beside it. And he had not said one word to her about Darrow Innes, or about Feryn on the barge, or about Monday, or about the board.

It was not that he had forgotten. He knew that was not it as soon as he went looking. He had carried it up the lane on Monday and back down again, and all through Tuesday at the post, and he had decided at every turn that he would tell her when there was something to tell. When it was real. When there was a name.

There had been a name for three hours.

"Tonight," he said.

Vell did not say anything. She looked at him over the card, and kept looking, until he understood that she had known the answer before she asked. Then she looked back down at what she was writing.

"Before the lamps," she said. "The widow's particular."

---

The fourth stair up to Lira's room talked. It had a creak in the middle that no amount of stepping to the side could get round, and she had told him once that she knew everybody in the house by how they managed it. When he came up that evening she had the door open before he reached the landing.

"You're early," she said. "And you went round the fourth on the left. You never go round it on the left." She stood in the doorway with her hair down and a shirt of hers half mended over one arm. "What's happened?"

"Can I come in?"

"Before the lamps." She stepped back. "Sit on the chair. Not the bed. The widow listens."

He sat on the chair. It was the only one, and it rocked. Lira sat down on the end of her bed with the shirt in her lap, and the evening light came in through the cracked pane she had mended with paper, the one that looked out toward Torvin's chimney, and lay across the boards between them.

He told her.

He told it the way he had told Feryn, from the beginning: the board, the fourteen names, the drawer. Vell's table on Monday. The three conditions. Feryn on the wall, a name, a letter going down the river tonight to a yard on a coal wharf. Darrow Innes. Bronze Rank 1. Iron Path.

Lira did not say anything while he talked. She sat with the shirt and the needle in her lap and did not sew, and she did not look at him; she looked at the floor between his boots. When he got to the letter going down the river, she put the needle into the shirt, very carefully, and left it there.

Then she looked up.

"You arranged this without asking me first."

"I was going to tell you tonight. Vell said—"

"That's telling." Her voice was quite level. It was the level of her voice at the barrel, which he had learned was the level she used when everything underneath it was not. "Telling's when it's done. Asking's when it isn't." She folded the shirt in half, and then in half again. "You went to Vell on Monday. You stood at the post with me on Tuesday, and this morning, and did forty strokes and let me chalk you a ring, and you'd already been to Vell. And then Feryn came up on a barge, and gave you a name, and you said yes to it, and it's in a letter." She put the folded shirt down beside her. "And now you've come up my stairs, round the fourth on the left, to tell me."

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"Vell made it a condition," said Lira. "Didn't she. That I be told." It was not a question. "*Told.* She knew you, Cael. She's been keeping a book on you since the autumn, and she knew exactly what you'd do, and she made a rule about it, and you still went and did it."

"I hadn't signed anything."

He heard how it sounded as it came out. So did she. She did not answer it. She only looked at him, and waited, and he sat in the rocking chair and heard it go on sounding in the small room for some time after it had stopped.

"Iron," said Lira quietly. "An Iron Bronze. I stood at that barrel in the sleet and watched a Bronze put you on your back in the first exchange, before you'd seen anything at all. I watched you get up at eight. I watched you go into the fourth knowing you couldn't read it fast enough, because you'd decided you had to know. And I didn't say a word, because it was yours to decide, and you'd decided it with me. We'd sat on that roof and gone through it. Every piece." She was very still. "This one's bigger than Feryn. And I'm hearing it after the letter's gone."

He had a great many things he could have said. He could feel them lining up behind his teeth, all of them true: that he had been going to tell her; that it had moved faster than he had meant it to; that he had wanted a name before he brought her anything, so that she would have something real to look at; that he had kept Vell's condition to the letter. All of it was true, and all of it was the wrong size, and he knew, sitting there, that if he said any of it she would hear an excuse and be right to.

So he put all of it down, and said the thing underneath it.

"I didn't ask you because I was afraid you'd say no."

Lira did not move.

"And then I'd have had to listen to you," he said. "Because you'd have been right, or near enough, and I'd have known it. And I'd decided already, on Monday, at the board. I didn't want to have to undecide it." He made himself look at her. "So I did it in an order where you couldn't. I don't think I said that to myself. But that's what I did."

It was very quiet. Somewhere below them the widow was moving about in her shop, putting the shutters up.

"I've done it before," he said. "To Hesk. I kept five things from him for months, and told myself every week that I'd tell him when I understood them. I kept the step from you for three weeks. You made a rule about it, and I said it back to you, and I meant it." He looked at his hands. "It's the worst thing I do. I do it when I'm frightened of what somebody will say. And I did it to you, on the one thing that's yours as much as mine."

Lira looked at him for a long time. It was the look she gave a stance at the mark, the whole of it, from the feet up, before anything had moved.

"Right," she said at last.

She picked up the folded shirt, and put it down again on the other side of her.

"Tell me all of it, then," she said. "Properly. All the parts you didn't, because you thought I'd say no to them. What Feryn said about Iron. What Vell said about the crowd. What you're going to do about it, if you know, and what you don't know, if you don't." She drew her feet up onto the bed and sat with her arms round her knees. "If there's a piece you kept back because it'd frighten me, I want that piece first. I'd rather be worried and informed," she said, "than calm and useless."

So he told her all of it. It took a long time. He told her Feryn's first thought on the barge and Vell's *not all of that is the kind of looking you want*. He told her Feryn's two years outside the keepers' tables. He told her what Feryn had said about Iron taking things and not giving them back, and that he did not know what to do about that yet, not even slightly, and that it frightened him, and that he had not said so to Feryn or Vell. She asked questions, short ones, and each of them went to a joint, and by the end of it she was not looking at the floor any more. She was looking at the wall above his head, the way she looked at nothing when she was adding.

"Iron," she said. "We'll have to find out what it does from the inside. I don't know anything about it. Nobody at the academy fought Iron against Wind girls; they thought we'd run away from it." She pressed her chin on her knees. "We will, as well. Some of the time."

"Then you're—"

"I'm in." She said it flatly. "I was always going to be in. That was never the question. You'd have known that, if you'd asked." She looked at him then, straight, and there was nothing level left in her voice. "But hear this, because I'm only going to say it once. I'm not angry any more. I've decided not to be, and I've done it. But it was given. You didn't earn it tonight, and you haven't paid it off by telling me now. It's just given." She held his eyes. "This forgiveness doesn't renew automatically. If you do it again, with something this size, I don't know what I'll do. I honestly don't. I only know it won't be this."

"I won't."

"Don't promise me. Show me." She unwound her arms from her knees. "In a month you can promise."

The light on the boards had gone orange and was going grey. She looked toward the window, toward Torvin's chimney beyond the paper mend, and he thought she was going to send him home. Instead she said, quite differently, almost to herself:

"You're more like him than you probably realize."

"Hesk?"

"He knew about you for years. He sat on it. He decided on his own what you could carry, and when, and you found out in a side room with a word on a plate." She turned the needle in the folded shirt. "And he'd have done this as well, I think. What you're doing. Made himself so known nobody could put him in a drawer." She looked back at him. "Both halves. That's what I mean."

"Is it meant kindly?"

Lira considered it.

"Half of it," she said.

Down in the street somebody was lighting the first lamp, and the widow's voice came up the stairwell, not loud, saying a word that could have been anybody's name and was plainly meant for both of them. Lira stood up.

"Go home," she said. "Before she comes up. Grey half tomorrow, at first light. Not after it." She held the door. "And Cael."

He stopped on the landing.

"Go round the fourth on the right," said Lira. "Like you always do. I want to know it's you."

# Chapter 55 — The Term

Lira heard him go round the fourth stair on the right, and then she heard the street door, and then she sat on the end of her bed in the near dark and let herself shake.

It was not much. It was her hands, mostly, and something in the backs of her knees. She sat and waited for it to finish, the way she waited for a drill to finish teaching her something, and it took longer than she would have liked. When it was done she lit the candle with the third match, because the first two broke, and sat looking at the flame.

She had not been lying. She had decided not to be angry and she had done it, there in front of him, and it was done. That was the easy part. She had always been able to put anger down when she chose to, as you could put a stone down if you had picked it up yourself. What she could not put down was the thing that had been under the anger, which she had not let him see, and which had come up through the floor of her as soon as he was gone.

An Iron Bronze. Eight years for money.

She got up and went to the window and stood with her forehead nearly on the paper mend. Across the roofs Torvin's chimney was a black shape against a sky that still had some green in it. He would be in the yard by now, or on the stairs, going up past the brothers' door to the cot under Yeni's lamp. He would sit on the end of the cot and write it all down. He would write *Lira, told* and the day, and something about the fourth stair, probably, because he wrote things down like that. And then he would write that it was the worst thing he did, because he had said so to her face and he would not let himself say a thing out loud that he had not also put in the book.

She thought about the sleet.

She had stood at the barrel with her arms folded on the top of the rope, the way she always stood, and watched a man in a good coat stroll about the circle with his hands half open. Then the air had come down out of nowhere on top of Cael and folded his guard flat and put him on his back before he had seen anything at all. She had not moved. Her arms had stayed folded on the rope. She had stood there and counted with Vell, under her breath, because counting was something to do with her mouth, and at six he had rolled over, and at eight he had stood up, and she had not moved.

Afterwards he had said she looked calm. She had let him say it.

She had not been calm. She had been doing the thing she had learned at the academy, in the examination hall, on the morning she skipped the middle of the deflection in front of three examiners and a slate. She had done it clean. She had done it better than the form; she had known it was better while she was doing it, and she had seen in their faces that they knew it too, and that it did not matter. She had stood still in the middle of the floor while they wrote, with her hands at her sides and her face giving them nothing at all. Then she had walked out of the hall, very straight, and down the back stairs where the coal went up, and sat on the third step from the bottom in the dark, and come apart there, quietly, where nobody could see it but the coal.

She had come apart afterwards, and nobody had ever known. She had been rather proud of that, for a long time.

At the barrel in the sleet she had done the same thing, only without the stairs, and it had nearly not worked. That was what she had been carrying round since the winter without looking at it. At the fourth exchange, when Feryn came in fast and put his hand where Cael was going and turned him over, she had been halfway through the rope before she knew it. Her hands had been on it and her weight had been going. Then Vell had called it, and she had stopped, and nobody had seen. But she knew.

And this one was bigger.

She turned away from the window and sat down on the rocking chair he had sat on, which was still warm, and put her elbows on her knees.

She could stand back. That was the first thing she made herself look at, because it was the thing that had been sitting in the corner of the room since he said *Darrow Innes*, waiting to be looked at. She could coach him from the post in the grey half, and do it well. She could build him the best two weeks anybody had ever built for a Copper, and then, on the day, stand somewhere further off than the barrel, by the gate, say, with the girls from the wall. She would not have her hands on the rope. She would not count under her breath with Vell. Nobody would think less of her. Most of the people who trained fighters in this district did not stand at the rope at all; they stood at the back, or went to the eel stalls and waited to be told.

She looked at it honestly, all of it, the way he would have wanted her to, and the way she would have made him.

Then she put it down.

She would not be any use by the gate. She knew how he fought now better than anyone in the district, better than Vell, perhaps, though Vell would never have allowed it. She knew where his hips went square when he was tired and where his left foot turned out on the back corner, and what his ears did when he was going to say *gone* and did not want to. She knew that he nodded before he moved, and that Dessa had read it, and that a Bronze who had been doing this for eight years would read it sooner. And she knew that the touch to her chin from the barrel had told him about the nod in Dessa's third exchange, and that he had seen it and believed it in the middle of a fight. Nobody by the gate could do that. Nobody but her could do that.

So she was in. She had said it to him and it had been true when she said it, and now she said it to herself in her own room with nobody listening, and it was still true.

That left the other thing, the one the stairs had been for.

---

She took the tin out of her pocket.

It was a flat tin that had once had throat sweets in it, with a picture of a lady in a hat on the lid, worn nearly off. She had carried it since the autumn. She had counted it on this bed perhaps a hundred times, and a fortnight ago she had counted it and found that there was enough in it for a cart. Fenrow, or further. She had sat on the low wall at the Cinder House and told him so, and then told him she had put it back in her pocket, and she had meant that too.

She opened it and counted it again now, by the candle, out of habit. It was the same as it had been. It did not know anything had happened.

*During,* she thought, *I'll be at the barrel.* She would be where she always was, with her arms on the rope and her face giving away nothing at all, and she would watch him and tell him what she saw, with her chin and her fingers and once, perhaps, with his name. She would not come through the rope. She would not count under her breath with Vell. She would coach him, all of her, from the first exchange to the last, and she would not let one bit of her be anything else while he was in there, because he would look at her, and he had to see a coach.

*And after—*

She sat with that for some time. It was harder than the first part. The first part she had done before, at the academy and in the sleet. The second part she had never done. She had always gone down the back stairs.

She looked at the cracked pane, and the paper over it, and Torvin's chimney gone black now beyond it, and said it out loud, quietly, to see what it sounded like in the room.

"During, I coach," said Lira. She heard her own voice go flat and steady on it, the barrel voice, and was glad. Then, more slowly: "After, I get to be a person."

It sounded strange. It sounded like something somebody would say who was not her. She said it again, and the second time it sounded a little more like her, and the third time she found that she meant it. After the call, whatever the call was, she would not go down any stairs. She would let it come up wherever she was standing, in front of whoever was there, him included. If it was fear, he would see it. If it was the other thing, he would see that. She had spent a long time being the girl who came apart only where nobody could see, and she had been proud of it, and she thought now, sitting on a warm chair in the dark, that it had been a kind of lie. It was not a large one. But he had told her the truth about himself tonight, the worst of it, out loud in this room, and she did not see why she should keep a smaller one about herself.

That was a term. She had set it, and she would keep it, and nobody but her would ever know it had been set.

She pinched the candle out.

In the dark she thought about Iron. She knew nothing. She knew that it took a blow and did not give it back; she had seen a Copper Iron from Fenrow take a long right from Dessa on the forearms and not even look at it. She knew that power was no good against a thing whose whole Path was taking power in. You could not hit through it. You would break your hands trying, and he had good hands, and they were the only ones he had.

So not power. She lay down on the bed in her clothes and looked at the ceiling and added.

Power went in and went somewhere. Everybody said so. *It goes in and goes somewhere, and you never see it again.* Very well. If you could not hit through a thing, you could perhaps hit it in the same place, again and again, before it had finished putting the last one away. She had a strike like that herself, one strike, short and straight from the hip. She had spent two weeks at the post on it when she was new to the district, and nothing about it was meant to hurt anybody. It was meant to arrive in the same place every time. She had taught it to him in the autumn. He had liked it, because it was the one strike he owned.

*Precision that piles up,* she thought. *Not one that ends things. One that adds.*

It was her way. It was the way she had always wanted to do everything and had never been let: one small true thing, the same small true thing, until it added up to more than anybody had thought it could. Nobody at the academy had ever let her finish adding anything.

She was asleep before she had finished adding this.

---

In the morning, before it was light, she went down through the widow's shop with her boots in her hand and out into the little yard behind it, where the widow kept her barrels of tallow and her broken things. The old straw tick was where it had been since the autumn, rolled and tied against the wall under a sack, too lumpy to sleep on and too good, the widow said, to burn.

The widow was up. She was always up; she was standing at her back door in her shawl with a cup, watching the sky. She looked at Lira, and at the tick, and at Lira again.

"I'll give you a mark for it," said Lira.

"It's not worth a mark."

"Then give me change."

The widow considered this, and gave her change, out of her apron, in coppers. Lira took the mark out of the tin to pay for it, and stood holding the open tin in the grey light with one coin fewer in it. It was the first thing she had ever bought out of it. She had saved it for a door, and then for a cart, and the first thing it had paid for was a lumpy straw mattress to tie round a post.

She shut the lid, and put the tin back in her pocket. Then she put the tick on her shoulder and went up through the lanes with it, at first light, toward the Cinder House.

When Cael came into the yard the post in the grey half was fat.

Lira had wrapped it from the height of his knee to the height of his head in an old straw tick, folded double, and tied it round and round with a rope that had seen better days on a barge. The grey canvas with the chalk columns on it was hanging from a nail on the wall behind, where it would be safe. She was standing beside the post with her sleeves rolled and the chalk in her hand, and the look on her face was the barrel look, and he understood that the last evening was finished with, and that she would not mention it, and that he should not either.

"Hit it," she said.

He hit it. It went *whuff*, softly, and took his fist in up to the knuckles, and gave nothing back.

"Again."

He hit it again. *Whuff.* There was no mark. There was no sound worth calling a sound, and nothing jarred up his arm, and when he took his fist away the straw swelled back slowly into the shape it had been, as if he had never touched it.

"That's what it's like," said Lira. "Or that's what I think it's like. I asked Doss last night in your kitchen, and he said near enough, only it's worse, because the post doesn't look at you." She came round the post and stood beside him. "You can't hurt it. You can't knock it down. It won't tell you when you've done well. You'll hit it a thousand times and at the end it'll look the same as it does now." She held out her hand, palm up. "Give me your fist."

He gave it to her. She rubbed the chalk across his knuckles, hard, until they were white, and let go.

"Five," she said. "The same place. As fast as you can make them true. Not as hard. As true."

He put five into the straw at the height of a man's lowest rib. He did it fast. He felt each one go in and come back. When he stepped away there were five small white marks on the ticking where his knuckles had kissed it, and they were spread across the width of his whole hand and a little more.

"That's a man's side," said Lira, looking at them. "All of it. That's not a place."

"They were all on the rib."

"They were all on his ribs, if he's got ribs the size of a door." She brushed the marks off with the flat of her hand. "Again."

He did it again, and again. She stood at his shoulder and watched his wrist, not his fist. After the fourth time she took his wrist in her hand and turned it a few degrees, so that the first two knuckles came in square instead of the last two. After the sixth she told him he was pulling the strike back before it had finished going in, to get the next one out faster, and that every one of them was arriving a little short because of it. After the ninth she said nothing at all for a long time and only watched.

"Every one of those wanted to be the one that counted," she said at last.

"Isn't that the idea?"

"Not here." She tapped the post. "Not one of them counts. The first doesn't, and the second doesn't, and the fifth doesn't. Throw him a strike that has to count by itself, and he'll take it, and put it wherever he puts things, and stand there, and you'll have spent it." She put her finger on the straw at the height of a lowest rib, on one small place. "Forget the strike. What you want is somewhere on him that hasn't finished healing yet. Then you put the next one in it before it has. Then the next."

"That's backward from everything I've done since the autumn," he said. "I've spent all winter making every one I throw worth throwing."

"Then do it backward." She chalked his knuckles again. "You're good at learning things twice."

He thought, while he hit, about a fight he had watched in his second week in Ardenmere, from the rope, with Lira beside him and the notebook on his knee. A Bronze Iron against a Stone woman with grey at her temples. It had gone seven exchanges, and the thing he had written down about the Iron man was that he struck short and hard, with no reach at all, and that everything he hit, he seemed to hit in exactly the same place.

He stopped with his fist half drawn back.

"They do it," he said. "Iron. That's what they do themselves."

Lira looked at him.

"The Bronze in my second week. Against the Stone woman. He hit her in the same place every time. I wrote it down. I didn't know why." He looked at the five white marks, which were a little closer together this time, nearly inside a palm. "Maybe that's how they think. Because it's how their own bodies work. Things pile up in one place."

"Then he'll know what you're doing," said Lira slowly. "When you start doing it."

"Yes."

"And you'll have to do it anyway."

"Yes."

She looked at him for a moment, with her head on one side, and then she nodded, short, and he saw something go across her face that he had not seen there since before Feryn. It was gone before he could name it.

"That," she said, "is the first useful thing either of us has said about Iron." She chalked his fist. "Five. Same place. True."

By the end of the third morning the five white marks would fit under two of her fingers. By the end of the fifth they were coming closer together still. They had also begun, though neither of them said so, to arrive faster than the straw could swell back between them.

Doss came down into the kitchen at the hour he always came down, an hour before his watch, to eat what Torvin's wife had left for him under a cloth. He found Cael at the table with the Log open and a candle, and sat down across from him with his bad knee out straight under the bench, and ate for a while without talking.

"The girl asked me about Iron," he said at last.

"She told me."

"I told her what I could. She didn't write any of it down. She just listened, with her head on one side, like a hen." Doss broke his bread. "You'll want it as well, I suppose."

"If you'll tell it again."

Doss was quiet for a while. He was a big slow man with a flat face that had been hit a great many times, and when he was thinking his scarred knuckles went still on the table.

"Twice," he said. "At Bronze. One season and a half, I was Bronze in her book. Two of those were Iron men. Different men. Same thing." He looked at the candle. "You hit a Shield, it shines. You know you've hit it. You hit Stone, you feel the grit go flat; you know. Force shoves you after. Ember, you get the heat in your face. Something comes back. Every Path I ever hit, something came back off it and told me I'd been there." He put his bread down. "Iron, nothing. You put your best into it, and it goes in, and that's all. Like dropping a stone down a well and never hearing it land."

"And then?"

"And then you do it again. And again. And somewhere in the third exchange you start to think you never threw it. That's the bit nobody tells you." Doss looked at his own hands on the table. "You stop believing your hands. They tell you they've landed, and you can feel they've landed, and the man in front of you is stood there like he's waiting for a cart. So you start to think your hands are lying. And once you think that, you're done. You start holding back, to save them. Or you start swinging wild, to make them prove it." He picked up his bread again. "Either way he's got you, and he's not done a thing."

Cael wrote it down. *Doss: nothing comes back. You stop believing your hands. Then you're done.*

"Was there anything," he said, "that he did after? After you'd landed a good one?"

Doss chewed for a long time.

"He'd wait," he said at last. "The second one would. After a big one. Not long. You'd think he was being polite." He shrugged. "I thought he was being polite. I was fifty-one fights in and I thought an Iron Bronze was being polite to me." He looked at Cael, and there was something nearly like a smile at the corner of the flat mouth. "Write that down as well. Write down that I was a fool."

Cael wrote down *He'd wait. After a big one.* He did not write down the other part.

The woman who kept the chalk board at the bottom of the lane of yards was sitting on her stool beside it in the sun, as she always was, with her slate of prices and a tin cup of something hot. He had walked past her perhaps three hundred times since the autumn. She had grey at her temples and a way of sitting very square on the stool with both feet flat, and it was only on the second morning of the straw post, walking down from the old man's gate, that he looked at her properly and knew her.

He stopped.

"You fought the Iron man," he said. "In the autumn. At the Cinder House. My second week. Seven exchanges, and you put your hand up."

She looked at him over the cup for a while.

"I did," she said. "You're the circuit boy from Torvin's. The one who watches feet." She drank. "I heard you've got an Iron coming."

"Bronze. From down the river."

"Bronze." She set the cup on the edge of the board, between *Tuesday* and *a quarter-mark the hour*. "Well. He hit me in the same place every time. You'll have seen that, if you were watching."

"I wrote it down."

"I counted after. In the evening, from the marks." She touched her own side, low, under the arm, where her coat lay against it. "Eleven. All in a place you could have covered with a cup." She looked up the lane, toward the old man's gate, and back. "And every time I got one in on him, a real one, he'd stand there a moment before he came on again. Not long. Like a man who's carried a full bucket across a yard and wants to set it down before he picks up the next." She shrugged. "I thought I was hurting him, at first. I wasn't. He was only putting it somewhere."

"Where?"

"If I knew that," she said, "I'd have won."

He thanked her. She went back to her slate. When he was halfway down the lane she called after him, not loudly, without turning round on the stool:

"He'll let you hit him. The first while. Don't let that fool you into thinking he's slow."

He wrote it down at the bottom of the lane, against the wall, standing up. *Chalk-board woman (the Stone woman, wk 2): he'd stand a moment after a good one. Setting a bucket down. Putting it somewhere.* Then, under it, because two people had told him the same thing without knowing each other: *Doss said it too. He'd wait.*

He looked at it a long time. Two was not three. It was not a pattern yet. He drew a small ring round it, and left it open.

Darrow Innes said yes on the Sunday.

The answer came up the river in Feryn's hand, on the back of a carter's slip, and Feryn brought it to the Cinder House himself, after the card, and put it on Vell's table without a word. *He'll come. Sunday fortnight. Straight book, full gate, her rules, her call. Says to tell the keeper he saw her at Fenrow and he'll take her word over a magistrate's.*

Vell read it. Then she turned the ledger round to a clean page, dated it, and set down the terms beneath: the purse, the split, the same as any Sunday, which Cael had heard her say to a hundred fighters and said back to her now, word for word, standing at the table. It went until somebody put a hand up, or could not go on, or she called it. Once he was over the rope, nobody stopped it but her. What happened inside was his to carry. Nobody was making him do it.

When he had finished, Vell did not hand him the pen. She looked past him, along the rope, to where Lira was standing at the barrel with her arms folded on the top of it.

"Has she been told?"

Lira came across the dirt to the table before he could answer. She stood beside him and looked at Vell and not at him.

"I've been asked," said Lira. "Late." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to. "He knows."

Vell looked at the two of them, one and then the other, for a long moment. Something happened at the corner of her mouth that did not happen often.

"Then sign," she said. She turned Feryn's slip over and gave him the pen.

He wrote his name on the back of the carter's slip, under Darrow's terms, where it would go back down the river to the man who had sent them. It was not her book. Her book got a line in her own hand, as it always did: *assessed Cu (vouched)*. Then she took one of the bills for lamp oil out of the drawer of her table and wrote the card on the back of it, larger than he had ever seen her write anything. *LAST BOUT, SUNDAY FORTNIGHT. DARROW INNES (Br 1, Iron Path) against assessed Cu (vouched).* She wrote a second one, and a third, and kept going.

"Every board," she said, and held the first of them out without looking round.

It was not the pie boy who took it. It was Red Cap, who had been standing at the end of the table for some minutes without anybody noticing him, and the other boy beside him, the one whose brother was in the carters', both of them very clean and very serious. Red Cap took the first card and the other boy took the second, and they looked at each other once across the table, and Vell counted out the rest of the cards between them, five each, evenly. Then they were gone through the gate in the wall at a run, in two directions, toward boards that would never once be on the same street.

Cael watched them go.

"I gave them the lists," said Vell, to her ledger. "I'm told it's quicker."

# Chapter 56 — Being Seen

He had written to Hesk on the night of the fourth stair, all of it, by the bead of Yeni's lamp, and posted it in the west bag at noon the next day. Darrow Innes and the board and the fourteen names. Vell's three things, and the one he had kept only to the letter. Lira's room, and what he had said in it, which had been harder to write down than to say. *I'm telling you the next thing as it comes,* he had written at the end, *including the part where I did it wrong.*

So when the letter from Hesk was against the salt crock on the Monday, he thought for a moment, coming in at the kitchen door, that it was the answer. Then he saw the date in the corner, in Hesk's square hand, and counted, and knew that it could not be. It had been written the night after Coss's pie stall and the cooper's roof. It had been a week on the road. It had passed his own somewhere between here and Denvash, two letters going opposite ways in two leather bags on two carts, and neither of them knowing.

He took it out to the low wall by the pump. The pear tree had finished with its flowers. The last of them lay brown in the corner of the yard where Torvin's wife had swept them, and the tree had put out small green leaves in their place, all at once, as if it had changed its mind about something.

He broke the seal.

*Cael,*

*You asked me a question a month ago and I said I'd answer it when I had an answer I'd sign.*

It was the question. He had asked it in the Ilsev letter, at the end, on the night of the roof after the post. *If whoever made that mark wanted me gone, would any of that matter?* Hesk had kept it. *Your question I'm keeping.* And now here it was, answered, in a letter written before Hesk could possibly have known that the question had stopped being a question and become a card on every board in the district.

He read the clock. Three families, and a card woman, and one loose spring the size of a fingernail paring. He could see Hesk's bench as he read it, the lamp and the drawer marked *Springs, fine*, and the back off a thing that everybody else had only told stories about. Then he came to the part that was the answer, and stopped, and read it again with the pear leaves moving over the page.

*It isn't a wall. I won't tell you it is. A wall would keep things out, and nothing you've got there will keep anybody out who's set on coming in. But a thing that's known can't be put in a drawer without somebody asking where it went.*

*A drawer.*

Hesk did not know about the drawer. He could not know. Coss had said it at a pie stall in Ardenmere, on the same day Hesk was writing this in Denvash, and nobody had told either of them what the other had said. And Hesk had reached into the dark from his own bench, a week behind, and put his hand on the very word.

He sat very still on the wall for a while. Then he read on.

*A thing that's talked about gets brought to a bench, in the end, and somebody takes the back off. The quiet things are the ones that go out with the ashes.*

*Being seen is not the same as being safe. But it's not nothing.*

He read that twice. It was the plainest sentence in the letter. It did not promise him anything. Hesk never promised him things he could not put his name under, and he had not done it here. He had said exactly as much as was true and stopped, the way he stopped a measurement at the last mark he trusted.

*Keep being known. Keep me told. I'm proud of you past anything I've the words for, and I'll say so properly when I've found them.*

*— H.*

He sat with it on his knee. *I'll say so properly when I've found them.* That was like Hesk too. He would not say a thing until he could say it right, and he would rather leave it in a letter as a promise than send it half made. Somewhere in Denvash, at a bench, a man was still looking for words, and had told him so.

He did not copy anything into the Log. He folded the letter along its own folds and put it in the front of the Log, inside the cover, on top of the other one, so that the two lay together. *You're describing a home.* And *it's not nothing.*

Lira read it that evening on the side wall of the Cinder House yard, after the post, with the bread. She read it twice, as she read all Hesk's letters, and when she came to the drawer she stopped and looked at him over the paper and did not say anything. When she came to the end she folded it and gave it back to him.

"He doesn't know about Darrow," she said.

"Not yet. Mine's on the road. They'll have passed each other."

"He doesn't need to." She tore the bread and gave him the bigger half. "He's answered it anyway. He's only answered it before you asked."

He tried them both together on the Tuesday night, in Torvin's yard, facing the trough.

He had not meant to. It was not in the plan; nothing borrowed was in the plan, and he and Lira had settled that on the first morning of the straw post without either of them having to say it. But it had been sitting in him since Feryn's wall like a question at the end of a page. There were two of them now, side by side in the dark behind his breastbone, the leaning one and the heavy one. He had only ever picked up one at a time.

So he stood with his back to the house and the moon on the whitewash, and went in.

He found the Wind first, as he always did, and held it, the readiness, the tilt. It was slow coming, but it came, a little, the ground going short under him by a finger's width. Then, without letting go of it, he reached past it for the other one.

The Wind went out of his hands as soon as he reached. It did not fade. It was simply gone, the way a thought goes when somebody says your name. He stood there with nothing.

He tried again. This time he went for the heavy one first, and began to gather it, the long breath, the middle going still and thick; he could see it start in his shadow on the wall. Then, half through the breath, he reached back for the Wind. The gathering broke. It did not go forward, or anywhere. It only came apart in the middle of him, like a stack of bricks with the bottom one pulled, and went nowhere at all, and the water in the trough lay flat and still with the moon in it and did not so much as shiver.

His arms went heavy anyway. That was the worst of it. He had got nothing, and the wet sand came into both arms from the shoulder down all the same, as if he had.

He sat on the low wall for most of an hour with his hands in his lap and thought about it.

One at a time. Slowly. The Wind came when it liked, and when he asked it came late and small. The other needed a whole breath, and showed in his middle like a stone in a sack, and cost him his arms. And the two of them would not sit in his hands at the same time; reaching for one let go of the other. He could not do it. He was not sure there was any *it* to do.

In the morning in the grey half he told Lira. She listened with the chalk in her hand. Then she went to the canvas hanging on its nail on the wall, where the post's columns had gone to keep them safe from the straw. Under the *P*, below the last ring, she made one stroke with no ring round it, and beside it, in her smallest letters, *both. nothing. arms anyway.*

She looked at it.

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"Now we know." She put the chalk in her pocket. "Now neither of us is going to stand at that barrel on Sunday hoping for something that isn't there." She went back to the straw post, and slapped it once with her open hand, so that it went *whuff*. "This is what's there. Five. Same place."

Feryn came back up the river on the Thursday with his coat buttoned to the chin and a cold, and three pages of notes folded in his pocket. He spread them out on the low wall at the side of the Cinder House yard, weighted with stones, while the Thursday card went on behind them in the circle.

"I went down and watched him," he said. "Saturday, at the coal wharf. Against a Force woman from the salt flats, Copper Rank 8, very good. He knew I was there. He didn't care." Feryn sneezed. "And I found three people up and down that river who've been in a circle with him and were willing to talk about it for the price of supper. Which you owe me, by the way. Both of you."

Lira had come round the rope. She sat on the wall on Feryn's other side and pulled the nearest page toward her.

"Tell it," she said.

"First thing. He gives you the first exchange." Feryn put his finger on the top of the first page. "Free. He hardly blocks and he hardly slips. He stands there and lets you empty your pockets into him, and watches you do it. The Force woman put a shove into him that would have moved a cart. He went back a foot. That's all." He looked at Cael. "He wants your best on the table early. All of it, where you can both see it. So that you both know."

"Know what?"

"That it's no good." Feryn sneezed again, and wiped his nose, and did not smile. "That's the whole of him, the way I see it. The Path's only what he stands on. What actually beats people is what they start thinking in the second exchange, when they've given him their best and he's still standing there looking mildly interested. The woman from the salt flats was good. She was better than good. By the third exchange she was hitting him like she was trying to wake somebody up, and by the fourth she'd stopped trying at all, and he put her down very gently and helped her up." He tapped the page. "She told me afterwards she didn't remember deciding to stop. She only remembered noticing she had."

Cael thought about Doss at the kitchen table. *You stop believing your hands.*

"You've lost to that," said Lira. It was not a question.

"Not to him." Feryn was quiet for a moment. He folded the corner of the page over, and then flattened it out again.

"To an Iron at Copper," he said, when I was twenty, in a tannery yard in a town you've never heard of. He wasn't very good. He wasn't a patch on Darrow. And I hit him for three exchanges and in the fourth I stood there with my hands up and I couldn't make them go. I'd stopped believing they'd do anything." He looked at the circle, where a Blade was getting up off the dirt. "I've never told anybody that. I've been telling people for four years I lost because the yard was wet."

Nobody said anything for a while. Lira, who had been looking at the notes, looked at Feryn instead, and then back at the notes, and Cael saw that she had put it away somewhere to be careful of.

"What did they say it felt like?" he said. "The three. Hitting him."

Feryn turned to the second page.

"They all said the same thing, and none of them had met each other, and none of them had the words." He ran his finger down it. "It goes away. That's all any of them could tell me. You hit him and it goes away from you, *in* and *back*, and *down*, one of them said, toward the back of him. Like hitting a man who's leaning on a door you can't see."

"Which side?"

Feryn looked up.

"Which side of him does it go to?" said Cael. "When it goes back and down. Left or right?"

"I didn't ask." Feryn frowned at the page. "I didn't think to. Does it matter?"

"How does he stand?"

"Right foot forward. Left back, and set. He sets that back foot as if he's nailing it to the floor; you can see it go down." Feryn lifted his own left heel a little off the stones, and put it down hard, to show. "He never moves it much. The front foot does all the walking."

Cael had a bill of the mender's in his pocket, for washers, and a stub of pencil. He turned the bill over on the wall and drew a man on the back of it as Corvane had taught him to draw a man, a stick figure, with the right foot forward and the left foot back and planted. Then he drew a line from the right shoulder, where a strike might land, across the chest and down. He drew another from the right ribs, across. Another from the jaw, down the neck and across. Another from the left shoulder, straight down. And all of them, if they were going back and down toward a left foot nailed to the floor, had to go through the same narrow place to get there: the low left side, where the ribs stopped and the hip began. Below that there was only one leg for any of it to go down.

He looked at it. Everything he had drawn ran downhill to one drain.

"You're drawing him like a gutter," said Feryn, looking over his shoulder.

"Everything has to get to the back foot," said Cael slowly. "Wherever it goes in. And there's one way down to the back foot, on that side. So everything crosses there." He put the point of the pencil on the low left side of the stick figure, where all the lines met. "There."

Lira had leaned over too. She looked at the drawing for a long time without saying anything.

"The chalk-board woman," she said. "He'd stand a moment after a good one. Setting the bucket down."

"Doss said it too."

"Putting it down that leg." Lira touched the drawing with one finger, just above the pencil point, very lightly, as if it might hurt it. "If it all goes through there, and he has to stop a moment every time to get it down—"

"Then if you put the next one in there while he's still putting the last one down," said Cael. "While it's still crossing."

Feryn looked from one of them to the other.

"That," he said, "is either the cleverest thing I've heard about Iron in eight years of listening, or the two of you have drawn a man on the back of a washer bill and fallen in love with the drawing." He sneezed. "Which is it?"

"I don't know yet," said Cael. He wrote, small, beside the point where the lines met: *the crossing.* Then, under it, because he would not call a thing true that he had only drawn: *Claim. Evidence: 3 (h), Doss (h), Stone woman (h). Ruling: pending.*

"Then find out," said Feryn, and folded his notes, and blew his nose. "Somebody owes me a supper."

On the tenth night they sat on the front steps of Torvin's after the lamps, which the widow would have said was too late, except that it was Torvin's steps and not hers.

It had been a long day. The five white marks on the straw would fit under one of Lira's fingers now, more often than not, and in the afternoon she had started making him do it moving: back three steps, in two, five strikes to one place while she pushed him about with her free hand, and then out again before she could catch his wrist. His knuckles were raw under the chalk. His shoulders ached in a new place, low, from throwing short. He sat on the third step with his forearms on his knees and the street going quiet in front of him: a cart being backed into a yard somewhere, a window shutting, a dog asking a question at the end of the lane and getting no answer.

Lira sat on the step above him with her knees drawn up. She had been quiet since the lamps, and he had learned that her quiet usually had something at the far end of it.

"I've been adding a thing all day," she said at last. "I can't make it come out."

"Say it."

"Say you win." She was looking at the street, not at him. "Say it all goes the way you drew it on Feryn's bill, and you put a Bronze down in front of three hundred people, and Vell writes your name. Then it's Monday." She turned her head. "I don't mean Monday at the post. I mean Monday in a drawer at regional. What's different in there?"

He thought about it properly, because she had asked him to.

"Nothing," he said. "The drawer's the same drawer. The mark's the same mark. Legal's as slow the Monday after as the Monday before." He turned his raw knuckles over and looked at them in the lamplight from the window behind. "Whoever put a mark on a docket isn't going to rub it off because a boy won something in a yard by the river."

"Then why?"

"Because—" He stopped. He knew the answer. He had known it at the board. But he could not find the shape of it out loud, and he sat there on the step with it half made in his mouth.

"It doesn't change what you are," said Lira. "It changes who'd have to explain themselves if something happened to you."

He turned round on the step and looked up at her.

She was still looking at the street. "It's his letter," she said. "Your grandfather's. I've been turning it over since Monday. *A thing that's known can't be put in a drawer without somebody asking where it went.*" She said the words carefully, as if they belonged to somebody else and she was handing them back. "Right now, if you went, the only person who'd have to explain it would be a man at a desk, to a drawer. After Sunday, if you went, he'd have to explain it to the bread woman. And Vell. And the boys. And three hundred people who paid at the gate." She put her chin on her knees. "That's not safe. He said it isn't. But somebody would have to stand up somewhere and say where you'd gone. And they'd have to say it to a lot of people."

He looked at her for a long time.

"You had it first," he said.

"I always have it first when it's you." She did not smile. "Now say the other part. The part you've not said to anybody."

He looked back at the street.

"I'm frightened I've read it wrong," he said. "Not Darrow. Them. I've read the whole district like a stance, and I've decided what it'll do if I go missing, and I've never once seen it do it." He made himself go on. "And there's a worse one. That I'll have made myself the easiest thing in Ardenmere to find. That somebody I haven't even thought of will look up because of the noise. And I'll have done that, and not you, and not Hesk."

Lira was quiet for a while.

"Good," she said.

He looked round.

"You'd be a fool if you weren't." She drew her knees in tighter. "Put it on the post. A stroke for it, and a ring, and *frightened* beside it in small letters. Then hit the straw anyway." She looked down at him then, straight, for the first time since they had sat down. "And you didn't do it, by the way. We did. On Feryn's bill. "I should have."

"You should have." She bumped his shoulder with her knee, not hard. "I'm still checking, you know. About that. Every day. I'll go on checking for a while."

"I know."

"Good."

The dog at the end of the lane asked its question again, and this time somebody's door opened and somebody told it to be quiet, and it was. They sat on the steps a while longer without saying anything, and it was not the kind of quiet that needed filling.

On the Wednesday before the bout, at first light, Coss was standing at the gate in the wall of the Cinder House yard.

In the autumn he had walked every lane of the district and asked nothing about the yards, and in the spring he had sat at a pie stall in his Sunday coat. "Some of it."

Coss nodded slowly. Then, as if he had decided to stop putting it off, he took a folded paper out of his inside pocket and held it out.

It was a district return. Cael had seen the form at the post, at the clerk's counter: a long sheet that went to regional every week, with boundaries and permits and pigs. Halfway down it, in the clerk's careful hand, under *Public notices posted in the district*, somebody had copied Vell's card word for word. *LAST BOUT, SUNDAY FORTNIGHT. DARROW INNES (Br 1, Iron Path) against assessed Cu (vouched).* Somebody else had drawn a single neat line under it in red.

"Your post's clerk copies every board in the district into his return," said Coss. "He always has. It's in the form. Nobody's read that column in twenty years." He took the paper back and folded it. "Somebody read this one. My section head put it on my desk on Monday, with that line under it, and went away. He didn't say anything. Last time he said a view. This time he didn't say anything at all."

He put the paper away.

"That's what I came to tell you. That nobody's saying anything. You don't know these people, and I don't either, not really, but I know the floor I work on. When it stops talking, it's because somebody's been told it needn't." He looked at Cael. "You've made it loud. Loud gets answered. I don't know what the answer is. I don't know who'd give it. I only know there's a desk somewhere that's read this, and I can't see it from mine."

"I know."

"Then don't do it." Coss's voice cracked on it, and he stopped, and started again, lower. "Not like this. Pull out. Say it's the ribs. Say anything you like. Nobody in this world ever blamed a boy for a sprain. Vell would take it. The Iron man would take it; he's touring, there's a gate every Saturday on that river." He put his hand flat on the gatepost, hard, as he had put his hands flat on the table either side of a pie. "Go back to being a line in brackets for a season. Let legal sit. Let them forget."

Cael looked at him.

He had never seen Coss like this. He had seen him level at a desk with a stamp, and careful in a reading room, and very still over a pie he had not eaten. He had never seen him not know what to do with his hands.

"I was quiet," Cael said. "All autumn. I did everything you'd have told me to. I kept my head down and my Path to myself and won where nobody was looking, and I didn't fight anybody anybody had heard of." He held Coss's eyes. "And a man walked a sweep through the market five weeks after I came, that you couldn't find the order for. And a mark went on my file the hour it was opened, that Ilsev couldn't find the end of. And then you came here in your own coat to tell me there was a view." He kept his voice level. "Quiet didn't stop any of it. Quiet's only what made it easy."

Coss did not answer.

"Somebody else wants me gone," said Cael. "Not you. Lira worked that out on a roof the day you came, and I think she's right, and I think you think so too. Somebody I've never met has decided I'd be better in a drawer, and they don't need a reason anybody can read. I can't out-quiet that." He looked at the creased brown coat, and the hand on the gatepost. "I've tried. I was better at it than anybody. It's the one thing they've already got."

Coss stood there for a long time with his hand on the post of the gate.

Then he took his hand away, and looked at it, as if he had not known it was there. He sat down on the end of the trestle bench just inside the gate, the one the girls from the wall used when there was nobody fighting, and put his elbows on his knees.

"I've a daughter," he said.

Cael did not say anything.

"She'll be fourteen at midsummer. She does her lessons at the kitchen table with her tongue between her teeth." Coss looked at the dirt between his boots. "When I go out on a file she asks me at the door whether this one's going to be interesting, and I tell her they never are. I told her that in the autumn, about yours." He was quiet a moment. "I haven't told her anything since. There's nothing in it I could tell a child."

He turned his hat round in his hands.

"I sit and watch her do her sums, and I think about your file. Thin, and odd, and walking off its road in three places. And I think, if it were hers, what would I want?" He shook his head slowly. "I don't know. What I'd want her to do, I couldn't tell you. I know what I'd want done for her. I'd want somebody to come and say it to her face, whatever they had, in their own coat. Not across a desk." He looked up. "That's all this is. It's no use to you. I know it's no use."

"It's some use."

Coss looked at him for a while longer. Then he stood up, slowly, a man who had been on a cart all night, and put his hat on.

"Then don't do it by halves," he said. "If you're going to be loud, don't stop at loud enough." He looked once more round the yard, at the post and the straw and the groove and Lira going through her corners at the far end without once looking their way. "I'll not come again before Sunday. I can't. I've no days left."

He went out through the gate. Cael watched him go down the lane in the grey light, in the creased coat, without looking back this time, toward the river and the noon cart.

Lira came back up the grey half when he was out of sight.

"He's frightened for you," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She held out the chalk. "Somebody up there ought to be."

That night, at the very back of the Log, below the line about the drawer, he wrote: *Coss has a daughter. Fourteen at midsummer. He came on the night mail on his own days to tell me nobody was saying anything.* He sat looking at it. Then he wrote one more line under it, and did not know quite why: *He asked me what he'd want if it were her. I think he was asking himself, and he came all this way to do it out loud.*

# Chapter 57 — The Crossing

Somebody had been at the board at the triangle in the night.

Vell's card was still in the middle of it, where the light was best, on the back of its lamp-oil bill. *LAST BOUT, SUNDAY FORTNIGHT. DARROW INNES (Br 1, Iron Path) against assessed Cu (vouched).* But under it, on the bare grey wood of the old door, somebody had written in charcoal, in large uneven capitals that sloped downhill toward the cooper's shed, *HESK-WARD*. Somebody else had underlined it twice. A third person, in chalk, had added *13 TO 1* beside it, and a fourth had crossed that out and written *SHAME*.

Cael stood and looked at it on the way up, at noon, with Lira beside him and his wraps over her arm.

"Red Cap," said Lira. "He can't do a *W*. Look at it."

The *W* was two *V*s that had not quite met. Cael looked at it for some time. In seven months the board had known him as a line in brackets, and now it had his name on it, spelt by a boy who could not do a *W*, in a hand that would wash off in the first rain.

"Come on," said Lira. "You're not fighting the board either."

---

The pie boy had been raking since first light, and he told them so at the gate in the wall, leaning on his rake with his face shining, before they were through it.

"New groove," he said. "Wider. She had me cut it a whole pace out, all the way round, and the rope after it. Then she had me rake it four times. Four. Then she came and looked at it and had me do it again." He pointed with the rake, as if they might miss it. "Benches on three walls. Trestles from the cooper. Two buckets. And three slates on the nails, look. Two men come up from Fenrow with their own chalk."

It was all true. The yard had put on a different stance in the night. The circle in the middle had grown, and its groove was fresh and dark and combed smooth, and the rope stood a pace further out on every side. Benches ran along three walls, not one, with a row of trestles behind them. On the nails by the gate there were three slates, each in a different hand, and two of the hands belonged to men Cael had never seen, sitting on upturned crates beside them with their chalk in their laps and their hats tipped forward.

He counted, because he could not help it. Sixty-three people in the yard at two hours out, most of them on benches they meant to keep. The girls along the top of the wall in a row, swinging their heels. A short queue in the lane already, with a man selling hot nuts up and down it out of a tray.

"How many?" said Lira.

"Sixty-three."

"Keep it." She was looking at the benches, not at him. "Tonight you'll want to know how many there were before."

He kept it.

The three slates all said eleven. Marrow's was on the first nail. Marrow himself was on his crate below it, writing out an over and under on how long the boy would last, and when he looked up and saw Cael going past he stopped writing, and looked at him for a moment, and put the chalk down on his knee. He folded his arms, as if there were some sums a man did not do in front of the person they were about.

His corner was on the shaded side by the iron stake. Somebody had swept it. He sat on the end of the nearest bench and held his hands out, and Lira knelt in front of him and began on the left wrap, and he watched the yard fill past her shoulder.

The bread woman had shut her stall. He saw her come in at the gate with a shawl over her head and her chin up, and pay, and go to the third row, where people moved along for her without being asked. She sat down with her arms folded, as if she meant to see for herself whether all this was worth a morning's bread. Sella was on the end of the second bench in her laced boots. Torvin's wife was beside her with a cloth bundle on her knee that was almost certainly a bun, and almost certainly not for Sella. Torvin had not come. Torvin did not come to the yard. *If you're carried home, they're to bring you in by the yard door.*

Doss stood at the back on account of the knee. Renn was on the sunny side, with his barge friends round him, and when he saw Cael looking he cupped his hands round his mouth and shouted across the whole yard, "*Feet!* Not his face! He was not big. That was the first thing everybody seemed to notice, because the sound went down on it like a breath let out. His hair was cut close and was the colour of wet sand. He had thought about it on the straw for two weeks, and in the end it was Hesk's bench that had decided him, not the straw. When a housing came back from a customer cracked, Hesk did not put the whole weight on it at once to see where it broke. He put a small known weight on one joint, and watched where it went; and then on the next joint; and the next. *Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.* You traced it by loading it a little at a time, in order, and watching.

So Cael loaded Darrow Innes a joint at a time.

One to the right side of the chest, short and true, Lira's strike, the one strike he owned. He felt it land and felt it go: in, and then sideways, as if someone behind the ribs had taken it from him and passed it along to the left. One to the point of the right shoulder: in, and across, and down. One, reaching, to the jaw, which he did not expect to matter and which did not; it went down through the thick neck and across like the others. One to the belly: in, and down, and left.

Every one of them landed. His knuckles told him so through the wraps, plainly. And every one of them went away into that broad still body like a stone dropped down a well, without a sound coming back up.

There it was, then. Doss's thing. His hands said *landed* and his eyes said *nothing happened*, and the two of them disagreed in the back of his head like two people arguing in another room. *Stop believing your hands,* said Doss's flat voice, *and you're done.*

He chose his hands. They were the instrument. He could trace what they told him.

Darrow let him. He did not block, or slip, or seem to mind. It was very little. It was nothing he could have shown anybody, or said in words to anybody but Lira. But the four before had gone into Darrow the way his fist went into the straw. That. There was something moving under that place on Darrow's side when he got there, still carrying the shoulder, or the belly, down toward a foot nailed into the dirt.

It was one feeling in two knuckles. It was not proof. But it was the first thing in the whole business that was his own.

Darrow took a long step back, without hurry, a little before the exchange need have ended, like a man ending a conversation when it has said what it came to say.

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

The yard let its breath go, and the noise came up off the benches like birds off a field.

"*Five!*" somebody at the slates was shouting, delighted. "*Five on him, and he never moved his feet!*"

Cael walked back to his mark with his thigh coming back to life in pins and needles. On the way he looked at the barrel.

Lira was where she always was, with her arms folded on the top of the rope and her face giving nothing to anybody who did not know it. As he looked, she unfolded one arm and laid two fingers, lightly, against her own left side, low, under the ribs.

He did not move his head. He put his thumb against his left hip, as if it were stiff, and took it away again.

At the nails a boy went past with white hands, and the chalk squeaked, and the first slate said fifteen.

In the second exchange Darrow stopped giving anything away.

He did not get faster. He did not seem to get anything. He simply began to walk forward, and kept walking, with the right foot doing all of it and the left coming after and setting, every step, tap and tap again, as if every new piece of the yard had to be nailed down before he would stand on it. In six steps Cael's back was a forearm's length from the rope by the iron stake, and three hundred people were very quiet.

It was the right side, low, under the arm. A short one, with no reach in it at all. Then the same place. Then, when Cael had got his elbow down over it, the same place again, under the elbow, so that the elbow itself was driven into his own ribs. They were not big. None of them was meant to end anything. They were meant to add. Cael understood that with a kind of cold amusement even while they were landing, because he had spent two weeks on the straw learning to do the same thing, and here was a man who had been born knowing it. *They do it themselves,* he had said to Lira at the post. *It's how their own bodies work.* He had not thought that he would be the post.

It hurt. It hurt more each time, and it would go on hurting more, and that was the point of it.

So he went for the crossing from the rope.

There was only one way to it from there. He had to come up under Darrow's right arm, while it was busy with his ribs, and go across the front of the man to reach the left side, which meant going past the right hand on the way and taking whatever it gave him. He did it. He took a short one on the forearm that rang the bone from the wrist to the elbow, right over the old burn, and he knew that by the evening it would be the colour of a plum. He put a short strike into the low left side on his way across, the third of the afternoon. Then he was out along the rope, and Darrow was turning after him, and the left foot came off the floor and went down again, *tap*, and *tap*.

And Cael put the next one in on the second tap.

He had not planned it. He had seen it on the straw, perhaps, without knowing he had seen it, in a hundred afternoons of the chalk-board woman's bucket and Doss's polite Iron man. The second tap was the setting down. That was when the left foot took it all, whatever was coming down the road. If there was a road. If anything was carrying anything.

So he went on doing it. He let Darrow walk him, and every time the left foot came up and went down and tapped, he put one into the low left side on the tap, short and dull and true, from whatever angle he could get, whatever it cost. He did not try to make any of them count. He could not have made any of them count if he had tried; most of them came out of a scramble, thrown going backward or going sideways along the rope, with Darrow's short ones landing in his own right side in return, add, add, add. One was a knee in a clinch he should never have let happen. One was an elbow as Darrow shoved him off, ugly and short, the kind of strike Lira would have made him do again. Nobody watching could have thought it was a plan. It did not feel like a plan from inside it. It felt like a boy being beaten slowly against a rope, and flailing.

He was counting them. He had been counting since the first two, because counting was the one thing he could do in there without spending anything. Five. Six.

On the sixth the place was slow.

The others had gone quiet under his knuckles as quickly as a door shuts. The sixth did not. The sixth arrived and found something still moving underneath; the hum under the boards was going when he got there, and went on going for half a beat after. Something in there was still carrying the fifth down to the floor when the sixth came in on top of it.

The seventh he put in going off the rope at last, sideways along it, out from under Darrow's arm, as he ducked a short one to the head. The same place.

And Darrow stopped.

He did not stagger. He did not do anything that the yard could see as a thing being done. He only stopped walking, in the middle of a step, with his right foot forward and his left foot set, and stood there a moment. Then he took his own left hand, without any hurry, and laid it flat against his own left side, low, under the ribs, as a man in a crowd lays his hand on the place where his purse is, not because he thinks it has gone but because he has felt something and wants to be sure.

He took the hand away. He looked at Cael. It was the first time that afternoon he had looked at his face first.

"You've found yourself a place," said Darrow quietly. His voice did not reach the rope; it was not meant to. "Some of them do. The ones that think." He shook his head, slowly, without any unkindness in it at all. "There's nowhere on me that's further from the ground than anywhere else, son. All of it goes down. All of it."

Cael did not answer. He had no breath to answer with, and nothing he wanted to spend it on.

*Yes,* he thought. *All of it goes down.*

*All of it goes down through one place.*

"End of the exchange," said Vell.

# Chapter 58 — As It Happened

In the third exchange Darrow Innes leaned on a gate.

That was what it looked like, from across the circle. He came off his mark when Vell said *begin* and did not walk Cael anywhere. He stood in the middle with his right foot forward and his left set, and brought his left arm down and rested the elbow on his own hip, easily, the way a man leans on a gate at the end of the day to talk to his neighbour. And he turned, not much, a quarter of a turn, so that his left side went a little away and his right came a little forward.

It was nothing. Anybody on the benches would have said he was resting. But the low left side was under the elbow now, and behind the turn, and every road Cael could think of to it went past both of Darrow's hands.

*He's found the door,* thought Cael. *And he's shut it.*

He tried anyway, because he had to know how shut. He went in low from the left, under the right hand, and the right hand came down and met his forearm and turned him away like a man turning a dog off a path. He went round and tried from outside, where the elbow was, and before his strike had properly left his hip Darrow's left forearm had come off the gate and was lying flat in front of the place, waiting. His knuckles hit it and stopped as if he had punched a doorpost.

It had been there first.

He went back toward his side of the circle with his knuckles stinging and looked at the barrel. Lira was looking at him. She lifted one hand and touched her chin, once, and folded her arm again.

*The nod.*

It went into him cold. He had been doing it all afternoon. Every time he had gone for the crossing, something in his neck had said *now* a sliver before the rest of him, as it had said it for Dessa all winter, and he had not once felt himself do it. And a man who read feet first and hands second and faces hardly at all had not needed to watch his face. Two exchanges had been enough, and a dozen small nods, and now the forearm got there before the fist.

He could not stop it. He had stopped it once, against Dessa, by deciding the whole of the exchange on his mark and letting every other plan go, and Dessa had told him on the dirt afterward that it was a thing you got to do once. It was done. If he went for the crossing now, he would nod, and the forearm would be there.

So he would stop going for it.

He thought of Feryn in the sleet. Feryn had built his air and held it and not let it go, and put his hand where the exchange was going to finish, where Cael was always going to arrive, and simply waited there for him. Cael had slipped round a thing that was not there, straight into the hand.

Darrow had to hit him sometime. Nobody won a bout leaning on a gate. And to hit, Darrow had to take the elbow off his hip; whatever else a strike of his did, it would finish with that arm out and away for a moment before it came back. That was where every one of Darrow's own exchanges ended. The gate swung open. Then it swung shut.

So Cael kept his strike in his hand and did not throw it.

He held it as Feryn had held his air, short and ready, and he did not let his neck say *now*, because there was no *now* to say. He went in close and stayed close, closer than any sense would have let him, inside a Bronze's reach where the Bronze could hit him whenever he liked. And he waited to be hit, so that he would be standing where the hitting ended.

He spent most of the exchange being hurt.

Darrow had felt the hum in his own side. Cael had seen him feel it, with his hand on it like a purse. Somewhere behind his mild face he had decided that this afternoon had gone on long enough, and he set about ending it without any hurry. He hit Cael short in the right side, in the place he had been adding to all through the second exchange, and the old place went white. He hit him again in it. Cael kept the strike in his hand and did not throw, and the arm went back to the hip, and the gate shut. He hit him a third time, and Cael rode half of it and could not ride the rest, and it bent him over, so that for a moment he was looking at the dirt and the front of Darrow's boot on it.

He saw the boot move.

It came in low behind his own right heel, the hook of the sweep, and in the same instant, above him, he felt a straight blow begin, short, down the middle. It was the kind that would put a boy with his heel taken flat on his back and keep him there. There was no ground to give. His heel was gone, and there was nowhere behind him or beside him that a foot could reach.

And the ground went short.

He had not asked for it. There was no time to ask for anything, and he had not asked for it since the trough. It came up out of the dark behind his breastbone as it had come in the sleet, already done before he knew it was happening, and the yard was a hand's width narrower than it had been. His weight was not where his heel had been. It was round the edge of the blow, on Darrow's left, at an angle no step of his own could have got to from where he stood. The straight went through the space where his chest had been, close enough that he felt it go by along his shirt.

The yard went silent.

Three hundred people, the crows on the eaves, the man with the hot nuts in the lane: all of them stopped at once, the way a room stops when somebody drops a plate. In the middle of it he heard one of the Fenrow men at the nails say something short to the other. Then he heard chalk.

So that was spent. He had kept it in the dark for two weeks like money sewn into a coat, and now three hundred people had seen it, and some of them would be telling it tonight in rooms he would never stand in, and telling it wrong, and telling it again. He felt it go out of him into the yard. It did not come back.

But he was on Darrow's left side, and Darrow's right arm was out at the far end of a blow that had found nothing, and his left was coming off the gate to turn.

The gate was open.

He let go the strike he had been holding all exchange. Short, low, on the left side, with nothing else in it at all. The eighth. It went in, and the place was still busy.

Darrow turned on him fast, for the first time all afternoon, and put a short one into his middle that took all his air out and doubled him. And as he went down over it, with nothing left in his chest, Cael put the ninth into the same place. It was the ugliest thing he had thrown in his life. Lira would have made him do it fifty times over.

Under his knuckles, this time, it was not like the floor over the river. It was like the gate of the market on a Saturday morning, when the carts have all come at once, and the first is still wedged in the gateway when the second runs up against its tail, and the third against the second. The inside of his lip had split, and he could taste it. His left forearm was a bar from wrist to elbow. The yard had gone quiet enough to hear him.

# Chapter 59 — By Name

In the morning the edges of the room were edges again.

He lay on the cot and tested it before he moved anything else. He looked at the window, and then quickly at the shelf, and the shelf was where he had sent his eyes and arrived there with them, not after. He did it twice more to be sure. Then he tried to sit up, and found out what the ribs thought about mornings.

Yeni was already gone, her blanket folded into its square. The wheezing man was asleep with his mouth open. Somebody had put a cup of water on the floor by the cot, where he could reach it without bending, and had turned Yeni's lamp, which was out, toward the wall, so that the first light from the window would not come off its brass into his eyes. He drank the water. It took both hands, and his arms answered him slowly and sorely, like men called to work on a holiday, but they answered.

It took him a quarter of an hour to dress. He went down the stairs one at a time, holding the rail and breathing like the man of ninety Lira had promised, and came into the kitchen.

Torvin's wife was at the range. She looked round at him once, all of him, from his feet to his face and back, the way Darrow had looked at him from across the circle, and turned back to the pot without a word. When he had got himself down onto the bench she put a bowl of oats in front of him with real milk on them and an egg broken into the middle, still soft. Then, in front of the bowl, she put a bun.

"It's not from me," she said. "It's from her."

Sella was at the end of the table in her boots, eating bread with great dignity. She did not look up.

"That's five," said Sella. "Five buns I've lost on you since the autumn. She keeps count. I'd lost count. She hadn't." She turned a page of somebody's old newspaper that she was not reading. "I'll bet against you again, you know. Next time. I've told everybody."

"I know."

"Somebody in this house has to be on the other side." Sella looked at him at last, over the paper, at the way he was sitting with his right arm held against himself. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"It's a compliment," said Sella. "On you it's a compliment." She went back to the paper.

Doss came in from his watch while Cael was eating. He stood in the doorway in his big coat with his bad knee and his flat face and looked at Cael for a while, and then came and sat down across from him and took the cloth off his own plate.

"I heard," said Doss. He ate a little. "I heard you believed your hands."

"Yes."

Doss nodded slowly, chewing. Then, without looking up from the plate, he put his scarred right hand flat on the table, palm down, beside Cael's bowl, for a moment, the way you put your hand on a horse that has done well. Then he took it away and went on eating.

Lira came to the kitchen door at the hour of the grey half and did not come in.

"You're not training," she said. "Don't ask. You're coming up to sit on the bench and watch me teach the wall girls to fall over, and then you're going to Vell's table, because she's sent the pie boy for you twice already this morning and he's getting cross."

"Red Cap," said Lira, following his eyes. "Before it was light. He's been telling everybody he knows where the place is."

"Sit down," said Vell.

"Yes."

It had changed. It had not changed much. Nobody stopped him, or crowded round him, or wanted to touch him. But at the fish steps the eel woman looked up from her tub and said "Hesk-ward" as he went past, and went back to her eels. At the corner by the well, the man with the barrow of kindling, who had never in seven months said anything to him, said "Morning," though it was the afternoon, and then looked embarrassed, and then said "Afternoon," and looked worse. Two women at the pump stopped talking and watched him go by, and he heard one of them say *that's him*, and the other say *he's smaller*, and the first say *they always are*.

The boys found him at the bottom of the market steps.

Then they stood there, appalled, and turned and began to argue in fierce low voices about who had said it first, and whose idea it had been to say it at all, and Cael went on past them up the steps. Halfway up he heard Red Cap say, quite loudly, that they had *agreed*, and the other say that agreeing wasn't the same as *doing it at the same time like a pair of geese*. That was Hesk all over. He would not send a thing until it was right, and when it was right he would not let himself near it again in case he spoiled it. He had found the words, and they were not the ones he had gone looking for, and he had said so.

On the Tuesday, two days after the bout, he saw the stranger in the market.

He had gone down for apricots, because the fruit woman had sent word by Red Cap that she had some, and because walking was supposed to be good for the ribs if he did not do too much of it. It was an ordinary afternoon. The market was half full, the slow kind of afternoon after a busy morning, with the stallholders sitting down on their crates and talking across the rows. He had bought the apricots and was standing by the fruit woman's stall eating one, not thinking about anything, which he had been learning to do, when he felt himself being looked at.

He knew the feeling. He had felt it from a mark a hundred times: the moment before a bout when you know the person across the circle has stopped looking at the yard and started looking at you. It was that. He did not turn toward it. He went on eating the apricot, and turned the rest of himself a little, as if to look at the knife-grinder's wheel, and let his eyes go where the feeling came from.

Across the market, at the end of the second row, there was a person standing still.

That was all, at first. In a market everybody moves. People walk, and stop, and bend over a stall, and straighten, and walk on; even the ones standing still are moving, shifting their baskets, looking for somebody. This one was not doing any of that. They stood by the end of the knife-grinder's bench, a little back from it, in plain clothes the colour of the market, with nothing in their hands, and they were not looking at the stalls, or at the grinder, or at the people going past.

They were looking at him.

Not at his arm held against his side, or at the bruise he knew showed at the edge of his collar. They were not looking at the boy who had beaten a Bronze. They were looking at him, the way Corvane had looked at Feryn's shirt: at the place where the thing was, not at the things on top of it.

He held very still. He made himself catalogue it the way he would have catalogued an opening stance, without spending anything, without turning his whole self toward it and giving away that he had seen.

Not Compact. He was as sure of that as he had been sure of anything. He had stood across a desk from Coss and across a table from Ilsev, and both of them carried their work about with them like a case: a hurry with edges, a stamp, a person above them who would want to see what they had done. There was no case here. There was no pin, no little black book with a strap, nobody anywhere behind this person who was going to read a report. There was only the looking.

And it was patient. That was the thing he would try to write that night and not manage. It was patient the way the old man in his gate was patient, and not at all like him: the old man was patient because he had nowhere he needed to be. This was patient the way a person is who has been somewhere a long time already and expects to be there a good while yet. It did not feel like a look that had started this afternoon. It felt like the first one he had been allowed to see.

He did not know how long it lasted. Not long. Exactly as long, he thought afterward, as it took him to understand that he was being looked at and that he was being let know it.

Then the person turned, without any hurry, and walked into the market, and the market closed behind them as water closes, and they were gone. He watched the place where they had been for some time. There was nothing there but the knife-grinder, bent over his wheel, and the sparks.

He did not go after them.

His ribs would not have let him, for one thing. But it was not his ribs. He had felt, in the length of that look, that it had been timed: that whoever it was had decided when he would see them and when he would stop, as Darrow had decided when the first exchange was over. A person who did that would not be found by a boy with a broken rib pushing through a market. They would be found only if they wished it. Going after them would have cost him everything he had left, and bought him nothing but proof that he had been allowed to look.

*Spend nothing you don't have to.*

He finished the apricot. He thanked the fruit woman, who looked at his face and did not ask him anything, and walked back up through the lanes to Torvin's at the pace the ribs allowed. He went through the kitchen and out to the yard and sat down on the low wall by the pump, under the pear tree.

He sat there a long time. He did not take out the Log. He sat and turned it over: the stillness, the plain clothes, the no-case, the look that had been allowed. He turned it over the way he had turned over every opponent he had ever had to read, from every side, looking for the joint in it.

He did not find one. He was still sitting there when the light began to go, and Yeni came to the kitchen door with her sewing, and looked at him for a while, and went back in.

# Chapter 60 — The Message

Lira came in through the kitchen at dusk with flour on her sleeve, because Yeni had found her at the widow's back door helping carry sacks, and had said four words to her, and Lira had put the sack down where she stood.

She came across Torvin's yard and sat down on the low wall beside him, on the left, and did not say anything. She tore the end off a loaf she had in her bag and held it out. He took it. They sat under the pear tree with the light going and ate the bread, and she waited.

"There was somebody in the market," he said at last.

He told it from the beginning, as she liked it, without saying what it meant: the apricots, the feeling from the mark, the knife-grinder's bench. A person standing still where everybody moves. No case, no pin, no book. The look, and how long it lasted. How it seemed to have been timed. How they had turned and the market had shut behind them.

Lira did not interrupt. When he had finished she sat with her chin on her fist and looked at the trough for a while.

"Not Compact," she said. "You're sure."

"As sure as I am of anything I've only seen once." He turned the bread over. "Coss carries his work about with him. So did Ilsev. You can see it on them, a sort of hurry with edges, and somebody behind them waiting for a report. Even the man with the pin in the autumn had it, and he was only borrowed." He shook his head. "This one had nothing behind them at all. They weren't going to tell anybody what they'd seen. They'd come to see it for themselves."

"That's not better."

"No."

"Is it worse?"

He thought about it honestly.

"I don't know," he said. "It's other. It's not the thing I've been afraid of since the autumn. It's a different thing, standing next to it." He looked at the bread in his hand. "And it came after. Two days after. Not in the autumn, when nobody knew my face. Not on Sunday, in the crowd, where it would have been easiest to stand and look. After. When there was a line in Vell's book and my name on the board in Red Cap's writing."

Lira was quiet.

"I made myself loud," he said, "so that the people I was frightened of would have to stand up and say where I'd gone. I thought about who else might hear. I said it to you on the steps. But I thought about it the way you think about rain on a day you've already decided to go out." He set the bread down on the wall. "Whoever that was, Coss's drawer may be nothing to them. I could have lit a lamp on Sunday for somebody who was never looking in drawers."

"Or," said Lira, "they'd found you already, and Sunday only made them decide to let you see."

He looked at her.

"You said it felt like the first look you'd been allowed," she said. "Not the first look." She shrugged. "I don't know either. I'm only adding. It comes out both ways."

They sat. The brothers were arguing on the stairs inside about a shirt, and the wheezing man's window was open, and somewhere toward the river a dog was asking the lane its one question.

"Can I say a thing," said Lira, "and you not argue with it till I've finished?"

"Yes."

"You saw this at the third hour after noon." She did not look at him. "Yeni says you came in at the fourth and sat on this wall, and didn't move. She came and got me at the end of the sixth. Not you. Yeni." She let that sit. "Two hours, Cael. On a wall. Turning it over on your own till you'd made your mind up what it was, so that you'd have something tidy to bring me."

He opened his mouth, and shut it.

"I'm not angry," said Lira. "That's the other thing, the one with Vell, and that's paid, and this isn't that. This is smaller. But it's the same shape." She turned her head at last. "You don't have to have it worked out before you come and get me. You don't have to have anything worked out. You keep forgetting there's two of us. You can bring me a thing in pieces and we can sit on a wall and look at the pieces together. That's what I'm for. It's half of what I'm for."

"What's the other half?"

"Telling you you're doing it again." She almost smiled. "You're carrying all of it by yourself because you think that's what being careful means. It isn't. Being careful is not doing it alone."

He sat with that. He tested it against two hours on a wall, and against the fourth stair, and against five letters to Hesk with five things left out of them, and it held each time.

"I'll try," he said.

"I know you will. And I'll check." She bumped his knee with hers. "You know I check."

"I know you check."

"Good." She stood up, and dusted the flour off her sleeve, which only spread it. "Eat that. You'll not think any better about strangers on an empty stomach, and you'll not think at all with ribs like that if you don't sleep." She held her hand down to him, on the good side, and he came up off the wall the old man's way, one piece on the next, and she watched him do it and said nothing about it at all.

At the kitchen door she stopped.

"Whoever it is," she said, "they looked at both of us on Sunday. I was at the barrel. They'll have seen me too." She considered it. "So it's not yours to carry. Not even half."

Coss opened the district return on the Wednesday afternoon, three days after, at his desk at regional, with the window behind him going grey.

He had not meant to read it. Nobody read the district returns. They came in every week from every post in the region in a canvas sack and were sorted by a clerk into a rack by the door, and stayed there a month, and were then tied up in bundles and sent down to the cellars. The only thing anybody ever did with them was count them, to be sure they had all come. But the Ardenmere return had been put on his desk on Monday a fortnight ago with a line under it in red, and since then he had taken it out of the rack himself every week before the clerk could tie it up.

It was in the clerk's careful hand, as always. Boundaries. Permits. A dispute about a gutter, referred back to the parties. And under *Public notices posted in the district*, the card from the Cinder House board, copied word for word as the form required, and then, because the clerk was a careful man and the form said *all matter posted*, the chalk that somebody had written underneath it:

*HESK-WARD. 4TH. VELL'S CALL.*

Coss read it twice. Then he put his thumb to the bridge of his nose and sat for a while with his eyes shut.

He had come back on the noon cart from Ardenmere ten days ago with his days all spent and a creased coat and a cold. He had told himself all the way back, through two nights of carters' benches, that he had done what he could, and that it had been nothing, and that the boy would do what the boy had decided. He had found, at his desk on the Monday after, that he could not keep from thinking about the date on the card. He had found on Sunday, at home, that he could not eat his dinner until the evening had gone far enough that it must be over. His daughter had asked him whether he was ill.

It was over. He had not known how until now. A chalk line in a clerk's copy of a district board, three days late, sent in a canvas sack.

He opened his eyes and drew the file across the desk to make the entry. That was the procedure. When a field agent learned anything of a subject under review, he entered it on the file, with the date and the source, so that whoever opened the file next would know what he knew. He had done it ten thousand times. He had the line already in his head: *Subject remains in the district. No compliance action taken. Legal review pending. Public notice (district return, this week) of a circuit result; no compliance bearing.* He uncapped his pen.

He opened the cover, and stopped.

There was a slip pinned inside it.

It was on the heavy grey paper they used on the floors above his, the paper that did not take ink well and was not meant to be written on twice. It had a printed head along the top, a narrow band of small type with a row of numbers in it. He knew the head. He had seen it twice before in eleven years, across the room, on other agents' desks, and both times the agent had closed the file very quickly and looked at nobody for the rest of the afternoon. He had never once seen it on a file of his own.

Below the head there was a code. It was not in his register. He knew that without looking, but he took the register out of his drawer anyway and looked, because that was the procedure, and it was not there. It did not have the regional prefix that the autumn's sweep had had; it did not have any prefix he knew at all. Under the code there was no office. There were no initials. There was no reason given, and no instruction, and no line saying what the file's agent was to do about it. There was a date.

The date was Monday. The day after.

He sat looking at it for a long time.

He did not know what it was. He was very careful with himself about that. He knew what a slip with that head meant, in the ordinary way: that somebody above his floor, above his section head's floor, wanted to know when this file moved. He did not know who. He did not know why. He did not know whether it had anything to do with the mark Ilsev had found in the spring, which had appeared on a docket and could not be traced; this one could be traced, in a sense, because he knew the paper and the head, but only as far as a floor he had never been allowed to stand on. He did not know whether it had anything to do with a chalk line in a clerk's copy of a board, or with a Bronze lying down on raked dirt in front of three hundred people, or with nothing at all. A thing could be dated the day after another thing without being caused by it. He had told a great many people that, over the years, when they had come to him with two dates and a story.

He only knew that it was another place where the file had walked off its road. That made four. He could still see the footprints going away into the snow, more of them now, all going the same way. He still could not see who was walking.

There was a thing he could do. The procedure allowed it. An agent who found a flag he could not read on a file of his own could send a query upward and ask, through the proper channel, what was required of him. He had the form in his drawer. He took it out and looked at it, and laid it on the desk beside the file.

He thought about his last query, the one about the sweep. It had come back to him in the spring pouch with a single word on it in a hand he did not know. *Noted.*

He thought about what it would mean to put his own name at the bottom of a question about this slip, and send it up to a floor where somebody read slips like this one. He thought about whether a man who asked that question would find, a month or so on, a slip of grey paper pinned inside his own file, in the room at the end of the corridor where the agents' records were kept. He did not think it was likely. He was not a man who thought things were likely without evidence. But he found, sitting there with the form in front of him, that he could not quite say it was not.

He had a daughter of fourteen at midsummer who did her sums at the kitchen table with her tongue between her teeth.

He put the form back in the drawer.

Then he closed the file. He did not make the entry. He did not write *subject remains*, or *legal review pending*, or *public notice of a circuit result*, or anything at all. The pen lay on the desk beside it with its cap off, and the ink dried on the nib.

He took out his working log.

He had kept it for eleven years, in his own hand, in a series of plain black books bought with his own money, one after another, on a shelf at home. It was his. Nobody had ever asked him to keep it and nobody had ever read it. In eleven years he had written in it every file he had opened and every file he had closed, and why, and what he had seen. He had written in it on the night of the sweep that the sweep was not standard, and on the night of the boy's objection that the objection was correct. He had written the *Noted* in it, and copied the word exactly, and the date it came. He had never once opened a file and not written down that he had.

He opened the log to the day's page. He looked at it for a long time. Then he closed it again, with nothing written, and put it in his inside pocket and buttoned his coat over it.

It was the first page he had ever left empty on purpose. He did not like it. He sat at his desk in the grey light from the window and did not like it at all, with his hand flat on the closed file, and he understood that he had just done the first thing in his working life that he could not have written down and then stood behind.

He did not know who he was keeping it from. That was the part he would lie awake with. He did not know whether he was keeping it from them, whoever they were, or from himself, or from a boy on a cot two days east who had asked him across a pie whether he knew what a flag was, and had watched him not move. He had told the boy the truth, then. He had not known anything about a flag. He did now. He knew exactly as much as a slip of grey paper and a printed head and a date could tell a man, which was almost nothing, and was more than he had known on any day before this one.

At the end of the day he put the file back in the drawer for things under review, and locked the drawer, and took the key home with him, which was not the procedure either.

His daughter was at the kitchen table with her lessons when he came in. She looked up at him with her pencil in her teeth.

"Was anything interesting?"

He hung up his coat, with the log still buttoned inside it, and stood a moment with his hand on the coat.

"Somebody I know won something," he said.

"Who?"

"Nobody you know." He sat down across from her, and drew her slate toward him, and looked at her sums. "You've carried the seven wrong."

She looked, and saw it, and rubbed it out, and did it again with her tongue between her teeth. He sat and watched her do it, and did not think about anything else for nearly a quarter of an hour, which was the longest he had managed in a month.

He wrote to Hesk on the Wednesday night, by the bead of Yeni's lamp, with the Log on his knee for a desk and his arm held against his side.

He told him the fourth exchange as near as he could, which was not near. He told him the crossing and the straw post and the bucket and the gate, and the nod, and Feryn's hand. He told him the yard door and the chair. He told him that Vell had his name at the start of a line. He told him about supper: that there had been none, because he had been asleep, and then an egg, and a bun that Sella had lost on him, and the bread woman's own loaf, which she had kept back for herself every morning for seven months and given away to him, and he did not know her name. He did not tell him about the stranger in the market. Then he sat a while, and he did tell him, in four plain lines at the end, because he had promised to tell the next thing as it came and he had already been told off once this week for sitting on a wall.

Then he turned the Log over to the back.

He read the line he had written on Sunday night, at the bottom of the eighth instance, under the struck-through drawings: the fence at the edge of the marsh.

It did not belong there. He saw that now. It was not about one bout or one half second. It was about all of it, every instance and both notices and whatever the next one was, and it ought to be somewhere he would see it every time he turned the book over, the way Hesk's letters were somewhere he saw them every time he opened it. So he turned to the inside of the back cover, where there was nothing yet but the board's grain, and copied it there, slowly, in his best hand.

*They're not separate things I switch between. They're one thing with parts I haven't found all of yet.*

The front of the book had three letters in it now, and the back had a fence. He looked at the two ends of the book for a while.

Then he turned to the last written page, under the eighth instance, and wrote the thing he had been turning over since the knife-grinder's bench. He had turned it over on the wall, and again with Lira, and all through the next day. It was short enough that it had to be right.

*Someone else wants me gone. I used to think that meant the Compact. I'm starting to think Coss doesn't know who he was talking about either.*

He read it back once. He did not change it.

Then he closed the Log, with its letters in the front and its fence in the back, and put it on the shelf beside Hesk's leather book, and lay down very carefully on his left side, which was the side that let him, and was asleep before Yeni had finished turning down the lamp.

In the morning he went up to the grey half, because Lira had said he could sit on the bench if he did nothing but sit.

The east wall's shadow lay long across the yard, as it did every morning, so that the Cinder House was split down the middle, a grey half and a bright half, with the groove going through both. The straw post stood in the corner of the grey half with Red Cap's fist still printed in it. Lira had taken the grey canvas off its nail on the wall and hung it back on the post, over the straw, where it had been all winter. The columns were on it in her chalk: the *gone* column, ruled off at forty, and the falls, and the long *W* with its strokes and rings and its small cost words, *same*, *less*, *less*, *same*, and the shorter *P*, with its four rings that said *trough*, and one stroke with no ring that said *both. nothing. arms anyway.*

He sat on the bench with his arm against his side and watched her.

She stood in front of the canvas with the chalk for some time. Then, at the top of a clean stretch of grey beside the *P*, she wrote a *W*, and beside it a *P*, and drew a short line between the two of them, joining them. Under that she made one stroke. *Sunday.*

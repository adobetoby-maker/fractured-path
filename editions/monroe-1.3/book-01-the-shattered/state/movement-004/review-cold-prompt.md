# Chapter 21 — The Pin

The market had not made up its mind about the weather.

A haze lay over the whole long slope of it, thin and white and the same colour as the sky, so that the awnings at the bottom of the rows, down by the river, were softer at their edges than the awnings at the top. The lanterns on the awning poles were still lit from the night, though nobody needed them. Somebody would come round with a hooked stick in an hour and put them out, and until then they hung in the haze at their different heights like lamps in a room that had forgotten it was morning.

Cael came down the second row with the bread under his arm and his left hand in his coat pocket, because the burn on that forearm had begun, in the last few cold mornings, to ache in a way it had not when it was new. It was the thirty-sixth day since Weaver's Row, a month and more of mornings in Ardenmere, and it was the slowest one he had seen.

He liked the slow mornings. On the busy ones the market was all noise, and you could learn nothing from noise except that it was there. On the slow ones you could see how the place was put together.

He had a month of it in his head by now, laid down the way he laid down everything, in order and by the hour. The bearded fish cousin set out his board first and packed it away first, and was gone by the time the haze lifted most days. The bread woman at the top of the second row put yesterday's loaves under this morning's, and gave him two from underneath now without being asked, and without the sour look. The nut boy's sister lit the charcoal under the drum, and the nut boy ate an apple on an upturned crate until the first customer came, and then threw the core over the awning into the next row without looking where it went. The fruit woman by the steps, whose name nobody had ever been told, sat behind her baskets with her hands in her lap and waited to be pointed at.

He knew the two errand boys, too, the one in the red cap and the one with the brother in the carters', who ran messages and small parcels up from the river to the yards and down again all day at a dead sprint, shouting insults at each other across whatever street they happened to be crossing. They had half a mark each riding on him, according to Lira, and he tried not to think about that.

This morning the rows were doing all of those things, and something was wrong with all of them.

It took him the length of three stalls to find it. It was not any one thing. No awning had come down and nobody was shouting. It was a tightness, very small, in how people were moving, as if every person in the market had been handed a cup filled to the brim and told not to spill it.

The pie man two stalls along had stopped calling his prices. He was still standing behind his tray with a pie in each hand, but he was looking past his customer's shoulder at something down the row, and the customer was waiting with her coin held out and did not seem to mind waiting.

A woman with a basket took her change from the bread woman and left with it still in her open hand, quickly, as though she had remembered a pot on the fire. A little girl who had been sent across the row for something came halfway back with it before her mother called her name, once, sharply, and the girl stopped and looked round to see what she had done wrong, and found nothing, and came on more slowly.

None of it would have meant anything to somebody who had come down the row for the first time this morning. For somebody who had come down it every morning for a month, it was like hearing a familiar clock miss a tick in the next room.

He looked where the pie man was looking.

Down at the bottom of the row, where the market levelled out by the fish stalls, a man was walking.

He was not doing anything else. He was not buying, and he was not calling to anybody, and he was not in any hurry. He was a man of middle height in a plain grey coat that had been brushed that morning, with a small book in his left hand and a pencil in his right, walking along the line of the fish stalls at the pace of somebody counting barrels in a cellar. Every few stalls he stopped, and looked, and sometimes said a word or two to whoever was behind the board, and sometimes wrote something in the book, and then walked on.

On the lapel of the grey coat, small enough that you would miss it unless you were looking for something to miss, there was a pin.

Cael was not near enough to see its shape. He did not need to be. He had seen that shape pressed into dark wax on the second morning after Kindling, on a paper that was still folded small inside the back cover of his old notebook, with its creases gone soft from being opened. It was a hand, holding up a balance.

His body wanted to stop walking.

He felt it want to, quite clearly, as though somebody had put a hand flat on his chest. A small cold current went up from his stomach through the middle of him and sat under his breastbone, and he knew the feeling, because he had last had it standing in a circle of pale wood on Weaver's Row while a brass plate failed to light.

He did not stop. He did not slow down. He went on down the second row at the same pace he had been going, with the bread under his arm, because a boy who stopped dead in a market and stared at a man in a grey coat was a boy who would be looked at, and he did not want to be looked at by anybody this morning, least of all by a man with a book.

---

The haze helped and the lanterns helped.

The awning poles in the market were all different heights, which he had thought on his first evening was carelessness and had since decided was simply what happened when sixty people put up sixty poles over forty years without asking each other. It meant that the lanterns hanging from them made a kind of uneven ruler down every row. The red lantern on the pie man's pole hung low, at about the height of a tall man's chin; the bread woman's hung high, with a crack across its glass; the cracked one beside the cheese stall had never been lit in all the mornings he had passed it.

If he kept the man between the lanterns, he could tell how far away he was without looking at him for more than a moment at a time.

So he did that. He bought the dried pears from the fruit woman by pointing at the basket, and she weighed them out for him with her eyes on the bottom of the row, and gave him full weight. He watched the grey coat pass under the low red lantern, and then under the next one. The man had turned up the third row now, the one that ran parallel to Cael's on the far side of the awnings, and he was coming up it at the same unchanging pace, one stall at a time.

It was the same thing he did in the circle, he realised, and it steadied him a little to realise it. He ran the distance and the angle and the two or three places he could go if the distance closed faster than it should. Here those places were a gap between the cheese stall and the rope-seller, which led through to the fourth row, and the steps at the bottom, and the open end of the row behind him. He ran them without deciding to and kept them running while he walked.

The man was not hunting. That was the next thing he saw, and he made himself see it carefully, because it was the kind of thing a frightened person could get wrong in either direction.

A hunting man looked at faces. This man looked at stalls. He stopped where a stall had been there a long time, and where there was somebody behind it who would know things, and he did not ask anybody to show him a card. Nobody was stopped. Nobody was taken by the arm. He moved through the market the way the mender moved a straightedge along a frame, slowly and from one end to the other, looking for the place where it was not true.

At the nut stall, he stopped.

Cael was close enough by then to see it, across the third row, through a gap in the awnings where a canvas had been rolled back. The nut boy was standing up behind the drum with the scoop in his hand. The man said something to him, short.

And the nut boy, who in a month Cael had never once seen look at the pointer of his scale, who weighed everything by watching his customer's face, looked down at the pointer. He tipped nuts into a paper twist with his eyes on the brass needle the whole time, as carefully as if it were the first scale he had ever been given, and handed the twist across, and took the coin, and said something with his eyes still on the needle.

His sister went on turning the crank on the drum. She did not look up at all.

The man wrote in his book. Then he walked on, up the third row, toward the top of the market, where the rows ended and the street began.

That brought him, in about forty steps, to the same place Cael was going.

Cael did the arithmetic and did not like it. If he kept his pace he would reach the top of the second row at almost the same moment the man reached the top of the third, and the two rows came out side by side onto the street there, with nothing between them but a water trough and a post. He could slow down. Slowing down was the thing his body wanted, and that was exactly why he did not trust it.

He kept his pace.

At the top of the row the woman with the basket of eggs, who sold from a blanket by the trough, stood up to shake out her blanket just as he came level with her. He had seen her do it at this hour on a dozen mornings. He let her be where she was. He went round the end of the blanket on the trough side, and the blanket came up between him and the third row in a slow flap of brown wool, and when it came down again the man in the grey coat was walking past the post an arm's length to his right.

Cael did not look at him. He looked at the egg woman's blanket, settling, and at the street ahead.

He saw the man anyway, the way you see something at the edge of a lamp. The grey coat was good cloth and had been mended at one elbow by somebody who knew how. The book was small, black, with a strap round it. The boots were dark and dry and ordinary. Cael looked at them, because Torvin had said *not from round here, by his boots*, and he could not tell anything from them at all. They could have been anybody's.

The man did not look at him either. He did not seem to look at anything in particular. He walked across the top of the market and down the short street toward the river, toward the lower bridge and the squat brown building that stood at the head of the fish steps.

Cael had catalogued that building in his first week, along with everything else. It had a board on its front wall with a few official notices nailed to it that nobody read, and a door that was usually shut, and it was the only place on this side of the river that had anything to do with the Compact. The nut boy had called it the post. Nobody had ever told him what went on inside it, and he had never seen anybody go in.

The man in the grey coat went in. The door shut behind him.

Cael went on walking.

---

He did not go back to Torvin's.

He had meant to, and his feet had the way home in them, two lanes up and one across, but when he reached the corner where he should have turned he went straight on instead, down toward the river. He did not decide it all at once. It decided itself one step at a time, the way the long way round decides itself when you are tired and do not want to be seen going in at your own door.

If there was one man, there might be two. If there were two, the second would be the one that mattered, because the first had been walking in a grey coat with a pin on it and a book in his hand, where the whole market could see him. Nobody who meant to find a particular boy would do it like that.

But somebody who meant to find a particular boy might send one man out in a grey coat to walk the market, slowly, where everybody could see, and another man to stand somewhere quiet and watch who moved when the first man came by.

He went along the lane of the river workshops.

The shutters were all down at this hour, the doorways open to the haze, and the cobbler was in his doorway with his last between his knees. The lantern woman was in hers, under her ceiling of broken lanterns. Cael stopped in front of her doorway as if he were looking at the lanterns, which was a thing people did, and she did not look up from her soldering.

There were thirteen lanterns hanging there today, and four of them still had most of their glass. He looked at the street behind him in the curved glass of the nearest one, small and bent and upside down at the edges, the far side of the lane sliding round the glass like water.

A cart. Two women carrying a rolled carpet between them. A dog. Nobody standing still.

He went on.

At the end of the lane, past the doorway with no sign, he turned up the cut. It was a passage so narrow between the cooper's wall and the back of the bucket-maker's shop that two people could not pass in it without one of them turning sideways, and it went up in a series of crooked steps toward the four lanes by the well. Halfway up there was a doorway, bricked in long ago, which made a shallow box in the wall about the depth of a man's shoulders.

He stepped into it and stood still and counted to sixty.

It was the hardest sixty he had ever counted. His heart was going faster than it had in the second exchange against Petra. It seemed absurd to him, standing there with dried pears in his pocket and a loaf under his arm, but it was true, and he noted that it was true.

The cut was very quiet. He could hear the river below, and the cooper's mallet beginning on the other side of the wall, slow, like somebody knocking at a door who did not expect an answer. He could hear his own breath. At forty a cat came down the steps from above, saw him in the doorway, stopped with one foot lifted, and went back up the way it had come with great dignity.

At sixty, nobody had come up the cut behind him.

He came out of the doorway and went back down the cut the way he had come, which was the last thing anybody following him would expect, and out into the lane of workshops again, walking the other way past the lantern woman, who looked up this time, and looked at him, and went back to her hinge.

The mender's doorway was open. The mender did not talk in the mornings, and he did not talk now. But he was standing at his bench with the loupe in his eye and a clock in pieces in front of him, and as Cael went by he took the loupe out and looked at the street, briefly, the way he looked at a frame before he leaned on the lever, and then put the loupe back.

Cael went up the broad steps by the carriers' hut, past the hut itself, where the ink-fingered clerk was sorting letters and did not look up, and round by the long way to the four lanes at the well with the iron cover. There he took the lane he never took, the one that went east toward the dyers' sheds, and walked down it for the length of a long breath. Then he turned round, as though he had remembered something, and walked back.

Nobody in the lane turned round with him.

He came out at last into the lopsided triangle where the three streets met, and stopped in front of the board, and read it. It had a new notice on it since yesterday, a card for the Sunday, in Vell's upright pencil on the back of a bill for candles. He read it twice, and then the one under it, and the one under that, and while he read them he watched each of the three streets in turn, out of the corner of his eye, for as long as it took to read a notice.

People came and went in all three. None of them came out of the haze and stopped, and none of them looked at the board, or at the boy reading it.

One man, one route, one morning.

He stood there with the bread going stale under his arm and turned that over. It was not a manhunt, and it was not a man looking for a face. It was smaller than either. It was a man in a good coat walking a market slowly, from one end to the other, and writing in a book, and going into the only building in the district that belonged to the people who had stamped one word on his card.

He found that it frightened him more than a manhunt would have. A manhunt you could at least understand. This could mean anything, or nothing, and he had no way yet of knowing which.

---

He wrote it down as soon as he was alone, on the low wall by the pump in Torvin's yard, under the pear tree that had given up growing tall and grown wide instead.

He wrote it in the front of the Log, because it was a thing that had happened and not a thing he was afraid of, and he wanted it to stay that way. There were no columns for it. He wrote it in the margin with the other facts that would not go in columns, under the fish man's nod and the polite man at Torvin's door.

*Thirty-sixth day, morning. Market, a slow day, haze. A man in a grey coat, Compact pin on the lapel (the hand and the balance), a small black book with a strap. Middle height. Walked the fish stalls and then the third row, one stall at a time, unhurried. Stopped at stalls that have been there a long time. Spoke a word or two. Wrote. Asked nobody for a card. Stopped nobody. Did not look at faces. Did not look at me. Went into the post at the head of the fish steps. One man. I went the long way round and saw no second one.*

He read it back. It was all true, and he had seen all of it himself, and there was no *h* anywhere in it.

Then he sat with the pencil and found that he wanted to write the next thing, and did not know what the next thing was.

His hands were still not quite steady, so he did what Hesk would have done, and gave them something to do. He took the pears out of his pocket and ate one, slowly. Then he took the bread out from under his arm and looked at it, and found that he had been holding it so hard all the way round the long way that he had crushed the top of the loaf flat.

He was looking at the flattened loaf when Torvin came out through the kitchen door with an empty bucket, and stopped, and looked at the loaf too.

"Bread woman sell you that?"

"I did it."

Torvin considered that, and seemed to accept it as a reasonable thing for a person to do to a loaf. He went to the pump and began to work the handle. The water came up in long gulps and rang in the bottom of the bucket.

Cael watched him fill it. Then, because he had decided on the wall that he would gather what he could before he let himself decide what any of it meant, and because Torvin had run this house for eleven years and had a ledger, he said, "Do the Compact walk the market often?"

The pump handle stopped at the top of its stroke.

"Define often," said Torvin.

"I saw a man this morning. With the pin. Walking the rows." Cael made his voice as level as a fact. "It's the first I've seen."

"Then you've been here about long enough to have seen one, if you were going to." Torvin brought the handle down. The water gulped. "Eleven years I've had the sign up. I've seen four. Four in eleven years. That's not a habit. That's weather. It comes when it comes, and nobody's ever told me why, and I've never asked."

"What happens after?"

"Mostly nothing. Man walks about, writes in his book, goes away." Torvin pumped twice more and let the handle rest. "Once, the second one, they took a woman in to the post for the afternoon. Turned out she owed a guild fee from somewhere upriver, before she ever came here. She paid it and came back and complained about it for a month. That's the worst I know of." He lifted the bucket off the spout. "They've no great love for this side of the river. Costs them more than it's worth to come and look at us."

He stood with the bucket hanging from one hand and looked at Cael. It was the look he gave coins on their third count, slow and thorough, checking his own figures and not anybody else's.

"Why are you asking?"

"Curious."

"Course you are." Torvin did not push. He picked up the bucket and turned toward the kitchen door, and then said, over his shoulder, in the same flat voice he used for the rent, "If you want more than my four, ask up in the yards. The fighting people count pins better than I do. It's bad for a yard if the wrong sort of attention comes to stand at its gate."

He went in. The door banged. Through it, a moment later, Cael heard Torvin's wife say something about the bucket and Torvin say, "I know," in the tone of a man who had known for eleven years.

---

So in the afternoon, instead of going to the mender, he went up into the yards.

It was not one of the mender's afternoons, but he would have had to go anyway if it had been. He could not have sorted keys today. He went up the lane where the ground began to rise, with the walls on either side of it and the flat smack of hands on canvas coming over them, and he asked his question the way he would have tested a joint he did not trust: once, lightly, in one place, and then somewhere else.

He asked the woman who kept the chalk board at the bottom of the lane with the yard prices on it, while he pretended to read the prices. He asked a man filling buckets at the trough, and the two girls he had seen on his first afternoon drilling the block, who were sitting on a wall eating, and who recognised him, which he had not expected.

"You're the one who ran Brenna round," one of them said, before he could ask anything.

"Yes."

"She's still talking about it."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's very good." The girl took a bite of her bread. "What do you want?"

He asked them about the pin.

He never asked anybody twice, and he never asked in a way that made the asking itself a thing to remember. Everybody's answer came out much the same, the way Torvin's had. Somebody's aunt had seen one, two summers ago. Somebody else remembered one the winter before last. Pins came through the district now and then, not often, and walked about, and went away again, and nothing much came of it.

"Was it a big one?" said the girl on the wall. "With the dogs?"

"There were no dogs."

"Then it was nothing." She brushed crumbs off her knee. "When it's something, there are dogs."

He did not know whether that was true, and he did not think she did either, and he filed it with an *h* beside it, because it was what she believed.

At the top of the lane he stopped at the last gate, the one with the old man in it.

The yard was as it had been every time he had passed it: a square of dirt with a post, the woman with the shoulder working through her six positions alone in the middle, and the old man in his corner in the shade, standing in the stance that looked like nothing else in the lane. The woman reached the fourth position, and held it, and her shoulder stayed down. She went on to the fifth.

The old man's pale eyes came round to Cael at the gate, and went to the left hand in his coat pocket, and came back up.

"Afternoon."

"Afternoon," said Cael. He had his question ready. He had asked it six times that afternoon, smoothly, without weight. Standing in front of this gate he found that he did not want to ask it smoothly. "There was a man with a Compact pin walking the market this morning. Does that happen often? Up here, I mean. Do people notice?"

The old man did not answer at once. He watched the woman in the middle of the yard finish her sixth position and go back to the first.

"Up here they notice," he said. "Up here they notice everything that might come and stand at a gate." He shifted his weight, the small shift from one foot to the other that Cael still could not find the reason for. "They don't come for nothing. That's the first thing. Whatever they tell you, and whatever it turns out to be, somebody somewhere wanted a man walking that market this week and not last week. Pins don't walk themselves out of the post."

"Everybody says it's nothing much."

"Everybody's mostly right. It mostly comes to nothing much." The pale eyes rested on him. "But I'll give you a thing you didn't ask for, boy, because you came to the gate and asked properly instead of asking sideways. Don't stand about wondering whether it was you he was walking for. You'll never find that out by wondering, and it'll eat your evenings." He turned back to the yard. "Wonder what changed."

Cael stood at the gate.

"Somebody upstream of that man decided this month was different from last month," the old man said, to the yard. "Find out what's different. You'll learn more from that than from chasing the fellow in the coat. He's only the end of a string."

"Thank you."

"I'm not being kind. I'm telling you where to look." The stillness came down over him again, like a lid, and Cael understood that he had been given everything he was going to be given.

He walked back down the lane slowly. The flat smack of hands on canvas came over the walls on either side, and somewhere a woman was laughing at somebody who had fallen over, and the haze had burned off at last and left the afternoon thin and bright and cold.

*Wonder what changed.*

It was better than anything he would have come to by himself. He could feel how much better, as clearly as he had felt the difference between Hesk's straightedge and his own eye on the spice balance. It took the question off the man in the grey coat, where there was nothing to hold on to, and put it somewhere he could stand and look.

What was different in the district this month from last month?

He knew the answer before he had reached the bottom of the lane. He tried to find another, honestly, all the way down past the chalk board and the trough and the two girls on the wall, who waved, and he could not find one.

A month ago, a card with one word on it in square brackets had come into the district along the east road at dusk, in the pocket of a boy with a bag strap scuffed by a cart, and nobody at the edge of the district had asked to see it.

That was what had changed. That was the only thing he could find. It was him.

# Chapter 22 — The District's One New Variable

Lira did not like to eat at Torvin's. She said it was because Torvin's wife looked at her as though she might steal a spoon, which was not true, and Cael suspected the real reason was that a table full of boarders was a table full of people who would ask her things.

So in the evening they went down to the eating house by the lower bridge, where Amrit Sole cooked.

It was a long, low room half below the level of the street, so that you went down three steps to the door and sat with the river going past at the height of your shoulder outside the little windows. There were two long tables and a bench along each, and a counter at the back with a hatch in the wall behind it, and through the hatch you could see the kitchen. It was all steam and copper and one large man with a shaved head moving through it without hurry, the way he had moved through the circle.

Amrit saw them come in. He did not stop what he was doing. But a little later, when the girl who carried the bowls came out, she set down in front of Cael a bowl with twice the bread beside it that she set beside anybody else's, and when Cael looked at the hatch, Amrit was looking at a pot.

"He's decided you're his," Lira said. "He does that. He decided I was his after my third loss. I've never paid full price for bread in here since."

"I lost to him."

"You lost to him properly." She broke her own bread. "That's the kind he keeps."

It was a fish stew, mostly potato, with something green floating in it that Cael could not name and did not ask about. It was hot, and it was good, and he ate half of it before he found that he had stopped tasting it and was thinking about a grey coat.

He told her. He told it the way he had written it in the margin, in order, nothing added, because that was the only way he knew how to tell a thing so that somebody else could weigh it properly. He told her about the market going tight, and the nut boy looking at his pointer, and the egg woman's blanket, and the post at the head of the fish steps. Then he told her about the long way round, the lantern glass and the doorway in the cut and the sixty he had counted, and the lane he never took. He told her about Torvin's four, and the girls on the wall and their dogs, and the old man.

Lira did not interrupt. She ate while he talked, slowly, and when he got to the old man she stopped eating and set her spoon down in the bowl, carefully, the way you put something down when you want both hands free to think with.

"A pin," she said. "On this side of the river."

"Walking. Not hiding it."

"No. They don't hide it. That's the point of it." She turned her cup round on the table by its handle, once, and then again, the way she sometimes walked round the post before she struck it. "They come through. They do. People check that nobody Unranked has drifted somewhere they shouldn't be living, or that nobody's running anything big enough and loud enough to make somebody across the river look foolish. That happens." She looked up. "But I've been in this district five months, Cael, and that's the first pin I've heard of."

"Torvin's seen four in eleven years."

"Then Torvin and I agree. It's rare." She put both hands round the cup. "And this district talks. You've seen it. Two errand boys bet half a mark on you and inside a day the woman at the water bucket knew. If pins walked the market every season, somebody would have told me about it in my first week, the way they told me about Marrow. Nobody did. So either they really are as rare as Torvin says, or I've been listening to the wrong people for five months." She said it flatly. She did not make it larger or smaller than it was. "I don't think I've been listening to the wrong people."

He had come to rely on that more than he had known. In Denvash, when a thing was frightening, people had given him the version of it that was easiest to carry. Hesk had done it out of love for years. Lira did not do it at all. She told him the shape of what she actually thought and then let him pick it up, and it was heavier, and it was the right weight.

"The old man said not to wonder whether it was me," he said. "He said to wonder what changed."

Lira's eyebrows went up a little. It was the look she gave him on the barrel when somebody in the circle had done a thing she had not expected to be done well.

"That's good. That's very good." She thought about it. "That's better advice than you'd get from most people who know you, let alone a man in a yard who's said six words to you in a month."

"Seven, now. And he didn't know I meant me. I asked it as if I didn't."

"Then it's better still. He had nothing to gain by it." She picked up her spoon again and did not use it. "So. What changed?"

He had known since the bottom of the lane that she would ask, and he had had the whole walk home and the whole walk down here to find a different answer. He had not found one.

"Nothing I can find," he said. "Except me."

Lira did not say anything for a moment. Out in the dark beyond the little windows the river went by, black and quick, carrying a lantern on the stern of a late barge.

"That's not nothing changing," she said at last, quite quietly. "A month ago a boy walks in off the east road with a card nobody looks at. That's the district's one new variable. A month later the district gets the first pin anybody can remember. I don't like that arithmetic either."

"No."

"It doesn't mean it's the right answer." She said it at once, as if she had been waiting to. "Two things can land close together and have nothing to do with each other. That happens. It's not just something people say to make themselves feel better." She looked straight at him. "But you don't get to call it a coincidence because you'd like it to be one. You go and look for the other answer. And if you look properly and there isn't one, you have to sit with the one that's left."

"I looked all afternoon."

"I know you did. I can see it on you." Her mouth did something at one corner. "You've got the face you had after Petra."

He did not know what face that was, and he did not ask. He looked at his stew, which had gone cold, and at the double bread beside it, which was Amrit's way of saying something.

"How long," said Lira, and her voice had changed, "do you think you've got? Before it isn't a man walking a market. Before it's a man at Torvin's door asking for you by your name."

"I don't know."

"No." She did not try to give him a number, and he was grateful for that too. She sat with him in the not knowing for a while, with the river going past, and then she said, in her ordinary voice, the one she used in the grey half of the yard, "We'll map it. That's what we do with a thing we can't see the edges of. We go round it until it's got edges. This has edges somewhere. We just haven't walked far enough yet."

"You make it sound easy."

"It's not easy. It's the only way either of us knows how to do anything." She pushed his bowl an inch toward him. "Eat that. Amrit will be hurt if you leave it, and he'll show it by giving you less bread next time, and you'll deserve it."

He ate it cold. It was still good.

There was a letter waiting at Torvin's.

Yeni had it, as she had had the last one, sitting cross-legged on her cot with a shirt across her knee and her sewing kit open beside her. She held it out without looking up from the seam.

"West bag. Noon."

"Thank you."

"Twice," said Yeni, to her needle. "Twice is a habit. He'll keep writing now."

It was Hesk's square hand on the outside again, *Cael, care of Torvin's, ROOMS (the O leans)*, and this time no other hand had needed to add anything, because the runner knew the way. Cael sat on his cot by the window, where there was still a little grey light, and turned it over twice before he broke the wax, because he could not stop himself, though he knew this time that it would not say anything terrible. The last one had not.

It was one sheet, written on both sides, all in the same ink.

*Cael,*

*Your letter came on the twenty-eighth day, and I've read it four times, which beats the fifth entry. I'm writing back the same night, so you'll know it's the same night.*

*A ladder that doesn't ask to see a card. I won't pretend I'm easy about it. I'm not. But I know the kind of place, and I know the kind of person who goes looking for one, and I'd have been surprised if you hadn't found it. I won't tell you not to climb it. You'd climb it anyway, and then you'd stop telling me about it, and I'd rather have the telling.*

*Two losses, each longer than the last. You say you're not trying to win yet. I believe you, and I'm glad, and I'm keeping the letter in case you ever try to tell me otherwise.*

*The mender sounds like a man worth working for. Anybody who checks after every lean on the lever is somebody you can learn from without his saying a word. Don't touch his clocks.*

*Tell me about Lira. Not what she does. You told me what she does. Tell me what she's like.*

*Nothing more from the guild. A man came and looked at the workshop and asked what the drawer of springs was for, and I told him, and he wrote it down. That's all. That's the guild's way. It may be months.*

*Garrik says the arm's still running clean.*

*Two losses, each longer than the last.*

He sat with the letter on his knee and looked out at the dark coming down over the rooftops.

Hesk had written that sentence on the twenty-eighth day, at the kitchen table, in the lamplight, the same night his letter had come. On the twenty-eighth day, three days' walk west of here, Cael had been dropping under a backhand in the grey half of the Cinder House yard and coming up in somebody else's coat. He had already lost to Amrit Sole by then, with his hand up, and to Petra Voss with his face in the dirt. He had been 0 and 4, not 0 and 2. And at midnight on that same night he had stood in Torvin's yard reaching for a thing that was not there until he stopped counting.

Hesk had known none of it. Hesk had been writing to a boy who had lost twice and was not trying to win yet, and was looking, and that boy had been real when the letter left Ardenmere, and had stopped being quite real by the time it arrived in Denvash, and was a long way from real tonight.

He had known in his head that the post was slow. He had worked out the days on the road. But he had not felt it until now, with Hesk's careful sentences on his knee, answering questions he had stopped asking and congratulating him on something two weeks old. It was like talking to somebody across a valley, where you shouted and waited, and the answer came back clear and kind and a long time after you had moved on from the place where you shouted.

*Tell me what she's like.*

He did not know how he was going to answer that. He thought it would take him some time.

He did the front of the Log first, because that was the rule.

The front had nothing in it for today except the margin note he had written on the wall by the pump. He read it over, and added one line under it, because it was a fact and he had seen it himself: *Torvin: four in eleven years. Once a guild fee. Mostly nothing. (Torvin, h.)* Then the girls on the wall: *When it's something there are dogs (h; I don't think she knows).* Then the old man, in his words as nearly as Cael could remember them: *Don't wonder if it's you. Wonder what changed (h).*

He looked at that last line for a while.

Then he closed the front and turned the book over and opened it from the back, and wrote the thing he had not been able to write on the wall in the afternoon.

*What changed: me.*

He looked at the three words. They were the truest thing he had written all day, and they were not a ruling, because a ruling was what you did next and he did not know what he would do next. They were not a guess, either, because he had looked for the other answer properly and not found it. They were simply what was left.

*Lira says don't call it a coincidence because I'd like it to be one,* he wrote under it. *She's right. I don't know that it's about me. I can't find anything else it could be about.*

*I keep thinking it should feel bigger. It was one man. He didn't look at me. He didn't ask anybody my name, as far as I could see. If I hadn't been watching the market for a month I wouldn't have known anything had happened at all. I think that's what frightens me. A thing that big shouldn't be that quiet.*

He stopped there, because he could feel himself beginning to write the same fear three different ways, and the back of the Log was for things that would not go in the columns, not for walking round and round the same thing until it wore a groove.

He took out a clean sheet instead and wrote to Hesk.

It was harder than the last letter, and he understood why before he had written the first line. Hesk would read this on about the forty-fourth day, at the kitchen table, in the twenty minutes of sideways light, and whatever had happened by then, Cael would not be able to tell him. And whatever Hesk wrote back would come on about the fifty-first day, a week after that, answering a boy who had seen one pin and did not know what it meant. By then that boy would be a fortnight gone, the way the boy who had lost twice was gone tonight.

So he could not write the fear. The fear would be two weeks stale by the time anybody could do anything about it, and Hesk would have to carry it alone at the table for a week first.

He could write what he had seen, though, and that would still be true when it arrived.

*Hesk,*

*Your letter came today, the thirty-sixth day. You answered a boy who'd lost twice. It's four now: a Blade Path man, a Shield Path woman, an Ember Path cook who gave me a burn on the arm (it won't mark, everybody says, and they're all very firm about cold water), and a Force Path girl of seventeen who was quicker than me at everything. Each one lasted longer than the one before. Four, five, six, seven exchanges. The woman who keeps the book said the numbers out loud to me after the last, as if she were reading them off a ladder.*

*I'll tell you about Lira properly when I've worked out how. It isn't a short answer.*

*This morning I saw a man from the Compact walking the market. Pin on his coat, a book in his hand. He walked the rows slowly, from one end to the other, and wrote things down, and asked nobody for a card, and went into the post by the river. He didn't look at me. Torvin says he's seen four in eleven years and nothing much comes of them, and everybody I asked said the same. An old man in the yards told me not to wonder whether it was about me but to wonder what had changed. I've been wondering. I'll tell you what I find.*

*I'm all right. I'm telling you this because you asked for things as they come.*

*Tell me something ordinary. Tell me what's on the bench. I'd like to know what's on the bench.*

*— Cael*

He read it over. He did not tell Hesk what he had concluded about what had changed. Hesk would get there himself, at the kitchen table, about two lines after Cael had stopped writing, because Hesk always did. Putting it on the page would only have made Hesk read it in Cael's hand, as a thing Cael was sure of, a week late, when Cael was not sure of anything.

He did not tell him about the grey half of the yard, either, or the coat, or what he had boxed in the back of the Log. That was still three things. He counted them, and folded the letter, and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

She gave him his report before the bread, as she had every morning for a week now. She did it while they were walking the box drill at the slow speed, before the fast one, without looking at him, the way she might have read out the weather.

"Yesterday you looked at my hands three times in the eight corners. Not in the first set. After the third set, when you were tired." Back left. Front right. He said each thing back to her as she said it, so that he would have it exactly. Afterward, on the low wall, he wrote them into the front of the Log in her words, each with *(Lira, h)* beside it, in the evidence column, where she had told him on the first morning they belonged.

She never gave him a ruling. He had noticed it on the second morning and watched for it on every morning since, and she never had. She told him what she had seen and stopped. What he did about it was his. She had said on the first morning that it was a worse Arbiter than the real one, and he had come to think it was a more honest one, because it never once told him what he was.

"I'm doing the box."

"You're doing the box and thinking about the pin. Your front right was a hand short twice." She took the bread off the top of the post and tore it. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever it is you've decided this morning that you're going to do and haven't told me yet. You've got that face too."

He took his half of the bread.

"Have you ever bought from her?"

"And she'll have heard it from whoever heard it." Lira nodded slowly. "That's a good question. It's the kind she likes. It's got an answer." She brushed crumbs off her hands. "It'll cost you more than you think, and you'll pay it, and you'll go away with three sentences. She never gives more than three. Don't argue the price. She doesn't argue, and she'll think less of you, and her thinking less of you is worse than the price."

"How much more than I think?"

Lira named a figure. It was nearly four days at Torvin's.

He sat on the wall with the bread in his hand and did the sum against the pouch, which he could see in his mind as clearly as if it were laid out on the counter in its stacks. With the mender's two afternoons it would go about four weeks, more or less. Without four days of it, it would go a little less than that.

"You don't have to," said Lira.

"I know."

"I mean it. Knowing won't stop him walking the market. It won't take the pin off his coat." She stood up and set her feet in the grey half. "It'll only tell you what he wanted."

"That's what I want."

She looked at him for a moment, and then she nodded, as if he had told her the weight of something and she had checked it and found it right.

"Then go after noon. She's not there in the mornings. And don't go by the lane past the post." She brought her hands up. "Catch."

He posted the letter at noon, at the carriers' hut at the bottom of the fish steps, and watched the clerk with the inky fingers weigh it on the little brass balance and drop it in the west bag. He did not go up the steps afterward, past the brown building with the board on its wall. He went the other way, along the river and round by the dyers' sheds, the long way to the other market, as Lira had said.

The other market was as it had been the first time: a long lane with a wall down one side, and people along the wall at no particular distance from one another, some of them with things laid out in front of them and some with nothing at all. The man with the practice weapons was there, and the woman with the unpicked guild armbands, and the crate of ledgers with their covers torn off. Somewhere in that crate, he thought, Lira had found the four lines on her first night, and paid less than a pie for them.

The small old woman sat behind her bare table near the far end, upright, in her grey shawl, with her hands folded on the wood exactly as they had been folded a month ago. She might not have moved in all that time. There was nobody in front of her.

He made himself walk the lane once, from end to end, at the pace of somebody going somewhere else, as he had the first time. Then he came back, and stopped in front of the table.

She looked at him. Her eyes were very dark and very steady, and he had the feeling he had had the first time, of being followed for three steps by somebody who did not much care where he went.

"I'd like to ask a question."

She did not answer that. She waited.

He took the coins out of his coat. He had counted them that morning on the wall, in a separate stack, and they were the coins Lira had named, nearly four days at Torvin's. He laid them on the table between her hands without saying how much they were, because she would know, and because Lira had said not to argue. She did not look at them.

"A man with a Compact pin walked the food market yesterday morning," Cael said. "He stopped at some of the stalls and said things. I'd like to know what he was asking about."

The old woman looked at him for what seemed like a long time and was perhaps the length of three breaths. He had the strange sense that she was not deciding whether to answer, but deciding how much of the question he had actually asked, and how much he had only thought he was asking.

Then she spoke. It was a low, even voice, no louder than it needed to be.

"He asked the fish cousins, the nut boy and the bread woman which houses on this side take lodgers without papers, and they all named the same six, because everybody here knows the same six. At the post he asked for their lodging list, and the post doesn't keep one, because nobody here would answer it. He asked the carriers' clerk which of the six had letters going west to Denvash, and the clerk told him Torvin's, because the clerk is young and thinks it's clever to know things."

She stopped.

That was three. Cael waited, in case there was a fourth, and knew there would not be.

The coins were gone. He had not seen her take them. Her hands were folded on the bare wood exactly as they had been, and her eyes had already left him and gone back to the lane.

He stood there for a moment longer.

Then he said, "Thank you," because it seemed the thing to say, and she did not answer, and he walked away up the lane, past the crate of coverless ledgers and the armbands and the wooden blades, at the pace of somebody going somewhere else, though he was not.

He had wanted to know, and now he knew, and he understood at last what the nut boy had meant on his first morning. You could not hand it back.

*Which of the six had letters going west to Denvash.*

The man in the grey coat had not been walking the market to see what there was. He had been walking it to find out where one thing was. He had asked about lodgers without papers, because a boy who had been put out of his own house would be a lodger without papers. Then he had asked about letters going west, because that boy would write home.

And he had not asked a single person about the yards. The old woman had not said so, but she had not needed to. She had told him everything the man had asked, and fighting was not in it. Nobody had been asked whether there was a boy in the circuit. Nobody had said *the circuit kid*.

Cael walked home along the river with that turning over in him, and found it was two things at once, as most things seemed to be.

The polite man at Torvin's door nine days ago, the one with the boots, had asked for *the circuit kid*, and had been given nothing. The man in the grey coat had asked about lodgers and letters and nothing else. Either they were two different men asking two different questions, or they were one man who had asked the same question two different ways. He did not know which. He could not think of a way to find out that would not cost a great deal more than four days at Torvin's.

But whoever had sent the grey coat out into the market had sent it looking for a lodger with letters going home to Denvash. And the one place in the district where everybody knew exactly who he was and what he did, the place that had his losses written down in a ledger to the exchange, nobody in a grey coat had thought to ask about at all.

He did not know what that meant yet. He wrote it down that night, in the front, in the margin, with an *h* beside it, and the cost beside that, in figures.

*Paid nearly four days at Torvin's. Three sentences. Worth it. I don't know yet what for.*

# Chapter 23 — At the Month

"It's the month," Lira said, on the thirty-eighth morning, when the shadow had gone off the post.

He knew it was the month. He had known it lying awake before the brothers dropped their boots, and walking up to the yard in the dark, and all the way through the box drill, and Lira had known that he knew it. That was why she said it, he thought, so that it would be outside his head where they could both look at it.

"Renn said a month," he said. "On the fish steps. Vell said she'd hold it at the month."

"Then let's go and see whether she's holding it."

Vell's table was out in the shade of the back wall, as it was every morning when there was a card coming, with the ledger squared to its edge and the tea at her elbow. Renn was standing in front of it.

He had a sack on his shoulder, a long canvas one with a strap, packed tight and knotted at the neck, and he was wearing a coat Cael had not seen before. It was heavy and oiled and too warm for the yard, the kind of coat men wore on the river in winter. He was talking to Vell with his free hand moving, and Vell was listening to him with the expression of somebody listening to rain.

He heard them on the dirt behind him and turned round, and when he saw Cael he grinned.

"There he is. I was going to leave word." He swung the sack down off his shoulder onto the dirt, and it landed with a thump of something heavy and folded. "You've healed."

"Mostly."

"Mostly's what you get." Renn looked him up and down, the craftsman's look, the one that checked a joint. His eyes went to the left forearm, where the burn showed pink under the pushed-back sleeve, and stayed there a moment. "Ember?"

"Amrit Sole."

"Amrit." Renn whistled softly. "He doesn't burn people. He must have liked you." Then he turned back to Vell, and then to Cael again, and spread his free hand. "Here's the thing. There's a place on the grain barges going upriver. Three weeks of poling and carrying, there and back, and they're paying what they pay when nobody else wants to go in the cold. I'd be a fool to turn it down, and I've got a mother who'd tell me so."

"You're going today."

"Noon. They load at the long quay." Renn rubbed the back of his neck. "So I can't give you the second look at the month. I've asked her to hold it." He nodded at Vell. "Till I'm back. Three weeks, a bit more if the river's bad."

Vell said, without looking up from the ledger, "I'll hold it till the turn of the month after. Not past. If you're not back by then, it lapses, and you can come and ask me again like anybody else."

"I'll be back." Renn turned to Cael. "And I'll tell you the other half, since you'll work it out anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from me." He was not grinning now. "I don't want it at the month. Not now. Not with that one standing between us." He did not say which one. He did not have to. "I want to come back with something you haven't seen. And I want you to have learned something I haven't seen either. Otherwise it's the same bout twice, and I'll win it, and neither of us will know anything we didn't know a month ago."

"You'd win it?"

"I'd win it. You'd last five." Renn picked up his sack again and swung it onto his shoulder. "Five's not enough for what I want to look at."

Cael had been ready, for a month, to stand in front of this table and hear a date. He found that what he felt, now that he would not, was not disappointment. It was something stranger. It was relief, and under the relief a kind of respect that he had not expected to feel for the man who had put him on his back.

Renn had not forgotten the third exchange. He had been carrying it upriver and down for a month, on his shoulder with the sacks, and he wanted to come back to it ready.

"Three weeks," said Cael.

"A bit more if the river's bad." Renn started for the gate. Halfway there he stopped and turned, walking backward, and raised his voice so that it carried to the pie boy, who was raking the far side of the circle, and to the crows on the eaves. "And if anybody asks where I've gone, tell them I've gone up the river to rest. Teaching that one to last four took it out of me."

The pie boy laughed. Lira made a noise in her throat.

"He's been saying that on the fish steps for a fortnight," she said, when Renn had gone through the gate. "That he went easy on you. That he let you have the four because he could see you were keen. That every bout you've lasted since, you learned from him."

"Some of it I did."

"Don't ever tell him that. He'll have it painted on a sign." She looked at Vell. "Who now?"

Vell had picked up her pen and was writing a short line in the ledger. When she had finished she laid the pen across the page and looked up at Cael, the flat look, the thorough one.

"Come back tomorrow morning," she said. "Before the yard fills. I'll have somebody for you."

"Who?"

"Tomorrow." She picked up her tea. "I haven't decided. When I've decided, I'll tell you, and not before, and not because you asked."

On the cooper's roof, in the afternoon, with the tar warm under them and the first wall of the Ranked core going up the hill in the thin light, Lira would not guess.

"I could," she said. "I could give you six names. It'd be one of the six, probably, and you'd spend tonight getting ready for all six, and you'd get ready for five of them for nothing." "I don't know how to have an afternoon off."

"I know. It's one of your worst qualities." She lifted the arm and looked at him from under it. "Try anyway. It's like the box drill. You think about it until one day you don't need to."

He tried. Somewhere down there Renn was loading. Vell did not open the ledger again until she had the yard to herself.

That was not hard on a Saturday. There was no card on a Saturday. The public house's back door stood open for the air, and somebody inside was scrubbing a floor and singing about it, badly. The pie boy had raked the circle and gone off to wherever the pie boy went. Only the crows were left, along the eaves, watching the dirt for things that were not there.

She sat on her stool in the shade with a fresh cup of tea and turned back through the book.

She did not often read back. The book was for other people to read. It was there so that anybody who came to the table in a year, or ten, could open it and find out what had happened in this yard on a given night and who owed whom what. It was not there for her to sit with. But there were five entries in it now under no name at all, only *unrated*, and they had begun to sit beside each other on the pages in a way she found she wanted to look at all together.

She found the first. *Renn (Cu 3, Blade) over unrated debut (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.), fourth exchange, by the flat. Called.* And under it the eight lines she had not needed to write.

Then Petra. Seven. *Saw the lean from the second exchange. I am nearly sure of it. Moved on it every time and was late every time. Moved earlier in the third and she missed. She looked at them in a row and saw what anybody would have seen, which was a boy who lost and lasted, and lasted longer each time. That was not rare. She had seen it before. But that was not what was in the entries, if you read them properly.

What was in the entries was that he saw. Every time, against every one of them, he had found the thing the fighter across from him did not know they did. He had found the heel, the dip, the shimmer and the breath, and Petra's lean, which Vell herself had not seen in a year and a half of watching Petra. Then he had found the thing in himself that the other fighter used against him. He had written down Brenna's temper, and Amrit's bait, and Petra's learning him back. None of that was strange. All of it was ordinary work. It was the ordinary work of a good watcher, done better than she had seen it done by anybody in his first month, and better than most people did it in their third season.

She did not need a new word for that. She had a great many words for that.

She turned back to the first entry, to the third exchange, and read again what she had written there, in a smaller hand than the rest because she had written it slowly.

*Defender off the line before R.'s strike began. Not a read. I watched for the read and there was none.*

She dipped the pen and wrote one word in the margin beside it.

*Atypical.*

She looked at it after she had written it.

It was a word she kept for herself. She had used it in this book, and in the one before it, and the one before that, eleven times in something over thirty years. She knew it was eleven, because she kept a count on the inside of the back cover, a row of small strokes in the corner, the only tally in the book that was not about money. Now there were twelve.

It did not mean rare. Rare things happened in the yard every week. It did not mean good, or dangerous, or a thing to tell the Compact about, or to keep from them. In her book it meant one thing only, and she had decided what it meant on the night she first wrote it, when she was not much older than the Wind girl was now.

It meant: *I saw this with my own eyes from this stool, and it does not fit anything else I have ever seen, and I do not know what it was. I am writing it down so that nobody, including me, can ever decide afterward that I did not see it.*

That was all. It was a peg in a wall, so that she could find the place again.

Of the eleven before this, she remembered every one. Four had turned out to be nothing much: a fighter on a strange night, a fever coming on, a man who had been drinking and should not have been allowed over the rope. Two had turned out to be the start of somebody who later went across the river and was not seen in Ardenmere again. One had been the Ash Path man, the first time he did not stop. And four she still did not know about, and never would, because the fighters had stopped coming and the book did not follow people out of the gate.

She did not know which kind this was. She did not try to guess. Guessing was not what the word was for.

---

Marrow came across the yard while she was looking at it, with his slate under his arm and his good coat buttoned against the cold.

He did not work on a Saturday, but he came by on most of them, because there was usually a card to be made for the Sunday and he liked to know it before anybody else did. He stopped at the end of her table and smiled.

"The boy," he said pleasantly. "Renn's gone up the river."

"He has."

"So you'll want somebody else for him." He tapped the slate against his palm. "I've a man from Fenrow who'd take him. Iron-rated over there, Copper fourth here, lost his last two. He'd come for a debut's purse. He'd last the boy four or five, and the boy would last him, and I'd have a nice clean over to lay."

"I'm not looking for an over."

"Everybody's looking for an over." Marrow's smile did not change. "The yard's started coming for him, you know. Not many. A few. Two errand boys with half a mark each. I could fill the rope for him if he lasted somebody I could price."

Vell looked at him for the first time.

"I don't match people to suit your slate," she said. "I never have. You know that, and so does everybody who's ever stood at that rope. That's why they stand at it."

"Of course." Marrow inclined his head, as if she had told him the weather. "Of course. I only mention it." He looked at the book, upside down, the way people did, and at the margin where she had just written. She saw him read the word and saw it mean nothing to him. Then he went off across the yard and out through the gate, with the slate under his arm, unhurried, a big man in a good coat who had never once in his life been caught doing anything he could not explain.

She watched him go.

She did not trust Marrow. She had never trusted Marrow, and had decided a long time ago that she did not need to, so long as his book and hers never touched. But Marrow had noticed the boy, and Marrow did not notice things that would not one day be worth money, and she wrote that down too. She did not put it in the book. She put it in her head, where she kept the things that were hers.

There was another thing she kept there, from Thursday night.

Three different people had come to her table before the first bout to tell her that there had been a pin in the food market that morning, walking the rows. Three people was a great many for something that had not touched the yards at all. She had listened to each of them and said *I heard*, and had not asked anything, because there was nothing in it for the book. The book had stayed clear of the Compact for as long as she had kept it, in the plainest way there was. It never wrote down anything the Compact would want to read. It wrote what happened in this yard, between the rope and the wall, and nothing else.

*Nothing to register,* the margin said, beside the first entry.

She had decided on that first morning, with the Wind girl standing in front of her table and her chin up, that she did not need to know what the boy was. She decided it again now, for the same reason, and found it was still the right decision. If she ever needed to know, she would watch until she knew, and then she would write down what she had seen, and not before.

---

So it came back to the same question, which was the only one that was hers to answer. Who next?

She had told him herself, at the table after Petra, that he would come to the end of lasting. *Then you'll have to do something besides last.* She had meant it as a fact, not a warning. Now Renn had gone up the river and left a gap, and she had a choice to make for him, and she found she wanted to make it carefully.

She went down the card in her head.

Baro would not do. Baro would teach him only that every opening was a lie, and she had watched the boy sit on the barrel with his notebook through Baro's afternoon and come away with that already written down. Besides, Baro did not fight anybody who might learn him, and the boy was nothing but somebody who might learn him.

Brenna would want it, and Brenna would be no use. It would be the same bout again with a little less running, and Brenna would be angry at the end of it whichever way it went.

There was a woman at Fenrow with an Ember Path that was a good deal less kind than Amrit's. She would burn him and teach him nothing new, and Vell did not let people be hurt in her yard for nothing.

Petra again would be speed again, and he knew already that he was losing on speed. A second loss to Petra would only tell him the same thing louder.

What he needed was somebody who could not be beaten by lasting, because whoever it was would last better than he did. It had to be somebody who did not beat people so much as stand still while people beat themselves. Against somebody like that, a boy whose whole craft so far was to give ground and watch and survive would find, about the fifth exchange, that giving ground and watching and surviving were exactly what that fighter wanted him to do. Then he would have to do the other thing, or lose again.

It also had to be somebody who would not hurt a fourteen-year-old more than a bout required. That mattered to her more than anything else on the list, and it shortened the list a great deal.

Vell turned it over, and kept coming back to the same name, and in the end she stopped pretending to herself that she was still deciding.

Dessa came in through the gate at the end of the afternoon, as she did most Saturdays, to collect her terms for the Sunday.

She was about twenty, square through the shoulders and solid all the way down, with her hair tied back in the way of somebody who had stopped thinking about her hair years ago. She walked across the yard without looking at the crows or the eaves or the circle, straight to the table, and stopped in front of it with her hands at her sides. She had been sitting at the top of the Cinder House's Sunday cards for most of the season. Everybody who had come at her had come the same way, eager, and gone down in the same way. Lately people had stopped coming at her at all, and Vell had had to go to Fenrow to find anybody who would.

"Second bout tomorrow," Vell said. "The Fenrow man. Copper Rank 3, Iron Path. He's eager."

"They're all eager." Dessa did not smile, though something near her eyes was amused. "Next Sunday?"

"Next Sunday, if you want it. And the Thursday after that, I want to put you in with the unrated boy."

Dessa's eyebrows moved, very slightly. It was the most expression Vell had seen on her face in a month.

"The one who ran Brenna round for four."

"That one."

"Brenna says he's a rabbit."

"Brenna says that about anybody she can't catch."

"He went seven with Petra." Dessa said it slowly, as a fact she was turning over. "Nobody goes seven with Petra."

"He did."

Dessa was quiet for a moment, with her hands at her sides, looking at the ledger and not at Vell. Then she said, "He'll come and watch me first."

"Very likely. You're on tomorrow and next Sunday. He'll be in the benches for both, if I know him, and he'll write down everything you do."

"Good." Dessa said it without any heat at all. "Let him watch. I'll be fighting the Fenrow man, not him. And on the Thursday I'll be fighting him, not his notebook." She looked up. "How old?"

"Fourteen."

"Then I want to hear him say the terms. To me. Before."

"You always do."

"I always do." Dessa nodded, once, as if a thing had been settled. Then she did something Vell had not expected. She stayed where she was, when she would usually have gone, and asked a question. "Has anybody ever sat and watched me twice before they fought me?"

Vell thought about it honestly, because it was an honest question.

"Not since the spring," she said. "And he didn't learn anything."

"No. He didn't." Dessa looked at the circle, the groove in the dirt gone soft at its edges in the cold. "I'd like to see what it looks like when somebody does."

She went. Vell watched her go out through the gate, square and unhurried, and did not look at the crows on the way out either.

---

When the light had begun to go, Vell made the card.

She made it every week, for the board at the crossroads, in pencil on the back of whatever paper there was. This week it was the back of a bill for lamp wicks. The pie boy would take it down in the morning and nail it up over last week's, and by noon the whole district would know what was on at the Cinder House on Sunday and the Thursday after.

*Sunday, Cinder House. Second bout: Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against the Fenrow Iron (Cu 3).* That was how the yard knew him, and she wrote him the way the yard knew him, and the rest of the card went under it.

Then, on a separate line, for the Thursday after next:

*Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against unrated (vouched).*

She looked at that line for a while.

People sometimes asked her why the book did not carry the names of unrated fighters, and she sometimes told them, and they mostly did not understand, because they thought it was a rule about fighters. It was not. It was a rule about the yard.

A name went into the book when the yard came to see it. The plainest way to know whether the yard was coming to see somebody was the card. When people started stopping her in the lane and asking *is he on this week?*, by name, and she found she had to write the name on the card for the board because people would come looking for it there, then the yard had come to see him. After that the name went in the book, every time, because the book had to be able to answer what the yard had asked.

Dessa's name had gone on the card two seasons ago, the week people started asking for her. Renn's last year. Brenna's after her third win. They were all in the book by name now, because the yard had come for them, and it would be a lie to write them down as anything less.

The boy was not there yet. A few people had begun to come for him, Marrow said. Two errand boys with half a mark each, and a fish man who nodded. That was not the yard. When it was the yard, she would know, because she would be making a card one evening and find she could not leave his name off it without a dozen people asking her why.

She wrote *unrated*, and left it.

Then she turned the ledger to the bottom of Petra's entry, where there was a little space left. There, in the smaller hand she kept for the things that were hers and not the circuit's, she wrote the last line.

She had begun writing these lines the year of the Ash Path man, and she wrote all of them the same way, as if to somebody else: to whoever would sit on this stool after her and keep this book. She did not know who that would be. She had never let herself decide. But she had come to believe that if the book was going to outlast her, it would need more than results. It would need somebody, some evening, to be able to turn back and find out not only what had happened in this yard but what the keeper had thought ought to happen next, and why, so that they could decide for themselves whether she had been right.

*Recommend the boy be matched against increasingly credible opposition. Dessa first. Not to break him. To find out what he does when lasting stops working.*

She blotted it, and closed the book, and sat with her hand flat on the cover while the singing inside the public house finished, and the scrubbing, and the light went off the top of the wall.

---

"Dessa," Vell said, the next morning, before the yard had begun to fill. "Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Thursday after next, second bout."

Cael stood in front of the table and felt the name go into him. He had heard it on his first afternoon, behind him at the board, from two men arguing about Brenna. He had seen it on the old result pinned near the bottom of the board, gone pale in the rain. He had never seen her fight.

"She's on tonight," Vell said. "Second bout. And again next Sunday. The public house puts the benches out on a Sunday." She picked up her tea. "Sit in them."

"I will."

"I know you will." She drank. "She'll want to hear you say the terms. Herself, to her face, before. She does that with the young ones. Don't be offended by it."

"I'm not."

"Good." Vell set the cup down. "She's the best defender on my card, and she has been all season, and she wins by waiting. You've spent a month showing me that you can wait." She looked at him. "Now you'll find out what happens when the other fighter can wait longer."

He went out through the gate in the wall and down to the crossroads, because he could not help it, and found the card already nailed up over last week's, in Vell's upright pencil on the back of a bill for lamp wicks.

*Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against unrated (vouched).*

He stood and read it for some time.

Lira found him there, on her way up from the four lanes with the bread, and read it over his shoulder, and gave a long, low whistle.

"Dessa," she said.

"Have you fought her?"

"No." Lira tore off a piece of the bread and gave it to him without looking at him. "Vell's never put me in with her. I asked once, in the summer. She said I wasn't ready to be patient." She looked at the card for a while longer. "I wasn't. I'm still not, very." Then she turned and looked at him, and her face had the morning brightness in it, the one that did not come out later in the day. "Third row. Tonight. I'll get there early and sit on the end, and you'll get there earlier and sit in the middle, and neither of us will say a word to each other until it's over."

"Why not?"

"Because you'll be watching her," said Lira, "and I'll be watching you watch her. And I want to see what it looks like."

# Chapter 24 — Four Pages

On Sundays the public house carried out three rows of plank benches on trestles and set them along the shaded wall of the yard, behind the rope, and charged a small coin for each place on them. The front row filled first, with people who wanted to be splashed. The second row filled with people who had come with friends. The third row, against the wall itself, where you could lean your back on the cold stone and see over everybody's heads, filled last, and mostly with old men.

Cael was in the middle of it before the lanterns were lit, with the Log open on his knee at a clean page, and his coin paid, and the cold of the wall coming through his coat.

Lira came in a little later and sat on the far end of the same bench, five people away, and did not look at him. He did not look at her. It was like a game they had agreed on without either of them being quite sure of the rules.

The first bout went by, two Copper Rank 2s, Blade and Shield, and he watched it with the part of him that always watched and did not write anything down.

Then Vell stood up behind her table. "Second bout," she said, and the yard changed its sound.

He had heard that change before. It was the sound of a yard that has come for one particular thing and has just been told the thing is about to start. It was not loud. It was a kind of settling, people shifting their weight on the benches and leaning, the noise along the rope going down and then coming back lower and closer.

"Dessa. Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Against the Fenrow Iron, Copper Rank 3, Iron Path."

The Fenrow man came over the rope first, in a single long step, rolling his shoulders. He was big and young, with the kind of arms that had been lifting things since before he was old enough to be asked, and he walked to his mark as if he expected it to be further away than it was.

Dessa came over the rope after him and walked to hers.

She did not look at the rope. That was the first thing Cael wrote down, because it was the first thing he saw. Everybody looked at the rope when they came over it. Renn had grinned at it, and Brenna had scowled at it, and Amrit had nodded to it, and even Petra, who looked at nothing but her opponent, had let her eyes go once along the rope as she came in, the way you glance at the sky before you go out. Dessa walked to her mark with her eyes on the dirt in front of her, and then on the Fenrow man's feet, and that was all.

She was not tall, but she was solid all the way down. Her hair was tied back so tightly that her face looked pulled, and she stood with her weight settled low and her hands down, not raised, loose in front of her hips.

Then she set her feet.

He saw it happen. It was what Joren had done at the low wall in Denvash, and what the grey-templed Stone woman had done on his first afternoon here. The loose grit round her boots shivered all at once, as if somebody had struck the ground underneath it with a hammer, and lay down flat. Nothing else about her changed. But she looked, after it, less like a person standing on the dirt than a thing that had been built there.

*Sets her feet before anything,* he wrote. *Grit goes flat. Hands low.*

"Begin when you're ready," said Vell.

The Fenrow man was ready at once. He came off his mark straight and hard and threw a long right at her head, and Dessa's forearms came up and took it.

They did not block it, exactly. They were there when it arrived, the way a gatepost is there when a cart comes round a corner too fast. The punch landed on the outside of her left forearm with a sound like a mallet on a stump, and she did not move. Her feet did not move, and her shoulders did not move. The Fenrow man's arm stopped as if it had hit the end of a chain, and he shook his hand out at his side, and came again.

He came again eight times in the first exchange. He threw everything, high and low and across, and three times he threw a feint first. The first feint moved her. Cael watched for it, and saw it: her weight went across by perhaps the width of a thumb, and came back. The second feint moved her less. The third did not move her at all.

She never struck back once. She stood on her mark and let the Fenrow man hit her forearms until he broke off on his own, breathing hard, and walked back to his mark shaking both hands.

Cael looked down at his page and found he had written, without deciding to, *She doesn't spend anything. He spends everything.*

At her mark, Dessa's lips moved.

He nearly missed it. It was very small. Her mouth moved the way a person's mouth moves when they are counting under their breath, and he watched her chest and saw that it was moving with the count, in and out, slow and even. Four in. Four out. He counted it with her twice to be sure.

*Counts at her mark. Four and four. Breathing.*

In the second exchange the Fenrow man stopped feinting, because the feints were not buying him anything, and began hitting the same place. He hit the outside of her left forearm, over and over, in the same spot, the way a man drives a nail. On the fourth time, Cael saw her forearm give, very slightly, a finger's width, and the Fenrow man saw it too. On the fifth he put his whole weight into it, and his hips, and both feet, and leaned in after it to drive her off her mark.

She went back half a step.

It was the first time she had moved her feet in the whole bout, and the Fenrow man went after the half step at once, the way anybody would. He followed her in with his weight still forward and his hands still busy with the forearm.

Dessa dropped her weight and swept.

It was low and fast, far faster than anything she had done all night, a long flat arc of her right leg along the dirt that took back the half step she had lost and everything standing on it. The Fenrow man's front foot went out from under him. He came down on his side across the groove with a sound that went right along the benches, and before he had finished landing, she was back on her mark with her hands low and her feet set, and the grit round her boots shivered once and lay flat again.

The yard made a noise, but it was not the noise of surprise. It was the pleased, knowing noise of people who had seen this before and had come to see it again.

The Fenrow man got up. He looked at her across the circle, and Cael watched him decide not to be angry, and watched the decision not hold.

The third exchange was short. The Fenrow man came in angry and fast and threw a punch from so far back that Cael saw it start in his shoulder, and Dessa took it on the forearm, and took the next one, and when he leaned in after the third she went back half a step again and he followed again, because he was angry, and she swept again.

This time he did not get up as quickly.

"Called," said Vell. "Stone Path."

---

Cael had four pages by the time the yard had begun to empty, and he had not looked up from them for some time, and when he did, Lira was standing in front of him.

"Well?" she said.

He looked at the page.

"She doesn't beat people," he said. "She stands there while they beat themselves."

"Everybody knows that."

"I know. But I didn't know what it looked like." He turned back a page. "She sets her feet before anything else. She doesn't strike back in the first exchange at all. She lets them spend. Feints stop working on her after the third one, and I think the third one was only that soon because he was being obvious. She counts at her mark, four and four, and I think it's breath. And the only time she moves forward is to take back ground somebody's just taken off her." He stopped. "She never looked at us. Not once. Not coming in, not going out, not at her mark. I don't think she knows we're here."

"She knows," said Lira. "She just doesn't think it's any of her business."

They went out through the gate together into the cold. The lanterns along the lane were few and far apart, and between them the dark was the real dark, and he walked with the Log under his arm and the four pages running in his head.

"You didn't write anything in the first exchange," Lira said, when they had gone two corners.

He looked at her.

"I was watching you watch her. That was the deal." She did not look back at him. "You didn't write a word in the first exchange. You held the pencil over the page the whole time and didn't put it down. In the second you wrote all the way through. In the third you stopped again." She was quiet for a few steps. "And when she swept, the first time, you didn't look at the sweep. You looked at her feet coming back to her mark afterward."

He had not known that. He thought back and found it was true.

"That's not a ruling," Lira said. "I'm only telling you what I saw."

"I know."

"Write it down, then." She stopped at the well with the iron cover, where she always stopped. "Same time tomorrow."

The mender had a box of weights for him on Monday.

They were brass, a whole family of them, from the size of a fist down to the size of a fingernail. Somebody had dropped the box on a stone floor, the mender said, and some of them had chipped, and a chipped weight was a liar. He wanted every one of them checked against a set of standard weights that lived in a velvet-lined case on the top shelf, which Cael was allowed to touch with a cloth and nothing else, and he wanted the liars set aside.

"How do I know which ones are liars?"

"You weigh them," said the mender, who talked in the afternoons, though not much. "Against the true ones. Every one. Slowly."

It was the slowest afternoon's work Cael had ever done. The balance the mender gave him was the fine one, with a glass case to keep the draught off, and it took a long time to settle each time. He had to put the true weight in one pan and the doubtful one in the other and close the glass and wait, and wait, while the beam swung and slowed and swung less, and came at last to rest. Only then could he tell whether the pointer stood on the mark or a hair to one side of it.

If he opened the glass before it had finished settling, he had to start again. He did that twice, early on, and then not again.

Somewhere around the twentieth weight he found that he had stopped wanting it to be faster. He was simply waiting, with his hands flat on the bench and his eyes on the pointer, and the waiting had become a kind of watching, and the watching was all he was doing.

He thought about Dessa standing on her mark with the grit lying flat round her boots, letting a man hit her forearm, and counting.

At the end of the afternoon there were nine liars set aside on the cloth, all of them small, and the mender came round the bench and looked at them without the loupe and nodded.

"Pin came by my door," the mender said, putting the velvet case back on the shelf. "Thursday morning."

Cael kept his hands flat on the bench.

"Stood in the street and read my sign." The mender lifted his chin at the doorframe, where the four words were cut into the wood and filled with black paint. *IF IT'S STILL HERE.* "Read it twice. Looked at me. I looked at him. He went on." He sat down at his own end of the bench and took up the clock he was working on and screwed the loupe into his eye. "Didn't ask anything. I'd have told him nothing if he had."

"Why are you telling me?"

The mender did not answer that for a while. He turned a tiny screw a quarter turn, and looked, and turned it back an eighth.

"Because you went past my door the same morning," he said, "the long way round, and then back again the other way. And you're not a boy who goes the long way round for nothing." He did not look up. "Same time Wednesday."

---

There was a door in Torvin's kitchen that Cael had never seen open.

It was at the far end behind the range, a low narrow door painted the same brown as the wall, and he had taken it for a cupboard for most of his first week. Then one evening he had heard it close, very quietly, and boots go along the passage to the yard, and he had understood that somebody lived behind it. He had worked out since that whoever it was came in at dawn when the brothers were going out, and slept through the day, and went out again at dusk when the house sat down to supper. He was a shut door and a pair of boots on the passage and a smell of pipe smoke under the door, and nothing else.

On the Wednesday night, the door was open.

Cael came down to the kitchen late, after the others had gone up, to sit at the end of the table with the Log, because the kitchen was warm and the lamp there was better than Yeni's. A man was sitting at the other end of the table with a bowl of the evening's soup and a pipe laid beside it, unlit.

He was heavy and grey and somewhere near fifty, with a broad, flattened face that had been hit a great many times a long time ago and had healed in a slightly different shape. He sat with one leg out straight under the table, the right one, as though the knee did not like to bend. When he lifted his spoon, Cael saw that the knuckles of both his hands were thick and white with old scars.

He looked at Cael in the doorway and went back to his soup.

Cael sat down at the far end and opened the Log.

They sat like that for some time, with the range ticking as it cooled and the lamp hissing and the man eating his soup slowly, the way men eat who have learned to eat when there is food and not before. Cael wrote up the Monday morning's report, and the weights, and the mender's pin, and then turned back to the four pages on Dessa and read them.

"Dessa," the man said.

Cael looked up. The man was not looking at him. He was looking at his soup.

"I can read upside down too," the man said. "Most of us can, who've stood at that table." He ate another spoonful. "You've got her on Thursday week."

"Yes."

"Vell's a hard woman." He said it without any complaint in it, as a man might say that winter was cold. "Good. But hard." He put the spoon down in the empty bowl and picked up the pipe and looked at it, and did not light it. "Doss," he said.

It took Cael a moment to understand that it was a name.

"Cael."

"I know." Doss turned the pipe over in his fingers. "Torvin's wife talks. I'm on the warehouses, nights. Watching other people's grain." He seemed to think about whether to say anything else, and to decide he would, once. "I was in that book. A long time ago. Bronze, in Vell's book, for a season and a half. Then I wasn't." He tapped the knee under the table with the stem of the pipe, twice, lightly, as if knocking on a door to see whether anybody was home. "That was that."

Cael did not know what to say, so he said nothing, which seemed to be the right thing.

Doss got up. It took him two movements, the second of them careful, and when he was up he stood a moment with his weight on the good leg until the other one agreed to have him. Then he took his bowl to the basin by the range, and rinsed it, and set it upside down to dry, and went to the low brown door.

With his hand on it, he stopped.

"Don't hurry her," he said, to the door. "Nobody's ever beaten that one by hurrying. Plenty have tried."

He went in. The door shut very quietly. After a while there was the smell of pipe smoke under it, and then boots going along the passage to the yard and out, and the house was quiet.

Cael sat with the Log open on the table.

Then he wrote, in the margin of the Dessa pages, with an *h* beside it: *Doss (Bronze once, Vell's book): don't hurry her.*

The grey coat did not come back.

He looked for it every morning on his way to the yard, and every afternoon on his way to the mender's, and every time he passed the top of the fish steps. He looked at the brown building with the board on its wall that nobody read and the door that was always shut. He did not see the man again, or any other pin. The market went back to its ordinary rhythm by the Saturday, and the nut boy went back to weighing by faces. The pie man called his prices. Nobody came to Torvin's door that Torvin mentioned, and Torvin would have mentioned it, because he liked telling the table how little he had said.

It should have made it easier. In a way it did. But it also meant there was nothing to watch, and a thing you could not watch was harder to put down than a thing you could.

He found he was glad of Dessa.

She was something to watch. She was a problem with edges, the kind Lira had said they would walk round until they found, and every morning, before the bread, Lira gave him her report, and every evening he turned to the Dessa pages and read them over, and every night they were a little less like notes and a little more like a person.

Lira's reports that week were short.

"You breathed through your mouth on the eight corners yesterday, from the fourth set on. Not today." "You flinched at a crow on Tuesday. I'm putting it in because you'd want it in." "Left catch late twice, first set. Not after." "You looked at my feet before my hands every time this morning. You didn't use to." She said each one as she had said them all, flatly, while they walked the box drill at the slow speed. She never said what any of it meant.

He wrote them all down in the evidence column with her mark beside them, the crow included.

The Dessa pages were a different kind of thing, and he noticed the difference on the Friday night, sitting at the kitchen table with the four pages open, and was surprised by it.

They had started as a defender. *Sets her feet. Grit flat. Forearms like a gatepost. Doesn't spend. Feints stop working after three. Counts four and four. Sweeps only to take back ground.* That was all true, and it was all the kind of thing he had written about Renn's heel and Brenna's dip.

But round the edges of the defender, without his quite deciding to, he had begun writing other things.

*Ties her hair again between bouts, very tight, though it isn't loose.* *Gave the Fenrow man the water cup first at the bucket, before she drank.* *Didn't look at the crowd going out, either, but nodded to the pie boy.* *When she counts at her mark her eyes are on his feet and nothing else. Her eyes don't count.*

None of that would help him on the Thursday. He knew it would not. He could not think of any way that the hair, or the water cup, or the pie boy would help him find a single opening in a frame like a gatepost. But he had not been able to leave them out, and when he read the four pages through all at once, they did not read like Renn's entry, or Brenna's. They read like somebody.

He turned that over for a while.

He had watched Renn as a machine to be taken apart, and Brenna as a temper, and Petra as a speed. That had been right, as far as it went. But it occurred to him, sitting there with the lamp hissing, that Dessa was a person who had decided a long time ago how she was going to stand in the world, and then stood that way. She had not chosen it to win bouts. She had chosen it first, and the bouts came after. He thought that if he understood why she had chosen it, he would understand her guard better than any number of feints could teach him.

He did not know why she had chosen it. He did not think he would find out from the benches.

But he thought it was the right question, and he wrote it at the bottom of the fourth page, small, under all the rest, so that he would not lose it.

*What is she waiting for?*

On the second Sunday she fought a Copper fourth with a Blade Path, a lean, patient man from the other side of the district who had watched her beat the Fenrow Iron from the front row a week ago and had plainly learned from it.

He did not come at her. That was the first thing. He stood on his mark in the first exchange and let his blade come up, a pale steady edge along his right forearm, and then he simply waited, as she did.

The yard laughed at that, a little, and then stopped laughing, because nothing happened for a long time.

Cael, in the middle of the third row with his back against the cold wall, did not laugh. He leaned forward with the Log on his knee and watched two people who had both decided that the other one would have to move first. It was the strangest exchange he had ever seen. It lasted nearly two minutes and neither of them touched the other. At the end of it Vell called the break without either of them having struck, which he had not known she could do.

In the second exchange the Blade man moved first, because somebody had to, and Dessa let him spend, and he spent less than the Fenrow man had, and more carefully. He went for the same place on the forearm three times and then somewhere else and then back again. He was very good. He was the best fighter Cael had seen on the Cinder House dirt since Baro. And he could not make her move.

But it was costing her. Cael saw that in the third exchange, and he made himself go slowly and be sure of it.

Her count at her mark had got longer. Four and four had been her count against the Fenrow man the whole night through. Now, after the second exchange, it was six and six, and after the third it was seven and seven, and in the fourth, when the Blade man caught her forearm twice in the same spot and then a third time, Cael saw her shoulder come down on the left side. Her whole left side came down and in, very slightly, as if she were carrying something heavy on the right and leaning away from it.

*Tired,* he wrote. *Favours the left when tired. Fourth exchange.*

The fifth exchange the Blade man pushed her off her mark, a half step, and she swept and took it back, and he was ready for the sweep and jumped it, and the yard shouted. In the sixth he did it again, and she swept again, and this time he did not quite jump it, and went down on one knee, and Vell called it.

The yard was on its feet, nearly all of it. It had been a long bout, the longest Cael had seen her fight, and it had been close, and they had liked it.

He was not on his feet. He was looking at Dessa.

She had turned away from the circle when Vell called it, and gone back to her mark, because that was where she went. She went there after every exchange and every bout, whether she had won or not, as if it were the only place in the yard that was hers. Her weight was down on the left side still, and the Blade man was getting up, and the yard was shouting. Then, as she came to the mark and turned to face the empty circle, her weight came off her back foot.

It came off early.

It was so small that he did not trust it the first time. He shut his eyes and opened them and went back over it, the way you go back over a figure you have just added, and it was still there. When she came back to her mark and set herself, her feet went down and the grit shivered, and her frame came up round her, forearms and shoulders and hips. The back foot was supposed to be the last thing down, the thing that locked the whole frame into the dirt. Tonight, at the end of six exchanges, it came off the ground and down again a half beat before the frame had finished forming above it.

For a half beat, the frame was up and the foot under it was not.

He had seen it before. He knew that now, with a cold, clear certainty that was almost like being hit. He had seen it at the end of the bout against the Fenrow Iron, a week ago, as she walked back to her mark after the second sweep. He had seen it and not trusted it and not written it down, because once was not a pattern and he had not been sure he had not imagined it.

He had not imagined it. It was there at the end of both bouts, and only at the end. It came only once, after she was tired, when her count had got long and her left side had come down. It was the frame re-forming late, because she had re-formed it so many times that night that the last re-forming came a half beat after the first part of her had already decided it was finished.

He opened the Log to the fourth page and wrote one line, and underlined it, and sat looking at it while the yard emptied round him.

*When she's tired, her back foot comes down late. The frame forms before the foot. For half a beat, she isn't set.*

Under that, smaller:

*Seen twice. Once a bout. Only at the end. Never seen it twice in one bout.*

Lira was standing in front of him when he looked up. He did not know how long she had been standing there.

"You found something," she said.

"I found something."

"In the last exchange. After it. When the yard stood up." She was not asking. "Everybody stood up and looked at the Blade man getting off the dirt. You sat down harder and looked at her feet."

He showed her the line.

She read it twice, in the lantern light, and then looked at him, and he saw her not ask a single one of the questions that were in her face, because they were his to answer and Thursday's to settle.

"Four days," she said.

"Four days."

"Then come on." She held out a hand to pull him off the bench. "It's freezing, and you've got four pages to turn into a fight, and I want my bread."

# Chapter 25 — Spending and Banking

"Quiz," said Lira, on the morning of the fiftieth day, in the grey half, with the frost still white along the bottom of the east wall.

They had stopped the box drill early. She had not given him a report that morning. She had said, coming in through the gate, that there was nothing in him today worth reporting except nerves, and she did not count nerves, because everybody had them and they told you nothing.

"Her tell before the frame's set," she said. "When she's tired."

"Back foot comes down late. Half a beat. The frame's up and the foot isn't under it."

"When?"

"At the end. Once a bout. After she's re-formed it so many times that the last one comes late."

"How many times have you seen it?"

"Twice."

"Once each bout."

"Once each bout."

"Never twice in the same one."

"No."

Lira walked round him once, slowly, in the grey half, the way she walked round the post before she struck it.

"And if she goes low instead? If she sweeps before you've done anything?"

"She doesn't. She only sweeps to take back ground somebody's taken off her. She did it four times in two bouts and every time she'd just been moved."

"Two bouts," said Lira.

He waited.

"Two bouts isn't a pattern. It's two bouts." She did not say it unkindly. She said it in the voice she used for his feet. "You're good at this, Cael. You're better at it than anybody I've ever watched. You're also fourteen, and you've seen this woman fight twice, from a bench, against two men who weren't you." She stopped in front of him. "Leave room for a version of her you haven't seen."

He turned that over all through the strikes at the post.

Lira had had him at the post every morning for the two weeks, after the box drill and before the bread. She had given him one strike, only one, short and straight from the hip, with the back foot driving and the shoulder following and the fist arriving last, a hundred times a morning. It was the first thing she had ever taught him that was for hitting somebody rather than for not being hit. She had said, on the first morning of it, that if he was going to have one strike he had better have it properly, and that one strike he owned was worth ten he had seen.

He had it properly now. He could feel it in the morning when he woke, in his back foot and his hip, the way he could feel the box drill.

"What is she waiting for?" he said, between strikes.

Lira looked at him.

"I wrote it at the bottom of the fourth page. She waits better than anybody I've seen. I don't think she does it to win. I think she decided to be like that first and the winning came after." He struck the post. "If I knew what she was waiting for, I'd know more about her guard than any number of feints will tell me."

Lira did not answer for a while. She took the bread off the top of the post and tore it, and gave him the larger half.

"Everybody's waiting for something," she said at last. "Most people don't know what. I'm not sure she does." She ate. "But that's a better question than anything else in your four pages. Keep hold of it."

---

The yard was full by the time the lanterns were lit, and the benches were out though it was a Thursday, because the public house knew a full yard when it saw one coming.

He came early, the way he came everywhere, and stood by the water bucket in the corner by the drain and made himself look at things.

The rope was three deep on the shaded side and two deep in the sun, though there was no sun, only lanterns and the cold coming down out of a clear sky. The benches were full to the third row. There were faces he knew in all of them now, and that was a new thing, and he looked at it as carefully as he had looked at anything.

The fish man with the beard was in the second row with his arms folded. Torvin's wife was by the gate, and beside her the woman who slept in her boots, eating a sweet bun. The two errand boys were at opposite ends of the rope, as far from each other as the yard allowed, and both of them were watching him and not each other, which he thought was probably the first time in their lives they had both looked at the same thing. Further along the rope stood the girls from the wall in the lane of yards. Brenna was near the front with her two friends, her face already red, though she had done nothing yet but stand there.

Doss was by the gate in the wall, leaning on it, with his bad leg out straight and his pipe unlit in his hand. He must have come before his night at the warehouses. He saw Cael looking, and did not nod, and did not look away either.

Marrow came along the rope with his slate.

Cael read it without moving his head. *Dessa*, and a short price. *Unrated*, and a long one. But the long one was not as long as it would have been a month ago, and he could see why. Somebody had been paying on him, a little, all week, and Marrow had moved the number to meet them. Under the two names, on its own line, *Over/under: 5 exch.*

Marrow came level with the water bucket and looked at him for a moment, pleasantly, the way a shopkeeper looks at a scale that has begun to weigh against him. Then he wrote something on the slate with his chalk and went on.

"Second bout," said Vell.

The yard did not go quiet. It went lower. He had heard it go lower for Dessa on both Sundays, and now it was going lower for her again, and he found it strange to stand inside the sound and know that some small part of it was for him.

Dessa came across the yard toward him before he had reached the rope.

Her eyes never once went to the rope or the benches. She came straight to where he stood by the bucket and stopped in front of him, square and solid, with her hair tied back so tightly that it pulled at the corners of her eyes. Close to, she was older than he had thought from the benches, nearer twenty than not, with a small white scar through one eyebrow and a face that did not seem to do very much.

"Vell will have told you," she said. "I want to hear the terms from you."

"She told me."

"Then say them."

So he said them, to her face, the way he had said them to Vell on the first morning, in his own words and his own order, slowly, checking each one before he set it down. Unrated. He fought who Vell put across from him. It ended when Vell called it, and not before, and not for any other reason. Nobody stopped it but Vell. What happened in it was his to carry, whichever way it went. Nobody was making him.

Dessa listened to all of it without moving. When he had finished she looked at him for a breath longer, the way he had seen her look at the Fenrow man's feet.

"You were in the third row," she said. "Both Sundays."

"Yes."

"With a book." It was not a question. "Good." She turned and went to her side of the circle.

He stepped over the rope.

"Dessa," said Vell, from her table. "Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause, which was the same small pause it always was. "Begin when you're ready."

Dessa set her feet. The grit round her boots shivered and lay flat. Her hands came up a little, loose, in front of her hips, and her eyes went to his feet and stayed there.

He did not go in fast. Speed against her was a gift, and she took gifts. He had watched the Fenrow man give her everything he had in the first exchange and walk back to his mark shaking his hands.

He went in at three-quarter pace and began asking questions.

A touch at the high line, toward her face, with no weight in it. Her left forearm was there before it arrived, and he let it arrive anyway, lightly, and felt what the Fenrow man must have felt: not a block but a post, a thing set into the ground that his hand stopped against. A step across to her left. Her frame turned with him, all of it together, without her feet moving at all. A feint low, at the body, which he had no intention of finishing.

Her weight went across by the width of a thumb, and came back.

He showed her a second feint, high, the same way. Her weight went across by less. He showed her a third. It did not move her at all.

*Three,* he thought. *Same as the Fenrow man. She learns your lies before she learns anything else about you.*

He touched her guard twice more, once on each forearm, and it was like knocking on a door that nobody intended to open. His knuckles stung. She did not strike back. She did not chase. She stood on her mark with the grit lying flat round her boots and let him spend, and he spent, and when he broke off and circled back to his own mark, he had bought nothing except the knowledge that his hands hurt.

Somewhere along the benches, a man laughed.

It was the laugh people gave the first exchange of a mismatch, when the small one danced round the big one and could not get in. Cael did not look at it. He looked at Dessa at her mark.

Her lips moved. Four and four. Her eyes were on his feet.

*Me spending,* he thought. *Her banking. That's the first exchange. That's what everybody's first exchange looks like.*

The second exchange he went in wrong on purpose.

He had planned it on the benches, and on the wall by the pump, and at the mender's with his hands flat on the bench waiting for a beam to settle. He did not try to get through her guard. Nothing in four pages said the guard could be broken by anybody, let alone by a boy who weighed less than she did. He tried instead to make her keep building it.

Every time she turned her frame to meet him, she had to build it again where she had turned it. Her feet stayed set, but the rest of her had to come round, forearms and shoulders and hips, and settle, and lock. He had watched her do it a hundred times from the third row. It was very fast, and very good. It was also not free. He had seen her count go from four to seven over the course of a long bout, and that was what it cost.

So he made her build it on his clock instead of hers.

He came in off the half beat, a stutter-step and then nothing, stillness held a moment too long. Then two touches where she expected one, then none where she expected two. He went across to her left and back to her right before she had finished turning left. He gave her nothing she could rest against, no rhythm, and every time he changed his mind she had to change her frame to meet it.

She did. She turned and built and locked each time, without hurry, without wasted movement, and she never once came after him, and never once looked anywhere but his feet. She did not spend.

But he could hear her breathing now.

Once, coming off a stutter-step, he let himself get a thumb's width closer than he had meant to, and felt rather than saw how much of her was waiting for exactly that. It was like leaning over the edge of a well and feeling the cold come up out of it. He was not eager. He went back out at once, the way he had come in.

He broke off. His own lungs were working harder than he liked. Her frame stood exactly where it had stood at the start, and to anybody on the benches the exchange had been nothing at all, a boy hopping round a wall for the best part of a minute.

"Hit her, then!" somebody shouted, and laughed, and somebody else told him to be quiet.

Cael did not look at either of them. He watched Dessa go back to her mark.

He watched her go the way he had watched the Fenrow man's feet and the Blade man's blade, because he had nothing else to do with the next breath but watch. And as she came back to the mark and set herself, and the grit shivered, the back foot, the last thing that should lock her into the dirt, came down a half beat behind the rest of her.

He saw it.

It went through him like cold water. It was not the end of the bout. It was the second exchange. She was not tired the way she had been tired at the end of six exchanges against the Blade man, with her left side down and her count long. But he had made her build her frame perhaps thirty times in the last minute, on a clock she had not chosen, and the thirty-first had come a half beat late.

He had not expected to see it at all tonight until the end. He had never seen it twice in a bout. He did not know whether he would see it again.

At her mark, Dessa's lips moved. Six and six.

He stood on his own mark with his hands loose and his face as still as he could make it, and he did not move.

Everything in him wanted to go now. The window was there. He had seen it with his own eyes not three breaths ago. If he went now, fast, straight in while she was still counting, he might be inside the frame before the back foot came down. Every muscle in his legs said *now*.

And that was exactly what four pages said she was waiting for.

He had written it on the first Sunday, in the third row, with the cold of the wall coming through his coat. *She doesn't beat people. She stands there while they beat themselves.* Every eager fighter in his notes had seen something, and gone for it, and found her waiting at the end of it with her weight already low and the sweep already coming. Impatience was what she lived on. He had not sat through two Sundays on a plank bench to hand her his in the last minute because his legs wanted it.

The tell did not mean *go now*. The tell meant the window would be there the next time she had to build her frame on his clock. It meant he could make it come.

So he banked it.

He breathed. He let his shoulders come down. He looked at her feet as if he had seen nothing at all. And when Vell said, "Begin," he went in exactly as he had gone in the first time, as if he had learned nothing and planned nothing and had nothing left but questions.

He made the third exchange look like the first.

The same three-quarter pace. The same polite touch at the high line, which her forearm met. The same step across to her left, which her frame turned to follow.

And then, before it had finished turning, the step back to her right, the one he had used in the second exchange. Her frame came round to meet it, as it had come round thirty times already, and began to build itself again where it had turned. Her back foot came down to lock it.

He was not waiting for it. He was already going.

He had timed it from the third row, the gap between the frame and the foot. He had clapped its rhythm on his knee on a plank bench until the old man beside him had asked him to stop. He went into it the way you go through a door you have walked through in the dark a hundred times, without looking for the handle.

For a half beat the frame was up and the foot was not under it. It was the only half beat in three bouts of watching when she was standing on the dirt instead of built into it.

He hit her.

It was the strike from the post, the only one he owned: short and straight, the back foot driving, the hip turning, the fist arriving last. It went in under her left forearm, which had come round a hair too high, into the soft seam below her collarbone where the frame had not yet arrived to cover it. He felt it go through her and not into her. It was the difference between hitting a door and hitting the person leaning on it.

She went back half a step.

She went back off her mark, the mark nobody moved her from. It was the first ground she had given in the bout, and he had seen what came after the first ground she gave, four times, in two bouts, from the third row.

He was already going left.

He went off her line before her weight had finished dropping, while the yard was still drawing its breath at the half step. Every eager fighter in his notes had followed that half step in. He went the other way, outside, two long steps along the curve of the groove. And the sweep came exactly as it had come four times on the Sundays: low and flat and fast, far faster than anything else about her, a long reclaiming arc along the dirt that took back the half step and everything standing on it.

Nothing was standing on it. It went through the place where a fighter following her in would have been, and he was two strides wide of it, off her line, with nothing touching him, and already turning back to face her.

Dessa came up out of the sweep and onto her mark and set her feet. The grit shivered and lay down.

Then she looked at his face.

It was the first time she had looked anywhere but his feet all night. It was very quick. Her eyes came up from his boots to his eyes and stayed there for the length of a breath, and he saw something go across behind them. It was not anger and it was not surprise. It was the look of somebody adding a new figure to a sum she had thought she had finished. Then her eyes went back down to his feet, and the frame was up, and the exchange was over.

The yard was making a new sound.

It was not loud. It was a low, turned-head murmur that ran along the rope and up into the benches and back. Eighty people were revising something all at once, and some of them had money on it. On the second bench somebody was asking somebody else what had just happened, and somebody else was explaining it wrong. Near the front, Brenna said something to one of her friends, sharp and short, and did not laugh.

At her mark Dessa counted. He watched her lips. Eight and eight. It was the longest count he had seen from her on any night.

She did not look at the yard.

His notebook was empty for the fourth exchange.

He knew it, standing on his mark with his knuckles throbbing and his breath coming hard. Two Sundays on a bench had bought him one tell and one way to make it come, and both of them were used up. She would close that window now. Nobody as good as she was left the same window open twice. She had looked at his face, and she would not let a frame come late on his clock again tonight, not if he made her build it a hundred times.

What he had left was patience. And patience against Dessa was a plan that was only better than no plan in the way that a cup is better than nothing when the river is rising.

He went in anyway, at the same pace as before, expecting to spend a long time earning very little.

She advanced.

Every step forward he had ever seen her take, on both Sundays and in the first three exchanges tonight, had been ground taken back from somebody who had taken it. This one was not. She took it, and then another, and then a third. She came frame first, unhurried, with the grit shivering flat under each foot as it came down, and he understood the economy of it even as she came. Patience had never been the point with her. Spending was the point. And the cheap thing now, the thing that cost her least, was to finish a tired boy before his notebook grew another page.

It was quiet and complete. It was a wall deciding to arrive.

He gave ground.

He gave it along the long side of the circle. That was the one thing he had kept back, the one line in four pages he had not needed yet. He had noticed it on his first morning in the yard with Lira, and written it down on the first Sunday from the third row. The groove was not quite round. It was longer one way than the other, as if all the fighters who had walked it into the dirt had preferred the same side. Going back along the long side, he would have two more steps before the groove arrived at his heel.

He took the first of them. And the second.

Dessa did not follow him round the long side. She cut across it.

She did not hurry. She simply walked the short way, straight across the middle of the circle toward the place where he would be when he ran out of long side, and the two steps he had banked were gone before he had finished taking them. The groove was at his heel. He could feel its edge through the sole of his boot.

The yard had gone silent. It was the silence of eighty people all doing the same sum at once and not liking where it came out. He could hear the lantern nearest the gate creaking on its hook.

He was measuring her advance for a rhythm. He had refused her rhythm all night, and he looked now for one he could refuse, some step he could make come early or late. He found none. There was no rhythm left in her to refuse. She was only coming, frame first, with her eyes on his feet and her lips not moving at all.

Her weight rolled forward onto her lead foot.

What happened next, he did not decide.

His body was already going. It was not going back, though back was where every line of the exchange said back was; back was the groove, and the rope, and the dirt. It was not going sideways either, along the curve, where he had gone in the third exchange and where she would be waiting for him now. It went across.

His lead foot planted, and the whole of him turned on it, low, into the closing space beside her advancing shoulder. It was the space her frame was coming forward to fill, the space that was not there yet. Then it was there, exactly, for exactly as long as he needed it, and he was through it before it closed.

There was no thought in it. There was hardly any him in it.

It came the way a word comes when somebody says the first half of a sentence and you know the rest before they finish, in a voice you cannot place. And it had come once before, on a grey morning in this same yard, with Lira's backhand going over the back of his head and nobody watching but the crows.

He came out of it inside her frame.

He was past her forearms, at her right side, at an angle her guard had never needed to cover, because nobody had ever arrived there. Her weight was on her lead foot, still going forward into the place he had been. His was under him.

The strike was his. That part he could account for. He had made it a hundred times a morning for two weeks at a canvas post in the grey half, and his hip knew it and his back foot knew it, and it went where he sent it: short, rising, up into the hinge of her jaw under the ear, where nothing was guarding anything because nothing had been expected there.

Dessa went down.

She did not fall the way the Fenrow man had fallen, all at once. She went down in stages, a knee and then a hand, and sat back onto the dirt inside the groove. She got an arm under herself and told her legs to rise. Her legs did not answer. She tried once more, with great care, the way a carpenter tries a joint he has just glued, and they did not answer again.

She sat on the dirt and looked at nothing for a moment. Then she lifted her right hand off her knee and held it up, open, the palm toward Vell's table.

"Called," said Vell. Her voice was exactly as level as it always was. "Conceded. Unrated."

Four times he had stood inside that noise while the yard made it for somebody else.

It came over the benches in bits: somebody shouting, somebody groaning, coins clicking from hand to hand along the rope, somebody laughing at somebody else's face. Somewhere near the gate a voice he knew went up high and cracked, and he thought it was one of the errand boys, and did not look to see which. Under all of it there was a smaller and stranger sound. It was his own name, said by people he did not know, here and there along the rope, as if they had been told it and were trying it out.

He stood in the middle of it with dirt down one side of his neck and his knuckles split.

His hands began to shake. It was the ordinary kind, he thought, spent muscle and finished nerves, the kind he had felt after Petra. It went up his forearms and into his shoulders, and he let it.

Under the shaking he found nothing else.

He reached for it. It was the way you reach for a word you had a moment ago, standing quite still in the middle of the yard with the noise going on round him. He reached for whatever had turned him across the closing space, the way he had reached for the drop twice in the grey half and come back empty. There was nothing to reach for. It had gone back into its room and shut the door. It had not left so much as an ache to show that it had been there.

What it had left was the feeling afterward. He stood with his feet wider than he set them, and his hips round further than he turned them, and his weight on a foot he did not usually trust. It was like standing in somebody else's coat again, with the sleeves ending where he did not expect.

He made himself stand properly. He brought his feet in and his hips round and his weight back where it belonged, deliberately, one piece at a time, the way he would have reset a gauge.

Then he filed it. *Second instance.* He did it in his head, where nobody could see, and he knew while he was doing it what number it really was, and he left that for later.

He crossed the circle to Dessa.

She was on her feet already, working her thumb along the line of her jaw, testing the hinge of it with the detachment of somebody checking another person's joinery. The woman from the water bucket came toward her with a cup and Dessa waved her off.

"Both Sundays," she said. "The third row. With a book." It was not a question. "The window you went into was measured. And you were off my line before the sweep, which means you'd counted that too."

"Yes."

"People will tell you that was luck." She worked her jaw once more, side to side, and seemed to decide that it would keep. "It wasn't luck. That was the cleanest read anybody's made on me since I came to this yard, and you made it from a bench in two weeks." She looked at him, properly, the way she had looked at his face in the third exchange. "What's your Path?"

"Nothing to register."

Her eyebrows moved, the scarred one and the other, a small honest movement.

"I got ready for you," she said slowly. "Unrated boy, four losses, lasts a long time, gives ground. I know how to fight a boy like that, and I'd got it ready, and I brought it." She looked at the groove, where the dirt was scuffed in a long curve across her mark. "But there's no book on you. You haven't any Path habits I could learn, or a school's lines I could cut off. Nothing anybody's written down. All of that tonight came out of four pages nobody else has read." She looked back at him. "The registry may think that's a hole in you. It isn't. Give me a Path I know, any night, over two weeks of you on a bench."

"Rematch?"

He had not known he was going to say it until it was said.

"No." There was no heat in it. "Not yet." She glanced at his hands, which were still shaking, and something nearly warm went across her face and was gone. "Go and fight a different problem than me for a while. I'd like to sit on the benches for once and watch you do it. Then we'll talk."

She turned to go, and then turned back.

"And eat something," she said. "Winning takes more out of you than losing does. Nobody tells you that."

She walked to her side of the yard. On the way, true to four pages, she did not look at the crowd. But she stopped at the water bucket, and took the cup the woman held out, and drank, and gave the cup back, and nodded to the pie boy.

---

Vell had the ledger open when he came to the table, as you did, win or lose.

She was writing. She spoke the record aloud as she wrote it, which was procedure; he had heard her do it after every bout.

"Result stands as called. Unrated, over Dessa, Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Fourth exchange. Conceded from the dirt." The pen moved. "Witnessed and entered."

Then she kept writing.

It was a second line, under the formal one. She did not read it out. He had learned to read upside down at nine, across the bench from Hesk, and he read it now, though his hands were shaking and the lantern light was poor.

At the start of the formal line, where a name would have gone, there was still no name. *Unrated (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.)*. It was what he was, as it had been every time.

Under it, in the same small upright hand:

*Third exchange built on the late re-forming of her frame. Timed it from the benches, two bouts, and made it come early by working her frame on his clock in the second. Studies before he fights, beyond anything his weeks in this yard account for.*

Vell blotted it, and closed the ledger over her thumb, and looked up at him with no expression at all.

"It's a compliment," she said. "Don't spend it."

# Chapter 26 — The Honest Sort

The yard did not let go of him for some time.

People he had never spoken to came up to him at the rope, and said things, and went away again before he had worked out what they had said. A man he did not know shook his hand, hard, and told him he had had him at the over and had not expected to be paid on the other thing as well. A woman told him he was too thin. One of the girls from the wall in the lane of yards came past and said, "Brenna's furious," with enormous satisfaction, and was gone.

Brenna herself did not come near him. But when he looked across the yard she was standing by the gate with her friends, red-faced, and she was looking at him. She gave him one short nod, the kind you give across a street to somebody you have decided you will have to take seriously, and turned away.

He found the errand boys at the gate.

They were not running. He had never seen either of them standing still before, and it changed them. They looked younger than they did at a sprint, and smaller, and they were standing closer to each other than he had ever seen them stand. The one with the brother in the carters' was counting coins out of a little cloth purse into the other's palm, one at a time, with a face like a slammed door.

The boy in the red cap had his hand held out flat and his eyes on the coins, and he was not saying anything at all, which Cael suspected was costing him more than anything he had ever done.

The last coin went into the palm. The boy in the red cap closed his fist on it. Then he looked up and saw Cael, and his whole face came open like a shutter.

"*Two,*" he said. "I said two. Two more, I said, the night of the cook. And the girl with the freckles was one, and this was two." He held up the fist. "Two."

"I said never," said the other boy, "and I'd say it again."

"You'd lose again."

"I'd *say* it again."

The boy in the red cap was not listening. He was digging in his pocket with his free hand. He came out with something wrapped in a twist of greased paper, warm, and pushed it at Cael so hard that Cael had to take it or let it fall.

"Pie," he said. "I bought it in the third exchange, when she went back off her mark. I knew." He looked at the paper in Cael's hands, and then at Cael, with great seriousness. "That's your share."

He turned and ran. The other boy stood for a moment, looking at the empty purse, and then he ran too, after him, and they went off down the lane in the dark shouting at each other in exactly the old way, and the sound of it went round the corner and kept going.

Cael stood by the gate holding a warm pie.

"Eat it," said Lira, at his elbow. He had not heard her come. "Dessa told you to eat something. I heard her."

He ate it. It was the pie woman's, two stalls along from the honest-weight man, and it was mostly gravy. He thought it was the best thing he had ever eaten.

Along the rope Marrow was wiping his slate clean with the side of his hand, slowly, the whole of it. He looked up as he finished and saw Cael by the gate, and smiled the good smile, the one he knew the price of, and did not say anything. He did not need to. He had been paying out all along the rope for the last quarter of an hour.

"Don't," said Lira.

"I'm eating a pie."

"Keep eating it."

She walked him back to Torvin's and did not say anything for the whole of the way.

It was not the silence of the walk home after Renn, when she had been saving something up. He could feel the difference. She was not saving anything. She was simply walking beside him at his pace through the cold, with her hands in her pockets, while the questions stacked up beside her like crates on a quay. He could feel every one of them. She did not ask one. She had learned that after a bout he was a long way down, like a swimmer who has gone deep, and that anything she said before he came back up would go straight past him.

At the door under the leaning O, she stopped.

"Write it up," she said. "I'll be around."

She did not say where. He went in.

Yeni was on her cot with the lamp turned up and a shirt across her knee, and she looked up from the seam as he came in, which she did not usually do.

"Well?"

"Won."

The needle stopped. It stayed stopped for long enough that he noticed it. Then it went on, in and out.

"Took you long enough," Yeni said to the shirt. "Torvin's wife's won her bun back off the one in the boots. She had you at five. The boots had you at never." The needle went in. "She'll be impossible."

He sat on the end of his cot by the window with the Log, and found that his hands had stopped shaking somewhere on the walk home, and that he had not noticed them stop.

He did the front first. That was the rule.

*6. Dessa. Copper R5, Stone Path. Won. Conceded, fourth exchange.*

He wrote it exchange by exchange, the way he wrote all of them, what he had predicted against what had happened, in the three columns.

The first exchange took four lines. *Claim: feints stop working on her after three. Evidence: one moved her a thumb, two less, three nothing (Fenrow, me). Ruling: show her three at most, and only to find out how quickly she's learning tonight.* And: *Claim: hitting her guard buys nothing. Evidence: four touches, knuckles. Ruling: don't hit the guard. Hit the time it takes her to build it.*

The second exchange took six, and most of them were about the cost of a frame that had to be built again every time it turned. He made himself write those carefully, because that idea would go beyond Dessa. Anybody who had to set themselves, Shield or Stone or Iron or anything else that stood still to be strong, would have to set themselves again when they turned, and every setting cost something, and a person who made them turn on his clock instead of theirs was spending their breath instead of his own. That was the kind of thing the Log was for. Ideas that went beyond the one fighter were the whole point of it.

*Claim: the late foot can be made to come early, by making her build her frame often, fast, on my clock. Evidence: second exchange; saw it then, not at the end as on the benches. Ruling: if somebody's strength has to be built, make them build it. Then wait.*

The entry for the third exchange ran longest of all.

He wrote it slowly, every step of it, as he had made it. There was the same pace as the first exchange and the same touch at the high line and the step across to her left. There was the step back to her right before her frame finished turning, the frame building, the back foot coming down late. Then the strike, the post strike, under the left forearm into the seam below the collarbone. Then her half step back off her mark, his two strides left along the groove before her weight dropped, and the sweep going through the empty place.

When he had finished he read it twice.

Then he allowed himself what he had paid for, and took his time over it. Two Sundays on a cold bench had turned into half a beat. Half a beat had turned into a strike, and the strike into the first time in his life he had walked out of a circle the winner. He had read her correctly. He had timed it correctly. He had been correct to go left. Every one of the three had been made out of watching and nothing else, and each of them had held under load, the way a bracket holds when every figure in it has been traced back to where it came from.

He could walk anybody through that page, move by move, and every move had a reason, and every reason was his.

Whatever the registry thought he was, that page was his.

He sat with it for a while longer than he needed to.

Then he came to the fourth exchange, and tried to put it in the columns, and could not.

*Claim: in the fourth, when she advanced, I went across into the closing space and came out inside her frame.*

That was true.

*Evidence: I was there. Her jaw. She saw it. The yard saw it.*

That was true too.

*Ruling:*

There was nothing to put there. He had not gone across. Something had gone across, and taken him with it, and put him down inside her frame with his strike ready. He could not do it again, and he did not know how it had been done, and a ruling that said *go across into the closing space* would be a lie, because he could not go across into the closing space. He did not even know where it had been until he was standing in it.

He drew one line through the three lines, neatly, so that the words could still be read underneath, and beside them in the margin he wrote *back*.

Then he closed the front of the book and turned it over.

*First instance.* And under it the morning in the grey half, the backhand going over, the coat. And the small box round *It wasn't mine*, which he had drawn so that he would know when he came back to it that he had meant it.

He turned to a clean page and wrote, at the top:

*Second instance.*

He wrote it down as plainly as he had written the first. There was no decision in it and no warning. It was not the plan, and it was nowhere near the plan. It came the way you know the end of a sentence somebody else is saying, in a voice you cannot place. He had reached for it, standing in the circle with the yard shouting, and there was nothing to reach for. It had left nothing behind.

It was a good sentence. It was the kind of sentence Hesk would have written about a spring that had failed its test twice, and it was careful and honest and it did not claim anything it could not prove. It had exactly the right amount of doubt in it.

And it was not true.

He sat on the end of his cot with the Log on his knee and the lamp hissing, and looked at the sentence, and knew it the way he had known the bracket would fail when the needle swung.

It was not true because the column was not true. The column started at the drop because he had decided, on the night of the drop, sitting exactly here, that it would start there. He had decided it on purpose, knowing what he had left out, and he had written down that he knew. He had told himself it was better to start a column somewhere, knowing it was short, than not to start one at all. That had been right, that night. It had been the most he could do that night.

But it was not that night now. Tonight he had written *Two instances is a pattern* under it, and the sentence did not know that it was standing on a column with its first two figures missing. Anybody who read it a year from now would think there had been two. He would think there had been two, himself, in a year, because he would have read the sentence so often that it had worn a groove.

That was what sentences did. He had found that out at the pump on the first day of the Log, with a torn page. You put a true thing in at one end and a guess came out at the other, in the same coat.

He put the Log down on the blanket, open.

He had never once taken it out.

He took it out now and unfolded it.

The creases had gone soft. The pencil had smudged a little where the folds crossed. It was the first page of the Log, the one he had torn out along the spine so that the binding would not loosen, and it said, in his own hand from the morning after Renn, with the ribs still hot:

*Renn. Copper Rank 3, Blade. He opens with two probes, high and across, off the front foot. The true strike loads in his back heel about a quarter of a second first. I read the heel in the second exchange and he answered with the sweep. In the third I got out of the whole figure—*

And then nothing, where he had stopped.

He read it three times. Then he took the pencil and finished the sentence, under it, on the torn page, in tonight's hand.

*—and I didn't do it. I didn't read the third cut. My eyes were still on his heel. My feet had already gone. Vell saw it. Lira saw it. Renn said it doesn't miss, and it missed. I said instinct. That stopped about a hand short of the truth, and I knew it while I was saying it.*

He looked at that for a long moment.

He stopped, because the sentence had run off the edge of what he knew.

"Borrowed," said Lira.

He looked at it from a few sides.

"Maybe," he said. "I don't know yet."

That was the thing about her he had still not found a name for. And then, when it mattered, she could sit on a step and let a silence be exactly the size it needed to be, and not one inch bigger. They sat in the noise of the street for a while. A cart went by somewhere with its lamp swinging, and two doors down somebody was having an argument about a window. The district went on about its evening, and did not care what the registry said.

And in that quiet he found the rest of it, ready to be said.

"I wrote tonight that it was the second time," he said. "In the back of the book. And then I sat and looked at what I'd written, and it wasn't true, and I wrote down what was." He turned round on the step to face her. This part needed saying to her face. "I've been counting from the wrong place. On purpose. Tonight I stopped."

Lira looked at him in the lantern light. He saw a question come into her face, and saw her look at it, and saw her put it down again, carefully, beside her, the way she had put down *borrowed*.

"All right," she said.

"I'm not going to guess what it is. Guessing's how you write the wrong thing in the book and then trust it because it's in the book." He made himself go on. "But when I know what it is, really know, you'll be the one I tell first. Before anybody. Before Hesk."

Lira was quiet for a moment.

"I'd have been very offended," she said, "if it had been anybody else."

Then she smiled. It was the real one, not the one she gave strangers or Marrow, and it went all the way up to her eyes and stayed there. She bumped his shoulder with hers, lightly, the left one, the Renn one, which did not hurt any more. "It was a good fight, too. That's apart from all the rest of it. You did good work tonight, Cael. You spent those two weeks well."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I didn't fight Dessa. I sat on the end of a bench and bit my knuckle." She stood up and held out a hand to pull him off the step. He took it, and she pulled him up, carefully, on his good side out of habit, though he did not have a bad side tonight. "Come on. Torvin's wife puts something on with real vegetables in it when somebody in the house has fought. If we're not in before the brothers, there won't be any."

"I've had a pie."

"You've had half a pie. That boy's pies are mostly gravy." She opened the door. "And you look like somebody who's just written something down that he's been carrying for a month and a half. That takes more out of you than the fight. Nobody tells you that either."

He followed her in under the leaning O.

On the stairs, going up to put the Log away before supper, he found that he was smiling, and had been for some time, and had not noticed when he started.

# Chapter 27 — Unrated, Over Dessa

The kitchen at Torvin's was fuller than Cael had ever seen it, and louder, and it smelled of onions.

Torvin's wife had the big iron pot on the range, the one she used for oats in the morning, and it was full to the brim with something brown and thick that had carrots in it, and turnips, and barley, and pieces of something that might once have been a sheep. She was ladling it out into every bowl in the house with the air of a woman settling an account.

"Five," she said, as Cael came in, and put a bowl in front of the place at the end of the bench before he had reached it. "I said five. Sit down."

The woman who slept in her boots was at the other end of the table with her arms folded, and no bun.

"I said never," she said to Cael, as he sat. "I want you to know it was nothing personal. I'd seen you walk. You walk like a heron."

"Herons are very good at standing still," said Lira, sitting down beside him with her own bowl.

"Herons fall over," said the boots woman darkly.

The brothers were in, because the river was still too high for barges, and they had heard about the bout from a man on the fish steps who had heard it from his cousin. The cousin had been at the rope. They wanted it told, and they wanted it told twice, the second time more slowly, so that they could settle an argument between them about which side of Dessa he had gone round. He told it plainly, without making it bigger or smaller, the way you would describe a piece of work you had made and were pleased with, without needing anybody to be more impressed by it than the work deserved. He told them the third exchange all the way through.

When he came to the fourth, he said that she had advanced, and he had gone across instead of back, and found himself inside her guard. That was true. The brothers argued about it for some time afterward, happily, and did not ask how.

The wheezing man, who never came down to supper, came down to supper, and sat by the range with his bowl on his knee, and said nothing, and listened to all of it.

Yeni sat at the corner with a shirt across her knee, sewing between spoonfuls, and did not look up once. But when the boots woman said *heron* a second time, Yeni said, "Herons eat fish whole," without lifting her head. The boots woman did not seem to know what to do with that.

And at the far end of the table, where nobody usually sat, Doss was eating.

He had not been at a supper in all the weeks Cael had been at Torvin's. He sat with his bad leg out straight under the bench and his pipe beside his bowl, unlit, and ate slowly, and listened. Cael understood after a while that he must have come back from the yard to eat before going down to the warehouses. He would have been on his way to his night as soon as the bout was over, and had come here instead, and sat down.

Once, while the brothers were arguing about left and right, Cael looked down the table and found Doss looking at him.

Doss did not smile. He did not say anything. He gave one short nod, a small downward tip of his heavy flattened face, the kind men gave each other across a depot yard in Denvash. Then he went back to his bowl.

Cael sat with it. He found that it weighed more than he would have thought. It was not a congratulation. It was more like a line in a ledger, written by somebody who had once had his own name in a book and then not, who knew exactly what went into the book and what it cost.

Torvin came through from the front room with a second pot, a smaller one, which turned out to be more of the same. He set it on the table by the first, which was empty, and stood a moment with the cloth still in his hand, looking at Cael the way he looked at coins.

"Heard you put Dessa on the dirt," he said.

"Yes."

"Good." Torvin wiped his hands on the cloth. He seemed to think about leaving it there, and then not to. "She's sat on top of that yard all season. Every Sunday, same as a clock. Folk have stopped going in against her at all, my wife says, and a yard where nobody goes in against the top is a yard that starts to go stale." He folded the cloth over his arm. "Does a place good to see the top of it move. Does the district good. Reminds folk nobody stays up there for ever." He looked at the empty pot. "Does my rent good, too, if they come to see you again and drink while they're at it. So."

He went back out to the front room.

The table was quiet for a moment.

"That," said Torvin's wife, into the silence, with great satisfaction, "is the most he's said at one go since my sister's wedding."

Lira leaned over to Cael and spoke under the noise that came back after.

"Renn's going to be unbearable," she said.

"Renn's up the river."

"Renn left word on the fish steps before he went." She was trying not to smile and failing. "With three different people, so it would get round. He said that if you won anything while he was away, everybody was to know it was his teaching. That he'd given you four exchanges to learn in, out of the goodness of his heart, and you'd been living off them ever since." She ate a spoonful. "The fish man told me this morning. He said Renn was very particular that it should be told properly."

Cael laughed.

He did not decide to. It came up out of him before he knew it was there, loud, a real laugh, unguarded, the sound of it going down the long table and making the brothers look round. He had not heard himself make that sound since a kitchen in Denvash. He had not known until he heard it how long it had been.

Lira looked at him sideways, and did not say anything, and went on eating.

He thought, briefly, while the noise of the table went on round him, of Alis on Weaver's Row with her raised hand. And of Joren, walking home along the canal with his heels down on purpose, working at it. He wondered whether either of them would have known the boy at this table, with dirt still on his neck and a split knuckle and a pie in him and a bowl of Torvin's wife's stew. He was not sure that he would have known him himself, a month and a half ago.

He thought they might. He thought Joren, at least, would have known the laugh.

---

He wrote to Hesk that night, at the kitchen table, after the others had gone up.

The house was quiet round him. The range ticked as it cooled. Somewhere behind the low brown door there was no pipe smoke, because Doss had gone down to the warehouses with his leg and his pipe as soon as he had rinsed his bowl.

He worked out the days before he began. It was a habit now. His letter about the pin had gone in the west bag on the thirty-seventh day, and it would have come to Fen Street on about the forty-fourth. Hesk would have read it at the kitchen table that same evening and written back that same night, because that was what Hesk did. His answer would be on a carrier's cart somewhere on the merchant road at this moment, coming east, and it would come to Torvin's on about the fifty-first or fifty-second day. That was tomorrow, or the day after.

It would answer a boy who had seen one man with a pin and did not know what it meant. That boy was a fortnight gone. And this letter, about tonight, would come to Hesk on about the fifty-eighth day, a week after Hesk had already written to the boy with the pin. They were writing past each other, like two people walking toward each other on a long road in the dark, calling out.

He did not mind it as much as he had a fortnight ago. It was the way it was. You said the things that would still be true by the time they got there.

*Hesk,*

*I won tonight.*

*It was the sixth bout. A woman called Dessa, Copper Rank 5 in the book here, Stone Path, the best defender in the yard. She wins by standing still and letting people beat themselves on her. I sat on a bench and watched her fight twice, a week apart, and wrote down everything she did, the way you taught me to watch a stance. At the end of the second bout I saw a thing she does when she's tired. Her back foot comes down a half beat after the rest of her has set. In the second exchange tonight I made her set herself so many times, on my count and not hers, that it came early. In the third I went into it.*

*She said afterward that it was the cleanest read anyone had made on her since she came to the yard. The woman who keeps the book wrote a line under the result that she didn't read out, and I read it upside down, and then she told me it was a compliment and not to spend it. I'm trying not to.*

*I'm all right. Split knuckle on the right hand, nothing else. Torvin's wife cooked something with real vegetables in it.*

He stopped there, and held the pencil over the paper, because the next part was the part Hesk had asked for in his last letter. He had been carrying it for two weeks without finding a way to answer it.

*Tell me what she's like.*

He thought about Lira on the step tonight, setting *borrowed* down where he could reach it and not pushing it any closer. He thought about her keeping them in the grey half every morning without telling him why, for three weeks, until the morning she did. He thought about her hand on his shoulder in the mist, short and plain. He thought about her biting her knuckle at the rope, and saying *Nose* in a perfectly flat voice, and telling him he was not a natural because she would not tell lies to anybody she trained. And about her reading the notice twice, standing in the middle of the box drill, and saying *This is built*.

*You asked what Lira is like,* he wrote. *I've been trying to work it out for two weeks.*

*She's fifteen. She's quicker than me at everything except watching. She tells me exactly what she thinks, all of it, and then lets me pick it up, and it's always heavier than what other people give me, and it's always the right weight. Every morning she tells me what she saw me do the day before, and I write it down, and she never once tells me what it means. She says that's mine. She's the only person I've met here who asked me what the word felt like from inside, instead of telling me what it meant from outside.*

*I think she's the first friend I've had who I didn't start out with. Joren I had before I could walk. I had to find this one.*

*That's as near as I can get. I'll keep trying.*

He read it back. It was not enough, and it was true, and he thought Hesk would understand both.

There was one more thing he did not write.

He did not write about the fourth exchange. He had told the brothers that he went across instead of back, and that was true, and he told Hesk the same thing in fewer words, in the line about the win. But he did not tell him what had gone across, or that it was not him, or that there were four of them now on a page in the back of the Log in his own hand, numbered.

That made four things he had not told Hesk. He knew the number exactly. He had written in the back of the Log, on the night of the drop, that he would have to answer for all of them at once one day.

He had made a promise tonight, on the step, to somebody else, that she would be first.

He sat with that for a long moment, and found that it did not feel like a betrayal of Hesk. It felt like the opposite of what Hesk had done with Tuesdays and the file. Hesk had kept things until he was sure, and then had not told them even then, and Cael had found them out for himself and come to him angry. Cael was keeping this until he was sure, too. But he had told one person that he was keeping it, and why, and that she would be first. And he had written down in the Log that he was keeping it from Hesk, so that it could not hide from him.

It was not the same. He thought it was not the same. He thought he would have to answer for it anyway.

*— Cael,* he wrote at the bottom, and folded it, and put it in the leather book to keep flat for the morning.

---

He went up to the yard in the dark the next morning, the way he always did, by the lane where the ground began to rise. Lira caught him up at the corner by the chalk board, with the bread under her arm and her breath smoking, and they went up together without saying much.

The old man was in his corner, as he was on every morning Cael had ever passed that gate. There was no light in the yard yet but what came off the sky, grey and thin, and the woman with the shoulder had not come. He stood alone in the shade that was not yet shade, in the stance that looked like nothing else in the lane, with his feet set and his hands loose and his breath going up slow and white.

His pale eyes came round as they reached the gate. They went to Cael's right hand, to the split knuckles, and stayed there a moment, and came back to his face.

"Four," the old man said.

Cael stopped. Lira stopped beside him.

The old man's eyes went back to the wall. Cael thought that was all, as it had been all after Amrit, and had half turned to go on up the lane, when the old man spoke again, to the wall.

"You went the long side."

Cael looked at him.

"In the fourth. When she came at you." The old man shifted his weight, the small shift from one foot to the other. "That groove's longer one way than the other. Always has been. You knew it, and you went back along the long way to buy yourself two steps. That was well thought." He was quiet for a moment. "She cut across it. Took both your steps off you before you'd finished taking them."

"Yes."

"She'll know now that you know it's there. So will every fighter who was at that rope with their eyes open, and a few of them had their eyes open." The pale eyes came back to him, not old at all. "A thing like that works once. After that it's a habit, and somebody plans for it. Find another."

"I will."

"I know you will. That's why I'm telling you." The stillness came down over him again like a lid, and that was all.

They went on up the lane. Lira did not say anything until they were past the next two gates, and then she said, in a voice that was trying very hard to sound wounded and not managing it at all, "Five months. I've walked past that gate every morning for five months. I won a bout in the summer by a whole exchange, and he has never once told me about a groove."

"Maybe you didn't go the long side."

"I didn't know there was a long side." She shifted the bread to her other arm, as if it had offended her. "I'm going to go and measure it. With string. This afternoon."

---

Her report, in the grey half, was the longest she had ever given him.

She gave it walking the box drill at the slow speed, as always, with frost on the dirt and the crows along the eaves. She did not look at him once.

"First exchange. You showed her three feints and stopped. You didn't show her a fourth. Most people show her six. You touched her guard four times and shook your right hand after the third, so I knew it hurt." Back left. "Second exchange. You came in off the half beat nine times. I counted. You changed sides eleven. You let yourself get too close once, about halfway, and got out again before I'd finished breathing in. Your feet were quicker than I've ever seen them." Front right. "When she went back to her mark after the second, your eyes went to her back foot. I saw them go. Then you looked at her face, and then at nothing, and you stood still for so long that the man next to me said you'd frozen."

"I hadn't frozen."

"I know you hadn't. I'm telling you what he said." Back right. "Third exchange. You went into the gap before her foot came down. I couldn't tell you how long before; it was quicker than I can see. You hit her under the left forearm, and she went back, and you were going left before her weight dropped. Your feet went before her hips did. I watched them." Front left. She stopped walking.

"Fourth exchange," she said.

He waited.

"She came forward. Three steps. You gave her the long side of the circle, and she cut across it, and the groove was at your heel." Lira was quiet for a moment, looking at the dirt between her own feet. "And then you were going backward, and then you weren't going backward. And then you were inside her guard." She looked up at him at last. "That's all I've got. That's all I saw. I'm not going to tell you any more than I saw."

"I know."

"Write it down, then."

He wrote it down that evening, all of it, in the evidence column, in her words, with her mark beside it. When he came to the fourth exchange he wrote exactly what she had said, *then you were going backward, and then you weren't*, and nothing else, and he did not put anything in the third column beside it.

She tore the bread in half and gave him the larger piece.

"Vell says not this Sunday," she said, through hers. "She told me last night, when you'd gone to write it up. Rest your hands, she says. She'll have somebody the week after." She swallowed. "And Renn's still held, if he's back by the turn of the month."

"Did she say who?"

"She said she hadn't decided, and when she had she'd tell you, and not before, and not because you asked." Lira's mouth did something at the corner. "I think she says it to everybody. I think she's said it so many times she doesn't hear herself any more."

---

The pie boy was nailing the result to the board when they came down to the crossroads.

He had a hammer in one hand and three nails in his mouth, and a sheet of paper flat against the board with the heel of his other hand. It was the back of a bill for something, written in Vell's upright pencil, and he was nailing it up over the corner of last Sunday's card, where it would be the first thing anybody saw. There were already six or seven people standing round the board in the cold, waiting for him to finish so that they could read it. When he had got the third nail in, he stepped back and took the hammer out of the way, and they read it.

*Result, Thursday: unrated (vouched) over Dessa (Cu 5, Stone), fourth exchange, conceded.*

Cael stood at the back of the little crowd and read it over their heads.

There was no name in it. He had known there would not be. The board said what the book said, and the book said *unrated*, and that was what he was.

A man at the front read it aloud to the woman beside him, who could not see over his shoulder. "Unrated over Dessa," he said. "That's the boy. The one from Torvin's. The thin one."

"What's his name?"

"Don't know. Cael, somebody said. Something like that."

Lira, beside him, said nothing. She did not look at him. But he felt her not looking at him, and he knew she had heard it too.

*Something like that.*

He thought about Vell at her table on the Sunday morning, saying *When the yard comes to see you, I'll write it.* He had thought then that it was a strange rule. He thought now that he understood it a little better. Some of the yard had come to see him last night. Some of it had said his name. A man at the board this morning knew it, nearly. But the yard had come last night to see Dessa, mostly, and to find out whether the thin boy would last. That was not the same as coming to see the thin boy. When it was the same, he thought, Vell would know it before he did.

He found that he did not mind waiting for it. That surprised him, and he looked at the surprise, standing at the back of the crowd with his hands in his pockets, and found it was true.

The book knew what he had done. Every line of it was something Vell had seen from her stool, in her own hand, and every word was true. It did not have a word in square brackets in it anywhere. It had *unrated*, and *vouched*, and *over Dessa*, and a line underneath that he was not supposed to have read.

That was enough to be going on with.

"That's you," said Lira, at last, very quietly.

"That's me."

She bumped his shoulder with hers and turned away toward the four lanes, and he went the other way, down toward the river.

---

He went by the lane of the river workshops, because it was the way to the carriers' hut, and because he wanted to.

The mender was in his doorway with the loupe in his eye and a clock on the bench. He did not talk in the mornings, and it was still the morning, though not by much. But as Cael came level with the door he took the loupe out and looked up, at Cael's face, and then at the split knuckles of his right hand. They were scabbed now and stiff, and he had been holding the hand a little away from his coat without noticing.

"Something broken?" said the mender.

Cael stopped in the street.

"No," he said. "Something worked."

The mender looked at him for a moment longer, the checking look, the one he gave a frame before he leaned on the lever. Then he grunted. It might have been approval. It might only have been a man clearing his throat on a cold morning. He put the loupe back in his eye.

"Wednesday," he said, to the clock.

The carriers' hut was busy. The west bag was hanging on its hook on the back wall, half full, with WEST painted on it in tall black letters, and the clerk with the inky fingers was behind the counter weighing a parcel on the little brass balance. Cael waited his turn. He thought about the old woman in the shawl saying *because the clerk is young and thinks it's clever to know things*, and looked at the clerk, who was perhaps nineteen and had ink on his cuff as well as his fingers, and who was not looking at anybody in particular.

When it was his turn he handed the letter across. The clerk read the address aloud in his flat voice, *14 Fen Street, Outer District, Denvash*, and weighed it, and named the price, and dropped it in the bag.

"Torvin's," the clerk said, looking up. "Isn't it."

"Yes."

"Thought so." The clerk seemed pleased with himself. "There's nothing come back for you yet. West cart's only just in. If there's anything, the runner'll have it up to you by evening."

Cael looked past him, out through the open side of the hut onto the landing.

The west cart was standing at the bottom of the fish steps with its tailboard down, a long carrier's wagon with a canvas tilt over its back and two tired horses steaming in the cold. The carter was handing sacks of post down to a boy on the landing, and three passengers had climbed down off the tail and were standing about in the way people stand about when they have been sitting on a board for two days. One was a woman with a child. One was an old man with a crate of something that clinked.

The third was a man of about forty, in a plain dark coat creased from the road, with the collar turned up against the wind off the river. He had a small travelling bag in one hand. Under his other arm he held a flat leather case, the kind clerks carried papers in, and he was holding it in a particular way that Cael noticed without meaning to notice it. He held it loosely, against his side, the way you carry something that has very little in it, as if he had expected it to be heavier and had stopped being surprised that it was not.

The man looked up the fish steps at the district, the long slope of roofs going up toward the yards, and the haze over the market, and the leaning chimneys. He looked at it the way Cael had looked at the city from the rise on his first evening, as something he would have to learn.

Then he turned to the boy on the landing and asked him something. The boy pointed up the steps, to the top, where the squat brown building stood with its board of notices that nobody read.

The man nodded, and picked up his bag, and went up the steps toward it.

Cael watched him go for perhaps two breaths. He watched the way he did with everybody, without deciding to. A stranger off the west cart, tired, with a thin case and his collar up, going to the post. Then the clerk said "Next," behind him, and somebody pushed past him to the counter with a parcel, and he stepped out of the way and turned toward the steps himself.

He did not write the man down.

He would think about that afterward. In a month and a half in Ardenmere he had written down nearly every stranger he had seen: the polite man at Torvin's door, the grey coat in the market, the old woman in her shawl, the Fenrow Iron, the Blade man from across the district. He wrote things down. It was what he did. It was the thing he had done before he had anything else.

But it was the morning after the first win of his life, and the sun had come out over the river, thin and cold and very bright. He had a letter in the west bag and a split knuckle and four pages that had turned into a fight, and four more lines in the back of a book that had turned into the truth. There was a girl somewhere up in the four lanes who would be first. He went up the fish steps two at a time, into the light, past the brown building with its door already shutting behind somebody, and did not look at it.

He was thinking about Sunday week, and who Vell would put across from him, and how he would watch them.

# Chapter 23 — At the Month

"It's the month," Lira said, on the thirty-eighth morning, when the shadow had gone off the post.

He knew it was the month. He had known it lying awake before the brothers dropped their boots, and walking up to the yard in the dark, and all the way through the box drill, and Lira had known that he knew it. That was why she said it, he thought, so that it would be outside his head where they could both look at it.

"Renn said a month," he said. "On the fish steps. Vell said she'd hold it at the month."

"Then let's go and see whether she's holding it."

Vell's table was out in the shade of the back wall, as it was every morning when there was a card coming, with the ledger squared to its edge and the tea at her elbow. Renn was standing in front of it.

He had a sack on his shoulder, a long canvas one with a strap, packed tight and knotted at the neck, and he was wearing a coat Cael had not seen before. It was heavy and oiled and too warm for the yard, the kind of coat men wore on the river in winter. He was talking to Vell with his free hand moving, and Vell was listening to him with the expression of somebody listening to rain.

He heard them on the dirt behind him and turned round, and when he saw Cael he grinned.

"There he is. I was going to leave word." He swung the sack down off his shoulder onto the dirt, and it landed with a thump of something heavy and folded. "You've healed."

"Mostly."

"Mostly's what you get." Renn looked him up and down, the craftsman's look, the one that checked a joint. His eyes went to the left forearm, where the burn showed pink under the pushed-back sleeve, and stayed there a moment. "Ember?"

"Amrit Sole."

"Amrit." Renn whistled softly. "He doesn't burn people. He must have liked you." Then he turned back to Vell, and then to Cael again, and spread his free hand. "Here's the thing. There's a place on the grain barges going upriver. Three weeks of poling and carrying, there and back, and they're paying what they pay when nobody else wants to go in the cold. I'd be a fool to turn it down, and I've got a mother who'd tell me so."

"You're going today."

"Noon. They load at the long quay." Renn rubbed the back of his neck. "So I can't give you the second look at the month. I've asked her to hold it." He nodded at Vell. "Till I'm back. Three weeks, a bit more if the river's bad."

Vell said, without looking up from the ledger, "I'll hold it till the turn of the month after. Not past. If you're not back by then, it lapses, and you can come and ask me again like anybody else."

"I'll be back." Renn turned to Cael. "And I'll tell you the other half, since you'll work it out anyway, and I'd rather you heard it from me." He was not grinning now. "I don't want it at the month. Not now. Not with that one standing between us." He did not say which one. He did not have to. "I want to come back with something you haven't seen. And I want you to have learned something I haven't seen either. Otherwise it's the same bout twice, and I'll win it, and neither of us will know anything we didn't know a month ago."

"You'd win it?"

"I'd win it. You'd last five." Renn picked up his sack again and swung it onto his shoulder. "Five's not enough for what I want to look at."

Cael had been ready, for a month, to stand in front of this table and hear a date. He found that what he felt, now that he would not, was not disappointment. It was something stranger. It was relief, and under the relief a kind of respect that he had not expected to feel for the man who had put him on his back.

Renn had not forgotten the third exchange. He had been carrying it upriver and down for a month, on his shoulder with the sacks, and he wanted to come back to it ready.

"Three weeks," said Cael.

"A bit more if the river's bad." Renn started for the gate. Halfway there he stopped and turned, walking backward, and raised his voice so that it carried to the pie boy, who was raking the far side of the circle, and to the crows on the eaves. "And if anybody asks where I've gone, tell them I've gone up the river to rest. Teaching that one to last four took it out of me."

The pie boy laughed. Lira made a noise in her throat.

"He's been saying that on the fish steps for a fortnight," she said, when Renn had gone through the gate. "That he went easy on you. That he let you have the four because he could see you were keen. That every bout you've lasted since, you learned from him."

"Some of it I did."

"Don't ever tell him that. He'll have it painted on a sign." She looked at Vell. "Who now?"

Vell had picked up her pen and was writing a short line in the ledger. When she had finished she laid the pen across the page and looked up at Cael, the flat look, the thorough one.

"Come back tomorrow morning," she said. "Before the yard fills. I'll have somebody for you."

"Who?"

"Tomorrow." She picked up her tea. "I haven't decided. When I've decided, I'll tell you, and not before, and not because you asked."

---

On the cooper's roof, in the afternoon, with the tar warm under them and the first wall of the Ranked core going up the hill in the thin light, Lira would not guess.

"I could," she said. "I could give you six names. It'd be one of the six, probably, and you'd spend tonight getting ready for all six, and you'd get ready for five of them for nothing." She lay back on the tar with her arm over her eyes. "You've had a week. A pin and a letter and a woman in a shawl. Have an afternoon off."

"I don't know how to have an afternoon off."

"I know. It's one of your worst qualities." She lifted the arm and looked at him from under it. "Try anyway. It's like the box drill. You think about it until one day you don't need to."

He tried. He sat on the low parapet and looked at the three yards below, where people were hitting things in the cold, and at the river beyond the roofs with a line of grain barges moored along the long quay, and men going up and down the planks with sacks on their shoulders. Somewhere down there Renn was loading. He found, after a while, that he had stopped trying to have an afternoon off and was simply having one, and it was so strange that he noticed it and then stopped having it.

Lira, with her arm over her eyes, laughed at him without looking.

"I felt you stop," she said.

---

Vell did not open the ledger again until she had the yard to herself.

That was not hard on a Saturday. There was no card on a Saturday. The public house's back door stood open for the air, and somebody inside was scrubbing a floor and singing about it, badly. The pie boy had raked the circle and gone off to wherever the pie boy went. Only the crows were left, along the eaves, watching the dirt for things that were not there.

She sat on her stool in the shade with a fresh cup of tea and turned back through the book.

She did not often read back. The book was for other people to read. It was there so that anybody who came to the table in a year, or ten, could open it and find out what had happened in this yard on a given night and who owed whom what. It was not there for her to sit with. But there were five entries in it now under no name at all, only *unrated*, and they had begun to sit beside each other on the pages in a way she found she wanted to look at all together.

She found the first. *Renn (Cu 3, Blade) over unrated debut (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.), fourth exchange, by the flat. Called.* And under it the eight lines she had not needed to write.

Then Brenna, eight days later. Five exchanges. *Gave ground four exchanges on purpose. Found the dip on the step-through in the second; watched it; did not use it until the fifth. Went in on it. Too light to get through before she turned. Called.* And a line about the frustration, because she had watched the boy watch Brenna's temper the way a man watches a kettle, and had seen him learn something from it that most people never learned from either side.

Then Amrit. Six exchanges. *Read the shimmer from the first. Baited by a held shimmer in the fourth, paid with the right arm. Read the breath in the fifth, two of three. Burned on the third. Conceded at the right breath.* She had written *the right breath* in the book because it was the truth, and had said it to his face because it was rarer to hear than people thought.

Then Petra. Seven. *Saw the lean from the second exchange. I am nearly sure of it. Moved on it every time and was late every time. Moved earlier in the third and she missed. She moved her target in the fourth. He did not tell her what he was looking at.* That last line she had got from Petra herself, who had come to the table afterward and said so, with a puzzled face.

Four, five, six, seven, and the Renn night at the front of them.

She looked at them in a row and saw what anybody would have seen, which was a boy who lost and lasted, and lasted longer each time. That was not rare. She had seen it before. A stubborn one with a good eye could do it for a season or two, losing better and better, and the yard grew fond of them and bet on the over, and then one day they came to the end of how long it was possible to last, and either they found something else or they stopped coming.

But that was not what was in the entries, if you read them properly.

What was in the entries was that he saw. Every time, against every one of them, he had found the thing the fighter across from him did not know they did. He had found the heel, the dip, the shimmer and the breath, and Petra's lean, which Vell herself had not seen in a year and a half of watching Petra. Then he had found the thing in himself that the other fighter used against him. He had written down Brenna's temper, and Amrit's bait, and Petra's learning him back. None of that was strange. All of it was ordinary work. It was the ordinary work of a good watcher, done better than she had seen it done by anybody in his first month, and better than most people did it in their third season.

She did not need a new word for that. She had a great many words for that.

She turned back to the first entry, to the third exchange, and read again what she had written there, in a smaller hand than the rest because she had written it slowly.

*Defender off the line before R.'s strike began. Not a read. I watched for the read and there was none.*

She dipped the pen and wrote one word in the margin beside it.

*Atypical.*

She looked at it after she had written it.

It was a word she kept for herself. She had used it in this book, and in the one before it, and the one before that, eleven times in something over thirty years. She knew it was eleven, because she kept a count on the inside of the back cover, a row of small strokes in the corner, the only tally in the book that was not about money. Now there were twelve.

It did not mean rare. Rare things happened in the yard every week. It did not mean good, or dangerous, or a thing to tell the Compact about, or to keep from them. In her book it meant one thing only, and she had decided what it meant on the night she first wrote it, when she was not much older than the Wind girl was now.

It meant: *I saw this with my own eyes from this stool, and it does not fit anything else I have ever seen, and I do not know what it was. I am writing it down so that nobody, including me, can ever decide afterward that I did not see it.*

That was all. It was a peg in a wall, so that she could find the place again.

Of the eleven before this, she remembered every one. Four had turned out to be nothing much: a fighter on a strange night, a fever coming on, a man who had been drinking and should not have been allowed over the rope. Two had turned out to be the start of somebody who later went across the river and was not seen in Ardenmere again. One had been the Ash Path man, the first time he did not stop. And four she still did not know about, and never would, because the fighters had stopped coming and the book did not follow people out of the gate.

She did not know which kind this was. She did not try to guess. Guessing was not what the word was for.

---

Marrow came across the yard while she was looking at it, with his slate under his arm and his good coat buttoned against the cold.

He did not work on a Saturday, but he came by on most of them, because there was usually a card to be made for the Sunday and he liked to know it before anybody else did. He stopped at the end of her table and smiled.

"The boy," he said pleasantly. "Renn's gone up the river."

"He has."

"So you'll want somebody else for him." He tapped the slate against his palm. "I've a man from Fenrow who'd take him. Iron-rated over there, Copper fourth here, lost his last two. He'd come for a debut's purse. He'd last the boy four or five, and the boy would last him, and I'd have a nice clean over to lay."

"I'm not looking for an over."

"Everybody's looking for an over." Marrow's smile did not change. "The yard's started coming for him, you know. Not many. A few. Two errand boys with half a mark each. I could fill the rope for him if he lasted somebody I could price."

Vell looked at him for the first time.

"I don't match people to suit your slate," she said. "I never have. You know that, and so does everybody who's ever stood at that rope. That's why they stand at it."

"Of course." Marrow inclined his head, as if she had told him the weather. "Of course. I only mention it." He looked at the book, upside down, the way people did, and at the margin where she had just written. She saw him read the word and saw it mean nothing to him. Then he went off across the yard and out through the gate, with the slate under his arm, unhurried, a big man in a good coat who had never once in his life been caught doing anything he could not explain.

She watched him go.

She did not trust Marrow. She had never trusted Marrow, and had decided a long time ago that she did not need to, so long as his book and hers never touched. But Marrow had noticed the boy, and Marrow did not notice things that would not one day be worth money, and she wrote that down too. She did not put it in the book. She put it in her head, where she kept the things that were hers.

There was another thing she kept there, from Thursday night.

Three different people had come to her table before the first bout to tell her that there had been a pin in the food market that morning, walking the rows. Three people was a great many for something that had not touched the yards at all. She had listened to each of them and said *I heard*, and had not asked anything, because there was nothing in it for the book. The book had stayed clear of the Compact for as long as she had kept it, in the plainest way there was. It never wrote down anything the Compact would want to read. It wrote what happened in this yard, between the rope and the wall, and nothing else.

*Nothing to register,* the margin said, beside the first entry.

She had decided on that first morning, with the Wind girl standing in front of her table and her chin up, that she did not need to know what the boy was. She decided it again now, for the same reason, and found it was still the right decision. If she ever needed to know, she would watch until she knew, and then she would write down what she had seen, and not before.

---

So it came back to the same question, which was the only one that was hers to answer. Who next?

She had told him herself, at the table after Petra, that he would come to the end of lasting. *Then you'll have to do something besides last.* She had meant it as a fact, not a warning. Now Renn had gone up the river and left a gap, and she had a choice to make for him, and she found she wanted to make it carefully.

She went down the card in her head.

Baro would not do. Baro would teach him only that every opening was a lie, and she had watched the boy sit on the barrel with his notebook through Baro's afternoon and come away with that already written down. Besides, Baro did not fight anybody who might learn him, and the boy was nothing but somebody who might learn him.

Brenna would want it, and Brenna would be no use. It would be the same bout again with a little less running, and Brenna would be angry at the end of it whichever way it went.

There was a woman at Fenrow with an Ember Path that was a good deal less kind than Amrit's. She would burn him and teach him nothing new, and Vell did not let people be hurt in her yard for nothing.

Petra again would be speed again, and he knew already that he was losing on speed. A second loss to Petra would only tell him the same thing louder.

What he needed was somebody who could not be beaten by lasting, because whoever it was would last better than he did. It had to be somebody who did not beat people so much as stand still while people beat themselves. Against somebody like that, a boy whose whole craft so far was to give ground and watch and survive would find, about the fifth exchange, that giving ground and watching and surviving were exactly what that fighter wanted him to do. Then he would have to do the other thing, or lose again.

It also had to be somebody who would not hurt a fourteen-year-old more than a bout required. That mattered to her more than anything else on the list, and it shortened the list a great deal.

Vell turned it over, and kept coming back to the same name, and in the end she stopped pretending to herself that she was still deciding.

Dessa came in through the gate at the end of the afternoon, as she did most Saturdays, to collect her terms for the Sunday.

She was about twenty, square through the shoulders and solid all the way down, with her hair tied back in the way of somebody who had stopped thinking about her hair years ago. She walked across the yard without looking at the crows or the eaves or the circle, straight to the table, and stopped in front of it with her hands at her sides. She had been sitting at the top of the Cinder House's Sunday cards for most of the season. Everybody who had come at her had come the same way, eager, and gone down in the same way. Lately people had stopped coming at her at all, and Vell had had to go to Fenrow to find anybody who would.

"Second bout tomorrow," Vell said. "The Fenrow man. Copper Rank 3, Iron Path. He's eager."

"They're all eager." Dessa did not smile, though something near her eyes was amused. "Next Sunday?"

"Next Sunday, if you want it. And the Thursday after that, I want to put you in with the unrated boy."

Dessa's eyebrows moved, very slightly. It was the most expression Vell had seen on her face in a month.

"The one who ran Brenna round for four."

"That one."

"Brenna says he's a rabbit."

"Brenna says that about anybody she can't catch."

"He went seven with Petra." Dessa said it slowly, as a fact she was turning over. "Nobody goes seven with Petra."

"He did."

Dessa was quiet for a moment, with her hands at her sides, looking at the ledger and not at Vell. Then she said, "He'll come and watch me first."

"Very likely. You're on tomorrow and next Sunday. He'll be in the benches for both, if I know him, and he'll write down everything you do."

"Good." Dessa said it without any heat at all. "Let him watch. I'll be fighting the Fenrow man, not him. And on the Thursday I'll be fighting him, not his notebook." She looked up. "How old?"

"Fourteen."

"Then I want to hear him say the terms. To me. Before."

"You always do."

"I always do." Dessa nodded, once, as if a thing had been settled. Then she did something Vell had not expected. She stayed where she was, when she would usually have gone, and asked a question. "Has anybody ever sat and watched me twice before they fought me?"

Vell thought about it honestly, because it was an honest question.

"Not since the spring," she said. "And he didn't learn anything."

"No. He didn't." Dessa looked at the circle, the groove in the dirt gone soft at its edges in the cold. "I'd like to see what it looks like when somebody does."

She went. Vell watched her go out through the gate, square and unhurried, and did not look at the crows on the way out either.

---

When the light had begun to go, Vell made the card.

She made it every week, for the board at the crossroads, in pencil on the back of whatever paper there was. This week it was the back of a bill for lamp wicks. The pie boy would take it down in the morning and nail it up over last week's, and by noon the whole district would know what was on at the Cinder House on Sunday and the Thursday after.

*Sunday, Cinder House. Second bout: Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against the Fenrow Iron (Cu 3).* That was how the yard knew him, and she wrote him the way the yard knew him, and the rest of the card went under it.

Then, on a separate line, for the Thursday after next:

*Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against unrated (vouched).*

She looked at that line for a while.

People sometimes asked her why the book did not carry the names of unrated fighters, and she sometimes told them, and they mostly did not understand, because they thought it was a rule about fighters. It was not. It was a rule about the yard.

A name went into the book when the yard came to see it. The plainest way to know whether the yard was coming to see somebody was the card. When people started stopping her in the lane and asking *is he on this week?*, by name, and she found she had to write the name on the card for the board because people would come looking for it there, then the yard had come to see him. After that the name went in the book, every time, because the book had to be able to answer what the yard had asked.

Dessa's name had gone on the card two seasons ago, the week people started asking for her. Renn's last year. Brenna's after her third win. They were all in the book by name now, because the yard had come for them, and it would be a lie to write them down as anything less.

The boy was not there yet. A few people had begun to come for him, Marrow said. Two errand boys with half a mark each, and a fish man who nodded. That was not the yard. When it was the yard, she would know, because she would be making a card one evening and find she could not leave his name off it without a dozen people asking her why.

She wrote *unrated*, and left it.

Then she turned the ledger to the bottom of Petra's entry, where there was a little space left. There, in the smaller hand she kept for the things that were hers and not the circuit's, she wrote the last line.

She had begun writing these lines the year of the Ash Path man, and she wrote all of them the same way, as if to somebody else: to whoever would sit on this stool after her and keep this book. She did not know who that would be. She had never let herself decide. But she had come to believe that if the book was going to outlast her, it would need more than results. It would need somebody, some evening, to be able to turn back and find out not only what had happened in this yard but what the keeper had thought ought to happen next, and why, so that they could decide for themselves whether she had been right.

*Recommend the boy be matched against increasingly credible opposition. Dessa first. Not to break him. To find out what he does when lasting stops working.*

She blotted it, and closed the book, and sat with her hand flat on the cover while the singing inside the public house finished, and the scrubbing, and the light went off the top of the wall.

---

"Dessa," Vell said, the next morning, before the yard had begun to fill. "Copper Rank 5, Stone Path. Thursday after next, second bout."

Cael stood in front of the table and felt the name go into him. He had heard it on his first afternoon, behind him at the board, from two men arguing about Brenna. He had seen it on the old result pinned near the bottom of the board, gone pale in the rain. He had never seen her fight.

"She's on tonight," Vell said. "Second bout. And again next Sunday. The public house puts the benches out on a Sunday." She picked up her tea. "Sit in them."

"I will."

"I know you will." She drank. "She'll want to hear you say the terms. Herself, to her face, before. She does that with the young ones. Don't be offended by it."

"I'm not."

"Good." Vell set the cup down. "She's the best defender on my card, and she has been all season, and she wins by waiting. You've spent a month showing me that you can wait." She looked at him. "Now you'll find out what happens when the other fighter can wait longer."

He went out through the gate in the wall and down to the crossroads, because he could not help it, and found the card already nailed up over last week's, in Vell's upright pencil on the back of a bill for lamp wicks.

*Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) against unrated (vouched).*

He stood and read it for some time.

Lira found him there, on her way up from the four lanes with the bread, and read it over his shoulder, and gave a long, low whistle.

"Dessa," she said.

"Have you fought her?"

"No." Lira tore off a piece of the bread and gave it to him without looking at him. "Vell's never put me in with her. I asked once, in the summer. She said I wasn't ready to be patient." She looked at the card for a while longer. "I wasn't. I'm still not, very." Then she turned and looked at him, and her face had the morning brightness in it, the one that did not come out later in the day. "Third row. Tonight. I'll get there early and sit on the end, and you'll get there earlier and sit in the middle, and neither of us will say a word to each other until it's over."

"Why not?"

"Because you'll be watching her," said Lira, "and I'll be watching you watch her. And I want to see what it looks like."

# Chapter 12 — Terms

Lira was waiting for him at the board in the morning, sitting on the edge of a water trough with her canvas roll across her knees, eating an apple.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. I just make it look like I happened to be passing." She threw the core over her shoulder into the trough without looking, where it bobbed, and stood up. "Come on. Vell sets her table out at ten, and the first one there gets her before she's tired of people."

They walked up toward the yards side by side. It was a grey morning, cool, with a thin high cloud that took the colour out of everything, and the district was busy in the ordinary way of a weekday, carts and barrows and the smell of bread. Cael let two streets go by. He had decided in the night what he was going to say, and how, and he found as he walked that every version he had built in the dark sounded either like an accusation or like an apology, and he did not mean either.

He said it anyway, plainly, at the corner where the lane started to climb.

"What does it cost you?"

Lira did not pretend not to understand him. He liked her for that before she had answered.

"Who told you it cost anything?"

"Torvin. And Yeni." He kept walking, and so did she. "Torvin says a vouch is a loan. Yeni says it's your name. She says four months is all the name you've got."

"Yeni talks too much."

"Yeni hardly talks at all."

"Then she picked a strange thing to say it about." Lira walked half a street without saying anything. When she did speak, the lightness had gone out of her voice, and what was left was level and careful, the voice she had used to tell him about the examiner. "All right. If you go into that circle and fold in the first exchange, people will remember that I said you wouldn't. Next time I vouch for somebody, Vell will look at me a little longer before she writes it down. Next time I say a fighter's good, people will say, *she said that about the boy who folded.* It won't ruin me. I've lost four bouts in that yard and I'm still standing in it. But it'll cost me something I worked for, and I'd rather it didn't."

"Then why do it?"

"Because I don't think you'll fold." She looked sideways at him. "I could be wrong. I've been wrong before. But I've watched you for two days, and I don't think so, and if you want to know what my name's actually *for*, Cael, it's for this. It's for saying what I think in front of people who'll hold me to it. Otherwise it's just a thing I keep in a drawer."

He thought about the drawer marked *Springs, do not use,* and did not say anything about it.

"I'm not going to win," he said instead.

"No."

"I mean I'm not going to try to. I'm going to try to last. And I'm going to watch him. And I'm going to come out of there knowing more about how he moves than anybody on that rope." He heard himself saying it and realized he had not known until this moment that it was a plan, rather than a hope. "That's what I can do. That's all I can do. I wanted you to know before you put your name down, in case it's not what you thought you were vouching for."

Lira stopped walking.

They were halfway up the lane, outside a yard where two men were taking turns throwing each other over a hip onto a pile of straw, and the steady soft thump of bodies landing came over the wall like someone beating a rug. Lira stood in the middle of the lane and looked at him for long enough that he began to wonder whether he had made a mistake.

"That's exactly what I thought I was vouching for," she said. "Nobody wins their debut. Nobody sensible tries. The ones who try to win are the ones who get carried out." She started walking again. "And listen. Don't do anything stupid in there to protect my name. You get hurt doing something stupid for my sake and that costs me more than you folding would. I'm not paying for that."

"What counts as stupid?"

"Refusing to go down when you're down. Standing up when you should stay on the ground. Trying something you saw somebody else do on a sheet once." She shook her head. "Keep your feet, watch him, and when Vell calls it, it's over. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job," he said.

"And Cael."

"Yes."

"It's a good question," said Lira, not looking at him. "Asking what it costs. Most people never ask. They just take it." She lengthened her stride. "Come on. She'll have had her first cup by now."

---

Vell's table was set out in the shade of the back wall, where it had been the afternoon before, with the ledger squared to its edge and a cup of tea at her elbow and a pen laid across the open page. The yard was empty except for the crows. Vell was not doing anything. She was sitting on her stool with her hands folded on the table, looking at the circle, and she went on looking at it while they crossed the yard toward her, and only when they had stopped in front of the table did she move her eyes to them.

She looked at Lira first. Then at Cael. Then, briefly, at Lira again, with an expression Cael could not read, as if she were checking a figure against a column and finding that it had been entered in an unexpected place.

"Name," she said.

"Cael."

She did not write it down. She wrote something else instead, shorter than his name, and did not ask for a second one, and he did not give one.

"Age."

"Fourteen."

The pen stopped. It did not stop for long, perhaps the length of one breath, but it stopped, and Vell looked up from the page and looked at him properly, and he made himself stand still and be looked at. It was not an unkind look. It was a thorough one. It went over his face and his shoulders and his hands and the way he was standing, and he understood, as it went, that it was doing exactly what he had done to the Stone woman and the Blade fighter from the barrel yesterday, and that it was doing it far better.

"Path," said Vell.

"Nothing to register."

Her eyebrows moved, very slightly. That was all. She did not ask him what that meant. She wrote something short, three or four words, not in the column where she had written his name and age but at the side of the page, in the margin, and then she laid the pen down across the book.

"You know the terms," she said.

"I've had them explained."

"Explained isn't the same thing." She folded her hands again. "Here they are, so that nobody can say afterward they weren't. You come in unrated. You fight whoever I put across from you. The bout ends when I call it. Not when you'd like it to, not when the crowd's had enough, not when your friend at the rope can't stand to watch any more." Her eyes went to Lira and came back, without any particular weight. "Once you step over that rope, nobody stops it but me. And what happens inside it is yours to carry out of here, whichever way it goes. Nobody's making you do this. If that doesn't suit, say so now, and I'll strike the line, and no one in this yard will think less of you for it."

"It suits."

"Say it back to me."

He looked at her.

"Not because I think you didn't hear it," said Vell. "Because I've had grown men nod at that and then stand in the middle of my circle telling me they never agreed to anything. I don't let anyone in without hearing it in their own mouth. Young or old."

So he said it back. He said it as Hesk had taught him to call a load figure, the whole of it, in order, without adding anything or leaving anything out: unrated; whoever she put across from him; ended when she called it, not before, not for any other reason; nobody stopped it but her; what happened was his to carry, either way; nobody was making him. He did not try to say it in her words. He said it in his own, so that she would know he had understood it rather than learned it.

When he had finished, something eased at the corner of Vell's mouth. It was very small. It was gone before it had become anything as large as a smile.

"Who speaks for you?"

"I do," said Lira.

She said it quickly, and quite loudly, as if she had been waiting for the question and was afraid that if she waited any longer she might not get to it. Then she stood there with her arms crossed and her chin up, as though daring the table to doubt her.

Vell looked at her for a long moment.

"You," she said. "For an unrated fourteen-year-old with nothing to register."

"Yes."

"Four months, you've been in my yard." Vell's voice did not change at all. "In four months I've not seen you vouch for anybody."

"No."

"Why him?"

Lira opened her mouth and then, to Cael's surprise, closed it again. She thought about it. He watched her think about it, there at the table with Vell's eyes on her, and he saw her decide not to give the easy answer, the shrug, the joke about being bored.

"Because he stood in front of the board for eleven minutes," she said, "and when I asked him what he was, he told me."

Vell looked at her for one breath longer. Then she picked up the pen, dipped it, and wrote a line under Cael's name. He could not read it upside down. It was short.

"Renn's been asking me for work," Vell said, writing. "Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Eleven bouts this season. Eight wins." She did not look up. "He's what I've got for an unrated debut tonight. The ones who'd be easier aren't on the card, and the ones on the card aren't easier. He won't go gentle on you because of your age. That isn't how this yard works, and you'd not want it to. If somebody went soft on you the first time, you'd learn something about this place that isn't true, and it'd cost you later."

Cael thought about the notice on the board, pinned in the corner near the lost boot. *Renn (Cu 3, Blade) wants work. Any evening. Cinder House.*

He thought about Lira, four months in, losing to Renn in her second month while Marrow took the money.

"Him, then," he said.

Vell nodded, as though he had confirmed a measurement. "Fourth bout. After the lamps." She laid the pen down. "Don't be late. And don't thank me. I've not done anything for you but write your name in a book."

---

Lira took him to the quietest place she knew, which turned out to be the roof of a cooper's shed two lanes over from the Cinder House, reached by a ladder that the cooper apparently did not know she used. It was flat and tarred and warm from the morning, with a low parapet, and from it you could see the tops of three yards and, beyond them, the first wall of the Ranked core going up the hill in the grey light. Cael thought of a different roof, with a chimney and a water tank, and a grey dog barking at a drainpipe, and then made himself stop.

"Blade," said Lira. She sat cross-legged on the tar with a stick of charcoal and drew on the roof between them. "You saw it yesterday."

"Pale light. Along the forearm, from below the elbow. Out past the fist."

"How far past?"

"Eight inches. My eye says less."

"Your eye's going to keep saying less all night. Every time you look. Your eye has spent fourteen years learning how long an arm is, and it isn't going to unlearn that in an evening." She drew an arm on the tar, and a fist, and then a long thin shape past the fist, and then drew it again, longer. "Every time you look at it, add the eight inches on. Out loud, in your head. The moment you stop adding them, you'll get hit."

"What does it do? When it hits."

"Depends how he holds it." She turned the charcoal in her fingers. "It's got an edge and it's got a flat. The edge cuts. You saw the edge yesterday, on the Stone woman's shoulder. The flat just hits, like being hit with a strap of something hot." She looked at him. "Renn will use the flat on you. On an unrated debut, everybody with a blade uses the flat, unless you give them a reason to stop. It's a courtesy. It still hurts."

"Does he give reasons to stop?"

"No. He's careful." She drew a small circle on the tar beside the arm. "That's what eleven bouts and eight wins means. Not fast, or not only fast. Careful. He doesn't take risks he doesn't need to take. Against you he won't need to take any." She looked up. "Which is good. A careful fighter is a fighter who repeats himself."

"You've fought him."

"In my second month." She drew a line through the small circle. "He put me down in the third exchange. I'd read everything he did in the first two. I'd read it perfectly. I knew exactly what he was going to do, right up to the moment I was on the ground looking at the lamps." She put the charcoal down. "He's got a thing he throws in the third exchange, when he's decided you're worth throwing it at. I'm not going to tell you what it looks like."

"Why not?"

"Because knowing what it looked like didn't help me, and it won't help you. You'll be watching for it, and you'll miss everything else, and you'll still go down." She shrugged. "If he throws it, you go down. Everybody does. That's not losing, that's arithmetic. If he throws it at you, it means you made him want to, and that's a better result than most debuts get."

Cael wrote that down. *If he throws it, you go down. That means you made him want to.*

"Watch the first exchange," Lira said. "Don't try to do anything in it. Give him ground and look. Everybody on that rope will be watching to see whether you last. You watch what he does while he still thinks you're no trouble."

"And if I can't tell the difference? Between watching and just losing?"

Lira considered that seriously, which he had not expected.

"You'll be frightened," she said. "That's fine. Everybody's frightened. I'm frightened every time, and I stopped counting the times a while ago. Being frightened's not the problem. The problem is when you get so frightened you stop looking. If you notice you've stopped looking, that's when it's not watching any more. That's when it's just being hit."

He did not say anything for a moment.

He was thinking about a page in a leather book, in Hesk's hand, which he had read three times on a wall by the merchant road with crumbs on his knees. The fifth entry. It said that fear could be a tool, so long as it kept you looking, and that the only kind worth worrying about was the kind that closed your eyes. Lira had never seen that page. She had never heard of Hesk. She had arrived at the same place by a different road, through four lost bouts in a yard in Ardenmere, and she had said it to him on a cooper's roof in almost the same words.

He found that this steadied him more than anything she had said all morning. Two people who had never met, and who had learned it in two different ways, had told him the same thing. That made it a figure he could trace.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For telling me that."

Lira looked at him as if he had said something odd, and then shrugged, and picked up the charcoal again. "Hips," she said. "Do you know about hips?"

"Hands lie," said Cael. "Hips have to go where the weight goes."

She stared at him. "Who taught you that?"

"My grandfather. On Tuesdays."

"Then your grandfather," said Lira, "has saved me about an hour," and she drew a pair of feet on the tar instead, and started on those.

---

By the time the lamps were lit, the yard was full.

It was not full the way it would be full for a fight anybody had come to see. Lira had told him that, on the walk back down from the roof. Sixty people, perhaps a few more, which was a middling crowd for a middling evening, and none of them had come for the fourth bout. They had come for the second, which was a rematch between two Iron-rated fighters with a grudge, and by the time the fourth bout came round half of them would be at the water bucket or the pie boy or going home. Cael found this a relief. He had not wanted anyone to come for him.

The lanterns hung on their hooks along the three walls, and the light they threw was warm and uneven. The middle of the circle was bright. The edges went soft, and outside the rope the faces of the crowd came and went in the swing of the lamps, so that he could see a mouth and then not see it, an eye and then not.

He did not watch the first three bouts. He watched Renn.

Renn was on the far side of the yard, by the wall, with two friends, and for most of the evening he did not do anything at all. He stood with his weight on one leg and his arms folded and watched the bouts with mild interest, and talked to his friends between them, and laughed twice. He was twenty or close to it. He was built heavily through the chest and shoulders, not tall, and he stood the way men stand who have carried heavy things for a living, with his knees a little soft and his back straight. When he walked to the water bucket and back, his feet landed heel first and then flat, every step the same length.

Once, while the third bout was being called, he looked across the yard and caught Cael looking at him. He nodded. It was the nod you give somebody across a market, not unfriendly and not especially interested, and then he went back to talking to his friends.

Marrow was working the rope with his slate. When he passed the barrel at the corner, Cael read the slate without moving his head. *Renn*, it said, and beside it a number so small it was barely worth betting on. *Unrated, debut*, and beside that a much larger number. And underneath them both, on a line of its own, *Over/under: 1 exch.*

"What does that mean?" he said quietly. "The last line."

Lira had seen it too. Her face did not change. "It means Marrow's taking bets on whether you last more than one exchange," she said. "And his book says you don't."

"Is that a good bet?"

"For most debuts," said Lira, "it's free money."

At the table, Vell stood up. "Fourth bout," she said, and the yard did not go quite as quiet as it had for the first one, and somewhere along the rope someone laughed at something that had nothing to do with him.

Cael stepped over the rope.

---

The groove was a hand deep and he felt it under his boot as he crossed it, a dip and then level ground. Inside the circle the dirt had been swept and watered, and it was firmer than it looked, packed down by years of feet into something nearly as hard as a floor. The lamplight was very bright in the middle, brighter than it had looked from the barrel. He walked to the near side of the circle and stopped just inside the groove, and turned round.

Renn was already on his mark on the far side. He was rolling his shoulders, slowly, one and then the other, the way a man loosens up before he lifts something.

Lira's voice came back to him, in its own compressed rhythm, from the cooper's roof. *Add the eight inches. Out loud, in your head. Watch the first exchange. Don't try to win it. Give him ground and look.*

"Renn," said Vell. "Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Against unrated, debut. Vouched." A small pause, so small that probably nobody else in the yard noticed it. "Begin when you're ready."

Renn brought the blade up.

It came out of nothing along his right forearm, as the young man's had the afternoon before, but it was not the same. It was steadier. It did not make the faint high singing sound when he turned his wrist. It simply stood there in the lamplight, pale and hard and straight, with a thin bright line along one edge. To Cael's eye, before Renn had moved at all, it stopped about a hand past the knuckles. He made himself say that twice in his head, and disbelieve it twice.

*Plus eight inches.* He said it. *Plus eight.* He saw it, then, the way a drawing on a page changes when somebody tells you what it is: the real length of it, the place where the edge actually ended in the air, a long way further out than his eye wanted to believe.

Then Renn started toward him, strolling.

---

The first exchange taught him three things, and he learned them in order.

The first was that an amble from a Copper Rank 3 covered ground faster than other people walked. Renn did not seem to hurry, and then he was there, inside the distance Cael had thought was safe, and the blade was coming.

The second was that Renn opened with probes. Two of them, loose, one high toward the head and one across toward the body, thrown off his front foot with no weight in them at all, like a man tapping a wall to find out where the hollow part is. They were meant to be seen. Cael saw them. He gave ground along the curve of the groove, left, and the first went past his face with a faint hiss of air and the second went across the front of him with room to spare, hands high, knees bent, his arms doing nothing except being in the way.

At the rope, someone said something about a rabbit, and somebody else laughed.

The third thing was the important one, and he nearly missed it, and then he did not.

Renn's weight went back.

It went back through his rear foot, into the heel, and settled there, and his whole frame seemed to gather itself onto that heel the way a spring gathers when you press it. The probes had come off the front foot, light as talk. This was different. This was the frame loading. And in the instant after the heel settled—

The blade came round.

Cael saw it start and was late by the width of a breath. Most of him got clear. Not all of him. The flat caught him across the meat of the left shoulder as it went past, and it landed like an iron bar left too long in a stove, a broad hard slap that spun him a half-turn on his feet and drove the breath out of him in a grunt. Along the rope the crowd made its first real sound of the night, a pleased murmur that meant the evening was going the way everyone had paid for.

He did not fall. He finished the turn three steps back with his left arm hanging and his shoulder singing, and he was still on his feet, and still inside the groove.

Renn did not come after him. He walked back to his mark and waited for Cael to find his, and he rolled his shoulders again while he waited, in no hurry about anything. Cael could find nothing in his face to be afraid of, and nothing to be flattered by. He was a few minutes' exercise for a rated man between bigger bouts, the kind of evening a careful fighter takes to keep his arm warm, and Renn bore him no ill will and spared him no thought.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

The shoulder hurt in a bright, exact way, as though it had been drawn on him with a hot wire. Cael put the hurt in a box and set the box down. The heel he kept out, where he could see it.

*Probes off the front foot,* he thought. *Truth off the back heel. There's a gap between the heel and the blade, and tonight I spent it looking for the gap.*

*Next time it's mine.*

---

The second exchange began the same way. Renn ambled in. The probes came, high and across, off the front foot, loose and conversational, and Cael gave ground on the curve, and did not look at the probes at all. He looked at the heel.

It went back. It settled.

And this time Cael went when the heel went, without waiting to see the blade it was loading. He went left and back, early, before the blade had started to come round, a whole quarter of a second sooner than he had moved the first time—

The strike did not come round.

Renn dropped instead. He went low, very fast, his blade arm flung out wide and high as a counterweight, the pale edge drawing a bright line up into the dark above the lamps, and his whole heavy body swinging round, leg first, through the place where Cael's ankles ought to have been. He was going for the legs. There was no harm meant in it. It was the answer a careful fighter keeps for a beginner who has worked out one trick and is too pleased with it, and it ends with the beginner flat on the ground and the rope laughing.

But Cael had moved early. He had moved before the sweep committed, and his ankles were not where the sweep went looking for them. He jumped the tail of the swing, clumsily, arms flung out like a boy clearing a ditch, and landed with both feet under him, and found Renn right in front of him.

The bigger man was still crouched from the swing, twisted half round, his hips facing Cael full on and his blade flung up and out on the far side. A short arm could have reached him. For the space of half a beat there was nothing at all between Cael and the middle of him.

Cael saw it. He saw all of it, the hips and the turned shoulder and the blade on the wrong side, and he wrote it down in his head as clearly as if he had had the notebook in his hand.

He did nothing with it.

Then the moment closed. Renn came up out of the sweep in one smooth motion, stepped back two paces, and stood looking at Cael across the bright middle of the circle. And then he rolled his shoulders: left, then right, slowly, settling them. It was the same thing he had done before the first exchange. Cael filed it at once beside the heel. *Shoulders when he changes his mind.*

There was a half-smile on Renn's face. It was not for Cael, exactly. It was for the situation.

Something had shifted in the sound along the rope. It was no louder than before. But it had lost the comfortable note of people who already knew the ending, and here and there someone had stopped talking to watch.

At the table, Vell's pen moved again. It moved for longer.

---

When Renn came in for the third exchange, he did not amble.

He came balanced and quiet, with his weight in the middle of his feet, and the probes, when they came, were crisper, shorter, with something in them now. Cael understood that something had been decided across the circle. Renn had stopped exercising. He had found a puzzle in front of him, and he meant to finish it.

The probes came, high and then across, and Cael gave ground, and watched the heel.

The heel settled.

And then he was somewhere else.

He was off the line of the strike. He did not know when he had left it. His weight was already through, his feet already placed, and the blade went past through the place where his chest had been, and he had not seen it go. He had not read it. He had spent two exchanges and a shoulder buying the quarter of a second between the heel and the strike, and when the heel settled he did not spend it. There was no quarter second. There was the heel, and then there was this.

The second strike came out of the first one's follow-through. It was not a new attack. It was the first cut turned round and sent back, slanting low across, into the place where anyone who had got out of the way of the first strike would have to be standing.

He was not standing there.

He was standing outside its arc, with his weight already turning, and he had not chosen that either. His mind arrived at each new place after his body was already in it, and found it lit and waiting, and could not account for having come.

The third strike came up from below.

He did not see it start. He only felt the air of it, close, very close, a hand's width from his ribs on the left side, ripping upward past him into the empty space above the circle, and he heard the blade's faint hiss die away at the top of its arc. The yard had gone completely silent. In the silence he could hear the lanterns creaking on their hooks.

He was standing still. His heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth. He had no idea what he had just done.

*The watching,* he told himself, because whatever had happened needed a name, and any name would do for now. *Two exchanges of watching. The body learns.* He set the explanation over the thing and saw at once that it did not fit, as a lid cut for a smaller jar does not fit. Two exchanges of one man's rhythm did not come to that. Nothing he could put his finger on came to that. And the middle of a bout was no place to stand doing arithmetic.

He left the lid where it sat, crooked, and got his hands up.

Because Renn was still there, a pace and a half away, finishing the figure, his weight carried all the way through and up onto his front foot, his blade high and his ribs open. It was the second time tonight Cael had stood within reach of a fully committed Copper Rank 3. He had read nothing this time and got out anyway, and now the man was open, and Cael did the only thing he knew how to do, which was nothing he had ever practised.

He threw a short straight punch at Renn's shoulder.

Renn did not even turn toward it. His left forearm brushed it off, idly, as a man brushes off a fly, without breaking his stride and without bothering to make a guard of it. Then he stepped off, back to his mark, and the third exchange was over.

Cael had not touched him all night.

He knew that, standing on his mark with his fist still stinging from the forearm. He had read two exchanges and paid for one of them with a shoulder. Then something had happened in the third that he could not account for, and in the middle of it he had been handed the cleanest opening of the evening and had answered it with a punch a child could have swatted. Seeing a strike coming was one craft. Doing anything about it was another. He had spent two days, and an hour on a cooper's roof, and two exchanges in this circle building the first. He owned none of the second.

Across the circle, Renn was looking at him.

For the first time all night he was paying attention, the narrow, careful attention of a tradesman holding up a part that has come off his own lathe in a shape he did not cut. He did not say anything. Fighters did not speak in the middle of a bout. But he rolled his shoulders once more, left and then right, and something in the way he stood packed itself tighter, lower, more closely round its own centre.

*That's his serious weight,* Cael thought, and wrote it down in his head with the rest. *That's what eleven bouts and eight wins looks like when it stops holding back.*

---

The fourth exchange lasted four seconds.

Renn came in with no probes at all. There was no amble and no tapping at the wall. He came straight off his mark and threw a figure that was cousin to the one in the third exchange, strikes folded into each other, each one coming out of the end of the last. But it was stepped up past some point where reading it stopped being any use, because every strike shut the door the one before it had left open, and the whole of it came so fast that everything earlier in the evening seemed, looking back, to have been good manners.

Cael saw the first fold clearly. He saw the heel, and the first strike, and he moved, late.

He saw the second fold partly.

And he reached, the way you reach in the dark for a stair you were sure was there, for the thing that had moved his feet in the third exchange. He did not decide to reach. He was simply reaching, feeling about for it like a man patting his pockets for a key he had been holding a moment before.

There was nothing there. There were only his own plain reflexes, the ones that had been late all evening, and they were late again, and they had nothing to spare.

The flat of the blade took him across the ribs on the right side. It was a clean, driving, expert blow, with all of Renn's weight behind it this time, and it lifted him off his feet and turned him in the air and put him down on his side in the packed dirt with every bit of breath gone out of him at once.

The ground pressed cold against his cheek. Overhead the lamps rocked on their hooks, or else the yard did. The crowd had become a long way off, much further than the rope, and it was shouting.

"Match," said Vell.

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The one word reached every corner of the yard, the shouting and the lamplight and the boy lying in the dirt, and after it nothing else that happened in the circle counted.

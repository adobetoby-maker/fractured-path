# Chapter 7 — The Open Gate

The road came down off the rise in long, loose bends, and by the time Cael reached the bottom the last of the blue had drained out of the sky and left the valley to its fires. The nearest ones burned on either side of the road where it met the first houses, in iron baskets propped on stones, and the people who had lit them were selling things by their light.

There was no gate. He had known there would be none, because Hesk had said so at the kitchen table and the reference room had said so before that, but knowing a thing and walking through it were different. He found that his hand had gone into his coat on its own and closed round the registration card in the inside pocket, beside the bent bracket and the leather book. He walked the last fifty paces like that, with his thumb on the stamped edge of the card, ready to hand it to anyone who asked.

Nobody asked.

A woman on an upturned crate was selling hot cider out of a dented kettle, a ladle at a time, into whatever cup you brought. The man beside her had a basket of candle ends sorted by length. Across the road an old man sat behind a blanket spread with buckles, hinges and keys that no longer had locks, and he was asleep sitting up, with his chin on his chest and one hand resting on the blanket as though to keep it from blowing away. Two girls were tearing a sweet bun in half and then arguing about which half was larger. A dog lay in the warm circle of the cider woman's fire and opened one eye at Cael as he went by, then closed it again, having seen enough.

He stopped at the edge of the firelight and looked back. The road he had come down was a pale thread going up into the dark, and somewhere at the top of it was the place where he had stood an hour ago and underlined two words.

The road notes from the Denvash record office had said that Ardenmere's gate opened at dawn and closed at full dark, and that a traveller should not arrive after dark on the first night. He had walked the whole second half of the day with the sun at his back like a clock he could not stop looking at. Now he turned and looked across the river, where the Ranked core climbed its hill ring by ring, and he saw it at last. At the far end of the nearest bridge, where the road met the first of the walls, two lamps hung over a gate, and as he watched, a figure in a pale coat swung one leaf of it shut.

So the notes had been right. They had simply been about the other side of the river, the side with walls, and he had read them as if they were about this one, because he had not known this one had nothing to close.

He thought about the bracket bending on the rig in Hesk's workshop and the supplier's printed figure that had not been traced. This was not quite that mistake. This number had been true, and he had traced it, and he had still put it in the wrong place. In some ways that was worse, because there was nobody to blame for it but the person holding the pencil.

He took his hand out of his coat and left the card where it was.

---

The district closed over the road within a hundred paces.

He had seen it from the rise as a shape, and the shape had been enormous and blurred, a field of embers. From inside it was all edges. The road narrowed between buildings that leaned toward each other across it, two and three storeys high, with their upper floors pushed out further than their lower ones, so that the strip of sky between the eaves was barely wider than a cart. Laundry hung across that strip on lines strung from window to window, and in the dark the shirts and sheets looked like a second ceiling. Lanes went off on both sides, some wide enough for a barrow and some only wide enough for a person going sideways, and every one of them had a light somewhere along it and a smell coming out of it: onions, wet stone, tar, bread, something sweet burning.

People moved through all of it at the pace of a place finishing its day. A man went past with a long plank on his shoulder and turned it carefully at a corner without looking, as he must have turned it at that corner a thousand times. A woman carried a sleeping child in one arm and a sack of something in the other and argued pleasantly with somebody in a doorway without slowing down. Three boys a little younger than Cael ran past him in a knot, shouting about a wager, and one of them bumped his bag and shouted "Sorry, uncle!" over his shoulder, which made Cael look round to see who the uncle was before he understood it had been him.

Nobody looked at him twice. He had expected, without quite knowing he expected it, that a boy his age with a travelling bag would be noticed, the way a new face was noticed on Fen Street, weighed and placed. Here the district seemed to have decided long ago that boys with travelling bags were part of the weather.

He walked with his hands at his sides and his eyes open, and he counted, because counting was the thing he did when there was too much: four lanes on the left before the first square, six on the right. Twelve lamps in the square, two of them out. One well, with an iron cover and a woman sitting on it.

In the square someone with a Path was working. A heavyset porter in a leather apron was loading barrels onto a handcart, and he lifted each full barrel by its rim with one hand, the way Cael would have lifted a basket of eggs, and set it down without a sound. Force, probably. Copper, almost certainly; an Iron-tier Force practitioner would not be loading handcarts in the Unranked District at dusk, or if he were, there would be a reason worth knowing.

Cael watched the man's feet. They were planted wide and stayed planted, and all the work went up through his back and out through his arm, and his breathing did not change. Whatever his declaration said, the muscles under it were his own and had been doing this for years.

*Paths on everybody,* Cael thought. In Denvash he had known which of his neighbours had which Path because he had asked them, and written it down. Here he was going to have to learn to read it off people, from how they stood and what they carried and what they did not bother to be careful about.

He realized, standing in the square with his bag on his shoulder, that he was looking forward to it. It was the first thing since the rise that had felt like his own size.

---

He needed somewhere to sleep, and he needed it before the cider woman put out her fire.

He asked the woman on the well, because she was sitting still, and people who were sitting still usually had time for a question. She was perhaps sixty, with a shawl over her head and a pipe she was not smoking, and she looked at him and at the bag and nodded as though he had confirmed a bet she had made with herself.

"Cheap or decent?"

"Both, if I can."

"Then you want Torvin's." She pointed with the pipe stem. "Down there, second turning, two streets in. Sign says ROOMS. It doesn't say anything else, and neither will he, but the floor's swept and nobody steals your boots."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, I've got a cousin there. If you hate it, don't tell him I sent you."

The second turning was a lane with a gutter down the middle, and two streets in, on the left, was a narrow stone house with a wooden sign over the door. ROOMS had been painted on it by hand, a long time ago, in letters that had faded unevenly. The O had faded least and leaned hardest, so that the word looked as though it were about to fall over to one side and had been about to for years.

He knocked.

The man who opened the door was broad and heavy, past middle age, with a jaw he had not shaved that day or the day before and an expression of having opened this door too many times to be curious about who was on the other side of it. He looked at Cael's face, then at the bag, then at Cael's boots, which carried two days of the merchant road on them, and then back at Cael's face, in that order, without any hurry.

"Torvin," he said. It was his name, and it seemed to be all the introduction he thought the situation needed.

"I need a bed."

"Week or day."

"How much is each?"

Torvin named both prices. The week was cheaper per night by enough to matter, and dearer in total by enough to hurt, and Cael stood on the step and did the sum in his head against the pouch, which he had counted that morning at the waystation and could see in his mind's eye down to the last half-mark.

"Week," he said.

Torvin held out a hand the size of a shovel blade. Cael counted the coins into it. Torvin counted them again with his thumb, slowly, moving each coin from one side of his palm to the other, and when he had finished he counted them a third time. It did not seem like distrust. It seemed more like a man who had learned that his own attention wandered at the end of a long day and had decided to check it rather than trust it.

"Registration?" Cael asked.

He did not know why he asked. Perhaps it was only to see what the answer would be.

"Don't want it." Torvin put the coins in his apron pocket. "Don't want to see it, don't want to know it, don't want anybody coming to my door asking whether I've seen it." He looked at Cael for a moment longer. "That a problem?"

"No."

"Good." He stood aside. "Rules. Keep your space clean. Quiet after the lamps go out. If you've got to fight somebody, fight them in the street. Not in my house."

"I don't fight people."

"Then it's an easy rule." Torvin started up the stairs, and Cael followed him. The stairs were narrow and steep and turned twice, and every tread had a dip worn into its middle, as the record office steps in Denvash had. "Shared room. Five cots. Kitchen's at the back, you cook your own, first come first served. Water's in the yard. Anything you want to know that isn't rent, ask the others. They've been here longer than you."

At the top of the last flight he stopped with his hand on a door and looked down at Cael over his shoulder.

"You're young to be on your own."

"I'm fourteen."

"That's what I said." He opened the door. "Doesn't mean you can't. Means you're young."

---

The room ran across the back of the house under a low ceiling with a beam down the middle of it that a tall man would have had to duck. There were five cots, two along each long wall and one under the single window at the end, set far enough apart that a person could sit on the edge of one without touching the next. The floor had been swept that day; Cael could see the arcs the broom had left in the dust along the skirting. It was cleaner than he had expected, and smaller, and it smelled of soap and wool and the river, which was somewhere close behind the house.

Two of the cots were taken. On the one nearest the door a man lay on his back with a blanket pulled to his chin, an older man with grey stubble and his mouth a little open, breathing with a faint whistle at the top of every breath, like a kettle a long way from boiling. On the one beside the window a woman of about thirty sat cross-legged with a shirt across her knees and a needle going in and out of it so quickly that Cael could not follow the stitches, only their result, a seam closing behind her hand as if it were being zipped.

Two more cots had blankets folded on them and boots underneath them, and the one under the window was bare.

"New," the woman said. She did not look up. It was not quite a question.

"Yes."

"That one's yours." She tipped her head at the bare cot without taking her eyes off the seam. "Window's draughty, so you'll want your coat over the blanket. The brothers are on the river till late and they come in loud and go out early, and there's nothing to be done about it, so don't try. He's asleep." This was the man by the door. "He's always asleep. He wheezes. You get used to it, or you don't, and then you leave."

"Do people leave?"

"All the time. Somebody new has that cot every few weeks." She bit off her thread, knotted the end without looking at it, and held the shirt up to the lamp to judge the seam. Then, for the first time, she looked at him properly, a brief and thorough look that seemed to take his measurements as she might for a coat. "I'm Yeni. That's my sewing kit on the shelf. Don't touch it. Everything else in this room is anybody's unless somebody tells you different."

"I'm Cael."

"Mm." She folded the shirt and set it on a pile of three others, all mended. "Eaten?"

"Not since noon."

"Market's two streets east, toward the river. It'll be closing." She picked up the next shirt and found its tear without searching for it. "Go now if you're going."

The conversation was over. It had been neither warm nor cold, only short, and Cael understood as he set his bag down on the bare cot that he had just been told everything he needed and nothing he did not. He thought that in Denvash a conversation like that would have felt rude, and that here it was simply the way people saved their strength for the things that needed it.

He took the pouch out of the bag and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, with the card and the book, and left the bag where it was. Yeni watched him do it out of the corner of her eye and said nothing, which he took to mean that it was the right thing to do.

At the door he stopped.

"The sign outside," he said. "The O leans."

Yeni's needle paused for half a stitch. "It's leaned since before I came. He says if he repaints it he'll have to decide whether it's worth repainting the whole house, and he hasn't decided."

"How long has he been deciding?"

"Eleven years, I think." She went back to her seam. "Market, Cael."

---

The market was louder than everything around it put together.

It filled a long, irregular square that sloped down toward the river, three rows of stalls deep under a patchwork of canvas awnings, some striped, some patched, some only an old sail with the rope holes still in it. Lanterns hung from the awning poles, and because the poles were all different heights the light was all different heights too, so that walking down a row was like walking through a room in which someone had hung the lamps by guesswork. Most of the stalls were closing. Vendors were calling out last prices to clear what was left, and the prices dropped as Cael walked, so that a pie that cost one thing at the top of the row cost less at the bottom, as though the whole market were a slope that money rolled down.

He watched the haggling before he joined it. It was quick and cheerful and had a rhythm to it he did not know yet. A buyer named a number, the seller laughed at it, the buyer laughed back, and then they met somewhere in the middle and both looked pleased, as though the laughing had been part of the price. In Denvash's Outer Market haggling had been slower and more offended, full of sighs and hands pressed to chests, and he had always suspected the offence was a performance. Here nobody bothered to perform it. Here it seemed to be understood that everyone was trying to get the best of everyone else, and that this was fine, and that there was no need to pretend to be hurt about it.

He bought half a loaf from a woman who was packing up, and a bowl of something stewed and brown from a man who said it was mostly beans and partly a secret. He paid the man's first price for the stew, because he did not yet know what the second price should be, and saw the man register it and decide, kindly, not to say anything. He paid the woman for the bread after one round of laughing, and got it for less than she had asked, and felt absurdly pleased about it, considering that the difference would not have bought a candle end.

He ate standing at the edge of the market, with the bowl in one hand and the bread in the other, against a post where he could see down two rows at once.

Some of the vendors had Paths, and some of them used them. A woman at a frying pan had no fire under it at all, and the oil in it shimmered and spat all the same; she held the handle with her bare hand and moved her thumb along it now and then, as if turning something up. A boy at a fish stall was gutting with a knife and did not have a Path in it that Cael could see, and was very fast anyway. Every stall had a small slate or a scrap of board with its prices chalked on it, and some of the prices had been rubbed out and written in again so many times that the board had gone grey all over.

Halfway down the nearest row was a stall selling roasted nuts out of a drum over a charcoal brazier. A girl of about seventeen was turning the drum with a crank, and a boy of about Cael's age was weighing the nuts out into paper twists. He was weighing them without looking at the scale. He tipped the scoop, watched the customer's face instead of the pointer, and stopped pouring at the moment the face looked satisfied.

He caught Cael watching him and pointed the scoop at him across the row.

"You're new."

Cael finished his mouthful. "Is it that easy to see?"

"New people watch first and eat second. You've been watching me weigh for a quarter of an hour and your stew's gone cold." The boy filled another twist. "It's not bad. It's smart, mostly. It just means you'll spend a week finding out who's cheating you."

"Are you cheating me?"

"I haven't sold you anything." He twisted the paper shut with a flick. "But I could save you the week, if you're buying."

"Save me how?"

"I tell you who overcharges new people and who doesn't, who weighs light, who sells yesterday's bread as today's, and which pie man's pies have actual meat in them. Everything you'd learn in a week of getting it wrong, in about the time it takes to eat a twist of nuts." He leaned on the counter. "Price of a twist of nuts, plus a bit."

"What's the bit for?"

"The saving."

His sister reached across without stopping the crank and flicked him on the ear with one finger. It was a practised flick, accurate and not very hard, and he took it without turning round, as if it were a tax he had long ago agreed to pay.

"He's going to tell you we're the only honest stall in the market," she said to Cael. "We're not. We're about the third most honest. Don't pay him the bit."

"Third," the boy protested.

"Fourth on a wet day."

Cael found that he was smiling, and that it had come up out of him without being asked, the first one since the depot. He did not buy anything. He did not want to spend money he had not counted against the pouch, and he did not want to buy a thing the first time it was offered to him, in case that was the kind of mistake the boy would charge to tell him about.

"Maybe tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow it's the same price," the boy said, "but you'll have lost a day. Think about that."

Cael thought about it. He thought that it was very nearly a good argument and that the boy knew it was very nearly a good argument, and that both of those things together were worth something by themselves. He nodded to the sister, who nodded back with the corner of her mouth, and he took his bowl back to the stew man and went home through the closing market with the bread in his pocket for the morning.

---

The brothers came in a little after the lamps were lit.

They were both big and both young, with river mud to the knee and identical noses, and they came up the stairs talking about a barge that had grounded on a sandbar. They kept talking about it while they washed in the basin by the door, and while they ate something cold out of a cloth, and while they took their boots off. One of them noticed Cael on the window cot, said "New," in exactly Yeni's tone, and went back to the sandbar. Neither of them asked his name. Cael decided that he liked them for it.

He sat on the cot with his back against the wall and his coat over his knees, and took out the current notebook, and turned to a clean page.

*Day four. Ardenmere.*

*No gate on this side. The road notes meant the gate across the river, at the Ranked core. I read a true note and put it in the wrong place.*

*Nobody asked for my registration. Not at the edge, not in the square, not at the house. Torvin said he doesn't want it. He said it like a rule.*

*Torvin. ROOMS. A week paid. Counts coin three times. Rules: clean, quiet, fight in the street.*

*Yeni, about thirty, sews faster than I can see. Don't touch the kit. The wheezing man. The brothers, river barges, a sandbar.*

*Market by the river. Haggling here is laughing, not sighing. A woman fries without fire. A boy at the nut stall weighs by people's faces instead of the scale, and wants paying to save me a week.*

He looked at the last line for a moment and added: *He might be worth it.*

Then he took the leather book out of his coat, untied the cord, and unfolded the letter from between its pages. He read it through by the light of Yeni's lamp, from *Hesk* to *— Cael*, all of it, and found that it still said what he meant it to say, which was a relief, because things written at a waystation table at the end of a long day did not always survive being read the next night.

It did not say anything about the cart.

He held the pencil over the bottom of the page for a while. The cart was a day and a half behind him now, at the bottom of a dip between two hedges, and it had shrunk in that time to the size of a turnip in a bag. He did not write it in. He wrote something else instead, small, under his name.

*Write back to me care of Torvin's, the house with ROOMS on the sign, two streets in from the east road, Unranked District, Ardenmere. They'll know it. The O leans.*

He would post it tomorrow, if he could find out where letters were posted. It would reach Denvash in about a week. Hesk would read it at the kitchen table, in the twenty minutes of sideways light, and then he would read it again, and Cael could see him doing it so clearly that for a moment the room under the beam went far away.

He folded the letter back into the book and tied the cord.

Across the room Yeni had finished her last shirt. She tied off the thread, set the shirt on the pile, put the needle into a small felt book in the sewing kit, put the kit on the shelf, and looked at Cael on his cot with his notebook.

"Writing to someone?"

"My grandfather."

She considered that, and him, and then nodded once, as if he had told her something about himself that she could use later. "Good," she said. "People who write home stay longer."

She leaned over and turned down the lamp.

Below them, in the dark, a door shut, and Torvin's voice came up through the floorboards, not loud, carrying the way a voice carries when it has said the same word every night for eleven years.

"Lamps."

The last line of light under the door went out. The wheezing man whistled at the top of his breath. One of the brothers said something about the sandbar, and the other one told him to go to sleep, and he did. Out beyond the window the district kept up its low noise, voices and wheels and water, a city that did not go quiet so much as turn itself down, and Cael lay under his coat with his hand on the leather book and listened to it, and found that it did not frighten him.

That was new. He did not write it down, because he was not sure yet that it was true.

# Chapter 8 — Watching's Not Nothing

The brothers were gone before it was light. Cael heard them go, a great deal of whispering that was louder than talking would have been, and a boot dropped on the boards and complained at in a mutter he could not make out, and then the door. When he opened his eyes properly the window had turned grey, the wheezing man was still asleep with his mouth open, and Yeni's cot was empty and already made, its blanket folded into a square so exact that it looked like a joke about blankets.

He dressed under the coat, the way you dress in a room that is not yours, and went down the turning stairs to the kitchen.

It was a long, low room at the back of the house, with a black range at one end that somebody had already lit, a table down the middle scarred with knife marks, and a door at the far end standing open on a yard with a pump in it. A woman was sitting at the end of the table lacing a pair of heavy boots, and from the creases in her skirt and the state of her hair it was plain that she had slept in her clothes and had probably slept in the boots too, and had only taken them off to put them back on more tightly. She nodded to him without stopping. At the range, a broad, grey-haired woman in an apron was scraping out the bottom of a big iron pot with a wooden spoon.

"There's a bowl of this left," she said, without turning round. "It's yesterday's oats, so it's free, but if you burn the pot you scrub it."

"Thank you."

"I'm his wife." She tipped the spoon toward the ceiling, toward the house and the man who owned it. "He'll tell you he owns the place. He owns the sign."

The woman in boots snorted into her laces.

Cael ate the oats standing at the counter by the yard door, where he could see the pump and the wall and a square of sky over the wall that was turning from grey to white. Then he took the pouch out of his coat and counted it, all of it, on the counter, in small stacks of marks and half-marks, twice.

It came out the same both times. One week at Torvin's was gone from it, and one night at the waystation, and a bowl of beans and half a loaf. What was left would last. It would not last comfortably, and it would not last long enough for him to stop counting it, but it would last if he did not waste it, and wasting things was not one of the skills he had ever needed to practise. He wrote the figure in the back of the notebook and drew a line under it.

"You count like Torvin," said the woman in boots, standing up and stamping her heels down into the leather.

"He counts three times. I only count twice."

"Give it a month," she said, and went out through the yard.

---

Yeni came in from the yard with a basin while he was putting the pouch away, and she stopped in the doorway and looked at his bag, which he had brought down to repack.

"Your strap's going."

He looked. Across the strap, where it lay against his hip, there was a pale scuffed band he had last seen at the bottom of a dip in the road, between two hedges, with a turnip lying in the grass at his feet. Overnight the scuff had begun to fray. Two threads stood up out of the weave like hairs.

"It caught on something," he said. "On the road."

"It'll catch on something else and go." Yeni set the basin down, went upstairs, and came back with the sewing kit before he had worked out whether he was supposed to say anything. She sat down at the table, took the strap across her knee, threaded a needle by feel without looking at the eye, and laid a row of small tight stitches across the frayed band and back again. It took less time than he took to tie a boot.

"There," she said, biting the thread. "Now you owe me a favour."

"What kind of favour?"

"I haven't decided. I'll collect it when I need it, and you won't like it, and you'll do it anyway." She put the needle away. "That's how favours work here. Do they work differently where you come from?"

Cael thought about Ressa's extra loaf, which she had not let anyone pay for, and Garrik's afternoon of wheel bearings when he was eight.

"Mostly people didn't collect."

"Then they were either very rich or very soft." Yeni shut the kit. "Which?"

"Neither. They'd known me since I was born."

She looked at him for a moment, and something in her face went less brisk without becoming anything else. "Well," she said, "nobody here has. So you'll owe me." She stood up with the kit under her arm. "If you're posting that letter, the carriers are at the bottom of the fish steps, by the lower bridge. The west bag goes at noon."

He had not said anything about posting a letter. He thought back over the evening, and found the place: she had seen him write, and asked who to, and he had said his grandfather, and she had heard *letter* and *west* in that and filed them.

*She watches,* he thought. *She just doesn't write it down.*

---

He went to the market first, because the market was on the way and because he had thought about the boy's argument while he was falling asleep and had found it held.

The nut stall was open. The sister was building the charcoal up under the drum, and the boy was sitting on an upturned crate eating an apple, and when he saw Cael coming down the row he finished the apple in three bites, threw the core over the awning into the next row, and stood up as if a bell had rung.

"You lost a day," he said.

"I know. I've come to buy the rest of the week."

"Price of a twist of nuts." The boy held up a hand. "Plus the bit."

Cael paid it. The bit turned out to be about half the price of the nuts again, and he paid it without arguing, because the sister had told him not to and he wanted to see what happened when he did. What happened was that the boy took the coins, looked at them, looked at his sister, and put one of the smaller ones back in Cael's hand.

"For not haggling," he said. "Nobody never haggles. It's unnatural."

"It's nobody *ever* haggles," said the sister to the charcoal.

"That's what I said."

Then he talked, and Cael had been right to pay. He talked fast and without stopping, and pointed with a paper twist as he went, and what he gave Cael was not a list but a map, with people on it instead of streets. The bread woman at the top of the second row sold yesterday's loaves with this morning's on top, so you asked for the one from underneath and watched her face. The man with the pies was honest about the meat and dishonest about the weight, and the woman with the pies two stalls along was the other way round, and the trick was to buy pies from her and gravy from him. The fish was good if the fishmonger was the one with the beard and bad if it was the one with the hat, because they were cousins and shared a stall and only one of them knew anything about fish. The fruit woman by the steps gave short weight to anyone who called her *auntie* and full weight to anyone who called her by her name, and she had never told anybody her name.

"So what do you call her?"

"Nothing. You point." He pointed, to demonstrate. "She likes that best."

Cael had the notebook out by then and was writing as fast as he could, and the boy watched him do it with the deep, uncomplicated satisfaction of a craftsman whose work was being taken seriously.

"And past the market," Cael said, when the boy ran out of stalls. "The rest of it. What's where."

"That's not the week. That's extra."

"Then I'll find it myself."

The boy weighed this, and then shrugged, in the manner of somebody giving away a thing he had never been sure he could sell. "Houses are all round here, this side. That's where people sleep. Down by the water are the workshops, people who fix things and make things, the ones who can't pay what they charge for a bench across the river. Up north, where the ground starts going up again, that's the yards. Fighters, mostly. People training." He sniffed. "Some of them are good. Most of them shout."

"And?"

"And there's the other market." His voice did not drop, but it slowed down. "South of here. It isn't a market, really. It's people standing about. You can buy things there that you couldn't buy anywhere with a wall round it. Gear. Old stuff. Things nobody asks where they came from." He looked at Cael. "And answers. There's a woman who sells answers."

"What kind of answers?"

"Whatever kind you pay for." He picked up his scoop. "Don't buy from her unless you really want to know. People always think they want to know. Then they find out."

His sister turned the crank on the drum, and the nuts inside rattled round like rain on a roof, and she said, without looking at either of them, "That one's free. He tells everyone that one."

He found the carriers at the bottom of a flight of broad stone steps that went down to the water, slick with fish scales and river weed, beside the lowest of the bridges. It was a stone hut with a counter open to the street, two sorting tables inside, and a row of heavy canvas bags hanging on hooks along the back wall, each with a direction painted on it in tall black letters. WEST was the third one along. A young man with ink on his fingers took the letter, read the address aloud in a flat voice, weighed it on a little brass balance, and named a price that made Cael wince and pay without saying anything.

"About a week," the clerk said, putting the letter in the bag. "Bit more if the roads are bad. Bit less if they're not."

"And letters coming back. Do I come here for them?"

"Torvin's?" The clerk glanced at the address again. "No. Runner takes them round. He knows Torvin's." He looked at Cael for the first time. "Everybody knows Torvin's."

So it was done. The letter was in a canvas bag on a hook, and the bag would go up onto a carrier's cart at noon and out along the merchant road, past the waystation and the dip in the hedges, and in about a week it would be in Hesk's hands. Cael stood on the bottom step with the river going by at his feet and felt the weight of that more than he had expected to, as if he had put down something he had been carrying since the waystation without noticing how heavy it was.

The bridge went up from beside the carriers' hut in a long stone hump, wide enough for two carts, with a low parapet on either side. He climbed it as far as the top of the hump, where he could see the far end, and stopped there with his elbows on the parapet like a boy watching the river, which, as far as anyone could tell, was all he was.

At the far end of the bridge was the gate he had watched close the night before.

By daylight it was smaller than it had looked by lamplight, a stone arch in a stone wall with two guards on the near side of it and a little roofed booth where a clerk sat with a ledger. Everyone who crossed the bridge stopped at the booth. Everyone held out a card. The clerk looked at each card, and looked at the face above it, and either nodded or did not.

Cael watched them for a long time, and he counted.

A woman with a basket showed a card and was nodded through. Copper, he guessed, from the quickness of it; the outer ring was Copper's, and the clerk barely looked. A man in a guild coat showed his and was nodded through faster still, and walked on up the road toward the second wall without breaking his stride. Iron, Cael thought, or better, and going higher than the outer ring. Two porters with a handcart showed theirs and were let through to unload in the shadow of the arch, and no further, and they seemed to know exactly where the line was and did not step over it.

Then a young man in a patched jacket came up to the booth with a sack on his shoulder and held out a card, and the clerk looked at it and shook his head.

The young man said something. The clerk shook his head again, not unkindly, and pointed back along the bridge. One of the guards shifted his weight from one foot to the other, nothing more than that, but the young man saw it and stopped arguing. He turned round with his sack and walked back over the bridge toward the Unranked side, past Cael on the hump, with his face set and his eyes on the stones.

Cael watched him go down the steps toward the fish market.

*Unranked can't legally pass.* He had read it in the community hall, at the long table, and copied it into a notebook in the careful hand he used for facts. Copper got the outer districts. Iron got the city and, with papers, the guild districts. Bronze got the administrative core. Silver got everything and the roads between cities. It had been a table in a summary, and he had learned it the way you learn a table, by its columns.

Now he had watched it work on a person, and it was not a table. It was a man with a sack walking back the way he had come.

He took the registration card out of his coat and looked at it. Name, number, district of issue. On the classification line, where Pellin's stamp had come down, there was one word in square brackets, and nothing else: no tier, no rank, no Path. He thought about holding it out to the clerk at the booth, and he thought about the clerk's face, and he put the card away again without getting any closer to the gate than the top of the hump.

He did not need to try it. He could see where the line was from here.

---

The workshops along the river were all doors.

Every building on the waterfront had its ground floor opened up to the street, shutters folded back or taken down altogether, and inside each one somebody was making or mending something with the light coming in over their shoulder. A cobbler sat in the first doorway with a last between his knees and a mouthful of nails. A woman in the next one had a dozen lanterns hanging from her ceiling, all of them broken in different ways, and was soldering a hinge on a thirteenth with a fine iron that she warmed between her palms. There was a cooper, and a man who made nothing but buckets, and a woman with a loom so large it filled her whole shop and pushed her out to work on the doorstep.

At the end of the row, in a doorway with no sign at all, a man was mending something that Cael could not at first identify. It turned out to be the iron frame of a mangle, the kind used for wringing out laundry, bent in two places as though somebody had dropped a cart on it. The man had it clamped to a bench with blocks of wood under it, and he was bringing it back to true by hand with a long lever and a straightedge, a little at a time. He would lean on the lever, then lay the straightedge along the frame and squint down it, then lean again. He did not hurry and he did not stop.

Cael stood in the street and watched him for nearly half an hour.

It was not very interesting, and he could not stop watching it. It was the bracket on the rig. It was Hesk at the bench with the loupe, and Garrik with his head tilted over a turning hub, and his own forty minutes of filing a sample to find out the truth of a number. The man was not guessing. Every time he leaned on the lever he checked afterward to see what he had done, and when he had done too much, which happened twice, he did not curse or start again; he simply leaned the other way, a little less, and checked again.

Shelves ran up the walls behind him, and the shelves were full of things in the same condition the mangle had been in. A cartwheel with three spokes gone. A clock with no hands. A pump handle snapped in half and wired back together by someone who had not known how. A child's wooden horse with one leg missing and the other three very carefully mended.

On the doorframe, at eye level, somebody had cut four words into the wood with a knife and filled them in with black paint.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE*

That was all. Cael read it twice and could not decide whether it was the beginning of a sentence or the whole of one, and whether it meant the thing or the person who had brought it, and he thought that perhaps the man had cut it that way on purpose so that he would not have to decide either.

The man laid the straightedge along the frame one more time, squinted down it, and grunted. Then he looked up and saw Cael in the street, and looked at him the way he had looked at the frame, checking.

"Something of yours broken?" he said.

"No," Cael said. "I was just watching."

The man nodded, as if that were an answer he got often enough not to mind it, and went back to the lever.

---

The ground began to rise toward the north edge of the district, and as it rose the houses gave way to walls, and the walls had yards behind them, and the yards were full of people hitting things.

That was how it looked at first, from the street: noise and dust and the flat smack of hands on canvas, coming over the walls from every side. The yards were rented by the hour, according to a chalked board at the mouth of the first lane, which also gave the prices and, underneath them, a line that said NO PATH DAMAGE TO WALLS — YOU PAY. Some yards were bare dirt with a post in the middle. Some had a roof on poles at one end for the rain. One had a long row of straw targets along the back wall, most of them burnt black in the middle, and another had nothing in it but a single heavy chain hanging from a beam with a sack of sand at the bottom of it, which a woman was punching with her eyes closed.

Cael walked slowly along the lane and looked through the gates.

He had come here meaning to watch, and he found that he could do more than that now. He could read some of it. A young man in the second yard was throwing strikes at a post, and every strike started in his hips and his feet and arrived at the post a quarter of a second after his shoulder had told it to, and Cael saw the quarter-second each time, and thought of a grey picture on a sheet and Hesk's voice in the dark: *the hips have to go where the weight goes.* A pair of girls in the next yard were drilling a block, one striking and one turning the strike aside with her forearm, over and over, and the one who was blocking had learned to watch the striker's back foot instead of her hand, and the one who was striking had not yet noticed.

Paths were everywhere, if you knew what to look for. In one yard a man stood perfectly still with his feet flat while a friend shoved him, and the dust around his feet shivered once and lay flat, and Cael thought of Joren with a sudden sharpness that surprised him. *Stone.* In another a woman threw a short punch at a plank held up by her partner, and the plank did not break so much as jump out of his hands as if it had been kicked from behind. Force, or something like it. Nobody here wore a Path on a badge, and nobody needed to. They wore it in their shoulders and their feet.

He stopped at the gate of a yard near the top of the lane where a woman was working through a stance sequence alone. It was a slow sequence, six positions, each one held for a breath before she flowed into the next, and in the fourth position her right shoulder came up a finger's width too high. She knew it. She would reach the fourth position, feel it, drop the shoulder, and hold. Then she would go back to the beginning and start again, and the shoulder would come up again, and she would drop it again. Cael watched her do it eleven times. On the twelfth, the shoulder stayed down, and she held the position for a long breath with her eyes shut, and then went on to the fifth position as though nothing had happened.

He found that he had been holding his own shoulder very still.

"You training somewhere?"

The voice came from the far corner of the yard. An old man was there, Cael saw now, in the shade of the wall where he had not noticed him: lean and grey and somewhere past sixty, in a shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, with his feet set in a stance that was nothing like the woman's and nothing like anything else in the lane. He was not moving. He had the stillness of something that had been standing there long enough to become part of the wall, and when he spoke he did not turn his head.

"Not yet," Cael said.

"Watching's not nothing." The old man shifted his weight. It was a very small shift, from one foot to the other, so small that Cael would not have seen it at all a week ago, and he could not tell why the man had done it. "Half of training's watching. The other half's being too stubborn to stop when the watching tells you you're bad at something."

"Is that what she's doing?" Cael nodded at the woman with the shoulder.

"That's what she's doing. Twelve times today. Yesterday it was thirty." The old man's eyes moved to Cael at last. They were pale and steady and not at all old. "You saw the shoulder."

"In the fourth position."

"Most people watching her look at the sixth. That's the one that looks good." He shifted again, the other way. "You didn't look at the sixth. You looked at the one where she's wrong."

"Is that bad?"

"It's not where a bored person looks." Something that might have been a smile went across his face and did not stay. "What you're looking for, I couldn't tell you. That's your business. But you're not looking where most of them look, and that's worth more than you'd think in a lane full of people pretending to be serious."

Cael did not know what to say to that, so he said, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm telling you a fact." The old man settled back into his stance, and the stillness came down over him again like a lid. "Come back when you've got coin for yard time. There's always room for one more person who means it."

---

The other market was where the nut boy had said it would be, south of the food market, and it was not a market.

It was a long, wide lane with a wall down one side, and along the wall, at no particular intervals, people were standing or sitting, and some of them had things laid out in front of them and some of them did not. A man had a blanket of practice weapons, wooden blades and padded sticks and a pair of iron gauntlets with the rivets ground off, all of it worn and mended. A woman had armbands in guild colours, with the guild marks unpicked so carefully that only the needle holes showed. Someone had a crate of ledgers with their covers torn off. Nobody called out prices. People came along the lane in ones and twos, stopped, spoke quietly, and went on, and mostly nothing changed hands that Cael could see.

He walked it once, from end to end, at the pace of someone going somewhere else. He did not want to be seen lingering here before he knew what lingering meant.

Near the far end, behind a bare low table, sat a small old woman.

She was small and very upright, with her hands folded on the bare wood and a grey shawl round her shoulders, and she looked as though she had been sitting in that exact position for years and saw no reason to change it now. A young man was standing in front of the table. Cael slowed a little as he came up behind him, the way you slow when someone ahead of you on a path has stopped. The young man bent down, said something too quietly to hear, and put a small stack of coins on the table between the old woman's hands.

She did not look at the coins. She looked at him. Then she spoke, and Cael counted without meaning to: three sentences, short ones, in a low, even voice, not one word louder than it needed to be.

The young man straightened. All the colour had gone out of his face at once, as if somebody had pulled a plug, and he stood there a moment with his hands hanging. Then he turned and walked away up the lane, fast, without speaking to anyone, and Cael had to step aside to let him pass.

The coins were gone. Cael had not seen her take them.

He walked on past the table without stopping. As he passed, the old woman's eyes moved and followed him for three steps, unhurried and entirely unconcerned, and then went back to the lane, and he felt the attention leave him the way you feel a draught stop when a door is shut.

Three sentences, a small stack of coins, and a man who had thought he wanted to know.

He had nothing to ask her that was worth the price. But he understood, walking on, that he had just watched the whole of a trade with no goods in it, and that this told him more about how the district worked than anything else he had seen since the rise. In Denvash you bought information from the record office, slowly, with forms. Here you bought it from a person, in a minute, and you could not give it back.

---

The lane came out at a crossroads where three narrow streets met at odd angles, so that the open space in the middle was a kind of lopsided triangle, larger than any of the streets that made it. It was late in the afternoon by then. His legs had started reminding him that they had walked two days on the merchant road and a third one today, and he meant only to cross the triangle and find his way back toward Torvin's.

In the middle of it, on a post, there was a board.

It was a slab of weathered wood about waist high, nailed to a post that had been replaced at least once, because the new wood was a different colour from the old. Notices covered it, and notices covered the notices, layer on layer, so thick in places that the bottom ones had fused into the grain and become part of the board. Some were printed. Most were written by hand, in every kind of ink and every kind of handwriting, and pinned with nails, tacks, splinters and in one case a bent fork.

He read the top one because it was at eye level.

*Open card, Thirdweek. No tier floor. Unrated taken. Terms at the Cinder House.*

He read the next one.

*Fenrow, circuit bouts, the usual nights. Betting through the usual hands.*

And the next.

*New blood wanted. Ask for Dava.*

*Rematch: Tamsin and the Ashwood boy. Second time's the real one. Cinder House, Fourthweek.*

*Ledger-verified only. No walk-ins without a vouch.*

None of them said what they were for. That was the first thing he noticed, and it stopped him. Every one of them was an advertisement, and not one of them named the thing it was advertising, as though everyone who needed to read the board already knew, and anyone who did not know was not meant to.

So he read it the way he would have read a load sheet with the headings torn off: by its numbers and its repeated words. *Card. Bout. Rematch. Unrated. Tier floor. Ledger. Vouch.* Some notices had names in them, and some of the names had letters and numbers after them in brackets, the way the registry wrote a classification.

*Renn (Cu 3, Blade) wants work. Any evening. Cinder House.*

*Result, Secondweek: Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), third exchange, called.*

He looked at that one for a long time. *Cu 5, Stone.* Copper, Rank 5, Stone Path. The shape of it was the registry's shape, the same tier and rank and Path that Pellin would have written on a form, and yet the notice had nothing official about it at all. It had been written in pencil on the back of a bill for lamp oil.

*Over.* Somebody had been over somebody else. *Third exchange. Called.* Somebody had called it.

People were fighting each other. That was the thing the whole board was talking around. People were fighting each other on purpose, for money or for something else, in named places on named weeks, and somebody kept a ledger of it, and you could not walk in without someone to vouch for you, and the people who fought had tiers and ranks after their names that looked like the registry's and could not be, because nobody in the registry would have written them in pencil on a lamp-oil bill.

Two men walked behind him while he stood there, and he heard them without turning his head.

"—won't take Brenna again. Not after the first one."

"She will. She'll take it because she can't stand that it went to three."

"That's exactly why she won't. You don't take a fight you only won by being stubborn."

"Dessa? Dessa's never done anything in her life except by being stubborn."

They went on down one of the three streets, still arguing, and Cael did not know either name, and he filed them both: *Dessa. Brenna.* He had learned in the last year that a name filed early was a name you did not have to scramble for later, when it suddenly mattered and you could not work out why it sounded familiar.

He went back to the board and read it again from the top, more slowly.

There were tallies in the margins of some notices, little strokes in groups of five, in different inks. The rematch notice had been circled, once in black and once in red and once in something brown that might have been charcoal, by three different hands. Some notices were so old that the ink had run in the rain and dried again, and they were half covered by new ones, and from the layers he could work out roughly how often the fights happened: often enough that the board was never short of a fresh notice, and not so often that any one notice stopped mattering.

He thought about the man with the sack turning round at the booth on the bridge.

If there was a place in this city that weighed people by what they could be seen to do, and not by what was stamped on their cards, then it was here, behind this board, two streets from where he stood. That might be the most useful thing he had found since he walked out of the side room at Weaver's Row. It might also be the quickest way to get badly hurt by people who had been hitting each other for years while he had been reading about it at a long table under a window.

He suspected it was both. He was beginning to suspect that a great many things were both.

The light had changed while he stood there. It had gone long and gold across the triangle and then started to thin, and the board's shadow lay out across the dirt toward him like a finger. He had not written anything down. He had his notebook open in his hand, and the page was blank, and he had no idea how long he had been standing there.

That was strange enough that he noticed it. He always knew how long he had been standing somewhere.

He shut the notebook and walked back to Torvin's in the dusk, along streets that were not yet his but had begun, here and there, to be streets he had walked before. He would come back to the board in the morning. He told himself it was to see whether any new notices had gone up.

He knew while he was telling himself that it was not the whole reason, and he let himself believe it anyway, because he was tired and his legs hurt and it was easier.

# Chapter 9 — Eleven Minutes

He went back to the board a little before midday, which was later than he had meant to.

He had spent the morning learning the route between Torvin's and the crossroads properly, so that he would not have to think about it again: out of the lane with the gutter, left at the well with the iron cover, through the corner of the food market where the bread woman sold yesterday's loaves on top of today's, and then north up a street that narrowed as it climbed until the triangle opened in front of him. He walked it twice, once there and once back, and the second time he did not have to look at anything, and then he walked it a third time because that was the one he had actually come for.

The crossroads was busier than it had been the afternoon before. Carts were going through toward the yards with sacks of sand and coils of rope and, once, a load of straw targets stacked like loaves. A woman had parked a handcart at the corner where the widest street came in and was grilling meat on skewers over a little iron firebox, and the smoke from it drifted across the whole triangle and made Cael's stomach remind him that he had eaten nothing since the oats. Two small boys were chasing something between the legs of the people going past, something quick and low and brown that might have been a cat or a chicken or simply a rag on a string. Nobody stopped them. Nobody stepped on them either.

There were three new notices on the board.

One was for a bout at a place called Fenrow and was mostly a list of times. One was a lost-and-found, pinned in the middle of the fights as though the board belonged to everybody, which he supposed it did: *Lost, one boot, left, good leather, near the Cinder House yard. No questions.* The third was a result, in the same pencil hand as the lamp-oil bill, and he read it, and then he read the one under it, and then he went back to the top and started over.

He had a theory by now. It was not much of one. It went something like this: there was a place called the Cinder House, and it was where most of the fights on this board happened, or at least where you went to ask about them. There was a ledger, and somebody kept it, and the ledger was what made a result into a result. The letters and numbers after the names were ratings, and the ratings borrowed the registry's tiers and ranks, but the ratings were not the registry's, and he did not yet know whose they were or how they were given.

*Cu 5, Stone.* *Cu 3, Blade.* *Unrated taken.* He could see the shape of a ladder in it, with Copper at the bottom and something higher above, and fighters standing on its rungs. He could not see who had built the ladder, or what it was leaning against.

He was working through the tallies in the margins, trying to decide whether they were bets or results or something else again, when somebody behind him spoke.

"You've been reading that board for eleven minutes."

He turned round.

A girl was standing a few paces behind him with her arms folded. She was a little older than he was, perhaps by a year, with dark hair tied back in a knot that was coming loose on one side, and clothes that had been good once and had since been made to work for a living: a jacket with the cuffs turned back and stitched, trousers patched at both knees with a darker cloth, boots resoled at least once. Her face was interested. It was not friendly, exactly, and not unfriendly. It was the face of somebody looking at a lock to see how it opened.

"I wasn't counting," he said.

"I know. I was." She tipped her head at the board. "Somebody had to."

"Why?"

"Because it's my board and I like to know who's reading it." She came a step closer and looked past him at the notices, then back at him. "Most people take about as long as one notice to decide whether this is for them. Either they walk off, because it isn't, or they go and ask somebody, because it is. You've done neither. "It's a puzzle."

"It's a board."

"It's a board that advertises something without ever saying what." He pointed at the nearest notice, the one about the open card. "Nobody says what a card is. Nobody says what's being called, or who calls it, or what a vouch is. Every notice assumes you already know."

"That's because it's not for people who don't." She said it lightly, but she was watching him the whole time she said it, the way he watched people, and he found that he did not like it as much as he liked doing it. "If you have to have it explained, you're not ready to read it."

"Then how does anyone ever get ready?"

The girl's mouth moved at one corner. "They stand in front of it for eleven minutes until somebody comes along and takes pity on them."

"Is that what you're doing?"

"Pity?" She seemed to give it honest thought. "No. Curiosity. Pity costs me something. Curiosity's free." She unfolded her arms and held out a hand, which was more formal than anything else about her suggested she would be. "Lira."

"Cael." He shook it. Her palm was hard along the edge and across the base of the fingers, a ridge of callus he could feel through his own, and her grip was short and firm and let go before he had decided what to do with it.

"Just Cael."

It had been Caelen Hesk-ward on every form he had ever filled in, and in Pellin's office, and on the card in his coat. He had not decided to drop it. "Well, Just Cael," she said, "you're new. Not new to the district. New. You still walk like you think the ground's going to be a particular shape, and every time it isn't, you notice."

"Painfully. It took me a month to stop doing it." She leaned a shoulder against the post, beside the notice about the lost boot, as though she had leaned in exactly that spot a great many times. "That's not an insult, by the way. It's a professional observation."

"From what profession?"

"Several. Circuit fighter. Occasional problem." She ticked them off on her fingers. "And professional evaluator of new arrivals. Unpaid. I do it for the love of the thing."

He found that he wanted to laugh, and he did not quite, and she saw him not quite do it, and looked pleased.

"How long have you been here?" he asked.

"Four months." The lightness did not go out of her voice, but something under it slowed down and started choosing its words, as though the next part of the conversation had a floor that might not hold everywhere. "I was at an academy. Now I'm not."

"What happened?"

"Officially? Insubordination." She looked at the board instead of at him. "Unofficially, I was right in front of witnesses."

"About what?"

"Wind Path students learn a deflection in the second year," she said. "Three parts. You catch the strike here." She tapped the outside of her forearm. "You reset. Then you send it away." "And you didn't do it that way."

"I found a way to skip the middle. You don't reset. You take what the first part gave you and you spend it on the third, straight through." Her arm went from catch to send in one motion, without a pause in it, so smooth that he could not see where the middle should have been. "It's faster. It costs less. It works better against somebody who means it, because somebody who means it doesn't stand there and wait while you reset."

"So you did it in an exam."

"In my practical. Against a fourth-year." She put her arm down. "It worked. He went over. The examiner failed me for it."

"Why?"

"She said I'd got lucky. She said the sequence was taught as taught for reasons I wasn't senior enough to understand, and that performing an unapproved variant in a formal assessment showed poor judgement, whether it worked or not." Lira said all this in a flat, even voice, as though she had said it to herself so many times that the edges had worn off it. "So I asked if I could do it again, against somebody else, to prove it wasn't luck."

"What did she say?"

"She said that wasn't the point." For the first time something sharpened in her face. "So I asked her what the point was. If it wasn't whether the thing worked, what was the exam actually testing? And she didn't have an answer to that. She had a desk instead, and the desk won."

Cael thought about Pellin's office, the circle of pale wood, the brass plate that lit eleven seconds late. He thought about a form drawn from the back of a drawer, filled in with small quick strokes, and how nobody in that room had asked him anything, because there had not been anything to ask; the question had already been answered by someone who was not in the room.

"It wasn't testing whether you could do it," he said slowly. "It was testing whether you'd do it the way you were told."

Lira looked at him sharply.

"Yes," she said, after a moment. "That's exactly what it was testing. It took me three weeks to work that out, and I was angry the whole three weeks. You got there in about a minute."

"I've had practice being told what I am."

He had not meant to say that. It came out before he had looked at it, and it hung there between them, and he watched her decide whether to pick it up.

She did not pick it up. She set it aside instead, carefully, where they could both see it, and went on as if he had said something else.

"So now I'm here," she said. "Copper, Rank 2, Wind Path. Fast, not strong. I don't win by taking the hit. I win by not being where it lands." She said it the way Hesk described a gauge, practical and unromantic, a list of what the tool did and what it did not. "And I fight on that board. Which brings us back round to you and your eleven minutes. Are you reading it because you want to fight on it, or because it's the only puzzle in sight?"

He considered the true answer, which was that he did not know, and found that the true answer was also the only one that would be of any use.

"I don't know what I'd be fighting as," he said. "I don't have a declaration."

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. It was the first thing he had said that she had not been ready for. "Everybody has a declaration. Even the people with bad draws have something. A Rank 1, a passive, a thing you can do slightly better than you could before."

"I don't."

"Copper Rank 0, then. People say that, sometimes, as a joke, when somebody's first declaration is useless—"

"I don't have a tier," Cael said. "Or a rank. I've got a classification. The registry doesn't have a category for it."

Lira went quiet.

It was a different quiet from the ones before, which had been the quiet of somebody choosing words. This was the quiet of somebody who had stopped choosing anything and was simply looking. She was not leaning on the post any more. She was standing square to him, and she was looking at him the way he imagined she would look at an opponent who had just done something nobody had taught her to expect.

"I've not heard of that," she said. "And I thought I'd heard everything. This district gathers odd people the way a drain gathers hair. There's a man down by the river who says his Path only works underwater, and a woman in the yards who swears hers only switches on when she's lying, which means she's either the most useless person in Ardenmere or the most dangerous, and nobody's worked out which. I haven't heard of a classification with no tier at all."

Cael had decided something on the road east, though he had not known at the time that he was deciding it. He had decided it at the gate in Denvash, watching the guard's face draw back from his card, and again at the waystation, where the keeper did not look at his face at all. He had decided that the word was going to be said, sooner or later, to every person who mattered, and that the only part of it he could still choose was the order in which things happened. He could let people meet the word first and then meet him, or he could let them meet him first.

Nobody here had met the word yet. Lira had met him.

"[SHATTERED]," he said.

He watched her face when it landed, because her face was information.

It did not draw back. It did not soften into something careful, the way the clerk's had at the record office. She did not look away, and she did not look harder, either, in the way people look harder at something they have decided to be brave about. She simply took it in. He could see her taking it in, turning it over, setting it beside the other things she knew and finding that it did not fit with any of them, and then, instead of forcing it to fit, putting it down by itself where she could look at it.

"Huh," Lira said.

That was all, for a moment. Then: "I don't actually know what that means."

"Neither do I. Mostly."

"Does anyone?"

"The registry says it's deprecated."

"That's not knowing. That's being bored of the question." She frowned at nothing, at the air between them. "So nobody knows what it does. Not you, not the registry, not whatever clerk wrote it down."

"No."

"Then it's either the most interesting thing anybody's told me in four months," said Lira, "or it's the most elaborate way I've ever heard of avoiding saying whether you can fight." The corner of her mouth went up. "Possibly both."

"Possibly both," Cael agreed, and found that he meant it.

"Can you fight?" she asked.

"I don't know. I've never hit anybody on purpose."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what you'd need to know."

"I don't see the difference."

"You will." She stopped at his left shoulder and looked at the way he was standing. "Everyone worth watching in that yard began before they noticed they'd begun. Somewhere back there, you'll have moved some way without deciding to. Caught something. Got out of the way of something. Done a thing faster than you had any business doing it. Most people don't notice when it happens to them." She went on round to the front of him again. "I notice when it happens to other people. It's one of my few useful talents."

Across the triangle a cart came round the corner from the widest street, too fast.

It was a flat dray loaded with barrels, and the driver was half turned on his bench shouting something back at a man in a doorway, and the horse had taken the corner wider than the driver meant it to, and the near wheel was going to hit the skewer woman's handcart square in the side. Cael saw it all a beat before it happened: the line of the wheel, the handcart, the woman bent over her firebox with her back to it, turning skewers. He had started forward before he knew he had started, a step and a half, and his hand had come up, uselessly, with ten paces of open ground between it and anything it could reach.

Lira was already there.

He did not see her cross the ground. He saw her start, a single low step with her weight going forward into it, and then the next thing he saw clearly was her hand on the skewer woman's shoulder and the skewer woman coming backward off her feet into Lira's arm, and the dray going past through the space where the woman had been bent over her fire. The near wheel caught the corner of the handcart and spun it a quarter-turn on its own wheels, and the firebox rocked and settled, and a skewer fell into the dirt. The driver shouted an apology over his shoulder without slowing down, and nobody answered him.

The skewer woman said something short and heartfelt to the back of the departing dray, then picked up the fallen skewer, blew on it, looked at it, and put it back on the grill.

Lira let go of her shoulder and walked back across the triangle to the board, unhurried, as though she had gone over to ask the price of a skewer and decided against it.

"That," she said, "is what I mean."

"You didn't look like you were trying."

"I wasn't. That's the whole point." She rolled her right shoulder once, the way you roll a shoulder after carrying something, though she had carried nothing. "People think Wind makes you fast like a thrown stone's fast. It doesn't. Not at Copper. Not at my rank, anyway. At Copper it's more like the world gets slightly more negotiable round you, for about a second at a time. The distance between here and there gets a little less firm about how far it is." She shrugged. "I didn't decide to go. My feet had an opinion about that cart before I'd got round to having one."

"How often does that happen?"

"More, now. When I was new, I used to try to catch it happening and make it happen again on purpose, and the more I tried the less it did." She looked at the cart, which had gone on down the hill and was now some other street's problem. "My old instructor had a word for it. Not the examiner. Someone better, before her. She called it the gap."

"The gap between what?"

"Between the move you were taught and the move your body makes when there's no time to remember the lesson." Lira said it with a kind of fondness, the way people speak about a teacher they have stopped needing and not stopped missing. "Most people spend their whole time trying to close it. Make the trained thing and the fast thing the same, every time, no daylight. I thought that was the goal too, when I was at the academy." She shook her head. "I don't think so any more. I think the gap is where the interesting part happens. You just have to get through it enough times to see what's in it."

---

Cael was not listening to her any more, not entirely.

*Somewhere back there, you'll have moved some way without deciding to.*

His hand had gone to the strap of his bag. He had not brought the bag, he realized; it was on his cot under the window at Torvin's. His hand had gone to the place on his hip where the strap would have been, where the row of Yeni's small tight stitches lay across a pale scuffed band.

He was standing in long grass at the bottom of a hedge with a turnip at his feet.

He had been walking in the middle of the road with his eyes on the ruts, counting milestones and getting the arithmetic wrong, and then he had been on the verge, and he had not decided to be. His left foot had gone out onto the grass and his weight had followed it. Then the cart had come round the bend behind him, downhill, almost silent on the soft dust, and gone through the place where he had been walking so closely that the wheel hub brushed his strap.

He had told the driver he had heard it coming. He had told himself the same thing, sitting on the bench beside the man with the turnip in his lap. He had believed it, mostly, the way you believe a figure you have not traced because it is printed and looks like a figure.

But he had not heard it. He had heard nothing at all until it was past. He knew that now, standing at the board with his hand on a strap that was not there, as clearly as he had known it at the time and had then decided not to know it.

He took his hand away from his hip.

Lira was watching him. She had stopped talking somewhere in the middle of it, and he did not know when, and she was looking at his face and then at his hand and then at his face again, and he saw her decide not to ask.

He was grateful for that. He did not have anything to say yet that would have been true all the way down, and he did not want to hand her something that was only true halfway.

"I don't know if it's happened yet," he said. That was nearly honest. "I'd like to find out."

"That's a better answer than most people give me." She studied him for a moment longer, and he had the odd feeling of being added up. "Most people who don't know what they're made of do one of two things. She tilted her head. "You're doing a third thing. You're standing in front of the board trying to understand it first. Almost nobody does that, because it means admitting you don't already know."

"Is that rare?"

"Rarer than talent," said Lira. "Where I come from."

"Where do you come from?"

"Not here."

She said it lightly and pleasantly and finally, the way you close a drawer that sticks, with a little shove at the end so it stays shut. Cael heard the shove and understood it, because he had been doing the same thing all morning with a turnip cart.

He did not ask again.

Lira looked at the sun, which was past its height, and seemed to make up her mind about something. "Here's what I'll do," she said. "I train most mornings in the yard behind the Cinder House, before anyone's there. Come and watch. Don't fight. You don't know what you're working with yet, and if you walk into a circle blind, the only thing you learn is what the ground tastes like. Watch first." She hitched the canvas roll higher on her back. "See what your body does when it isn't being asked."

"Why would you do that?"

She shrugged, an easy, loose movement of the shoulders that looked as though it cost her nothing. He was fairly sure it had cost her something. "Because nothing this interesting has stood at that board since I got here, and I get bored, and in this district people who get bored do stupid things to entertain themselves. You're cheaper than most of the alternatives." She considered. "And because a word the registry can't file is exactly my kind of problem. I like the ones where everyone else has already decided there's no answer."

"That isn't really an answer either."

"No," Lira agreed, with no sign at all of being embarrassed about it. "You'll get a better one if you're still here in a month." She started across the triangle, then turned and walked backward for three steps, which she did without looking behind her and without hitting anything. "Cinder House. Tomorrow. Before the market gets loud. If you're late, I won't wait, and I'll tell everyone you were frightened."

"I won't be late."

"I know," Lira said, and turned round again, and was gone into the widest street before he had decided whether that was a compliment.

---

He stood at the board for a while after she had gone, without reading it.

The crossroads had filled up while they talked. The skewer woman had a queue now, as though nearly being hit by a dray had been good for trade. A man on the far side of the triangle was swinging a pair of weighted cords on the ends of his fingers, round and round and over his head, faster and faster, the metal caps at their ends catching the sun at the top of every turn, and a small crowd had gathered round him at a respectful distance. It was not a Path, Cael was fairly sure. It was only a thing practised for so many years that it had started to look like one, and the crowd made the low noise people make in front of skill they do not understand but recognize anyway.

He thought about the word, and saying it.

He had said it out loud to a stranger for the first time in his life, and it had cost him something and paid him something, in nearly equal amounts. In Denvash the word had gone ahead of him. By the second morning people had known it before he was close enough to see them knowing it, and they had arranged their faces in advance: the courier's eyes sliding away, Garrik's hand turning the wheel, the gate guard's face drawing back from the card. He had spent three days being met by the word with himself trailing behind it.

This time he had arrived first. He had stood here and been read for eleven minutes by someone who did not know anything about him except how he read a board, and she had made up her mind about that much, and only then had he handed her the one fact that could have changed all of it. It had not changed all of it. It had changed something, but not that.

It was a very small thing to have, he thought: not power, and not safety, only the order of things. But it was the first advantage of any kind he had held since he stepped out of the circle, and he had used it on purpose, and it had worked.

He took out the notebook, found a clean page, and wrote.

*Lira. Copper Rank 2, Wind Path. Circuit fighter, she says. Fifteen, maybe. Four months in the district. Thrown out of an academy for doing a deflection better than the book. Says the book was testing obedience, not the deflection. She's right.*

*She pulled a woman out from under a cart before I'd finished seeing it. At Copper, she says, Wind makes the world "slightly more negotiable" for about a second.*

*"The gap": the move you were taught, against the move your body makes when there's no time to remember the lesson.*

*Offered to let me watch her train. Cinder House yard, mornings.*

He stopped, with the pencil touching the page. Then he wrote the last line, which was not information, and which he put in the notebook anyway, because the notebook was where he kept the things he believed before he was ready to say them to anybody.

*First person outside Denvash who's looked at [SHATTERED] like a question instead of a verdict.*

---

That night at Torvin's, after the lamps were out and the brothers had gone to sleep in the middle of an argument about a rope, he lay on his cot under the window with the notebook on his chest and the pencil in his hand, and could not see the page, and did not need to.

He had decided, in the dip between the hedges, that a cart going past close was not information. He had decided it again at the waystation, holding the pencil over his letter to Hesk, and he had left the cart out of the letter so that Hesk would not have to carry it, the way Hesk had once decided not to make a thing real before it was. He had been angry at Hesk for that, nearly a week ago now, in a room with a crooked tree in the window.

*Write everything down,* Hesk had said at the kitchen table, *even the things that don't seem to matter. You won't know which ones matter until later.*

He turned to the back of the notebook, past the archive entry and the gate entry, and wrote in the dark, slowly, feeling for the lines with the edge of his hand.

*Day three. The road, the dip between the hedges, after midday. I was walking in the middle of the road. Then I was on the verge. Then a cart came round the bend behind me, fast, downhill. The hub touched my bag strap.*

*I told the carter I heard it coming. I don't think I did.*

He stopped there. There was more he could have written, and some of it was waiting to be written, pressing at the end of the pencil. He could have written *Lira says*, or *Hesk wrote*, or the line from the leather book about the body learning before the mind. He could have drawn an arrow from this page to something else and written a word beside the arrow.

He did not. He did not know what the word would be. He had a fact now, written down, true as far as it went, and a fact was a thing you could build on later, if it held. You did not have to build on it tonight.

He closed the notebook and put it under his pillow, and listened to the wheezing man's breath catch and whistle and go on, and to the river behind the house, which he could hear now, low and patient, a sound he had not known was there the night before.

# Chapter 10 — The Rules

The house on Fen Street had made the same sounds for more than twenty years, and on the evening Hesk came back from the depot alone, it went on making them as if nothing had happened.

He let himself in by the front door and stood in the hall with his coat still on. The clock over the stove ticked. The second floorboard from the kitchen door creaked when he crossed it, as it always had, and he found that he had stepped on it on purpose, to hear it, and was annoyed with himself. The cupboard by the stove had to be closed with two fingers or it rattled for a minute. He closed it with two fingers. It did not rattle.

Cael's door was open a hand's width.

He had left it that way the night before, out of a habit that was ten years out of date, and in the morning Cael had not closed it either. Hesk stood in the hall and looked at the narrow dark gap between the door and the frame, and at the edge of the bare bed he could see through it, and he did not close it.

He took his coat off and hung it on the peg. Then he went through to the workshop, because the workshop was where he had always gone when the house was too quiet, and lit the lamp over the bench and sat down in front of a half-built level for the survey office. He picked up a file and put it down again.

On the bench, under the lamp, was the drawer marked *Springs, do not use.* He pulled it out an inch. Inside were the springs that had taught him something, a little tangle of them in the corner, and beside them a space he had cleared that morning without thinking about why, a space about the size of a small bent bracket.

He shut the drawer.

There were several things in his life he had prepared for over a long time and still not been ready for when they came. He had expected this to be one of them, and he had been right, and being right did not help as much as it should have.

---

He went to Ressa's in the morning because he always went to Ressa's in the morning.

The side door was open with the ovens going, and the smell came half a block to meet him, and he followed it as he had followed it for more than twenty years, since before there was a boy to follow it with him. Ressa was behind the counter with her sleeves pushed up. Two loaves were already wrapped and waiting at the end of the counter, side by side, as they had been waiting every morning for as long as Cael had been old enough to eat one.

Ressa saw them at the same moment Hesk did. Her hands, which had been reaching for his coins, stopped.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. It was a small thing, two loaves in brown paper, and it stood on the counter between them like a third person.

"One," Hesk said.

"One." Ressa unwrapped the second loaf without a word and put it back on the rack, and the paper she folded and set under the counter to use again. Then she took his coins for the first loaf, and Hesk put down more, for the loaf she had put in Cael's bag on the morning he left.

She pushed the extra coins back across the counter with one floury finger.

"That one was his," she said.

"It came out of your oven."

"It went into his bag. That makes it his." She did not look up at him. She had a way of not looking up that was not at all like Garrik's; it was the way a woman avoids looking at a man so that he can keep his face. "You'll want to know if he ate it."

"He'll have eaten it on a wall," said Hesk. "Somewhere about the first milestone. He won't have been able to wait."

Ressa nodded, and something eased at the corners of her mouth, and she gave him his loaf, and he took it home and ate half of it standing at the kitchen table in the sideways light, which came in at its usual angle and lay across the table for its usual twenty minutes and did not seem to know that anything had changed.

At about ten there was a knock at the workshop door, the side door that opened onto the lane.

It was Joren. He stood in the lane in his wool coat with his feet planted flat and his hands in his pockets, and Hesk saw at once that he was keeping his heels down, and that he was keeping them down on purpose, because his whole body leaned very slightly forward with the effort of it. He had a folded paper in one hand. It had been folded small and opened and folded again so many times that the creases had gone soft.

"Sir," Joren said. "Has he written?"

"Not yet. He'll get to Ardenmere tonight, if the road's kind. He'll need somewhere to live before he's got an address to write from." Hesk looked at the paper. "And then it's a week on the carrier, at least."

"A week." Joren took that in, and Hesk watched him decide to be patient about it. It was not something Joren had ever been good at, and Hesk watched him do it anyway, which was more than most people managed at fourteen or at fifty. "I found this in the wall. Our wall, by the canal. He put it in the gap."

"I know. I was there."

"He said he'd come by after." Joren's voice did not wobble, quite. "He didn't. He wrote that it was wrong of him. It wasn't—" He stopped. "I wasn't angry. I want him to know I wasn't angry. I just stood there. I stood there yesterday too, the whole hour, and he wasn't there either, because he'd gone, and I didn't know."

Hesk said nothing, because there was nothing he could say that would make Joren's two mornings in front of a wall any shorter.

"When you write to him," Joren said, "will you tell him I read it standing up? Flat-footed. The whole time." He lifted one boot an inch and put it down again, deliberately, flat. "Tell him I'm working on it."

"I'll tell him."

Joren nodded, and folded the paper once more along its soft creases, and put it inside his coat. Then he walked off along the lane toward the canal with his heels down, slowly, as if the ground had become something he had to think about, and Hesk stood in the doorway and watched him until he turned the corner.

The letter from the guild came on the second morning after the depot, by the same grey-and-brass courier, with the same satchel worn pale along the strap.

It was shorter than the registry notice and more polite, which made it worse. It said that the guild had received notification from the Compact Registry under Statute 14, Section 9, concerning the residence at 14 Fen Street, Outer District, held under the standing of registration 22-1190-H. It said that in accordance with the guild's obligations the standing of that member was now under review. It asked the member to submit, within thirty days, a written account of present residential arrangements at that address, and it said that the reviewing officer would be in touch in due course regarding any further requirements. It was signed with a name Hesk did not know, above a title he did, and below the signature someone had written *Routine* in a different ink and a different hand, as if to be kind.

He read it twice at the kitchen table. Then he went into the workshop and took out a sheet of good paper and his steel pen, and wrote the account.

He had thought it would be easy. It was one sentence of fact. He had written ten thousand sentences of fact in his life, load figures and tolerances and the readings of dials, and every one of them had been easier than this one.

*The person named in the registry notice of the second day no longer resides at this address.*

He looked at it for a long time. It was true. It had been true for two days. It was exactly the sentence the review wanted, and it was exactly the sentence the notice had been built to make him write, and he had known that when he read the notice at this table with Cael beside him and had said so out loud: *It's easier to make a family choose.* He had chosen. He had walked Cael to the depot and watched him go down the merchant road until he was a shape, and this sentence was the receipt.

He added a second sentence, because the first one looked too much like an apology.

*He left on the morning of the third day for another city, where he is lawfully resident, and he goes with my full support.*

That was also true. The review did not need it. He left it in.

He sealed the account and set it by the door to go to the guild house, and then he put the guild's letter not in a drawer, where he would not have to look at it, but on the shelf above the bench, propped against the clock, where he would see it every time he lifted his head.

He did not know yet what the review would come to. He thought it would probably come to nothing, slowly, the way the Compact made most things come to nothing, with a great many forms. He thought it might not. He had decided long ago that the risk was his, and he was not going to start pretending otherwise now just because the risk had arrived with a signature on it.

He stood in the hall with his coat half on and his hand on the door, and the clock over the stove said ten past seven, and he thought about the narrow green door at the side of the hall, and the two-and-one knock, and Garrik's hand on a turning wheel three mornings ago, keeping its rhythm while Hesk and the boy walked past. He thought about sitting in a folding chair in the dark with Garrik four seats away and neither of them saying anything for two reels.

The woman nodded slowly. "That's a long way for a boy who watches like that," she said. "They'll not know what they've got." She took a biscuit out of the tin and put it into his hand, though he had one already. "You tell him we said so. Give him that."

He did not turn round. He stood with the loose end of the film in one hand and the other hand on the reel, and he did not move, and Hesk could see from the set of his shoulders that he had heard every word.

The lamps went down. The second reel ran, clean, from first frame to last.

Afterward, when the others had gone, Garrik stayed at the projector and wound the film back with the little crank, round and round, much more slowly than it needed. Hesk stood by the door with his coat on and waited.

"I didn't look up," Garrik said to the reel.

"I know."

"I meant to. I had the wheel up and I heard you coming down the street and I thought, *I'll look up when they're level,* and then you were level, and I didn't." The crank went round. Hesk said nothing.

"When my wife died," Garrik said, "I couldn't look at anybody for a month. Not at you. Not at the girl from the bakery. I used to cross the street. I thought I'd got past it." He stopped cranking. "Turns out I'd only got past it for things I'd already done once."

Garrik looked round at last, and for the first time all evening he looked Hesk in the face, and his own face was red above the square white beard and not at all composed.

"When you write," he said. "Tell him the arm's running clean. Tell him it's his washer, and I've not touched it, and I'm not going to." He turned back to the reel. "That's all. Tell him that."

Hesk sat down at the kitchen table at half past ten with the lamp lit and a clean sheet of paper and the steel pen.

He wrote *Cael*, and stopped, and looked at it.

He had no address. That was the first thing. He did not know where the boy was sleeping or whether he was sleeping at all, and any letter he wrote tonight would sit on this table for a week, at least, until the carrier's runner brought one up Fen Street with a return direction on it in a careful, slanting hand. He was writing to nowhere. He knew that perfectly well and he went on anyway, because Joren had stood in front of a wall for an hour with a folded paper and Garrik had wound a reel back twice as slowly as it needed to go, and Hesk was not going to be the only one on Fen Street with nothing in his hands.

*Cael,*

*You'll have got there by now. I don't know where you are. I'm writing this anyway and I'll send it when I have somewhere to send it.*

He crossed it out. It sounded like a man feeling sorry for himself.

He took another sheet.

*Cael,*

*Ressa had two loaves wrapped the morning after. She put one back. She wouldn't take money for the one she put in your bag; she says it was yours. I told her you'd have eaten it on a wall somewhere about the first milestone. Tell me if I was right.*

That was better. He went on.

He stopped there, with the pen lifted.

The guild's letter was on the shelf in the workshop, propped against the clock. He could see it in his mind as clearly as if it were on the table in front of him: the polite paragraphs, the stranger's signature, the word *Routine* in another hand.

He knew exactly what he wanted to do with it. He wanted to leave it out. He wanted to leave it on the shelf and write to Cael about bread and washers and Joren's heels, and say nothing about a review that might come to nothing, because if it came to nothing then the boy would never have had to carry it, and if it came to something there would be time enough then. *Don't make a thing real before it is.* He had lived by that sentence for years. It's routine, they say, and slow, and it will most likely come to nothing. I'll tell you how it goes, as it goes. He read it back. It did not make the review any smaller. It made him feel, for the first time in six days, as though he were not standing in the house on his own.

There were other things he did not write. There were things he had not written in thirty-two years, and he did not begin them tonight, and he did not let himself think about them any further than the edge of the page.

He did not seal the letter. He laid it flat on the table under the clock and set the pen beside it, so that he could add to it tomorrow, and the next day, and every day until the carrier's runner came up Fen Street. Then he turned out the lamp and went to bed, past the door that stood open a hand's width, and left it open.

Cael was at the Cinder House before the sun had cleared the roofs.

It was a public house on a corner two streets below the yards, a squat stone building with a sign over the door that had once been a painting of a hearth with a fire in it. Weather had taken most of the paint, so that what was left was a black square with some orange flecks round the edges, more ember than fire, which he supposed was the point. The shutters were up and the chairs inside were stacked on the tables. The whole building had the stillness of a place that only came alive at certain hours and regarded the rest of the day as something to be waited out.

The yard was round the side, through a gate in a wall. He found it by following the sound.

It was a square of packed dirt about thirty paces across, with high walls on three sides and the back of the public house on the fourth. The ground had been swept and watered so that it would not rise in dust, and in the middle of it, worn into the dirt, was a circle. It was not painted or chalked. It was a shallow groove, a hand deep, that years of feet had walked into the ground the way water walks a channel into stone, and it was very slightly out of round, longer one way than the other, as though the fighters who made it had all preferred the same side. Lanterns hung on hooks along three of the walls, unlit. A few crows were working the edges of the yard where last night's crowd had dropped things.

Lira was in the far corner with a post.

It was a thick wooden post sunk into the ground and wrapped in canvas gone grey with use, and she was striking it. Short strikes, very controlled, with the edge of the hand and the forearm and once or twice the heel of the palm, and between the strikes her feet moved in small steps that never seemed to go anywhere and never stopped. It looked less like fighting than like a long argument with the ground that she was winning on points.

She did not stop when he came in. She said, between one strike and the next, "You're on time."

"You said not to be late."

"People say a lot of things." Strike. "Mostly nobody listens." Strike, step, strike. "I'm mildly impressed. Sit. Watch. I'll talk while I do this. Ask things when you want to; you'll learn it faster that way than if I make a speech."

He sat on an upturned crate by the wall and took out the notebook.

Lira stopped, with her hand flat against the post, and looked at him.

"You're going to take notes," she said, "on me hitting a post."

"Is that a problem?"

"No. It's the most you thing I've seen you do, and I've known you a day." She laughed, a short, real laugh that surprised her as much as him, and went back to the post. "All right. The circuit. First thing. You've been reading that board like the circuit's a place. It isn't."

"Then what is it?"

"It's a habit." She struck the post, low, and stepped round it. "A few hundred people in this district all decided, one at a time, that they wanted to find out what they could actually do. Not what a guild says they can do. Not what an evaluation says. What they can do against somebody who means it. And there was nowhere official to find that out, because officially, people like us aren't supposed to want to. So they started finding out anyway. In yards like this. And they kept doing it, long enough that the habit grew rules."

"Who made the rules?"

"Nobody." Strike, strike. "That's the part people from outside never believe. Nobody sat down and wrote them. They just—happened. The way a path happens across a field because everybody's walking the same way."

"Like that." He pointed his pencil at the groove in the ground.

Lira followed the pencil and looked at the circle as though she had not seen it in a while. "Yes," she said. "Exactly like that."

---

She worked through the rules while she worked the post, and he wrote them down.

A fight in the circuit was called a bout, and a bout was made of exchanges, not rounds. An exchange began when the fighters came off their marks and ended when they broke apart, and it could last two seconds or two minutes. Bouts were arranged between the fighters, or between whoever spoke for them, and the terms were agreed in advance. And every bout had a Ledger-keeper.

"Ledger-keeper," Cael said.

"The person who writes it down." Lira stopped and wiped her palms on her trousers. "Every venue has one. At the Cinder House it's Vell. She keeps the book. She calls the bouts. She decides when it's over. Everybody on both sides of the rope has to trust her to write down what happened, and nothing else, because the whole circuit is only worth anything as long as the book is honest. If the book lies once, nobody believes it again, and then there's no circuit, there's just people hitting each other in yards." She shrugged. "Which there'd be anyway. But it wouldn't count."

"And if somebody cheats?"

"Then that venue stops getting fighters. Nobody comes back. That's it. That's the whole of the punishment." She went back to the post. "No Compact. No guild. No warden. Just everybody deciding at once not to come back."

"Is there a rule against killing?"

Lira hit the post once, hard, and let her hand stay against it.

"There isn't a *rule*," she said. "It isn't written down anywhere. There's nowhere to write it. But everybody acts as if there's one, and everybody's afraid of what happens to anyone who forgets, so it works about as well as a written rule would. Better, maybe." She looked at him over her shoulder. "Paths are open. Whatever yours does, you can use it. That's half of why people come here instead of fighting in a guild hall with someone in a white coat counting. But you don't kill anyone, and you don't keep going after Vell's called it, and you don't embarrass whoever vouched for you."

"What happens if you do keep going?"

"There was a man," Lira said, "before my time. I only know it from people telling it. Bronze in the circuit, Ash Path, very good, very angry. Twice he kept going after the call. Vell warned him twice. The third time he put somebody down so badly they had to send across the river for a healer, which costs more than most people here earn in a year, and gets noticed by people we'd rather didn't notice." She went back to the post. "No yard in Ardenmere would have him after that. Not one. Last anyone heard, he was fighting under another name three towns over, for a tenth of what he used to draw here. It takes a while for a thing like that to travel. But it travels."

Cael wrote *Ash Path, Bronze. Three calls. Gone.* He looked at it, and added: *Vell called it.*

"The ratings," he said. "On the board. The letters after the names."

"Ah. The ratings." Lira went into a quicker combination, three strikes and a step, three strikes and a step, and talked in the gaps. "Circuit ratings borrow the registry's words, Copper, Iron, Bronze, Rank 1 to 10, because everybody already knows them. But they're not the registry's. The circuit doesn't care what your Arbiter said about you. It cares what Vell's seen you do."

"So your circuit rating could be different from your registry tier."

"It usually is. That's why half the people here are here. Guild rejects. People who failed an evaluation once and couldn't pay for the next one. People who've got a good Path in a bad family. People who never had a guild at all." Strike, step. "Your registry tier's a word somebody stamped on you at fourteen. Your circuit rating's a running total of what you've done since."

"And if you've done nothing?"

"Then you're unrated. Everybody starts unrated." She glanced at him. "Nobody stays unrated long. Either you climb, or you stop coming."

"How does Vell decide the rating? Is there a formula?"

Lira laughed again, and this time it was not a surprised laugh. "If there is, she's never shown it to anyone. As far as anyone can tell the formula is, *I've watched a great many fights and I have opinions.*" She stopped and picked up a water skin from the foot of the post. "You win convincingly, against people who are rated themselves, often enough, and your rating goes up. You lose badly often enough and it doesn't. But it's not only wins. How you lose matters. Sometimes more."

"How does it matter?"

"Somebody who gets carried out of the circle because they wouldn't concede, and somebody who reads it right and concedes at the right moment and walks out. Which one gets offered more bouts?"

He thought about it. "The one who walks out."

"Most people from outside say the other one." She drank, and held out the skin to him, and he shook his head. "The circuit doesn't pay for stubborn. It pays for interesting to watch and hard to read. That's not the same as strongest, though strongest doesn't hurt."

"What's your rating?"

"Higher than my registry rank," said Lira. "Lower than I think it should be." She capped the skin. "Vell and I are in a long, polite disagreement about it."

"Who's winning?"

"Vell. She's got the book." Lira set the skin down. "I lost my first four bouts here. Four. Some of them badly. I was better trained than nearly everyone I fought, and I lost anyway, because doing a technique in an academy hall and doing it against somebody who's trying to put you in the dirt are not the same thing at all, and the only way anybody learns that is with their face."

"What changed?"

"I stopped fighting the way they taught me and started fighting the way that worked for what I've got." She shrugged. "It sounds obvious when you say it. It took four losses before I believed it." She looked at him for a moment with her head on one side. "You might have it easier there, you know. You haven't got an academy's way to unlearn. Whatever you build, you'll be building it from the ground. That's slower at the start. It might be faster after."

"That's a strange sort of advantage."

"Most of the good ones are."

---

There was one more name before the morning was out, and Lira said it with feeling.

"Marrow," she said. She had gone back to the post, and she hit it a little harder as she said the name, as though it had been his face. "You'll see him this afternoon. Big man, very clean hands, a slate on a string round his neck. He runs the book."

"The ledger?"

"No. The *book.* The bets." Strike. "Vell's book says what happened. Marrow's book says what people think's going to happen, and how much they'll pay to be right. He sets the odds. And he shaves them."

"Shaves them how?"

"A little. Never enough to call cheating. Just a hair the wrong side of fair, every time, on every bout, so that over a season it adds up to a nice house across the river for Marrow and a lot of people wondering why they never quite win." She stopped and pointed a finger at Cael. "Do not ever bet with Marrow."

"I haven't got any money to bet with."

"Good. Stay poor. It's safer." She went back to her combinations, and then, between one strike and the next, as though it were nothing, she added: "He had me at five to one against, my second month. Against Renn."

"Who won?"

Lira hit the post once more and let the strike sit there, her palm flat on the canvas.

"Marrow," she said.

By the time the sun was high enough to reach the bottom of the yard, she had stopped, and sat on the crate beside his, and they had gone through the rest of it: how a bout began (Vell's word), how it ended (Vell's word again, or one fighter's own, or the plain fact of somebody unable to get up), what you owed to the table afterward (you reported, win or lose, and stood there until she was finished with you), and what a vouch was.

"Somebody who's fought here says they know you," Lira said, "and that you'll not waste the yard's evening."

"And if you do?"

"Then they've vouched wrong, and everyone remembers." She stretched her arms over her head until her shoulders cracked, and did not look at him. "It's not a thing you do for just anybody."

He wrote it down. He looked at it after he had written it, for longer than the words needed, and then he turned the page.

By early afternoon the yard had begun to fill.

# Chapter 11 — Homework

They came in by ones and twos at first, and then in knots, through the gate in the wall and round the side of the public house, until the empty yard of the morning had turned, without any single moment when it happened, into a place where something was going to happen.

Somebody strung a rope round the circle on short iron stakes, an arm's length outside the groove, so that the crowd had a line to stand behind. Somebody else came out of the back of the public house with a bucket and a ladle and went round the rope selling water, and then a boy came out with a tray of hot pies and did better. The lanterns along the walls stayed dark. It was afternoon, and the light came down into the yard over the west wall, and lay across half of the circle and left the other half in shadow. The shadowed side was where people chose to stand. Cael worked out why after a minute, when he saw a man shading his eyes on the sunny side: from the dark side you looked into the light, and saw everything, and from the light side you looked into the dark.

Lira took him to an upturned barrel at the corner of the rope, on the shadowed side, where the wall of the public house met the yard wall. From the top of it he could see the whole circle and most of the crowd.

"Best seat," she said. "Don't tell anyone."

"Why is it empty, then?"

"Because it's next to the drain." She pointed with her chin at a grating in the corner, and the smell came up to meet the pointing. "Everything good has a cost. You'll get used to it."

A woman came out of the back door of the public house carrying a folding table.

She set it up in the shade of the back wall, on the edge of the yard nearest the circle, without looking at anyone. She unfolded its legs one at a time and pressed each into the dirt with her boot to seat it. Then she went back inside and came out with a stool and a book. The book was a thick ledger in a dark cover, worn soft at the corners and darker still where hands had held it, and she set it on the table and squared it with the table's edge, as though the angle mattered.

She was somewhere in her fifties, not tall, with grey working through dark hair that she wore pulled back hard from her face. She moved without hurry and without wasting anything, and when she walked out to the circle and along the groove, slowly, looking down at it, Cael saw that she walked heel first and then flat, every step the same length, measuring.

He knew that walk. He had last seen it on a white sheet in a back room in Denvash, on a man nearly twice her size.

"That's Vell," Lira said, unnecessarily.

Vell walked the whole groove. Twice she stopped and pressed at the ground with the toe of her boot, and once she called something over her shoulder, and the boy who had sold pies came running with a rake and smoothed out a patch where the dirt had rucked. Then she went back to her table and sat down and opened the ledger to a clean page, and only then did she look at the crowd, a single sweep from one end of the rope to the other, as if she were counting it.

Her eyes went past Cael on his barrel without stopping. He was nearly sure they had paused.

---

A large man with very clean hands was working his way along the rope with a slate hung round his neck on a string.

He was dressed better than anyone else in the yard, in a dark coat with good buttons, and he was perfectly friendly to everybody, and people kept putting coins in his hand and he kept writing on the slate with a stub of chalk and saying something low and pleasant to each of them. He laughed a good deal. Every few minutes he wiped the slate with his sleeve and wrote it again.

"Marrow," said Lira, in a voice like a closed door.

Cael watched him for a while. Then he took out the notebook and, without being sure why, began to write down what was on the slate each time Marrow wiped it and rewrote it. It was two columns, two names, a number beside each. The first bout's numbers started at one thing, and as people came to him and paid, the numbers moved. They moved toward the name more people were paying on, which made sense. But they moved a little further each time than the money did. Cael could not have said how he knew that. He did not know how much money was going on each side. But the numbers had a rhythm, and the rhythm leaned, very slightly, the same way every time, like a wheel with one spoke a hair too long.

"What are you doing?" Lira said.

"Counting."

She looked at his page. She looked at it for a long moment, and then she looked across the yard at Marrow, who was laughing at something a man had said and writing on his slate.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't let him see you doing that." She did not take her eyes off Marrow. "I mean it. People who count Marrow's numbers have a way of finding out that the yard's less friendly than they thought."

He found that he was obscurely pleased about it, in the way he was pleased when a measurement came out where he expected, and that he was also a little frightened, and that the two feelings sat side by side without either one making room.

The first two fighters were both Copper.

A woman of about thirty, broad through the hips and shoulders, with short hair and her sleeves rolled up past the elbow. "Against Copper Rank 2, Blade Path." A lean young man, quick in every movement, who came over the rope in a single hop and rolled his neck from side to side as he walked to his mark.

They stood on opposite sides of the circle, just inside the groove.

They were flat, and wide, and very still, and the loose grit round her boots shivered once and lay down, as the grit round Joren's boots had done at the low wall, a world away and a week ago.

*Rooted,* he wrote. *Or something like it. Higher rank than Joren's.* He looked at the young man. *Blade, Cu 2. Weight forward. Right arm low and back, behind his hip.*

"Begin," said Vell.

The young man brought up his blade.

Cael had read about Blade Path and had seen it once, on a flickering grey sheet, too fast to follow. He had never seen it close. It came out of nothing along the young man's right forearm, from just below the elbow and out past the knuckles, a narrow blade of pale light, steady as a ruled line, with none of a flame's flutter in it and none of a lamp's spill. "Longer than that," Lira said quietly beside him.

"Whatever your eye just told you," she said, without looking away from the circle. "Longer than that. Everyone's eye lies about it. It's light, it's not steel, it doesn't look like it should reach as far as it does." She tapped two fingers on her own forearm, in the air beyond her fist. That surprised him. The young man answered at once. She took it.

Cael saw her decide to take it. The cut cost less than the distance.* He stopped, and thought, and wrote: *If she'd dodged it properly she'd have been two steps further out, and he'd have cut her on the way in next time anyway, deeper, because he'd know her line. In the first exchanges the Iron man had come straight at her, every time, and every time he had been met by the wall of her stance. By the third he had stopped coming straight. By the fourth she had seen what he was doing, and she had stopped turning; she let him come in at his angle and met him side-on, which was weaker, but which kept both her feet on the ground.

*She's giving up strength to keep her feet,* Cael wrote. *He's giving up power to make her move. They're both paying for something.*

In the sixth exchange something changed in both of them at once, and it took him a moment to see what it was.

They were tired. They had been tired for a while, he realized, but in the sixth exchange it started to make decisions for them. The Iron man's strikes were still in the same place every time, but he took a breath between them now, a short one, and she saw the breath and began to time her steps to it. The Stone woman, for her part, had begun to set her feet a fraction later each time, so that there was a moment, at the start of every stance, when she was not quite rooted yet. He saw that too. Cael watched them both see it, one after the other, and he watched them both try to use what they had seen, and it was like watching two people play a board game who had each just noticed the other's mistake.

In the seventh exchange the Iron man struck at the moment before her feet set.

He did not strike hard. He did not need to. He hit her guard while she was still settling into it, and she went back a whole step and then another, and her heel came down on the edge of the groove and slipped, and for an instant she was off balance with her weight on one foot and the Iron man was already turning to bring the next strike round.

She held up her hand.

It was not a slap on the ground. It was an open palm, raised, the way you raise your hand in a room to say that you want to speak. She held it there, breathing hard, with her heel on the edge of the groove.

The Iron man stopped his strike in the air.

"Called," said Vell. "Conceded. Iron Path."

And the crowd, which had been quiet for most of the bout in the way people are quiet when they are watching something slow and difficult, did something Cael had not expected. It applauded. It did not cheer, as it had for the Stone woman's lock in the first bout. It clapped, steadily, all along the rope, the shady side and the sunny side together, and the clapping went on while the two of them touched fists and walked to Vell's table, and the person it was for was not the winner.

"Why are they clapping for her?" Cael said.

"Because she read it right." Lira was clapping too, not very loudly. "Another breath and he'd have had her down, and maybe hurt, and she'd have been out for a month. She saw it a strike early, and she stopped it, and she'll be back in the circle next week." She stopped clapping. "She'll get more bouts offered off the back of losing that than he will off the back of winning it. He's dull. Strong and dull. She's interesting."

"How you lose matters," Cael said.

"How you lose matters," Lira agreed. "You were listening. I was starting to think you only listened to people's feet."

At the table, Vell was writing. It took longer than the entry for the first bout, three or four lines, and when she had finished she said something to the Stone woman that made her laugh, and the Stone woman went off to the water bucket with her shoulders down and her hands loose, as though it had been a good afternoon after all.

---

He had six pages by the end of it. He read them back while the crowd was thinning out of the gate and the pie boy was going round with the last of his tray, and he found that he had written more in one afternoon about two fights than he had written about anything since the archive.

Lira was watching him read.

"You're not watching it like everybody else," she said.

"How am I watching it?"

"Like you're going to be tested on it." She leaned back on her hands on the barrel lid. "Everybody else on that rope was watching it to see who won, and to see if they'd been right, and to have something to talk about at dinner. You were watching it like homework."

He thought about that. It was not untrue. He thought that the difference was simply that he did not know yet what he was going to need, and so everything might be the thing he needed, and so he had to keep all of it. He did not say that.

"Is that bad?"

"No," Lira said. She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a slightly different voice, "No. It's not bad."

She did not say anything else about it. But on the walk back down from the yards, through the long gold light of the end of the day, he noticed that she was walking a little closer to him than she had in the morning, and that she was looking at him from time to time out of the side of her eye, as he imagined she might look at an opponent she had begun to take seriously, rather than at a curiosity she had decided to be kind to.

---

She walked with him as far as the crossroads.

The board stood in the middle of the triangle with the evening light on it, orange and low, so that every notice threw its own small shadow across the one beneath it. The skewer woman had gone. The man with the weighted cords had gone. The triangle was full of people walking home, and none of them stopped at the board, and Lira did not stop either. She stopped at the edge of the triangle and turned round and folded her arms.

"You're going to put your name down," she said.

It was not a question, and he did not treat it as one. "Probably."

"Not probably." She shook her head. "You've decided. You decided in the second bout. Somewhere about the fourth exchange you stopped writing and you just watched, for about ten breaths, and when the pencil started again it moved differently. Faster. Shorter. Like a list of things you were going to need." She said it with plain satisfaction, the satisfaction of someone who enjoyed being right about people and saw no reason to hide it. "I'm not showing off. You ought to know what your face does when you've made up your mind, because you plainly don't, and somebody should tell you."

"What does it do?"

"Nothing. That's how I could tell. Everything else about you is always doing something." She tipped her head. "It means you can't bluff me. Which is going to be either very useful or very annoying, depending on what you end up wanting."

He did not know what to say to that. He found that he was not even sure she was wrong.

"How do I put my name down?"

"Go to the Cinder House tomorrow, before the bouts, and find Vell at her table. Tell her you want a bout. Unrated. She'll match you against somebody." Lira's voice had gone practical. "She'll ask your age. She'll ask your Path. She'll ask who vouches for you."

"Do I need somebody to vouch for me?"

"You don't *need* it." Lira looked at the board, not at him. "Nobody can stop you walking up to that table on your own. But an unrated debut with nobody speaking for them gets matched carefully, which means cheaply, which means Vell puts you against somebody who won't teach you anything, because nobody knows whether you'll fold in the first exchange or last the whole bout, and she won't spend a real fighter's evening finding out." She was quiet a moment. Then, quickly, as if she wanted to say it before she had finished deciding to: "I'll vouch for you. If you want."

He had not expected it. It took him half a breath too long to answer, and she saw that half a breath, and for once she did not say anything about it.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I don't think you'll waste the yard's evening." She shrugged, the easy shrug that looked as if it cost nothing. "And because I don't vouch for many people, and I'd like to be right about one of them. Most people I could vouch for, I'd be wrong about. Most people don't stand in front of a board for eleven minutes."

"I'd want that," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." The corner of her mouth went up. "If you go down in the first exchange I'm going to tell everybody I've never seen you before in my life and you must have followed me there."

"Would they believe you?"

"I'm very convincing," said Lira. "Tomorrow. Cinder House, the middle of the morning, before Vell's had too much tea and started being kind to people. Bring the notebook. You'll want it after, whatever happens."

She walked away across the triangle toward wherever she went in the evenings, and did not look back. Cael stood at the edge of the crossroads and watched her go until the widest street had taken her.

Then he looked at the board, and at the word *vouch* on the bottom notice, written in brown ink by somebody who had pressed hard enough to dent the paper. *Ledger-verified only. No walk-ins without a vouch.*

He remembered Lira's voice in the yard that morning, with her face turned away from him, stretching her arms over her head: *It's not a thing you do for just anybody.*

He had been offered something, and he had taken it, and he had no idea what it cost.

Torvin was at the kitchen table that night, which Cael had not seen him do before. He was sitting at the end of it with a ledger of his own, a thin one with a cracked spine, and a stub of pencil, and he was adding columns with his lips moving. His wife was at the range. Yeni was at the other end of the table with a shirt across her knee. The woman who slept in her boots was eating soup with her boots on.

Cael sat down across from Torvin and waited until he had reached the bottom of a column.

"What does a vouch cost?" he said.

Torvin looked up. He looked at Cael for long enough that the pencil, which had been about to start the next column, came to rest on the page.

"Who's paying?" he said.

"The person who gives it."

"Then it costs them." Torvin went back to his column.

That seemed to be all. Cael waited, and Torvin's lips moved down the column, and reached the bottom, and he wrote a figure under it and drew a line. Then, without looking up, he said, "A vouch is a loan. You're the one borrowing. They're the one who's out of pocket if you don't pay it back."

"Out of pocket how? It isn't money."

"Didn't say it was money."

At the other end of the table, Yeni's needle had stopped.

"It's their name," she said. She did not look up from the shirt either. Cael was beginning to think that nobody in this house ever looked up when they were saying something important. "Round the yards, everybody's got a name, and the name's worth something. It's worth what people think it is. If somebody puts their name next to yours and you go down in a heap the first time you're touched, then the next time they put their name next to somebody, people remember the heap." The needle went in and out. "Do it twice and their name's worth nothing. Then nobody'll take their word for anything. Not for a fighter, not for a debt, not for what they saw happen."

"How long does it take to make a name worth something?"

"Depends who you are." Yeni bit the thread. "Who's vouching for you?"

"Lira."

Yeni's hands stopped. Across the table, Torvin's pencil stopped too.

"The Wind girl," Torvin said. "Four months."

"You know her?"

"Everybody knows her," said Yeni. "She lost her first four bouts in that yard and kept coming back. Nobody keeps coming back after four. Now people bet on her." She looked at Cael at last, a long, measuring look, the same look she had given him the first night when she had seemed to be taking his measurements for a coat. "Four months is a short time to build a name, for her. It's all the name she's got."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Torvin's wife scraped something in a pan. The woman in the boots finished her soup.

"Well," Torvin said, and turned the page of his ledger, and started on the next column.

---

Upstairs, with the lamp still lit and the brothers not yet in from the river, Cael sat on his cot and read back over the day.

Six pages on two bouts. The Stone woman's cut. The Iron man's angle and the sixth-exchange breath. The open palm raised at the edge of the groove, and the clapping. The rules, in a list down the margin: *Ledger. Vell's word. Exchanges, not rounds. Concede with a hand. Report to the table. No killing (not a rule; worse than a rule).* Marrow's numbers, leaning the same way every time, like a wheel with a long spoke. *8 in. past the fist.*

He thought about the Stone woman in the first bout, choosing a small cut now over a larger one later, and he understood, sitting there, that this was closer to tomorrow than any of the morning's rules. He was not brave. He was fairly sure of that. He was going to walk into the circle because staying out of it would cost him more, over time, than walking in. He would be paying for something. He would just be paying now, small, instead of later.

But he would not be the only one paying.

He turned to a clean page and wrote at the top of it, in the private hand, smaller than the rest.

*Lira's vouching for me tomorrow. Torvin says a vouch is a loan and I'm the one borrowing. Yeni says it's her name, and four months of it is all the name she's got.*

*She didn't tell me that. She made it sound like nothing.*

He looked at the two lines for a while. Then he wrote one more under them, and underlined it, once.

*Find out what it costs her before I let her spend it.*

# Chapter 12 — Terms

Lira was waiting for him at the board in the morning, sitting on the edge of a water trough with her canvas roll across her knees, eating an apple.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. I just make it look like I happened to be passing." She threw the core over her shoulder into the trough without looking, where it bobbed, and stood up. "Come on. Vell sets her table out at ten, and the first one there gets her before she's tired of people."

They walked up toward the yards side by side. It was a grey morning, cool, with a thin high cloud that took the colour out of everything, and the district was busy in the ordinary way of a weekday, carts and barrows and the smell of bread. Cael let two streets go by. He had decided in the night what he was going to say, and how, and he found as he walked that every version he had built in the dark sounded either like an accusation or like an apology, and he did not mean either.

He said it anyway, plainly, at the corner where the lane started to climb.

"What does it cost you?"

Lira did not pretend not to understand him. He liked her for that before she had answered.

"Who told you it cost anything?"

"Torvin. And Yeni." He kept walking, and so did she. "Torvin says a vouch is a loan. Yeni says it's your name. She says four months is all the name you've got."

"Yeni talks too much."

"Yeni hardly talks at all."

"Then she picked a strange thing to say it about." Lira walked half a street without saying anything. When she did speak, the lightness had gone out of her voice, and what was left was level and careful, the voice she had used to tell him about the examiner. "All right. If you go into that circle and fold in the first exchange, people will remember that I said you wouldn't. Next time I vouch for somebody, Vell will look at me a little longer before she writes it down. Next time I say a fighter's good, people will say, *she said that about the boy who folded.* It won't ruin me. I've lost four bouts in that yard and I'm still standing in it. But it'll cost me something I worked for, and I'd rather it didn't."

"Then why do it?"

"Because I don't think you'll fold." She looked sideways at him. "I could be wrong. I've been wrong before. But I've watched you for two days, and I don't think so, and if you want to know what my name's actually *for*, Cael, it's for this. It's for saying what I think in front of people who'll hold me to it. Otherwise it's just a thing I keep in a drawer."

He thought about the drawer marked *Springs, do not use,* and did not say anything about it.

"I'm not going to win," he said instead.

"No."

"I mean I'm not going to try to. I'm going to try to last. And I'm going to watch him. And I'm going to come out of there knowing more about how he moves than anybody on that rope." He heard himself saying it and realized he had not known until this moment that it was a plan, rather than a hope. "That's what I can do. That's all I can do. I wanted you to know before you put your name down, in case it's not what you thought you were vouching for."

Lira stopped walking.

They were halfway up the lane, outside a yard where two men were taking turns throwing each other over a hip onto a pile of straw, and the steady soft thump of bodies landing came over the wall like someone beating a rug. "That's exactly what I thought I was vouching for," she said. "Nobody wins their debut. Nobody sensible tries. The ones who try to win are the ones who get carried out." She started walking again. "What counts as stupid?"

"Refusing to go down when you're down. Standing up when you should stay on the ground. Trying something you saw somebody else do on a sheet once." She shook her head. "Keep your feet, watch him, and when Vell calls it, it's over. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job," he said.

"And Cael."

"Yes."

The yard was empty except for the crows. Vell was not doing anything. "I've had them explained."

"It suits."

"Say it back to me."

He said it as Hesk had taught him to call a load figure, the whole of it, in order, without adding anything or leaving anything out: unrated; whoever she put across from him; ended when she called it, not before, not for any other reason; nobody stopped it but her; what happened was his to carry, either way; nobody was making him. "I do," said Lira.

"Yes."

The groove was a hand deep and he felt it under his boot as he crossed it, a dip and then level ground. Inside the circle the dirt had been swept and watered, and it was firmer than it looked, packed down by years of feet into something nearly as hard as a floor. The lamplight was very bright in the middle, brighter than it had looked from the barrel. He walked to the near side of the circle and stopped just inside the groove, and turned round.

Renn was already on his mark on the far side. He was rolling his shoulders, slowly, one and then the other, the way a man loosens up before he lifts something.

Lira's voice came back to him, in its own compressed rhythm, from the cooper's roof. *Add the eight inches. Out loud, in your head. Watch the first exchange. Don't try to win it. Give him ground and look.*

"Renn," said Vell. "Copper Rank 3, Blade Path. Against unrated, debut. Vouched." A small pause, so small that probably nobody else in the yard noticed it. "Begin when you're ready."

Renn brought the blade up.

It came out of nothing along his right forearm, as the young man's had the afternoon before, but it was not the same. It was steadier. It did not make the faint high singing sound when he turned his wrist. It simply stood there in the lamplight, pale and hard and straight, with a thin bright line along one edge. To Cael's eye, before Renn had moved at all, it stopped about a hand past the knuckles. He made himself say that twice in his head, and disbelieve it twice.

*Plus eight inches.* He said it. *Plus eight.* He saw it, then, the way a drawing on a page changes when somebody tells you what it is: the real length of it, the place where the edge actually ended in the air, a long way further out than his eye wanted to believe.

Then Renn started toward him, strolling.

---

The first exchange taught him three things, and he learned them in order.

The first was that an amble from a Copper Rank 3 covered ground faster than other people walked. Renn did not seem to hurry, and then he was there, inside the distance Cael had thought was safe, and the blade was coming.

The second was that Renn opened with probes. Two of them, loose, one high toward the head and one across toward the body, thrown off his front foot with no weight in them at all, like a man tapping a wall to find out where the hollow part is. They were meant to be seen. Cael saw them. He gave ground along the curve of the groove, left, and the first went past his face with a faint hiss of air and the second went across the front of him with room to spare, hands high, knees bent, his arms doing nothing except being in the way.

At the rope, someone said something about a rabbit, and somebody else laughed.

The third thing was the important one, and he nearly missed it, and then he did not.

Renn's weight went back.

It went back through his rear foot, into the heel, and settled there, and his whole frame seemed to gather itself onto that heel the way a spring gathers when you press it. The probes had come off the front foot, light as talk. This was different. This was the frame loading. And in the instant after the heel settled—

The blade came round.

Cael saw it start and was late by the width of a breath. Most of him got clear. Not all of him. The flat caught him across the meat of the left shoulder as it went past, and it landed like an iron bar left too long in a stove, a broad hard slap that spun him a half-turn on his feet and drove the breath out of him in a grunt. Along the rope the crowd made its first real sound of the night, a pleased murmur that meant the evening was going the way everyone had paid for.

He did not fall. He finished the turn three steps back with his left arm hanging and his shoulder singing, and he was still on his feet, and still inside the groove.

Renn did not come after him. He walked back to his mark and waited for Cael to find his, and he rolled his shoulders again while he waited, in no hurry about anything. Cael could find nothing in his face to be afraid of, and nothing to be flattered by. He was a few minutes' exercise for a rated man between bigger bouts, the kind of evening a careful fighter takes to keep his arm warm, and Renn bore him no ill will and spared him no thought.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

The shoulder hurt in a bright, exact way, as though it had been drawn on him with a hot wire. Cael put the hurt in a box and set the box down. The heel he kept out, where he could see it.

*Probes off the front foot,* he thought. *Truth off the back heel. There's a gap between the heel and the blade, and tonight I spent it looking for the gap.*

*Next time it's mine.*

---

The second exchange began the same way. Renn ambled in. The probes came, high and across, off the front foot, loose and conversational, and Cael gave ground on the curve, and did not look at the probes at all. He looked at the heel.

It went back. It settled.

And this time Cael went when the heel went, without waiting to see the blade it was loading. He went left and back, early, before the blade had started to come round, a whole quarter of a second sooner than he had moved the first time—

The strike did not come round.

Renn dropped instead. He went low, very fast, his blade arm flung out wide and high as a counterweight, the pale edge drawing a bright line up into the dark above the lamps, and his whole heavy body swinging round, leg first, through the place where Cael's ankles ought to have been. He was going for the legs. There was no harm meant in it. It was the answer a careful fighter keeps for a beginner who has worked out one trick and is too pleased with it, and it ends with the beginner flat on the ground and the rope laughing.

But Cael had moved early. He had moved before the sweep committed, and his ankles were not where the sweep went looking for them. He jumped the tail of the swing, clumsily, arms flung out like a boy clearing a ditch, and landed with both feet under him, and found Renn right in front of him.

The bigger man was still crouched from the swing, twisted half round, his hips facing Cael full on and his blade flung up and out on the far side. A short arm could have reached him. For the space of half a beat there was nothing at all between Cael and the middle of him.

Cael saw it. He saw all of it, the hips and the turned shoulder and the blade on the wrong side, and he wrote it down in his head as clearly as if he had had the notebook in his hand.

He did nothing with it.

Then the moment closed. Renn came up out of the sweep in one smooth motion, stepped back two paces, and stood looking at Cael across the bright middle of the circle. And then he rolled his shoulders: left, then right, slowly, settling them. It was the same thing he had done before the first exchange. Cael filed it at once beside the heel. *Shoulders when he changes his mind.*

There was a half-smile on Renn's face. It was not for Cael, exactly. It was for the situation.

Something had shifted in the sound along the rope. It was no louder than before. But it had lost the comfortable note of people who already knew the ending, and here and there someone had stopped talking to watch.

At the table, Vell's pen moved again. It moved for longer.

---

When Renn came in for the third exchange, he did not amble.

He came balanced and quiet, with his weight in the middle of his feet, and the probes, when they came, were crisper, shorter, with something in them now. Cael understood that something had been decided across the circle. Renn had stopped exercising. He had found a puzzle in front of him, and he meant to finish it.

The probes came, high and then across, and Cael gave ground, and watched the heel.

The heel settled.

And then he was somewhere else.

He was off the line of the strike. He did not know when he had left it. His weight was already through, his feet already placed, and the blade went past through the place where his chest had been, and he had not seen it go. He had not read it. He had spent two exchanges and a shoulder buying the quarter of a second between the heel and the strike, and when the heel settled he did not spend it. There was no quarter second. There was the heel, and then there was this.

The second strike came out of the first one's follow-through. It was not a new attack. It was the first cut turned round and sent back, slanting low across, into the place where anyone who had got out of the way of the first strike would have to be standing.

He was not standing there.

He was standing outside its arc, with his weight already turning, and he had not chosen that either. His mind arrived at each new place after his body was already in it, and found it lit and waiting, and could not account for having come.

The third strike came up from below.

He did not see it start. He only felt the air of it, close, very close, a hand's width from his ribs on the left side, ripping upward past him into the empty space above the circle, and he heard the blade's faint hiss die away at the top of its arc. The yard had gone completely silent. In the silence he could hear the lanterns creaking on their hooks.

He was standing still. His heart was going so hard he could feel it in his teeth. He had no idea what he had just done.

*The watching,* he told himself, because whatever had happened needed a name, and any name would do for now. *Two exchanges of watching. The body learns.* He set the explanation over the thing and saw at once that it did not fit, as a lid cut for a smaller jar does not fit. Two exchanges of one man's rhythm did not come to that. Nothing he could put his finger on came to that. And the middle of a bout was no place to stand doing arithmetic.

He left the lid where it sat, crooked, and got his hands up.

Because Renn was still there, a pace and a half away, finishing the figure, his weight carried all the way through and up onto his front foot, his blade high and his ribs open. It was the second time tonight Cael had stood within reach of a fully committed Copper Rank 3. He had read nothing this time and got out anyway, and now the man was open, and Cael did the only thing he knew how to do, which was nothing he had ever practised.

He threw a short straight punch at Renn's shoulder.

Renn did not even turn toward it. His left forearm brushed it off, idly, as a man brushes off a fly, without breaking his stride and without bothering to make a guard of it. Then he stepped off, back to his mark, and the third exchange was over.

Cael had not touched him all night.

He knew that, standing on his mark with his fist still stinging from the forearm. He had read two exchanges and paid for one of them with a shoulder. Then something had happened in the third that he could not account for, and in the middle of it he had been handed the cleanest opening of the evening and had answered it with a punch a child could have swatted. Seeing a strike coming was one craft. Doing anything about it was another. He had spent two days, and an hour on a cooper's roof, and two exchanges in this circle building the first. He owned none of the second.

Across the circle, Renn was looking at him.

For the first time all night he was paying attention, the narrow, careful attention of a tradesman holding up a part that has come off his own lathe in a shape he did not cut. He did not say anything. Fighters did not speak in the middle of a bout. But he rolled his shoulders once more, left and then right, and something in the way he stood packed itself tighter, lower, more closely round its own centre.

*That's his serious weight,* Cael thought, and wrote it down in his head with the rest. *That's what eleven bouts and eight wins looks like when it stops holding back.*

---

The fourth exchange lasted four seconds.

Renn came in with no probes at all. There was no amble and no tapping at the wall. He came straight off his mark and threw a figure that was cousin to the one in the third exchange, strikes folded into each other, each one coming out of the end of the last. But it was stepped up past some point where reading it stopped being any use, because every strike shut the door the one before it had left open, and the whole of it came so fast that everything earlier in the evening seemed, looking back, to have been good manners.

Cael saw the first fold clearly. He saw the heel, and the first strike, and he moved, late.

He saw the second fold partly.

And he reached, the way you reach in the dark for a stair you were sure was there, for the thing that had moved his feet in the third exchange. He did not decide to reach. He was simply reaching, feeling about for it like a man patting his pockets for a key he had been holding a moment before.

There was nothing there. There were only his own plain reflexes, the ones that had been late all evening, and they were late again, and they had nothing to spare.

The flat of the blade took him across the ribs on the right side. It was a clean, driving, expert blow, with all of Renn's weight behind it this time, and it lifted him off his feet and turned him in the air and put him down on his side in the packed dirt with every bit of breath gone out of him at once.

The ground pressed cold against his cheek. Overhead the lamps rocked on their hooks, or else the yard did. The crowd had become a long way off, much further than the rope, and it was shouting.

"Match," said Vell.

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The one word reached every corner of the yard, the shouting and the lamplight and the boy lying in the dirt, and after it nothing else that happened in the circle counted.

# Chapter 13 — Tonight's Answer

His breath came back to him slowly, a mouthful at a time, as if somebody were passing it to him through a gap in a door.

The first piece was very small and hurt all the way down. The second was larger. By the fourth he could get an elbow under himself, and then a knee, and he stayed like that on his elbow and his knee in the dirt with his head hanging, and made an inventory, because an inventory was a thing to do that was not lying in the dirt.

Ribs, on the right side. Bright and deep, a hot wide band from under his arm to the bottom of his chest, and every breath went round the edge of it instead of through it. But the breaths went, and nothing grated when they did, so probably nothing was broken. Probably.

Shoulder, on the left. Duller now. Warming up, slowly, toward the kind of ache that would be worse in the morning than it was tonight.

And his left forearm. He turned it over and looked at it in the lamplight. There was a long scrape down the outside of it from wrist to elbow, crusted with dirt from the circle, stinging now that he had noticed it. He had no memory at all of getting it. He looked at it with a kind of distant, clerkly annoyance, as he might have looked at a figure in a column that he had not written and could not trace. He did not know whether he had got it in the fall at the end, or in the sweep, or somewhere in the middle of the third exchange, where his feet had been and he had not.

He did not want to think about the third exchange. He put the forearm down.

A shadow came across the lamplight. Renn was standing over him. The blade was gone, and his right hand was held out, open, at the height of Cael's face.

Cael took it. Renn pulled him up off his knee in one easy movement, with the plain strength of a man who had spent years lifting things heavier than a fourteen-year-old and did not notice the difference, and held on to his hand a moment longer than he needed to, until Cael's feet had decided to stay under him.

"Four exchanges," Renn said.

He said it flatly, as a fact for the record, the way Vell might have said it. But he was looking at Cael's face while he said it, closely.

"You know what an unrated debut usually gives me?" he said. "One. If they're brave, two. I've never had four." He glanced down at Cael's ribs, at the way Cael was standing round them, and then back up. "And that third one."

He stopped. He shook his head, once, and it was not a shake of disbelief. It was the shake of a man putting something in a drawer to take out later, when he had time to look at it properly.

"That was a real figure I threw at you," Renn said. "It doesn't miss. It missed."

Cael did not say anything, because there was nothing accurate available.

"What's your Path?"

Renn asked it straight, without any suspicion in it at all. It was a craftsman's question, one tradesman to another across a bench: *what tool did that?*

"Nothing to register," Cael said.

Renn studied him. If he thought anything about that, he kept it, except that one corner of his mouth lifted a little, as though nothing said in the yard all night had pleased him more.

Then he patted Cael once on the right shoulder, the one that had not been hit, and nodded toward Vell's table, half a courtesy to Vell and half a direction to Cael, and then walked off to the rope. The pie boy went next, with his tray empty and his pockets heavy. *Renn (Cu 3, Blade) over unrated debut (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.), fourth exchange, by the flat. Called.*

That was the result. There was no name in it. The book did not carry the names of unrated debuts, only what they were; a name went into the book when the yard had a reason to need it, and most debuts never gave it one. It was two lines. It was all the book required, and it was all anybody coming to the book in a year, or ten years, would need in order to know what had happened in the yard tonight and who owed whom what.

Under it she had kept writing.

*First exchange: debut gave ground on the curve, did not answer. Took the flat on the left shoulder off the true strike. Stayed up.*

*Second exchange: moved on R.'s rear heel, before the strike. Early. Read it. R. answered with the sweep; debut cleared it, was offered the opening after, saw it (I watched his eyes), did not take it.*

*Third exchange: R. threw the full figure.*

She stopped there, as she had stopped when she was writing it, with the yard full and the boy standing in front of her holding his ribs.

She had kept this book, or one very like it, for longer than some of her fighters had been alive. Before that she had fought in yards like this one, and in better ones, for long enough to reach a rating nobody gave lightly and a body that told her about it every wet morning. She had seen, at a rough count she had once made on a winter night with nothing better to do, something over four thousand bouts. She had called nearly all of them. She did not remember most of them, and that was as it should be. The book remembered them. That was what the book was for.

She remembered Renn's figure.

She had watched him build it, over two seasons, a piece at a time. The first time he threw it, it had not been finished, and a woman with a Shield Path had walked through the middle of it and put him down. The second time it had been nearly finished. By the end of last season it had been finished, and she had watched him throw it nine times since, and she had written it down nine times. Nine times the third strike had come up the lane, and nine times somebody had been standing in the lane, because that was where the first two strikes put you. That was the whole of its design. It did not need you to guess wrong. It caught you where guessing right had sent you.

She had written it down on a night in the summer against the name of the Wind girl, the one who had stood in front of her table tonight with her chin up and said *I do.* The girl had read the first two strikes as cleanly as Vell had ever seen anyone read them, and the third had put her in the dirt, and she had got up, and come back the next week, and the week after that.

And the third one put me on my back, looking at the lamps. Everybody I've ever seen him throw it at has ended up looking at the lamps."

Her eyes were on his face. He could see them catching the lantern light.

"Tonight it went past a boy on his first night in the circle. By a hand." She tilted her head. "Somewhere after the second cut, you went where that figure doesn't send people. So I've got one question, and it's small, and I think it's fair."

He knew what it was before she asked it. He had known since the table.

"How?"

He had walked all the way from the yard without getting an answer ready, and he knew that had been on purpose. Getting one ready would have meant turning round to face the thing and measuring it, and here in the dark he could admit, a little, that he did not want to. There was nothing to measure it with. In the fourth exchange he had felt for it and his hand had closed on air. He could not have shown it to her. He could not have done it again here, between these walls, because she asked, or told her what it was built from, or promised her it would ever come back. It was one stone in a heap he could otherwise count, and it would not let itself be counted, and whenever his thoughts drifted toward it they discovered urgent business elsewhere.

"Instinct," Cael said.

It was true, as far as it went. It stopped about a hand's width short of the truth.

Lira looked at him for a long moment. He was learning that she had a very exact sense of when somebody had given her a real answer, or a false one, or a true answer with its legs sawn short. He suspected she had another sense besides, a rarer one, for when to send the bill for the difference and when to let it wait.

"All right," she said. "Instinct."

She started walking again, and he fell in beside her. She let three streets go by, the lanterns coming and going over them, before she said anything else, and when she did it was lightly, in the voice of someone making a note in a book of her own.

"That's tonight's answer," said Lira. "I'm writing down that it was tonight's."

---

They walked.

The district went about its late business round them: shutters going up, lamplight under doors, somebody in a window two streets over arguing cheerfully about the odds on something. A cart went by with its lamp swinging. A woman on a doorstep called a cat in by a name Cael did not catch, and the cat did not come.

His body sorted its pains into their long arrangements as he walked. The ribs on the right had settled into a deep, hot band that tightened when he breathed in and let go when he breathed out, so that every breath was a small negotiation. The shoulder had gone stiff and heavy, as though somebody had hung a weight from it. The forearm stung whenever his sleeve moved over it. Under all of them there was a larger tiredness, the plain whole-body ache of having been handled, for four exchanges, by a man who knew how to handle people.

He turned the evening over as he walked, the way you turn a bracket over in your hand to see where it bent.

He had lost. That was the result, and it stood as called, and it was in the book. He had not touched Renn once. He had been knocked down by the flat of a blade and had needed a hand to get up. If you wrote it in two lines, as the book did, that was all of it.

But he had walked into the circle with what Lira could tell him about Blade fighters, which was a great deal, and what he had read on a board, which was a little, and he had walked out with the whole working of one man. The probes and their rhythm. The heel. The shoulders, before he changed his mind. The sweep, kept back for readers who got pleased with themselves. The serious weight, and what it looked like when it came down. The folded figure in the fourth exchange, and how fast it really went. Every one of those was a thing he had not known at the start of the evening and knew now, and he had paid for every one of them, and he had them.

Bruises healed. What he had bought with them would not. If you were going to lose on purpose, the only way it was worth anything was if you collected all of what you had paid for, every piece, and sorted it and kept it and had it ready. A month out, at Vell's table, Renn was going to stop probing again, and Cael was going to be standing there with all of it.

Nearly all of it.

There was one thing from the evening he did not sort. He did not even pick it up. He let it lie where it had fallen, whole and unexamined, in a corner of himself where he would know it was there without having to look at it. He had been doing that with a turnip cart since the road, and now there were two things in the corner, and he did not let himself count them, because counting them would have been the first step toward a word.

So he went home at Lira's side through the cooling dark: fourteen, beaten in four exchanges, his shoulder and his ribs charging him for every step. And he was certain, quietly and against all the evidence of the book, that he had won something tonight that nobody on the rope had been watching for.

At the corner by the well with the iron cover, Lira stopped.

"This is me," she said, and pointed down a lane he had not been down. Then she looked at him, at the way he was holding his side. "Torvin's has a yard with a pump. Put the cold on the ribs before you lie down, or you won't get up in the morning. Not too long. Count it."

"How long?"

"You'll know how long." She almost smiled. "You count everything."

"Lira."

She had already turned. She turned back.

"You didn't lose your name tonight," he said. "Did you?"

Lira looked at him in the dark for a moment. Then she laughed, short and real, the laugh from the yard in the morning that had surprised her.

"No," she said. "Marrow did. He'd laid nothing off on the over." She walked backward a few steps down the lane, without looking behind her, without hitting anything. "Go and put your ribs under the pump, Cael. I'll see you at the yard."

And she was gone into the dark, and he stood at the corner by the well for a moment longer, holding his side, and then went home.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## The Architect

**LOCKED (existence by Book 8); SECRET (nature and intent until Book 13-14)**

The entity — or long-dead person's preserved will — who designed the Path system. The Architect built the Arbiter architecture, deployed it across Valdris, and has been dormant in it for centuries. **SECRET:** The Architect's error was categorical, not motivational. They believed the problem was [UNBOUND] potential. The Path system did not solve the problem — it suppressed the symptoms while eliminating anyone who could have addressed the root cause.

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). Diagnostic technique: **the reading** — contact, both hands, the healer's current run through the patient's architecture ("listening with my hands to how you're built"). Two modes, taught before either is load-bearing (B7 Ch7): the *surface* reading (hands on the hurt, seconds) and the *deep* reading (both hands, whole architecture, minutes, patient still and uninjured, at a mending's cost). Costs and limits: contact range only; healing spends the healer's reserve at roughly the rate of the damage repaired — no crowds, no self-healing; a Tide practitioner **cannot heal what she cannot read**; combat use is a *stall* (a hand on a wrist, a breath, the declaration doesn't render) at brutal cost — Oryn has done it three times in her life. Inside quiet ground: nothing.

**Cael's Tide-adjacent fragment (B7 Ch13):** partial; Iron; self and contact; the first fragment that does nothing in a fight; engagement field *clinical*; **the standing rule, in Oryn's hand and his: ask first, every time.** Consistent with B2's "Tide-adjacent" anomaly label and Book 15's "Oryn's Tide Path burns out."

## Continuity Rules

1. **The Compact Registry is the authority on Path classification.** Any scene involving official rank must be consistent with what the Registry would show for that character at that point in the story.

7. **The found family is permanent.** No companion exits the series without narrative justification. The loss in Book 12 is a choice, not a death — the companion is still alive, their relationship with Cael changed.

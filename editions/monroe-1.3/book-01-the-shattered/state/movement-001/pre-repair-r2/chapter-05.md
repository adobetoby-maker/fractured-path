# Chapter 5 — The Six O'Clock Light

He woke before it was light and lay still, doing what he always did on days that mattered. He took inventory: the ceiling, the boot-shaped stain in the northeast corner, the bag by the door, packed and tied and waiting with the patience of an object that knows what it is for, and the door to the hall, still open a hand's width.

From the kitchen came the sound of Hesk already up and filling the kettle. He set it on the stove rather than banging it down, like a man being careful not to hurry something he did not want to end. Cael stayed in bed a little longer than the inventory needed and listened to the house. The second floorboard from the kitchen door creaked when Hesk crossed it. The tap ran and stopped. A cupboard closed with a soft, deliberate click.

It was the cupboard by the stove, whose latch had worked loose two winters ago, so that if you shut it firmly it rattled for a full minute afterward, and Hesk always closed it with two fingers. Cael knew this house's sounds as he knew the streets outside it, completely, without ever having had to think about them, and he was thinking about them now. He supposed that was what leaving was. You noticed what you knew because you were about to stop being able to use it.

He got up. Dressing took longer than it should have. There was nothing to decide and everything else was packed, but his hands kept doing small unnecessary things, smoothing a sleeve, re-tying a lace that was already tied, and he let them. They seemed to know something he did not want to say out loud, which was that he would like this part to last.

Hesk had made the kind of breakfast that was not about food. There was bread from Ressa's, still warm, which meant he had been to the bakery before it was properly open, and the plum preserves from the jar on the high shelf that only came down for birthdays and first frosts, and tea made with the brass spoon and steeped the full time. Neither of them said anything about it, and neither of them needed to.

"There's a waystation about a day out on the merchant road," Hesk said, over the tea. "South side, low timber building, lamp over the door. Sleep there."

"Ardenmere's gate opens at dawn and closes at full dark, and I'm not to arrive after dark on the first night," Cael said. "I read the road notes at the record office."

"I know you did. Now you can say it back in my voice."

"I could say it back before."

"Not in my voice. You did it in the record office's voice." Hesk spread preserves on his bread with great care. "It's a dry voice. Nobody listens to it on a road at dusk."

Cael looked at him. "Is that going in the notebook, under *things I told you that you already knew*?"

"It might." Something in Hesk's face loosened, and he nearly smiled. "It's a long section."

Cael ate the bread, which tasted exactly as it always tasted, and that seemed wrong somehow, as though the bread ought to have noticed that everything else had changed. He chewed more slowly than usual and did not admit to himself why.

When they had finished, Hesk went into the workshop and came back with something small in his closed hand, which he put on the table in front of Cael before taking his hand away.

It was the lock gauge bracket, the first one, the one that had failed at less than twice working load with that small sound like a knuckle. It still had its faint new curve, as if it were considering a bow.

Cael looked at it and then up. "The drawer."

*Springs, do not use.* The ones that taught him something.

"Everyone needs one," Hesk said. "You'll have a bench of your own someday, and you'll want something in the drawer." He sat back down. "Start with that."

Cael picked it up. It was cold and small and slightly bent. He turned it over in his fingers and thought of the needle swinging and not coming back, *that's a lie somewhere*, and of forty minutes of filing and loading and reading a dial to find a number that had been printed and never traced. He put it in his coat pocket, next to the folded note for Joren.

"Thank you," he said, which did not seem like enough, though Hesk nodded as if it were.

Then Hesk got up and lifted something else off the workshop bench and set it beside the door. It was the finished lock gauge, in its brass housing, with the second bracket bolted to its back, the thick one that had held at three and a half times working load.

"It's due at the Fen Street lock this week," Hesk said. "It's on our way."

---

They walked to the depot together. It was not strictly necessary, because Cael had known the way since before he could remember not knowing it, but neither of them suggested skipping it. Hesk carried the gauge in a canvas sling, and Cael carried his bag and the satchel of tools.

The Outer District at six in the morning had its own texture, which Cael had soaked up so completely over fourteen years that he had never once looked straight at it, and he looked now.

Ressa's side door was open with the ovens going, and the smell of the first loaves was carrying half a block in every direction, as it always did. Ressa herself was out on the front step with a broom. She was a broad, quiet woman with flour to the elbows, and she had sold Hesk bread every morning of Cael's life.

She looked up as they came by, and her eyes went to the bag on Cael's shoulder and then to his face, and something in her own face eased into an understanding that did not need explaining.

"Safe travels," she said.

That was all. She did not ask where or why. Everyone within four streets had heard something, because news in the Outer District moved like water through the canal locks, finding every low place. Ressa had decided for herself that the right answer was two words and nothing else.

"Thank you."

She nodded and went back to her sweeping. Then, as they passed, without looking up, she said, "The one by the window's still warm. I put an extra in the bag your grandfather took."

Cael looked at Hesk, and Hesk looked at the sky.

"She put an extra in," Hesk said, "and wouldn't take for it."

By the time Cael turned to say something, Ressa had already gone inside, leaving the side door open and the smell coming out of it, and that was her answer.

Three doors down, Garrik was out in front of his shop with a cart wheel up on the stand. He was turning it slowly with one hand and listening to the hub with his head tilted, as he did every morning with the first wheel of the day before he had his tea. He said a wheel told you everything if you were quiet enough to hear it.

He did not look up as they passed. He did not look up in the particular, effortful way of a man choosing not to. His hand kept turning the wheel and his head kept its tilt, and his eyes stayed on the hub with a steadiness that cost more than looking would have.

Four nights ago, in the warm little room behind the curtain, Garrik had said *Well, well. Finally,* and patted the projector, and helped him shim a tension arm, and said *he's one of us*. Four nights ago, Cael had not had a classification. Now he had one, and it was a word that belonged to four people in a green book.

It hurt more than he had expected, which surprised him, because he had thought he had used up all the hurt there was. He made himself think about it properly as he walked, because that was the one tool he knew he had.

Some people met a hard thing by looking straight at it and some found it easier to look somewhere else, and neither had much to do with the person they were looking at or away from. Garrik's hand on the wheel was not saying *I think less of you.* It was saying *I don't know how to do this.* Those were different things, and he was nearly sure they were different.

Behind them, the wheel stopped turning.

Cael did not look back. He thought perhaps Garrik had looked up after all, once they were past and it was safe, and that perhaps it was the best Garrik could manage, and he decided to count it.

They passed the community hall, shuttered at the front, the reference room dark behind its windows and the green door at the side shut fast. Cael thought about the long table under the window and the summary on its shelf, with the four lines near the end that had started all this before it had any right to start.

Denvash was taking a great deal from him this morning, but it had never hidden the truth from him. It had left it on a shelf in a public room for any boy stubborn enough to read that far, and he felt an odd gratitude toward the room for that.

"Will you still go?" he said. "On Tuesdays."

Hesk walked several paces before he answered. "Yes. Someone has to tell Garrik when he's threading it backward."

"He threads it backward?"

"About one week in four. The biscuit woman says it's the only time the films make sense."

Cael laughed. It surprised both of them, coming out of him short and rough, and it hurt a little, and he was glad of it.

---

At the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, the lock-keeper was waiting for them.

He was a long, thin man in an oilskin coat, the one who had told Cael, months ago, *Cold water, and then not cold*. He came out of his steamed-up hut with a mug in his hand and nodded to Hesk and then looked at Cael, and at Cael's bag, and nodded again in a slightly different way.

"You'll want the upper wall," he said. "I've dropped the level for you."

The old gauge was still bolted to the stone beside the upper gate, a cracked dial in a green brass case that had been reading the water since before Cael was born. Hesk had it off in four minutes and laid it on the coping like a retired friend.

Then he held the new housing up against the wall, and Cael, without being asked, set the thick bracket against the marks the old one had left, found that two of the old bolt holes were sound and two were crumbled, chose new holes a thumb's width to the left, and drilled them by hand with the brace from the satchel while Hesk held the housing steady and said nothing at all.

It took a quarter of an hour, and the stone dust got into Cael's sleeves, and the lock-keeper drank his tea and watched with the deep contentment of a man watching other people work on his wall.

When the last bolt was in, Hesk stepped back and looked at Cael. "Call it."

"Open the upper gate," Cael said to the lock-keeper.

The lock-keeper set his mug on the coping, put his shoulder to the long beam, and walked it round. The paddles opened, and the water from the upper canal came pouring into the chamber, brown and loud, rising fast up the stone. The needle on the new dial lifted with it, smooth and true.

The whole wall shuddered under the weight of the moving water, a deep vibration Cael could feel through his boots, and he watched the bracket, not the dial, because the bracket was his, and the bracket did not move by so much as the width of a hair while the water rose to its level and the gate sighed open and the needle came to rest at the mark.

It held. He knew exactly why it held.

"That'll do," said Hesk.

The lock-keeper looked at the dial and grunted, which seemed to be his highest form of praise, and then he looked at Cael for a long moment.

"It'll be here when you come back," he said.

Cael did not know what to say to that, so he said thank you, and the lock-keeper went back to his hut with his mug and the old gauge under his arm.

The low wall stood a dozen paces up the canal path, waist-high and grey and holding nothing up, and there was nobody in front of it, because Joren would not come for his hour until after breakfast. The grit on the paving lay loose and ordinary, as it lay when nobody was standing there asking it to settle.

"One minute," Cael said, and Hesk waited, and did not ask.

When they were eight, they had found a gap between two of the top blocks, at the end nearest the canal, where the mortar had crumbled out just wide enough to slide a folded paper into. They had used it for a whole summer to leave each other messages, most of them maps to places that did not exist.

One of them, Cael remembered, a list of every word Joren knew that rhymed with *canal*, which had come to four, two of them made up. The gap was still there. He took the folded note from his pocket and slid it in deep, until only a white corner showed. Joren would see it. Joren would stand right in front of it for an hour, flat-footed, with nothing to look at but the wall.

Cael put his hand flat on the cold top of the wall for a moment, and then went back to Hesk.

"Joren," Hesk said. It was not a question.

"I didn't go and see him the day after. I said I would."

Hesk nodded slowly. "And now you've written."

"It's not the same."

"No," Hesk said, "but it's honest, and honest lasts longer." He started walking again. "He'll be all right. He's got a stance declaration."

"He's got a heel problem."

"He'll fix it. You showed him where it was."

Two streets on, a woman Cael half knew, somebody's aunt, a face from the market rather than a name, gave Hesk the ordinary nod of one neighbour to another. Then her eyes found Cael's bag, and whatever Hesk was carrying underneath his steady face, and her nod slowed and softened into something more like a held breath.

She did not say anything. Nobody, it seemed, had the right words for this. Cael was beginning to think that was not a failure on the street's part but an honest admission, because there were no right words, and people who said none were telling the truth about it.

Then they came out onto Lantern Street, which ran east toward the depot, and the light came in.

It was six o'clock, and the sun had just cleared the rooftops at the far end of the street and was coming down its whole length at a low, flat angle. It was gold, not yellow but the deep warm gold of brass that has just been polished, catching the dust from the bakeries and the smoke from the first chimneys and lighting them, so that the whole street seemed to be full of slow, bright, drifting specks.

It caught the edges of the cobbles, and the wet in a horse trough, and the side of Hesk's face, where it turned his grey beard briefly to the colour of wheat.

Cael stopped walking and looked at it, all of it, deliberately, as he would look at a page he meant to copy.

*This,* he thought. *This exact colour. I am going to keep this.*

He did not take out his notebook. Some things did not go in the notebook.

Hesk had stopped beside him, and did not ask what he was looking at, and after a moment Cael saw that he had turned to look down the street too, into the same light, with the same face. They stood there until the sun was clear of the rooftop and the gold had thinned to ordinary morning, and then they walked on.

---

The north transit depot was louder than the streets. Merchants were loading carts, and a family with far too much luggage was arguing, quietly and fiercely, about whether they had too much luggage. A team of draught horses stood steaming in their traces and their driver fed them from a nosebag and told them about his sister. In a little booth, a toll-taker was disputing a charge with a carter, and both of them had clearly had this exact dispute many times before and seemed to find it relaxing.

Two Bronze-tier practitioners in guild colours walked through the crowd with the unhurried ease of people who had never once in their lives waited in a queue, and the crowd opened for them without being asked. Cael watched their feet. Heel, flat. Heel, flat. It was the same measured walk as the Iron fighter's on the sheet.

*I'm going to watch everyone's feet for the rest of my life now,* he thought.

Hesk walked him to the far edge of the depot, where the paved yard ended and the merchant road began. The cobbles stopped in a ragged line there, as if they had simply run out of interest, and beyond them the packed earth ran east, pale and hard, between low walls toward the farmland.

They stood at the line.

Cael had imagined this moment a dozen ways over the past two days, and none of them had prepared him for how ordinary it felt. It was not smaller than he had imagined, exactly, but quieter, less like a scene than like a moment, the kind that turns out to have mattered more than it seemed to while it was happening. Around them the depot carried on.

The toll-taker and the carter reached their weekly agreement, the driver finished his story about his sister, and the family with too much luggage left one bag behind on purpose and walked off without looking at it. Cael found it almost offensive that nobody here knew this morning was different from all the others. Then, a moment later, he found it steadying, because the world was going to keep doing what it did whatever he did next, and there was something almost generous in that.

"You have the money," Hesk said. It was not a question but a checklist said aloud, because Hesk checked every load calculation twice, even when he was sure.

"Yes."

"The notebook. Not opened until the road."

"Yes."

"The bread Ressa put in."

"Yes."

"Find your feet before you find anything else."

"I remember."

"Don't trust the first person who's kind to you completely. Don't distrust them completely either. Watch, the way you already watch everything, and let the watching tell you which way to lean." Something shifted in Hesk's shoulders, the way it shifted at the bench when a repair turned out more delicate than it looked. "I know I'm repeating myself. I've had two days to say everything I wanted to say, and it turns out there isn't a version of two days that's enough."

"You could have another two."

"I could, and then another two after that, and I'd still be standing here finding things I hadn't said. That isn't a problem two more days fixes." His face held something that was trying to be a smile and was worn thin by the effort. "At some point I have to trust that what you're carrying is enough. Not because it's complete. Because it's what there is, and you're good at building on incomplete things. You've been doing it all your life."

Cael did not have an answer to that, so he did not try to make one, and Hesk nodded as if the silence had answered him anyway.

He looked for a moment as though he were going to say something else, something large, the size of what was actually happening. Cael watched him gather it and then visibly decide against it, in favour of something smaller and truer.

"Write to me," Hesk said. "Even if there's nothing to say. Especially then."

"I will."

Hesk reached out and put his hand on Cael's shoulder.

It was not a hug, not quite, but it was close enough that the difference did not matter. His grip was firmer than Cael expected, the grip of a man who worked brass with his hands all day and had never learned to make that strength any gentler than it needed to be, and in that grip Cael understood something he would not have believed if Hesk had only said it, which was that his grandfather was holding on exactly as hard as he was.

"Whatever you find out there," Hesk said. "Whatever you turn out to be able to do. You were worth raising before any of it, and you'll still be worth it no matter what it turns out to be. I need you to actually believe that. Not just hear it."

"I believe you believe it."

"That isn't quite the same thing."

"It's what I've got this morning."

Cael's voice caught on the last word, and he let it. Pretending otherwise in front of the one person who had never once asked him to perform anything suddenly seemed a waste of the little time left to be honest in.

Something in Hesk's face broke open, only a little and only for a second. It was not tears; Cael had never seen Hesk cry and did not see it now. But the cost showed plainly in his face, the face of a man doing something that cost him more than his composure had been built to cover.

"It's enough," Hesk said quietly. "It's more than enough. Go on, then. Before I decide it isn't."

Cael did not trust his voice to answer that, and so he said, "I'll write," instead, which was not the same thing, though Hesk seemed to understand it as close enough.

Hesk let go and stepped back.

Cael picked up the bag and settled the strap across his shoulder, the same shoulder Hesk had just held, and turned toward the road before he could lose the nerve the turning took. He stepped off the last cobble onto the packed earth.

He went.

He looked back once, from far enough down the road that Hesk was a shape rather than a person, a dark shape at the edge of the depot in a good wool coat, standing still, not waving, watching. Cael lifted a hand, and the shape lifted one back. Then he turned east and did not look back again, because looking back a second time would have made it harder, and he needed it to stay possible.

---

The district gate was a quarter mile on, and it was not much of a gate. It was a low stone arch where the Outer District wall met the open road, with a guard post beside it the size of a garden shed, which mostly existed so that someone could check merchant manifests. Carts were queued in front of it, and the guard on duty was working through them with the speed of a man who had done the same thing nine thousand times and expected to do it nine thousand more.

He flagged Cael down out of habit, not suspicion. "Registration."

Cael handed over his card. The guard glanced at it as he must have glanced at a hundred cards that morning, name and number and classification, and then he stopped glancing.

His eyes stayed on the stamped line. Cael watched it happen, the half-second in which the man's face went from routine to something it had not expected and had to decide, in that half-second, what to do about it. It did nothing dramatic. It did something smaller, and Cael thought more honest. It drew back, not the man's body but only his face, into the shape of someone deciding that the quickest way out of an uncomfortable thing was to make it as short as possible.

"Go on," the guard said.

He handed the card back without meeting Cael's eyes and waved him through the arch faster than he had waved the cart in front of him.

Cael walked through without saying anything. He did not look back at the guard. He wanted to, to see whether the man's face had already gone back to boredom, whether the whole thing had stopped mattering to everyone except Cael the moment his back was turned. He suspected it had. That, he thought, was the most useful part of the whole exchange: he was going to matter enormously to himself for a long time to come, and to almost everyone else, for exactly as long as it took them to finish the sentence.

Twenty paces past the arch, on the open road, with the wall falling away behind him, he stopped. He took out his notebook, because it was information, and information went in the notebook whether he liked how it felt or not.

*Gate guard, Denvash Outer gate. Saw [SHATTERED]. Didn't ask questions. Waved me through faster than routine.*

*People don't engage with what they're afraid of. They get you through the interaction as quickly as possible so they don't have to keep looking at it.*

*This is useful. Fear that wants to look away is fear that isn't paying close attention. Remember this.*

He read it over and started to close the book. Then he opened it again.

*Corollary, added a moment later, because it felt important enough to earn its own line: this will not always be true. Somewhere, eventually, I am going to meet the kind of fear that does the opposite — that looks harder, not away. I don't know yet what that looks like. I should.*

# Chapter 4 — The Record

The day after Kindling was the strangest day of all, because nothing happened in it.

No notice came, and no officer knocked. The sun came up at the usual time and lay across the kitchen table for its usual twenty minutes, Ressa's ovens went on at the usual hour, and the canal gate sighed on schedule. It was as though the whole city had agreed to pretend that yesterday had not occurred and was waiting to see whether Cael would play along.

Hesk went into the workshop after breakfast, and all morning Cael heard him in there: the vice opening and closing, files on brass, the creak of the rig's lever. Near eleven he went to the workshop door and looked in. Hesk was sitting at the bench with a gauge face in one hand and a jeweller's loupe in the other, perfectly still, not looking through the loupe at all. Cael went away again without saying anything. He was not sure Hesk had heard him come.

He spent the afternoon on the roof.

From the brick lip on the southern edge, if you leaned out a little, you could see the corner where Fen Street met the canal path, and the low wall. Joren was standing in front of it with his feet planted and his arms folded. Even from here Cael could see how still he was holding himself, flat-footed and stubborn in front of a wall that did not hold anything up. Every few minutes he unfolded his arms and looked up Fen Street, toward Cael's house, and then folded them again.

*Come by after,* Joren had said. *Tomorrow. Whatever it is.* And Cael had said he would.

He sat on the roof with his notebook on his knee and watched Joren wait, and tried several times to work out what he would say if he went down. *Hello. I got [SHATTERED]. It means I have to leave. No, I don't know where. No, I don't know what it does.

Show me how to fall down properly.* Every version he built fell apart under its own weight before it reached the end. The word was surely all over the quadrant by now, but he did not know whether Joren had heard it, and he found that he could not stand the thought of watching Joren's face find out.

So he did not go down.

Joren stood for his hour, and then for a little longer than his hour. Then he unfolded his arms, looked up Fen Street one last time, and walked away along the canal with his hands in his pockets.

Cael sat on the roof until the light changed. He had broken a promise to his oldest friend. It was not a large promise, but it was the first one he could remember breaking, and he wrote that down, because he did not want to be the kind of person who didn't.

---

The notice came on the second morning.

A courier in the grey-and-brass of the city administration brought it, knocked once, and waited exactly long enough to be polite, and Hesk answered. From the kitchen table, through the open doorway to the front room, Cael watched the courier's satchel, worn pale along the strap from going out every morning and coming back every night. He watched Hesk's signature on the receipt slip, slow and square, neither hurried nor hesitating.

Over Hesk's shoulder the courier's eyes found Cael at the table and stayed for half a second before they slid away. It was the look you give a thing you have been told about and do not know how to look at directly. When he left, his boots on the front step going down were quieter than they had been coming up.

Hesk brought the document into the kitchen and set it in the middle of the table, between the bread and the teapot. He stood looking down at it the way he looked at a block of brass stock before deciding where the first cut went.

"You should be the one to read it," he said.

The seal was dark wax pressed with the Compact's mark, a hand holding a balance, so cleanly that a machine must have done it. Cael thought, for no good reason, of the brass plate on Pellin's desk, and all that care poured into the outside of things. He broke the seal and unfolded the paper.

The language was official in the way official language works, holding its own meaning at arm's length in long sentences and passive verbs, so that everything was true and nothing was felt. He read it twice, because the first time had been like reading through water.

*By authority of the Denvash City Administration, acting under Compact Registry Statute 14, Section 9 (Non-Standard Classification, Residential Provisions):*

*The bearer of Registration 41-7843-V, classified [SHATTERED] as of this date, is hereby notified that residential access above Unranked District designation is revoked effective immediately. The bearer retains legal access to Unranked-designated residence.*

*Note: the registered residence at 14 Fen Street, Outer District, Denvash, is held under the standing of an Iron-tier practitioner (registration 22-1190-H). Continued cohabitation by a [SHATTERED]-classified individual may affect the standing-holder's guild status pending Compact review. The standing-holder is advised to consider residential arrangements accordingly.*

*This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer. It constitutes a residential and administrative provision only.*

He read the last part a third time. *This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer.* It was a strange thing to put in writing, because you only wrote that down if a great many people needed to be told it before they would believe it.

He set the paper on the table. The paper was good, heavier than the certificate had been. When the light from the window caught it he could see a watermark in the corner, a little official flourish, as though someone somewhere had decided that this document deserved nice paper. He almost laughed, and it did not come out.

Then he read the note about the residence again, *the standing-holder is advised to consider residential arrangements accordingly*, and understood, not the words, but the machine behind them.

"They're not expelling me," he said. "They're expelling you. From having me."

Hesk had come round to read it over his shoulder, and his face did what it had done in the side room two days ago, going still in the prepared way.

"That's the mechanism," he said. "It's built that way on purpose. It's easier to make a family choose than to be seen putting a fourteen-year-old out on the road."

"That's convenient."

"Very." Hesk set his hand flat on the table beside the notice. "It isn't a request, Cael. Don't spend yourself being angry at the choice. There isn't one."

"There's an Unranked quarter here," Cael said. "Down by the tanneries. The notice says I keep legal access to Unranked residence. I could live there and come to the workshop in the day."

"You could live there," Hesk agreed. "Inside the same walls, three streets from the Compact's district office, which checks that quarter every month, with every gate in this city pulling your record each time you pass it. And a review doesn't count houses, Cael. It counts people. A [SHATTERED] boy sleeping by the tanneries and working at his grandfather's bench all day is still a boy at 14 Fen Street, as far as the review is concerned." He shook his head. "In Denvash, the record is always waiting for you at the next gate. Ardenmere's Unranked District doesn't have one."

Cael looked at the line about guild status, *pending Compact review*. He thought about the years Hesk had spent being exactly what he was: a competent, quiet, reliable Iron-tier instrument-maker, with canal gauges and survey levels and a clock inside a walking stick, and a name on the guild roll that nobody had ever had reason to look at twice. Three lines of print could put all of that in question, over something Cael had not chosen and could not have stopped.

"You could refuse," he said.

It was not really a suggestion. He needed to hear how it sounded out loud, and it sounded exactly as impossible as he had known it would.

"I could. And in three months my registration gets reviewed, and in six I lose the workshop. In a year we're both in some Unranked District with no income and no standing, and no way for me to help you that isn't worse than this." Hesk said it without heat; it was a calculation, not an argument. "This isn't a fight I win by refusing. It's a fight I win by making sure you land somewhere with something in your hands."

"I don't want you losing anything because of me."

"I'm not losing anything because of you."

Hesk's voice did not rise. It went flatter and steadier, and that made it land harder than shouting would have.

"I'm doing exactly what I decided to do a long time ago," he said, "which is whatever it takes. This is the *whatever.* Don't make it smaller by apologising for it."

Cael did not have an answer.

They sat with the notice between them on the table, and neither of them moved it. Outside, the ordinary sounds of the street went on without them: a cart, a vendor calling. Three doors down, Ressa's flour being unloaded off a wagon sack by sack while somebody counted the sacks in a loud, bored voice. Cael found the ordinariness of it almost unbearable, and then, a moment later, steadying. The sacks would keep coming off the wagon and the count would keep going up, and whatever else happened, somebody would bake bread tomorrow.

"What happens to the workshop," he said at last, "if they actually review you?"

"Nothing today, and possibly nothing for months. The Compact moves slowly when slow suits it, which is most of the time." Hesk's voice had come back to its usual level, the one he used for load figures and hard truths alike. "I won't pretend there's no risk. There is. I've decided the risk is mine, and I decided that a long time before this morning."

"When?"

"When I took you in."

He said it simply, as if it were the least interesting fact in the room.

"You don't raise a child and keep the right to change your mind about the cost later," Hesk said. "That isn't raising. That's something else. I didn't do something else."

---

He went to the district record office in the afternoon.

It was three streets from the house, and he had been there twice before, both times as a curious boy researching something that might never apply to him; it applied to him now. The building was older than the certification office and did not care how it looked. Its stone had never been refaced, and its front steps had worn into a shallow dip in the middle from centuries of feet. Inside, it smelled of paper and dust and ink that had dried long ago and had no plans to fade.

The young woman at the front desk looked barely older than Alis's brother, and when Cael gave her his registration number, her pen stopped moving. It was only for a moment before she caught it and smoothed it away. But he saw it, and he saw something else cross her face just before she smoothed that away too, something that looked very much like sympathy.

She did not ask why he had come. She took a key from a hook and led him down a passage to a door at the back and unlocked it.

"Historical classification archive. The full summary's on the second shelf from the window, bound in green." She hesitated. "The reading table's by the window. The light's better there."

"Thank you."

She went back to her desk and left the door open behind her. That told him how fast information moved through a system that was supposed to be discreet. She had known his number without being told, when three mornings ago, before the circle, his number had meant nothing to anyone.

The archive was one long room, shelved from floor to ceiling, with a scarred reading table and a single chair under a window that faced north, as archive windows always seemed to, as though direct sunlight might hurt something. Dust turned slowly in the pale light, and he watched it for a moment before he made himself start. The full summary was where she had said, thick and green and heavy, and its spine cracked when he opened it, a small dry sound that seemed much too loud in the quiet room.

He worked back from the index and found the section, not where he expected it but near the very end, under a subheading he had never seen in the community hall's copy:

*Deprecated and Non-Standard Designations, Pre-Compact Era Holdovers.*

He read the subheading once. Then he turned the page.

The entry here was not four lines but several paragraphs, in the same tight, careful print.

*[SHATTERED]: Designation assigned where Arbiter evaluation finds practitioner architecture that does not resolve to a single recognised Path. The coupling between practitioner and Path framework presents as broken or incomplete. Ability manifestation in such cases has historically been irregular and difficult to predict, and practitioner risk has been assessed as elevated.*

*Of the four (4) recorded instances, disposition is as follows: three (3) practitioners are recorded as deceased within weeks of classification, cause of death listed as "practice-related incident" in Compact records. One (1) practitioner's disposition is recorded as unknown, last registry update dated 32 years prior to the present record.*

He read the last paragraph again, and then a third time, because it was doing something he needed time to take in.

He had known the number four the way you know a fact you read once and set on a shelf, and until this moment he had never turned it into people. Now he did. Four people with names, who had each stepped out of a circle somewhere and heard what he had heard, and three of them had been dead within weeks.

One was not accounted for. This was not a policy or a warning; it was a record of what had actually happened to the only other people who had ever stood where he had stood, with their feet in a worn circle, counting.

*Practice-related incident.*

He looked at the phrase for a long time, because it was doing a great deal of work. It could mean that they had died trying to use something they did not understand, or it could mean that someone had made sure of it, and he did not have the information to know which. What he suspected, without any evidence yet, was that the person who wrote it down had cared which one was believed, and that this was the only part of the sentence he could trust.

He took out his notebook and copied the entry word for word, the subheading too, in handwriting that came out neater than it needed to be, which was its own kind of tell. Then he sat with the green volume closed under his hands and did not read anything. He thought about the clerk at the front, young and briefly sorry before she caught herself.

He thought about how many people had sat in this chair over however many years the archive had stood, reading something that applied to someone they loved, while the room gave no sign of remembering any of them, having only its shelves and its dust and the patience of paper, which outlasts everyone who ever needs it.

He put the book back exactly as he had found it, its spine level with its neighbours. At the front desk the clerk looked up as he passed and opened her mouth as if to say something, and then closed it and nodded, and he nodded back.

The street outside looked exactly as it had two hours before, which was almost the worst part. The vendors were still calling, and a cart creaked past with a load of new-cut lumber that smelled of resin, and the world had once again declined to notice that his had moved. He did not decide on the walk home how he felt about any of it.

He decided something else instead: he was not going to be the fifth entry in that book, and it was so easy to decide that it worried him, because deciding things on a walk home was easy and staying alive long enough to make them true was going to be the hard part.

---

Hesk was in the workshop when he got back, with a half-built housing clamped in the vice and a tray of small tools beside it. He looked up as Cael came in, read something in his face, and put the tools down.

"You went to the archive."

"Yes."

"You found the full entry."

"Yes." Cael sat on the stool across the bench from him, the stool he had sat on since his feet did not reach the floor; they reached now. "Three of them died. One, nobody knows."

Hesk was quiet a moment. "I know."

"You knew before, and you didn't tell me."

"No." Hesk met his eyes and did not look away from it. "I told you what you needed to make decisions. I didn't tell you the thing that would only make you afraid without helping you do anything about it. I won't pretend that was a neutral choice, because it wasn't. I made a judgement, and you're allowed to think it was the wrong one."

Cael found, a little to his surprise, that he did not think it was wrong, or not completely. It was the choice of someone trying to hold two things at once: the job of getting Cael ready for what was coming, and the instinct not to hand him more weight than the day required, and those two things did not fit together neatly. They hardly ever did. Hesk's hands went back to the housing in the vice and moved through the work without hurry.

They were a craftsman's hands, which had done this motion ten thousand times and could keep doing it, steady and exact, while the rest of him held a conversation that mattered far more than the instrument ever would. That steadiness had always been a comfort, and it was a comfort now, and it took him a moment to see that the comfort and the anger were both there at once, side by side, neither cancelling the other.

"I need you to tell me things now," Cael said. "As they come. Not managed, and not timed for when you think I'm ready."

"All right." Hesk set down the file. "Then I need to tell you something now, while we're being plain."

"Okay."

Hesk looked at the housing, and then back at Cael. "The fourth record, the one with no disposition. Thirty-two years ago." He stopped, choosing the next words with the care of a man who had rehearsed them for years without knowing when he would have to say them. "I was twenty-two. I was doing two years with the Compact's regional logistics division, not fighting, just supply routing, the kind of posting they give to competent Iron-tiers who don't ask questions. I was there when that file was opened. I saw the beginning of it. I didn't see the end."

Cael had gone very still. "What happened to them?"

"I don't know." He said it plainly, and it had the weight of a true answer that had cost something to give. "That isn't an evasion. I mean I don't know. The case was reassigned, pulled out of regional oversight into something higher, something I didn't have clearance for, and the file went quiet. I asked about it twice, and both times I was told it wasn't my concern, in the tone that means *stop asking.*"

He let out a long breath.

"I left the division eighteen months later," he said. "I've wondered about it for thirty-two years, and I don't have an answer. I have a name I've never told anyone, and a promise I made myself the day I mustered out. I think you should know both of those exist, even though I can't finish the story for you yet."

"Will you tell me the name?"

"When you're older."

Cael opened his mouth, and Hesk lifted a hand.

"Not because I don't trust you. Because a name like that is a thread, and I want you strong enough to pull it without it pulling you first." He lowered his hand. "That isn't a managed timeline, Cael. It's a real condition. When you meet it, you'll know."

Cael did not write any of it down, not yet, and not in front of him. He held it instead, and turned it over as he had turned over the third exchange on the sheet in the community hall. Right now the shape of it mattered more than what was inside: a name never told, a file opened and then taken somewhere higher, and a promise.

"Okay," he said at last. "I believe you."

Hesk nodded once and picked his tools back up, and for a while they sat in the workshop together without talking. Hesk filed, and Cael watched the brass dust gather in a little gold drift on the bench, and it was, as it had always been, a way of saying several things at once.

---

That evening, after the light had gone out of the window and Hesk had gone quiet over his book in the front room, Cael packed.

It did not take long, because he did not own much: clothes, three notebooks, and a handful of small tools he had made himself in the workshop, more for remembering than for using. Among them were a file with a handle he had turned on Hesk's lathe at ten and a pair of fine tweezers ground from spring steel that closed a hair off true. He packed them anyway.

He held each notebook before he put it in the bag. The first was three years old, its spine cracked and its cover gone soft. It was full of a younger boy's observations so earnest that he could not be embarrassed by them: *Garrik says a bearing is happiest when it is doing nothing. The grey dog does not like the colour blue.* The second carried the year he had first noticed the gap between the Outer District and the Inner one.

The five pages about the wall had felt like an essay when he wrote them and felt now more like a warning he had written to himself without knowing who it was for. The third was the current one, with eleven descriptions of Kindling and two stars and a Stone Path declaration in Joren's round hand near the back. Folded into its back pages was a copy of a registry entry about four people he had never met and was never going to stop thinking about. He packed them in order, oldest at the bottom, the way you pack something you mean to keep adding to.

Then he sat on the bed with a single loose page from the back of the current notebook and wrote to Joren. It took a long time, and he tore up two versions before he kept the third, which was short.

*Joren —*

*I didn't come by. I said I would and I didn't. That was wrong of me and I'm sorry. I couldn't work out what to say.*

*It was [SHATTERED]. You can look it up in the hall. Don't read the green book in the record office. It isn't worth it.*

*I have to leave Denvash. Hesk's standing depends on it. I'm going east. I'll write when I know where I am.*

*Keep your heels down.*

*— C.*

He folded it in four and put it in his coat pocket.

Hesk came in as he was tying the bag. He was carrying three things, and he set them on the bed one at a time with the care of a man who had planned this for a long time and would not admit for how long.

The first was a cloth pouch, tied at the neck, heavier than Cael had expected. When he looked inside, the coins were all small ones, worn marks and half-marks, the kind that are put by one or two at a time rather than drawn out all at once.

The second was a book small enough to fit in a pocket, bound by hand in leather gone soft at the corners. A cord was wound round it to keep it shut, worn smooth in the place where it was always tied. Cael reached for it, and Hesk did not stop him, but Hesk did not let go of it right away either, and for a moment they both had a hand on it.

"Not tonight," Hesk said. "Read it on the road. It'll make more sense with some distance between you and this house."

Cael nodded, and Hesk let go, and Cael put the book in the bag without opening it.

The third thing was not an object. Hesk sat on the edge of the bed and waited, as if he knew something was coming and had decided to let it come.

"Joren's got a declaration," Cael said, to the bag. He had not meant to say it. "Alis can tell when her mother has a headache. The twins at the office came out holding certificates. Even the boy who didn't know if he wanted Force came out with Force." He pulled the strap tight. "Everyone I know is Rank 1 of something, and they've all started. I've got a word. I'm behind before I've begun."

"No," Hesk said.

It was not gentle. It was the voice he used when a measurement had been read wrong, and Cael looked up.

"Rank 1 of something means somebody already wrote down where it goes," Hesk said. "Joren's Path has a book. He'll follow it, and it's a good book, and in ten years he'll be wherever it says. Yours doesn't have a book. Nobody's ahead of you on it, because nobody's on it." He put a hand flat on the bag between them. "You're not behind, Cael. You're early. There's a difference."

Cael looked at the bag, with its money and notebooks and crooked tools and the leather book he was not allowed to open. It was a strange kind of complete: everything he owned that mattered, and none of it told him what he was going to become.

"I'll write," he said.

"You'd better."

Hesk's voice caught on the second word, very slightly, and he covered it by getting up and going to the window and working the latch, which did not need working. He opened it and shut it twice. Then he stood with his back to the room and his hand on the frame.

"Long walk tomorrow," he said, to the window. "Get some sleep."

He went out and left the door open a hand's width, the way he had left it when Cael was small and afraid of the dark, which he had not been for years.

Cael did not close it.

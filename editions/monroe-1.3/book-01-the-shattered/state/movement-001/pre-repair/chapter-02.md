# Chapter 2 — Tuesdays

Hesk found him on the roof in the afternoon.

The roof was where Cael went when he needed sight lines. That was what they called it, so that neither of them had to say what it really was. The access hatch was at the top of a ladder in the back hall. Beyond it was a flat stretch of tar and gravel, a chimney, a water tank, and a low brick lip around the southern edge where you could sit with your legs inside and look out over the northern quadrant.

He had been up there for two hours. The notebook was open on his knee. He had written nothing. It was mostly something to hold.

The hatch creaked. Hesk came up the ladder one-handed, which he could do, because in the other hand he had two cups of tea balanced on a saucer. He did this when he had something to say and was still choosing how to say it. Cael had worked that out at nine. He had never told Hesk he knew. It seemed like the kind of thing that would stop working if he did.

Hesk sat down next to him on the brick lip and handed him a cup.

They sat. Below them the afternoon moved through the streets. Carts. A cooper rolling a barrel along the gutter with a stick. Two practitioners in Copper armbands walking a delivery of lumber, one of them lifting his end with no visible effort, the other pretending she didn't need help with hers. A knot of children ran between houses shouting about nothing.

And the grey dog.

It lived somewhere on this block. Nobody knew where. It was large and grey and shaggy, with one ear that stood up and one that had given up, and it appeared on the street several times a day to bark at things only it could see. Cael had once spent a week trying to follow it home. He had lost it every time at the same corner.

It came out now, crossed the intersection below them at a steady trot, and barked twice at a drainpipe.

"Joren seems better," Hesk said.

"He is better. He's working at it." Cael turned his cup. "I sat him down in the street this morning."

Hesk's eyebrows went up.

"He asked me to," Cael said. "He's got a stance declaration. Rank 1, Stone. You can't move him while his feet are planted. But his heel comes up when he talks."

"His heel."

"At the end of sentences. When he wants them to land."

Hesk drank some tea and thought about this.

"He'll fix that," Hesk said.

"He fixed it twice before we stopped." Cael looked down at the street. "He's the right temperament for Stone, actually. Patient. He doesn't mind being pushed."

Hesk made a sound in his throat.

Cael knew that sound. He had grown up with it. Hesk had at least four versions, and this one was the one that meant *I have something specific, and I'm deciding where to set it down.*

Cael waited.

"You're not Joren," Hesk said.

"No."

"Whatever happens tomorrow—"

"Hesk."

Hesk stopped. He looked at the street. Then he said, more quietly, "—we'll figure it out."

Cael looked at him. Hesk kept looking at the street. It was either a deliberate choice to give Cael room, or it was just Hesk being careful. With Hesk, those were often the same thing.

"I know," Cael said.

Hesk nodded. Below, the grey dog barked at someone's window, and the window opened, and someone said something short to the dog, and the window shut.

"I talked to the district record office," Hesk said.

Cael kept his face still. "About what?"

"About non-standard classifications. The procedure. What happens."

Cael knew what he meant. He meant what happened when a Kindling produced something the standard system could not process. Cael had looked into this. He had found the footnote at the bottom of the same summary page as the four-line entry. *Practitioners presenting non-standard results at Kindling should report to the nearest Compact district office within forty-eight hours for standard compliance processing.* He had looked up what standard compliance processing involved. He had written it down. Then he had crossed it out, which he never did.

"And?" he said.

"It's manageable," Hesk said. "Whatever the result is. There are provisions."

Cael opened his notebook and wrote *there are provisions.* He did not yet know what it meant. But Hesk did not say things he did not mean, and Hesk had gone to the record office and asked. You did not ask about procedures for something you thought would never happen.

"How long have you been thinking about this?" Cael asked.

Hesk's face stayed level. "A while."

"Since my preliminary assessment."

"Before that."

Cael looked at him. Hesk looked at the street. The grey dog came back the other way, slower, as if it had forgotten something.

"You should have told me," Cael said. He was not angry. It was just the correct statement.

"There was nothing to tell until there was something to tell."

"I've been worried about it."

"I know."

"By myself."

Hesk was quiet for so long that Cael thought he might not answer. Then he said, "Yes. I know. I'm sorry for that." He turned his cup on the brick. "I didn't want to make a thing real before it was."

Cael turned that over. It was exactly the kind of thing he would have done himself, if they had been the other way round. That was not as comforting as it should have been.

"What do you actually know?" he said. "About my assessment. Not what you think. What you know."

Hesk was quiet again, longer this time.

"Your preliminary assessment showed irregular coupling patterns," he said finally. "The assessor called it uncommon variation. She wasn't wrong to call it that. But there are different kinds of uncommon." He paused. "The kind yours showed—I've seen it before. Once. A long time ago."

Cael had the pencil in his hand. He did not write anything.

"Who?"

Hesk shook his head. It was not *I won't tell you.* It was *not this story, not today.*

"It'll be clearer tomorrow," he said. "One way or the other."

"And if it's—"

"Then we figure it out." Hesk said it the way he set a part into a rig. Not hoping. Decided. "I mean that, Cael. Whatever it is."

Cael believed him.

He believed him the way he believed a load calculation Hesk had shown his work on. Not because he had to. Because the numbers came from somewhere, and Hesk had always shown where.

They sat until the light changed. The carts thinned. The children went in. The dog made one more circuit of the block and was not seen again. The noise of the district softened into the hour before dinner, when everything sounds slightly farther away than it is.

"Tea's cold," Hesk said. He looked into his cup as if it had disappointed him personally.

"You let it get cold."

"I did." He did not get up to make more. "Come down when you're ready. I'll start dinner."

He went down the ladder. He left Cael on the roof a while longer. Cael understood that as its own kind of gift. Nobody was asking him to look ready before he was.

---

Dinner was rice with whatever was left in the cold box. Hesk cooked like a man who had lived alone for a long time before Cael arrived, and who still thought of a meal as a problem with a solution. It was always adequate. Tonight Cael barely tasted it.

Hesk put the radio on.

It was a small brown set on the shelf by the stove, with a cracked dial and one knob that only worked if you pressed it in while you turned it. Hesk listened to it most evenings. Tonight it was the weekly Path athletics programme from the regional service. Two men with large voices were discussing the circuit season the way some people discuss the weather, at length, with strong opinions and no power to change anything.

"—and of course the Ardenmere quarter-final is the one everyone's still talking about," said one. "Third exchange. If you weren't there, I can't describe it to you."

"You've described it to me four times," said the other.

Cael put his fork down.

"The Wind Path practitioner," he said. "From Ardenmere. They've been on about her all month."

"Third exchange," Hesk said, without looking up from his rice.

"She did something you can't follow from the radio."

"She dropped her stance before the commitment." Hesk's tone was mild. "It looks wrong. It bought her the angle."

Cael stared at him.

"How do you know that?"

Hesk ate a forkful of rice. He chewed it. He swallowed.

"I watched the community hall broadcast," he said. "On Tuesday."

There was a pause.

Cael had not known that Hesk went to the community hall on Tuesdays. He had thought Hesk went to the guild house on Tuesday evenings to look over the trade notices. He had never asked. It had never occurred to him there was anything to ask.

He opened his notebook beside his plate and wrote, in the margin, *ask Hesk about Tuesdays,* and underlined it twice.

Hesk watched him do it.

"You could just ask," Hesk said.

"I'm asking."

"You're writing."

"Writing is how I ask."

Hesk set down his fork. He looked at the clock over the stove. It was a clock he had made, a plain round face with the hours marked in brass dots. The hands said ten past seven.

"What day is it?" Hesk said.

Cael looked at him. Then at the clock. Then back.

"Tuesday."

"The reel starts at half past." Hesk pushed back his chair. "Get your coat."

---

The community hall stood at the end of Chandler Street, two blocks from the canal. It was a long brick building with a slate roof that had been patched so many times it looked like a quilt. By day it held the reference room, the district notice board, a small kitchen, and a hall with a wooden floor where people held weddings, funerals and the occasional meeting about drains.

Cael knew the reference room better than his own bedroom. He had never once been in the hall after dark.

Hesk did not go in by the front. He went down the side of the building to a narrow door near the back, painted green, with a brass handle worn bright. He knocked twice, paused, and knocked once.

"You have a knock," Cael said.

"Everyone has a knock."

"You have a *secret* knock."

"It isn't secret. It's polite." Hesk did not look at him. "Garrik's hearing isn't what it was. Two and one means it's somebody who knows to come in."

The door opened. Garrik looked out.

Garrik was seventy, or near it, with arms like a bargeman and a white beard trimmed square. He was a retired Copper-tier, and he had fixed carts on Fen Street since before Hesk had moved there. He had once spent an entire afternoon explaining wheel bearings to Cael because Cael had asked, and because Garrik loved being asked. Cael had been eight. He still remembered every word.

Garrik's face went through several things at once when he saw who was with Hesk.

"Well," he said. "Finally."

"He found out," Hesk said.

"Took him long enough. Clever boy like that." Garrik stepped back to let them in. "Come in, come in, you're letting the warm out. Reel's threaded. And the good biscuits came."

Inside, the back of the hall had been made into a small room with a curtain. There were perhaps a dozen folding chairs. Eight of them had people in them. All were old. All looked up when Cael came in, and all looked at Hesk, and then back at Cael, with the same expression. It was the look of a club that had been waiting, a little impatiently, for a new member to be old enough.

At the front of the room hung a white sheet, pinned flat to a wooden frame. At the back stood a projector on a little iron cart. It was tall and black and covered in brass fittings, with two big reels mounted on arms above it like ears.

"The circuit office in the regional seat records the main bouts," Garrik said, patting the machine as if it were a horse. "Sends the reels round the district halls. We get them a week behind. We're the only hall in the quadrant still runs a projector." He said this with enormous pride. "I keep her going. Rebuilt the lamp housing twice. Your grandfather made me the gauge for the tension arm."

Cael looked at Hesk.

"How long?" he said.

"How long what?"

"How long have you been coming here?"

Hesk took off his coat and hung it on the back of a chair. He sat down. He did not answer right away.

"A while," he said.

That was the same answer he had given on the roof. Cael heard it, and filed it, and did not yet know where to file it.

A tiny old woman in a knitted hat held out a tin of biscuits. Cael took one. It was very good. She watched him eat it with satisfaction.

"Sit by your grandfather," she said. "He's the best watcher in the room. Don't tell Garrik."

"I can hear you."

"You can't, actually. That's rather the point of you."

Garrik turned off the lamps. The projector clattered and woke. A beam of white light leaned across the room, full of dust, and the sheet lit up.

---

The reel was the Ardenmere semi-final.

The picture was grey and flickered at the edges, and there was no sound except the clatter of the projector. It showed a square of packed sand under a high roof, with a crowd around it so thick it looked like a dark wall. Two figures walked out onto the sand from opposite sides.

One was a woman in a pale sleeveless tunic. She was lean and light on her feet.

"The Wind Path," Garrik said, from the back. "The one from the quarter-final."

The other was a man nearly twice her size, broad and heavy, with his hair tied back.

"Iron Path," said Hesk quietly. "Iron-tier, by the circuit rating. Rank 6 or so."

"How can you tell?"

"Watch him walk."

Cael watched him walk. The man walked like a man. That was all Cael could see.

"Not his body," Hesk said. "His feet. Where they land."

Cael looked at the feet.

The man's feet landed heel first, then rolled flat, every step the same length. There was no hurry in them. They looked like they had walked across a thousand floors exactly like this one.

"He's measuring the sand," Cael said slowly.

Hesk turned his head a little. In the flicker from the screen, Cael saw the edge of a smile.

The fighters touched hands. They stepped back. The bout began.

It was nothing like the radio. On the radio, everything happened in sentences. Here, almost nothing happened for what felt like a very long time. The two of them circled. The woman stayed out of reach. The man followed, slow and patient. Now and then one of them moved a hand and the other answered it, and that was all.

"Nothing's happening," Cael said, under his breath.

"Everything's happening," said Hesk. "Watch his hips."

"His hips?"

"Hands lie. Shoulders lie. A good fighter lies with everything above the belt." Hesk kept his eyes on the screen. "The hips have to go where the weight goes. And the weight has to go where the strike goes. So you watch the hips, and the feet under them, and you'll see the strike before the hand does."

Cael watched the man's hips.

On the screen, the man's shoulders dipped right. His hand flicked out right. The woman leaned away left.

His hips had not moved.

"That was a fake," Cael said.

"It was a question," Hesk said. "He wanted to see which way she leans. Now he knows."

The first exchange ended with nobody touched. The fighters reset to their marks.

"First exchange is a handshake," Hesk said. "You learn the other person's distance. Their reach. How they stand. You give away as little as you can and you take as much as they'll let you."

"And the second?"

"Watch."

The second exchange was faster. The man pressed forward. The woman gave ground, and gave ground, and then suddenly did not give ground but stepped across him, quick as a bird. Her hand came up toward his ribs. He turned. Her hand found only his forearm, and he swept her away with it, not hard, almost gently, and she skidded back across the sand on her feet.

A murmur went through the old people in the chairs. Somebody said, "Oh, nicely."

"Second exchange is questions," Hesk said. "Real ones. Each of them tries something they think will work. Not the best thing they have. Something they can afford to lose."

"Why not the best thing?"

"Because then the other one's seen it."

Cael sat back. He looked at the screen. The two figures were at their marks again, breathing, watching each other.

"So the third—"

"The third exchange," said Hesk, "is where they tell you the truth."

The third exchange began.

The man came forward. It was the same pressure as before. Cael watched his hips. They were square, and his feet were rolling heel to flat, heel to flat. He was going to commit. Cael could feel it coming the way you can feel a stair under your foot in the dark.

"There," Cael said. "There, he's going to—"

On the screen, the woman began to drop.

It was the move from the quarter-final. Cael knew it as soon as he saw it. She let her stance fall away from under her, all at once, as if her knees had forgotten their job. It looked like a mistake. It looked like she was falling. Her weight went low and sideways, under the line of his strike—

And the man did not commit.

His hips stayed back. His front foot, which should have landed, did not land. He let his own strike die halfway, and he stepped, not forward, but down and across. The woman came up out of her drop exactly where she had come up in the quarter-final.

He was already there.

His forearm met her shoulder. Not a blow. A wall. She came up into it with all her own speed, and it spun her, and she went down on the sand on one knee with her hand flat to keep herself from falling further.

The bout official stepped in. The woman stayed down on one knee for a moment. Then she stood, and touched her fist to her chest, and bowed her head to the man.

The old people in the folding chairs let out their breath together. The woman in the knitted hat said, "Oh, *poor* thing." Garrik said, "Knew it. Knew he'd seen it."

Cael sat very still.

"He watched the quarter-final," he said.

"Everyone watched the quarter-final," Hesk said. "Everyone in this room did. So did he. More carefully than we did, I expect." He leaned back in his folding chair. "A trick works once. After that, it's a habit. And a habit is something somebody can plan for."

"But she must have known he'd seen it."

"She did. I think she believed she was faster than his knowing." Hesk shrugged. "Sometimes you are. That night, she wasn't."

The reel ran on. There was a short bit of the crowd and some officials. Then the sheet went white, and the end of the film flapped round and round on its reel with a noise like a trapped bird, until Garrik switched it off.

The lamps came on. Everyone blinked.

"Well," said the woman in the knitted hat, turning to Cael, "what did you think?"

Cael opened his mouth and did not know what to say.

He had been to the community hall perhaps five hundred times. He had read every summary in its reference room. He had read about Paths and tiers and ranks and guilds and districts, and he had thought he understood them. He had not understood anything. He had been reading the hands.

"I thought," he said slowly, "that I've been watching people wrong my whole life."

There was a short silence. Then the old people laughed, all of them, a warm crackling laugh that filled the little room.

"Oh, he's one of us," said the biscuit woman. "Garrik, he's one of us. Give him another biscuit."

---

They stayed for the second reel. It was two Bronze-tier Blade fighters, very fast, and Cael could not follow it at all, and he did not mind. He watched the hips. Twice he saw a strike come before it came, and once he said "there" out loud, half a second early, and Hesk said nothing and did not need to.

They walked home after nine. The streets were empty and cold, and the lamps on Chandler Street made long yellow pools with darkness between them. Their breath went up white.

"How long?" Cael said again.

Hesk walked three lamps before he answered.

"Some years."

"Why?"

"Because I like it." That was true. Cael could hear that it was true. Hesk walked one more lamp. "And because I wanted to understand how people move when they have a Path in them. How a body changes, once it's carrying something."

"Why?"

Hesk did not answer that.

Cael did not ask again. He had the feeling of a door he had not known was there, standing slightly open. He did not want to push it. He was afraid of what it would weigh.

They turned onto Fen Street. Their house was dark, the workshop windows black.

"Hesk."

"Yes."

"The thing you saw before. The irregular coupling. The person." Cael stopped walking. "How did it go for them?"

Hesk stopped too. He stood under the last lamp before their door, with his hands in his coat pockets, and he looked at the ground.

He was quiet long enough that Cael thought this was going to be one of the things he did not answer.

"It was complicated," he said. "I know how it started. I don't know how it ended." He lifted his head. "I've spent longer than you've been alive not knowing the rest of it."

He went in and lit the lamp in the kitchen.

After a moment, Cael followed him in.

---

He woke at two in the morning and did not know why.

The house was silent. Hesk had gone to bed an hour after him; Cael had heard the reading lamp click off in the other room. The street outside had the deep stillness of a district where most people worked an early shift. Somewhere far off, the canal gate sighed once, letting the night water through.

He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

There was a water stain in the northeast corner, from a winter four years ago when the gutter had frozen. It was shaped a little like a boot. He had looked at it for four years.

Tomorrow the Arbiter would come.

He did not know what that meant. Nobody could tell you, because it was different for everybody, and because the people who had done it could only tell you after. *Sunlight. A door opening. Like remembering something you never knew. Brief. Heavy.* Eleven people had tried. One of them had not even tried.

He thought about Alis on the step of the certification office, folding her certificate in half and then flattening it out again.

He thought about Joren lying on his back in the street, laughing at the sky, and then not laughing.

He thought about the woman on the sand, dropping her stance under a strike that was not coming. Rising out of it into a forearm. Touching her fist to her chest.

He thought about Hesk, who had gone to the record office and asked about provisions. Who had seen it once, a long time ago. Who had sat on a folding chair in the dark every Tuesday for some years, studying how a body moves when it carries a Path, and had never said why.

*I know how it started. I don't know how it ended.*

Cael sat up.

His notebook was on the shelf beside the bed, where it always was. He took it down. He could not see the page. It did not matter. He had written this before, in other words and other notebooks, in the margins of other things. He did not need to see the page to write it.

*whatever it is, we figure it out.*

He closed the book and put it back on the shelf.

Then he lay down again. The house made its small night sounds around him: a joint in the old timber easing, the tick of the cooling stove downstairs, the patient breathing of a place that thinks nobody is listening. He had grown up inside those sounds. He knew them the way he knew his own name.

He listened to them for a long time.

Somewhere in the listening, without deciding to, he slept.

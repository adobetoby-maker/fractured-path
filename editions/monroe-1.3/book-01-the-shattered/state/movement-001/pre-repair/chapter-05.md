# Chapter 5 — The Six O'Clock Light

He woke before it was light and lay still, doing what he always did on days that mattered. He took inventory.

Ceiling. The boot-shaped stain in the northeast corner. The bag by the door, packed and tied, waiting with the patience of an object that knows what it is for. The door to the hall, still open a hand's width.

From the kitchen came the sound of Hesk already up. The kettle being filled. Not banged down on the stove; set there. It was the sound of a man being careful not to hurry something he did not want to end.

Cael stayed in bed a little longer than the inventory needed.

He listened to the house. The second floorboard from the kitchen door creaked when Hesk crossed it. The tap ran and stopped. A cupboard closed with a soft, deliberate click. That was the cupboard by the stove; its latch had worked loose two winters ago, and if you shut it firmly it rattled for a full minute afterward, so Hesk always closed it gently, with two fingers. Cael knew this house's sounds the way he knew the streets outside it. Completely. Without ever having had to think about it.

He was thinking about it now. He supposed that was what leaving was. You noticed what you knew because you were about to stop being able to use it.

He got up.

Dressing took longer than it should have. There was nothing to decide. Everything else was packed. But his hands kept doing small unnecessary things. Smoothing a sleeve. Re-tying a lace that was tied. He let them. They seemed to know something he did not want to say out loud, which was that he would like this part to last.

---

Hesk had made the kind of breakfast that was not about food.

Bread from Ressa's, still warm, which meant Hesk had been to the bakery before it was properly open. The good preserves. The plum ones, from the jar on the high shelf that only came down for birthdays and first frosts. And tea made the careful way, with the brass spoon, steeped the full time.

Neither of them said anything about it. They did not need to.

"Merchant road runs east from the north depot," Hesk said, over the tea. He used the voice he used for practical information, because practical information was easier to say than the other kind. "Two days on foot at a steady pace. Less if a cart takes you partway for a fare. There's a waystation about a day out. Low timber building, south side of the road, a lamp over the door. Sleep there."

"I read the road notes at the record office."

"I know you did. I'm telling you anyway." A small dry almost-smile. "Some things go in better heard than read."

"Ardenmere's gate opens at dawn and closes at full dark," Cael said. "Don't arrive after dark on the first night."

"There. You see? Heard it, and now you can say it back."

"I could say it back before."

"Now you can say it back in my voice."

Cael looked at him. "Is that going in the notebook? Under *things I told you that you already knew*?"

"It might." Hesk did not quite smile, but something in his face loosened. "There's a way of doing this morning where I say all the practical things and none of the other things. Because I've had more practice with the practical ones. I know that's what I'm doing. I'm doing it anyway. The practical things are true too, and you'll need them on the road whether I manage the rest or not."

"You don't have to manage the rest."

"I know." He looked into his tea. "I'm going to try anyway."

Cael ate the bread. It tasted exactly the way it always tasted. That seemed wrong somehow. Everything else had changed. The bread had not even noticed. He chewed more slowly than usual. He did not admit to himself why.

When they had finished, Hesk got up and went into the workshop. He was gone for a minute. When he came back, he had something small in his closed hand.

He put it on the table in front of Cael and took his hand away.

It was the bracket. The lock gauge bracket, the first one. The one that had failed at less than twice working load with that small sound like a knuckle. It still had its faint new curve, as if it were considering a bow.

Cael looked at it. Then he looked up.

"The drawer," he said.

*Springs, do not use.* The ones that taught him something.

"Everyone needs one," Hesk said. "You'll have a bench of your own someday. You'll want something in the drawer." He sat back down. "Start with that."

Cael picked it up. It was cold and small and slightly bent. He turned it over in his fingers. He thought of the needle swinging and not coming back. *That's a lie somewhere.* He thought of forty minutes of filing and loading and reading a dial, to find a number that had been printed and never traced.

He put it in his coat pocket, next to the folded note for Joren.

"Thank you," he said.

It did not seem like enough. Hesk nodded as though it were.

---

They walked to the depot together.

It was not strictly necessary. Cael knew the way. He had known it since before he could remember not knowing it. Neither of them suggested skipping it.

The Outer District at six in the morning had its own texture. Cael had soaked it up so completely over fourteen years that he had never looked straight at it. He looked now.

Ressa's side door was open. The ovens were going. The smell of the first loaves carried down the street the way it always did, half a block in every direction. Ressa herself was out on the front step with a broom, sweeping. She was a broad, quiet woman with flour to the elbows. She had sold Hesk bread every morning of Cael's life.

She looked up as they came by.

Her eyes went to the bag on Cael's shoulder. Then to his face. Something in her own face eased into an understanding that did not need any explaining.

"Safe travels," she said.

That was all. She did not ask where. She did not ask why. She had heard something. Everyone within four streets had heard something. News in the Outer District moved the way water moved through the canal locks, finding every low place. And Ressa had decided for herself that the right answer was two words and nothing else.

"Thank you," Cael said.

She nodded, and went back to her sweeping. Then, as they passed, without looking up, she said, "The one by the window's still warm. I put an extra in the bag your grandfather took."

Cael looked at Hesk. Hesk looked at the sky.

"She put an extra in," Hesk said, "and wouldn't take for it."

Cael turned to say something to Ressa. She had already gone inside. The side door stood open, and the smell came out of it, and that was her answer.

---

Three doors down, Garrik was out in front of his shop.

He had a cart wheel up on the stand and was turning it slowly with one hand, listening to the hub, his head tilted. It was what he did every morning, the first wheel of the day, before he had his tea. He said a wheel told you everything if you were quiet enough to hear it.

He did not look up as they passed.

He did not look up in the particular way of a man choosing not to. His hand kept turning the wheel. His head kept its tilt. His eyes stayed fixed on the hub with a steadiness that took more effort than looking would have.

Cael walked by. He did not say anything.

Two nights ago, in the warm little room behind the curtain, Garrik had said *Well. Finally,* and patted the projector, and said *he's one of us.* Two nights ago, Cael had not had a classification. Now he had one, and it had a word in it that belonged to four people in a green book.

It hurt more than he expected. That surprised him. He had thought he had used up all the hurt there was.

He made himself think about it properly, walking, because thinking about things properly was the one tool he knew he had. Some people met a hard thing by looking straight at it. Some people found it easier to look somewhere else. Neither had very much to do with him. Garrik's hand on the wheel was not saying *I think less of you.* It was saying *I don't know how to do this.* Those were different. He was nearly sure they were different.

He filed it. It cost something to understand that at fourteen. He understood it anyway.

Behind them, the wheel stopped turning.

Cael did not look back. He thought perhaps Garrik had looked up after all, once they were past, when it was safe. He thought perhaps that was the best Garrik could manage. He decided to count it.

---

They passed the community hall. The front was shuttered. The reference room was dark behind its windows. Down the side of the building, the green door with the worn brass handle was shut.

Cael thought about the long table under the window, and the summary on its shelf, and the four lines near the end of it that had started all this before it had any right to start. He felt an odd gratitude toward that room. Denvash was taking a great deal from him this morning. But it had never hidden the truth from him. It had left it on a shelf, in a public room, for any boy stubborn enough to read that far.

"Will you still go?" he said. "On Tuesdays."

Hesk walked several paces before he answered.

"Yes," he said. "Someone has to tell Garrik when he's threading it backward."

"He threads it backward?"

"About one week in four. The biscuit woman says it's the only time the films make sense."

Cael laughed. It surprised both of them. It came out of him short and rough, and it hurt a little, and he was glad of it.

---

At the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, he stopped.

"One minute," he said.

Hesk stopped too, and waited, and did not ask.

The low wall stood where it always stood. Waist-high, grey, holding nothing up. There was nobody in front of it. Joren would not come for his hour until after breakfast. The grit on the paving lay loose and ordinary, the way it lay when nobody was standing there asking it to settle.

When they were eight, they had found a gap between two of the top blocks, at the end nearest the canal. The mortar had crumbled out of it. It was just wide enough to slide a folded paper into. They had used it for a whole summer to leave each other messages. Most of them were maps to places that did not exist. One of them, Cael remembered, had been a list of every word Joren knew that rhymed with *canal.* There were four. Two were made up.

He found the gap. It was still there.

He took the folded note out of his pocket and slid it in, deep, until only a white corner showed. Joren would see it. Joren would stand right in front of it for an hour, flat-footed, with nothing to look at but the wall.

Cael put his hand flat on the cold top of the wall for a moment.

Then he went back to Hesk.

"Joren," Hesk said. It was not a question.

"I didn't go and see him. Yesterday. I said I would."

Hesk nodded slowly. "And now you've written."

"It's not the same."

"No," Hesk said. "But it's honest. And honest lasts longer." He started walking again. "He'll be all right. He's got a stance declaration."

"He's got a heel problem."

"He'll fix it," Hesk said. "You showed him where it was."

---

Two streets on, a woman Cael half knew nodded to Hesk as they passed. She was somebody's aunt, a face from the market rather than a name. It was the ordinary nod of one neighbour to another. Then her eyes found Cael's bag, and whatever Hesk was carrying underneath his steady face, and her nod slowed.

It softened into something more like a held breath.

She did not say anything. Nobody, it seemed, had the right words for this. Cael was beginning to think that was not a failure on the street's part. It was an honest admission. There were no right words. People who said none were telling the truth about it.

Then they came out onto Lantern Street, where it ran east toward the depot, and the light came in.

It was six o'clock. The sun was just over the rooftops at the far end of the street. It came down the whole length of Lantern Street at a low, flat angle, and it was gold. Not yellow. Gold, the deep warm gold of brass that has just been polished. It caught the dust from the bakeries and the smoke from the first chimneys and lit them, so that the whole street seemed to be full of slow, bright, drifting specks. It caught the edges of the cobbles. It caught the wet on a horse trough. It caught the side of Hesk's face and turned his grey beard briefly to the colour of wheat.

Cael stopped walking.

He looked at it. All of it. He looked deliberately, the way he would look at a page he meant to copy.

*This,* he thought. *This exact colour. I am going to keep this.*

He did not take out his notebook. Some things did not go in the notebook. Some things you wrote somewhere else.

Hesk had stopped beside him. He did not ask what Cael was looking at. After a moment, Cael saw, he had turned to look down the street too, into the same light, with the same face.

They stood there until the sun cleared the rooftop and the gold thinned to ordinary morning.

Then they walked on.

---

The north transit depot was louder than the streets.

Merchants were loading carts. A family with far too much luggage was arguing, quietly and fiercely, about whether they had too much luggage. A team of draught horses stood steaming in their traces while their driver fed them from a nosebag and talked to them about his sister. A toll-taker in a little booth was disputing a charge with a carter. Both of them had clearly had this exact dispute many times before and seemed to find it relaxing.

Two Bronze-tier practitioners in guild colours walked through the crowd. They moved with the unhurried ease of people who had never once in their lives had to wait in a queue. The crowd opened for them without being asked. Cael watched their feet. Heel, flat. Heel, flat. The same measured walk as the Iron fighter on the sheet.

He thought: *I'm going to watch everyone's feet for the rest of my life now.*

Hesk walked him to the far edge of the depot, where the paved yard ended and the merchant road began. The cobbles stopped there in a ragged line, as if they had simply run out of interest. Beyond them was packed earth, pale and hard, running east between low walls toward the farmland.

They stood at the line.

Cael had imagined this moment many times over the past two days. He had imagined it a dozen different ways. None of them had prepared him for how ordinary it felt. It was not smaller than he had imagined, exactly. It was quieter. It felt less like a scene and more like a moment, the kind that turns out to have mattered much more than it seemed to while it was happening.

Around them, the depot carried on. The toll-taker and the carter reached their weekly agreement. The horses' driver finished his story about his sister. The family with too much luggage left one bag behind on purpose and walked off without looking at it.

Cael found it almost offensive that the depot did not know. That nobody in the city knew this morning was different from all the others. Then, a moment later, he found it steadying. The world was going to keep doing what it did, whatever he did next. There was something almost generous in that.

"You have the money," Hesk said.

It was not a question. It was a checklist, said aloud. Hesk checked every load calculation twice, even when he was sure.

"Yes."

"The notebook. Not opened until the road."

"Yes."

"The bread Ressa put in."

"Yes."

"Ardenmere. The Unranked District. Find your feet before you find anything else."

"I remember."

"Don't trust the first person who's kind to you completely. Don't distrust them completely either. Watch. The way you already watch everything. Let the watching tell you which way to lean." Hesk stopped. Something shifted in his shoulders, the way it shifted at the bench when a repair turned out to be more delicate than it looked. "I know I'm repeating myself. I've had two days to say everything I wanted to say. It turns out there isn't a version of two days that's enough."

"You could have another two."

"I could. And then another two after that. And I'd still be standing here finding things I hadn't said." The almost-smile came back, worn thinner now by holding it. "That isn't a problem two more days fixes. At some point I have to trust that what you're carrying is enough. Not because it's complete. Because it's what there is. And you're good at building on incomplete things. You've been doing it all your life."

Cael did not have an answer to that. So he did not try to make one.

Hesk nodded, as if the silence had answered him anyway.

He looked, for a moment, as though he were going to say something else. Something large. Something the size of what was actually happening. Cael watched him gather it. Then he watched him decide against it, visibly, in favour of something smaller and truer.

"Write to me," Hesk said. "Even if there's nothing to say. Especially then."

"I will."

Hesk reached out and put his hand on Cael's shoulder.

It was not a hug. Not quite. It was close enough that the difference did not matter. His grip was firmer than Cael expected. It was the grip of a man who worked brass with his hands all day and had never learned to make that strength any gentler than it needed to be. And in that grip, Cael understood something he would not have believed if Hesk had simply said it. His grandfather was holding on exactly as hard as he was.

"Whatever you find out there," Hesk said. "Whatever you turn out to be able to do. You were worth raising before any of it, and you'll still be worth it no matter what it turns out to be. I need you to actually believe that. Not just hear it."

"I believe you believe it."

"That isn't quite the same thing."

"It's what I've got this morning."

Cael's voice caught on the last word. He let it. Pretending otherwise, in front of the one person who had never once asked him to perform anything, suddenly seemed like a waste of the little time there was left to be honest in.

Something in Hesk's face broke open. Only a little. Only for a second. Not tears. Cael had never seen Hesk cry and did not see it now. But a cost showed, plainly, in his face. It was the face of a man doing something that cost him more than his composure had been built to cover.

"It's enough," Hesk said quietly. "It's more than enough. Go on, then. Before I decide it isn't."

Cael did not trust his voice to answer that.

"I'll write," he said instead. It was not the same thing. Hesk seemed to understand it as close enough.

Hesk let go. He stepped back.

Cael picked up the bag. He settled the strap across his shoulder, the same shoulder Hesk had just held. He turned toward the road before he could lose the nerve the turning took.

He stepped off the last cobble onto the packed earth.

He went.

---

He looked back once.

He was far enough down the road by then that Hesk was a shape rather than a person. A dark shape at the edge of the depot, in a good wool coat, standing still. Not waving. Watching.

Cael lifted a hand.

The shape lifted one back.

Then Cael turned east and did not look back again. Looking back a second time would have made it harder. He needed it to stay possible.

---

The district gate was a quarter mile on.

It was not much of a gate. It was a low stone arch where Denvash's Outer District wall met the open road, with a guard post beside it the size of a garden shed. Mostly it existed so that someone could check merchant manifests. Carts queued in front of it. The guard on duty was working through them with the speed of a man who had done the same thing nine thousand times and expected to do it nine thousand more.

He flagged Cael down out of habit, not suspicion.

"Registration," the guard said. He was already reaching in his mind for the next cart's papers.

Cael handed his over.

The guard glanced at it the way he must have glanced at a hundred papers already that morning. Name. Number. Classification.

Then he didn't.

His eyes stopped on the classification line. Cael watched it happen. There was a half-second in which the man's face went from routine to something it had not expected, and had to decide, in that half-second, what to do about it.

The guard's face did nothing dramatic. It did something smaller, and Cael thought, more honest. It drew back. Not his body. Just his face. It took on the shape of a man deciding that the quickest way out of an uncomfortable thing was to make it as short as possible.

"Go on," the guard said.

He handed the papers back without meeting Cael's eyes. He waved him through the arch faster than he had waved the cart in front of him.

Cael walked through. He did not say anything.

He did not look back at the guard, though he wanted to. He wanted to see whether the man's face had already gone back to boredom. Whether the whole thing had stopped mattering to everyone except Cael the moment his back was turned.

He suspected it had. And that, he thought, was the most useful part of the whole exchange. He was going to matter enormously to himself for a long time to come. To almost everyone else, he would matter for exactly as long as it took them to finish the sentence.

Twenty paces past the arch, on the open road, with the wall falling away behind him, he stopped.

He took out his notebook. He wrote it down, because it was information, and information went in the notebook whether he liked how it felt or not.

*Gate guard, Denvash Outer gate. Saw [SHATTERED]. Didn't ask questions. Waved me through faster than routine.*

*People don't engage with what they're afraid of. They get you through the interaction as quickly as possible so they don't have to keep looking at it.*

*This is useful. Fear that wants to look away is fear that isn't paying close attention. Remember this.*

He read it over. He started to close the book.

Then he stopped, and opened it again, and added one more line below the others.

*Corollary, added a moment later, because it felt important enough to earn its own line: this will not always be true. Somewhere, eventually, I am going to meet the kind of fear that does the opposite — that looks harder, not away. I don't know yet what that looks like. I should.*

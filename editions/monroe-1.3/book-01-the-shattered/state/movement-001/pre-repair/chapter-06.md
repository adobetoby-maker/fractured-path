# Chapter 6 — The Rise

For the first few hours, the merchant road ran through farmland.

Fields on both sides. Low stone walls. A farmhouse every mile or so, with smoke going up straight from the morning fire. The traffic was light. A few carts went the other way, into Denvash, loaded with cabbages and firewood. A family went by on foot, pulling a hand cart with a sleeping baby tucked in among the sacks. Two merchants on horseback passed him at a trot and did not look down.

Cael had walked a great deal in his life. He had never walked like this.

In Denvash, walking had always meant moving through something he already understood. Streets had names. Distances had ends. There was always a corner coming, and he always knew what was round it. Out here, the road simply went on. It did not care about him. His own footsteps were the only steady rhythm in a landscape that otherwise offered him the odd bird and, very far off, the slow work of someone in a field he would never see up close.

He noticed things anyway. Noticing was the one skill he trusted himself to have, wherever he was.

Mist lay in the low places between the fields. It burned off unevenly as the sun climbed. The high ground cleared first. The hollows stayed white and ghostly long after, as though they were holding on to the night on purpose.

A farmer two fields over raised a hand to him without stopping his work. He was mending a wall, lifting stones from a heap and setting each one with a little twist, and he raised his hand between one stone and the next. Cael raised his back. For the space of that small exchange, he felt less alone than the empty road had been making him feel. It surprised him how much less.

Around the second hour, a dog joined him.

It came out of a gateway without any fuss. It was a thin brown dog with a white chest and one torn ear, and it fell into step beside him as though they had agreed to meet. It did not look at him. It did not ask for anything. It trotted along at his left side for most of an hour, matching his pace, with the loose, easy indifference of an animal that belonged to the road itself rather than to anyone on it.

"You're not the grey dog," Cael told it.

The dog did not deny this.

At a crossroads with a milestone, it peeled off toward a farmhouse without a backward glance, the same way it had come. Cael watched it go. He found he minded more than he would have expected to.

By midmorning his legs had started to complain. It was not pain, exactly. It was a dull, insistent awareness of themselves that he had never had to think about in the city, where distances were short and full of things to look at. The road notes had said to find a pace you could hold all day rather than one that felt brave. He slowed down. He found he could breathe properly again.

There was no reason to hurry. Ardenmere would be there in two days whether he arrived worn out or only tired.

---

He stopped late in the morning at a low wall beside the road.

It was waist-high and made of grey stone. It held back a field of stubble. He sat on it, and looked at it, and thought of the wall at the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, with a folded paper in the gap between two blocks.

Joren would have found it by now. He would have stood in front of it for his hour. Cael hoped he had kept his heels down.

He ate the bread Ressa had put in the bag. It was still faintly soft. It tasted of the corner, of the side door, of every morning of his life. He ate all of it, slowly, and brushed the crumbs off his knees for the sparrows.

Then he did the thing he had been telling himself he would wait for the road to do properly.

He took out the leather book.

It was smaller than he had thought, now that he had it in his hands with nobody watching. It fitted in his palm. The leather was soft with handling. The cord was worn smooth in one place, where it had been tied and untied a great many times, over what must have been years.

He untied it. He opened it to the first page.

It was Hesk's handwriting. But it was not the square, careful hand he used for load figures on the workshop slate. It was rougher, and it changed as the pages went on. Different inks. Different pressures. Some entries were pale and brownish with age. Some were still dark. It looked like something written in many sittings across a long stretch of time, by a man who had kept coming back to it.

The first page had only a few lines on it.

*If you have this, then it was what I thought it was. I am sorry. I would have given a great deal to be wrong. I can't change the word. What I can give you is what I've worked out, mostly by watching, because there was nobody I could ask without being asked why.*

*None of it is certain. Read it anyway.*

He turned the page.

*First: I don't know what [SHATTERED] actually means, and I want to be honest with you about that before I tell you anything else, because everything after this sentence is a guess dressed up as advice. What I believe, for whatever a belief is worth against a word like that: the name was chosen by people who were frightened, and fear naming a thing doesn't make the fear correct. It might mean broken. It might mean something outside the map entirely — not off it, just not yet drawn. I don't know which. I've had years to sit with not knowing, and I still don't have a better answer than that. Anyone who tells you they're certain is telling you more than they actually know.*

Cael read it twice.

He had been expecting something. He realised it only now, sitting on the wall, with the page under his thumb. Somewhere underneath the parts of himself he had looked at closely, he had expected that a man who had spent years preparing for this would have reached an answer. That the book would open, and the answer would be in it, in Hesk's handwriting, the way the answer to a load problem was always at the bottom of the slate.

There was no answer. There was a grown man saying he did not have one.

He sat with that for a while. And slowly he found that it was more use to him than an answer would have been. An answer could be wrong. An answer could be a number printed on a stock sheet that nobody had ever traced. This was not that. This was the true figure, measured by hand. *I don't know.* You could build on a figure like that. You knew exactly how much weight it would hold.

He turned the page.

*Second: your body is going to learn before your mind does. This isn't poetry, it's the most practical thing I can tell you. Whatever you can do, it won't arrive as a declaration in a formatted box the way it does for standard Paths. It will arrive as instinct — a moment where you do something before you've decided to do it, and only afterward do you understand what just happened. When that happens, don't perform it for an audience. Watch it. Learn what your body already knows before you try to explain it to anyone else, including yourself.*

*Third: don't perform weakness, and don't perform strength. Perform competence. People read confidence as a threat and helplessness as an opportunity. Competence, quiet and undramatic, is the only posture that buys you room to actually figure out what you are before someone else decides for you.*

He thought about Joren's declaration, in Joren's round hand at the back of the current notebook. *PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1.* A heading. A bracketed name. A sentence telling you exactly what you could do and exactly where it stopped. Joren had read it four hundred times. Joren knew where the edges were. Joren's whole Path was a set of edges, written down for him.

Cael would not get one. Not at Rank 1, not at Rank 10. There would be no box with his abilities in it, no line telling him what ended the effect. He would have to find the edges by walking into them.

He looked down the road. It ran east between its low walls, pale and empty.

*Perform competence.*

He did not know yet what he was competent at. But he knew what it felt like to be competent at a bracket. He knew the needle sitting still at three and a half times working load. He supposed it would feel something like that. He supposed he would know it when it happened.

He turned the page.

The ink here was different. Paler. Older, or newer; it was hard to tell. The handwriting was more careful, as though the man writing it had slowed down.

*Fourth: if the abilities do come, they will not come labeled. You will not be told what you have or where it came from. This is the part I have the least practical advice for, because I have never watched it happen to someone and I am working from theory rather than observation. My best guess: pay attention to what you find yourself able to do only under real pressure, not in practice. Whatever this is, I suspect it does not show itself for an audience of one, calm and unthreatened. It shows itself when something forces it to.*

*Fifth, and this one matters more than the others, so read it twice: you are allowed to be afraid and competent at the same time. I have watched people convince themselves those two things are opposites, that fear is proof you're not ready. It isn't. Fear that keeps you paying attention is a tool, not a flaw. The only fear worth worrying about is the kind that makes you stop watching. Yours won't. I've known you fourteen years. You don't know how to stop watching. It's the thing about you I'm least worried will fail you.*

He read the fifth one twice, because it told him to.

Then he read it a third time, because he wanted to.

He thought about the eleven seconds, counting in the worn circle while the sigil hung at the edge of his sight. He thought about his own hands on the kitchen table the morning the notice came, and how he had looked at them and named what they were doing instead of feeling it. He thought about copying the archive entry word for word, in his neatest writing, so that he would not have to simply sit there and know it.

He had thought, all his life, that the watching was a kind of hiding. A way of standing a little outside things so that they could not quite reach him. He had thought it was something he would have to grow out of.

Hesk did not think so. Hesk thought it was the tool.

Cael sat on the wall and let that settle. It did not settle all the way. But it went a good distance down.

There were more pages after the fifth. Many more. Some were notes on the Registry. Some were lists of cities, with lines through most of them. Some were just a sentence or two, in fading ink, with no number at all.

On a page near the middle, by itself, in ink so fresh it had barely gone dull, there was one more entry.

*When you don't know what you can do yet, watch someone who does. Your body will learn before your mind does.*

There was no number on it. It had been written recently. Months ago at most. Perhaps weeks.

Cael looked at it for a long time.

He thought about a folding chair in the dark behind a curtain. A white sheet on a frame. A projector ticking. A woman dropping her stance under a strike that did not come, and rising into a forearm. *Watch his hips. The third exchange is where they tell you the truth.*

Some years of Tuesdays. And then, at the end of them, this one sentence, written down so that Cael would have it after Hesk could no longer sit beside him in the dark and say *there.*

He understood, then, what Tuesdays had been for. He understood that the night before Kindling had not been a treat, or a distraction. It had been a lesson. The only lesson Hesk had been sure enough of to give. Hesk had taken him to the hall because he wanted Cael to know how to watch before he needed to.

He closed the book. He tied the cord. He held it in both hands for a moment, against his knees.

*I am not starting from nothing,* he thought. *I'm starting from this.*

It helped. Not all the way. But it helped, the way true things help more than comforting ones, even when the true thing is *I don't know.*

He put the book in the inside pocket of his coat, where the bracket was. Then he got up, because Ardenmere was still most of two days east, and the afternoon was not going to last forever.

---

The road dropped after midday into a long shallow valley with a stream at the bottom.

Hedges replaced the walls here. They were high and thick, and the road ran between them like a corridor. It narrowed. It bent. You could not see more than fifty paces in either direction. The air was still and close, and smelled of hawthorn and warm earth.

Cael walked with his head down. He was counting milestones. He had passed nine since Denvash. He was trying to work out his pace from them and from the sun, and getting different answers, and enjoying the problem the way he enjoyed the bracket.

He stepped sideways.

He did not decide to. One moment he was walking in the middle of the road with his eyes on the ruts. The next his left foot had gone out onto the grass verge, and his weight had followed it, and he was standing in the long grass at the bottom of the hedge.

The cart came round the bend behind him.

It came quickly. It was a farm cart, high-sided, heaped with turnips, pulled by one big dun horse going downhill at a trot that was not quite under control. The wheels were rimmed with iron, and the road was soft dust here, and they made almost no sound at all. The driver was slumped on the bench with the reins slack in his lap and his hat over his eyes.

The cart went through the place where Cael had been walking.

It went through it so closely that the near wheel hub brushed the strap of his bag where it hung at his hip. He felt the brush. He felt the wind of it on his face. A turnip bounced off the top of the heap and rolled into the grass at his feet.

Then the cart was past, and the driver came awake with a jerk, and hauled on the reins. The horse slowed, snorting. The cart stopped twenty paces on, half across the road.

The driver twisted round on his bench. He was a red-faced man with a grey moustache. He stared back at Cael, in the long grass, with the turnip at his feet.

"Lad!" he called. His voice cracked. "Lad, are you hit?"

"No," said Cael.

He looked down at himself. He was not hit. His bag strap had a pale scuff across it where the hub had touched. His heart was going very fast, he noticed. Faster than it had in the circle. He could feel it in his throat.

The driver climbed down. He came back up the road at a stiff half-run, an old man's run, and stopped in front of Cael and looked him up and down as if counting his arms and legs.

"I never saw you," the driver said. "I swear I never saw you. I was—" He took his hat off and wrung it. "I was resting my eyes. The old girl knows the road. Knows it better than me. I never thought there'd be anybody walking the bottom of the dip this time of day." He swallowed. "You'd have been under her. You'd have been right under the wheel."

"I heard you coming," Cael said.

"You never."

"I must have. I stepped out of the way."

The driver looked at him with his mouth working. Then he looked at the road, at the dust, at the iron-rimmed wheels standing quiet as a pair of plates. He did not say anything. Cael could see him not saying it.

"Well," the man said at last. "Well. Thank the sky for good ears, then. Mine are no use to anybody."

He would not let it go after that. He insisted. He was going as far as the waystation with his turnips, a few miles on. He would not hear of Cael walking it. It was the least he could do. It was a good deal less than the least, if you asked him. Get up, lad, get up on the bench, and here, have the turnip, it's yours, it's got your name on it now.

So Cael got up on the bench beside him, with the turnip in his lap, and the dun mare took them down the dip and up the other side at a walk this time, because the driver held the reins in both fists now and did not once close his eyes.

He was a talker. Cael learned that he had four daughters and one son, that the son was no good with turnips but very good with ledgers, that the waystation woman's stew was better than it looked and her bread was worse, and that he did not hold with the new lock fees on the Denvash canal.

"Where you headed, then?" he asked eventually. "On your own, at your age."

"Ardenmere."

"Ah." The man nodded as though that explained a good deal. "The big one. Family there?"

"No."

"Work?"

"I'll find some."

The driver glanced at him sideways. "What Path are you, lad? If you don't mind my asking. You've got a look about you. Fresh. Kindled recent, I'd say."

Cael felt the question land. He felt it go all the way down.

*Don't perform weakness. Don't perform strength.*

He could lie. He could say *Copper, Force* and watch the man nod and move on. He found he did not want to. It was not that lying was hard. It was that he had already watched Pellin's hand go to the back of a drawer, and the gate guard's face draw back, and he did not want to do that same thing to himself from the inside.

But he did not have to hand the word over either. Not to a turnip farmer on a road. Not today.

"Still finding out," he said.

The driver considered this. Then he laughed, a big comfortable laugh.

"Aren't we all," he said. "Aren't we all. I'm sixty-one and Copper Rank 4 and I'm still finding out what Stone Path is for, besides knowing when the turnips want lifting." He shook his head. "Ardenmere's full of people still finding out. You'll fit right in."

Cael looked down at the turnip in his lap.

He did not think about the bend in the road. There was nothing to think about. The cart had made more noise than the driver thought. He had heard the wheels without noticing that he heard them, the way you hear a clock. Then he had moved. That was all. Anyone would have.

He was sure of that. He was nearly sure.

He did not take out his notebook. There was nothing to put in it. *A cart went past close* was not information. It was just a cart.

---

The waystation was where Hesk had said it would be. South side of the road. A low timber building with a mossy roof and a lamp over the door that was already lit, though it was not yet dark.

The driver dropped him at the gate, shook his hand very hard, and told him twice more to thank the sky for his ears. Then he went round the back with the mare and the turnips, where the woman who kept the waystation was apparently expecting him.

Inside there was one long room. A stove at one end. A table with benches. A row of cots along the far wall, with blankets folded at the foot of each. Two of them already had people on them: a drover asleep with his boots on, and a thin woman knitting, with a small girl asleep against her side.

The keeper came in from the back. She was tall and bony, with her sleeves rolled up and her hair tied in a cloth. She named a price. Cael took it out of Hesk's pouch, in the small worn coins.

She counted them. Then she counted them again.

She did not once look at his face. Not when he gave her the coins. Not when she pointed him to the cot by the window. Not when she said, "Stew's at dark. Bread's on the table. Don't eat the bread." It was not unkind. It was the opposite, he thought. She did not want his face. She only wanted his coins to be right. Here, that was all anyone needed to be.

He sat at the end of the table, near the stove, and took out a clean sheet of paper and his pencil.

*Hesk,* he wrote.

He stopped. He looked at the word for a while.

Then he wrote the rest.

*I'm at the waystation. It's where you said. The lamp's lit. The woman counts coins twice, like the nail man at the market does, and she says not to eat the bread, which I think is good advice.*

*Ressa put an extra loaf in. I ate it on a wall. I think you knew she would, and that's why you didn't take any for yourself this morning. I noticed.*

*Garrik didn't look up. I think he wanted to. I think he couldn't. I don't think it's about me.*

*I left a note for Joren in the wall. In the gap at the canal end. Tell him, if he asks, that I meant it.*

*I read the book. I read the fifth one three times. I read the one about watching someone who does. I know what Tuesdays were for now.*

He sat with the pencil above the page for a moment. Then he went on.

*A carter gave me a ride the last few miles. He grows turnips. He gave me one. He is Copper Rank 4 Stone Path and he says Ardenmere is full of people still finding out.*

He looked at that line for a while.

He did not write how the ride had started. It had been nothing. It had been a cart on a bend. If he wrote it down, Hesk would read it a week from now at the kitchen table and worry about something that was already over, and there would be nothing Hesk could do about it except worry.

Cael held the pencil very still.

It came to him, sitting there, that this was exactly the choice Hesk had made. For years. *Don't make a thing real before it is. Don't hand him weight he can't do anything about.* He had been angry about that choice three days ago, in a room with a crooked tree in the window. Now here he was, a day out of Denvash, making it himself, about a turnip cart.

He did not know what to do with that. He decided to leave it in the notebook of things he did not know what to do with yet. It was getting to be a thick notebook.

He did not write the cart in.

*I'll send this from Ardenmere,* he wrote, *when I know where I'm staying, so you'll have somewhere to write back to. You'll get it about a week after I send it. I'm sorry about that. I would make the post faster if I could.*

*I'm all right. I'm tired, and my legs hurt, and I'm all right.*

*— Cael.*

He folded it and put it in the leather book, between the pages, to keep it flat.

The stew came at dark. It was better than it looked. The bread was worse than she had said. The thin woman shared her tea with him without asking whether he wanted any, and he said thank you, and she said, "Long road, is it?" and he said, "Not as long as yesterday," and she seemed to think that was a good answer.

He lay on the cot by the window and listened to the room. The drover snored in a long, patient rhythm. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, something moved in the hedge and was quiet. These were not his sounds. He did not know any of them. He lay awake a while learning them, and then he slept, without meaning to, before he had learned them all.

---

The second day was longer than the first.

His legs had stiffened in the night and took most of the morning to forgive him. The road climbed out of the farmland and into rougher country, with fewer walls and more sheep, and long slow hills that looked short until you were on them. There were no more turnip carts. He walked by himself. He watched the milestones. He watched a hawk working a field for nearly half an hour and never saw it catch anything, and found himself on its side by the end.

He thought about the book in his inside pocket. He did not take it out again. He did not need to. He had read it enough times on the wall that the lines came up by themselves, as he walked, the way the old descriptions on page seven did. *It might mean something outside the map entirely. Perform competence. Afraid and competent at the same time.*

And the four. They came up by themselves too. Three dead within weeks. One unknown.

He made himself do something with them, instead of letting them just come up.

He did not know their names. He did not know how old they had been, or where they had been from, or what they had found themselves able to do before whatever happened to them happened. He did not know if any of them had had a Hesk. He did not know if any of them had been given a book.

He did know that three of them had been dead within weeks. *Weeks.* Not years. Not after a long life of trying. Weeks. Whatever had happened to them had happened fast, and early, before they had found out what they were.

Before they had found their feet.

He thought about that word *practice-related*. He thought about the clerk, and the green book, and someone, thirty-two years ago, deciding which kind of true to write down.

Late in the afternoon, the road began to climb in earnest.

It went up a long bare hill in a series of slow bends. There were no hedges here, only grass and stones and the wind, which had picked up and was coming from behind him, out of the west, pushing at his back. The sun was low. His shadow went up the road ahead of him, long and thin, with a bag on its shoulder.

He came over the top.

He stopped.

---

Ardenmere lay below him in a wide river valley, and it was enormous.

He had read about it. He had read the reference section on the eastern cities three times. He had known it was larger than Denvash. He had not known what *larger* meant until he stood on the rise and looked.

The river came in from the north, wide and slow and copper-coloured in the low sun. It bent through the middle of the valley like an arm. On the far bank, in the crook of that arm, rose the Ranked core. It was walled, and the walls were stepped, ring inside ring, each ring higher than the last. He could see the districts climbing toward the centre. Copper, he thought. Iron. Bronze. At the very top, catching the last of the light, something white with towers.

He had five pages in a notebook about one wall in Denvash. He could see six walls from here.

And around them, outside them, spilling past every one of them, was the rest.

The Unranked District.

It was not a district. It was a city. It ran along both banks of the river and up the slopes on either side and out across the flat land to the south. It had no walls at all. It had no shape that he could find, except the shape of a great many people deciding where to live one at a time. There was old stone in it, solid and square. There were things that looked built, and things that looked stacked. Roofs went up on top of roofs. Bridges, three or four of them, crossed the river, and there was movement on all of them.

The sun went down while he watched.

The gold went out of the river. The white towers on the Ranked hill held the light a little longer than everything else, and then let it go too. The sky turned grey-gold, and then a deep, clear blue.

And the fires came out.

They came out one by one at first, and then all at once. Cookfires. Hundreds of them. More than hundreds. All across the Unranked District, in yards and on roofs and in doorways and along the river, little orange points of light, each with a thin grey thread of smoke going up from it and leaning east in the wind. A thousand fires. He could not count them. For once in his life he did not try.

Each one was somebody's dinner. Somebody who was not Ranked enough for the walls. Somebody making do.

He thought: *a lot of those people are still finding out.*

He stood on the rise with the wind at his back. He was very tired. His legs ached all the way down. He had a turnip in his bag and a bent bracket in his pocket and a book against his chest that said *I don't know* in his grandfather's handwriting.

He thought about the green book in the archive. Four entries. Three ended in a few weeks. One ended in a line that said nobody knew.

He was the fifth.

He had told himself on the walk home from the record office that he would not be the fifth entry in that book. It had been easy to say. He had said it the way you say things on a walk home. He said it again now, to himself, and it was not easy anymore. He could see now how big the thing was that he would have to say it to.

He took out his notebook. He opened it to a clean page, and wrote, in the last of the light:

*Three of them were dead within weeks. They didn't get time to find their feet. Whatever happened, it happened before they knew what they were.*

*So that's the first job. Not to find out what [SHATTERED] means. Not yet. First, get past the weeks.*

*Find somewhere to sleep. Find work. Watch everything. Show nobody anything. Be dull. Be competent. Be still here.*

*Then find out.*

He looked at it. Then he underlined *still here*, once, firmly.

He did not know what was down there. He did not know where he would sleep tonight, or who the first person to be kind to him would be, or whether he would be right to trust them. He did not know what the Unranked District did to people like him. He did not know what he was.

He knew he was not going to be an entry somebody else wrote.

He decided that. Standing on the rise, at the end of the second day, with a thousand fires coming up out of the dark below him, he decided it properly, the way you set a part into a rig and load it to see if it holds.

He put the notebook away.

Down in the valley, the road ran on toward the river, pale in the dusk, and where it met the first houses there was no gate at all. Only a wide space in the dark, with fires on either side of it, standing open.

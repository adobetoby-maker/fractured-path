# Chapter 1 — Page Seven

Everyone had a different word for what Kindling felt like.

Cael had collected eleven. They were on page seven of his notebook, the current one, in the order people had given them to him. He had started the list in spring. He had not meant to start a list. It had simply turned out that when you asked people a question they liked answering, they told you, and if you wrote it down, you had a list.

*Sunlight,* said the dried-fruit seller at the Outer Market, who had an Ember Path and eleven years of certainty about it. She said it while weighing his dates, without looking up.

*A door opening,* said the gate guard on Merchant Row. He was Copper-tier, Shield Path, and he had a great deal of time between carts. He said it twice, the second time slower, as if Cael might have missed it.

*Like remembering something you never knew,* said the older girl three streets over. She had Kindled two years ago. She explained it the way people explain a thing that has happened to them exactly once.

*Like a wheel seating on its bearing,* said Garrik, who fixed carts. *You feel it go home.*

*Warm,* said Ressa at the bakery. Then she thought about it and added, *Like the oven catching.*

*Like every timetable you own being right at once,* said the man who sold hand-copied transit schedules. Cael had laughed. The man had not been joking.

*Cold water, and then not cold,* said the lock-keeper at the canal gate.

*Like a bell you feel in your teeth,* said the boot-mender.

*Brief,* said the woman who ran the reference room at the community hall. She went back to her ledger after that. Cael had decided it was the most honest answer on the page, until the last one.

*Heavy,* said Joren, two days ago, on his way home from Weaver's Row. *Good heavy.* He had said it too fast, the way you hand over something hot.

*I don't know how to describe it,* said Hesk.

Cael had starred that one. Only that one.

---

It was the morning of the day before his Kindling.

There was no way to skip it and no way to ask for more time. Kindling happened in the year you turned fourteen. Your Path woke whether you wanted it or not. The only thing anyone actually scheduled was the certification appointment, and the city booked those a year ahead because the district made so many fourteen-year-olds. Cael had turned fourteen five days ago. His appointment was at eight tomorrow, on Weaver's Row.

He had known the date for a year. He had spent most of that year not thinking about it.

That had not worked.

He ate breakfast at the kitchen table while Hesk read the city notice sheet. The east window let in light at a low angle for about twenty minutes each morning. It came in clear and sideways and lay across the table like a ruler. The bread was from Ressa's bakery on the corner. She kept her side door open when the ovens were going, and the smell came half a block, so that walking home in the morning was mostly a matter of following your nose.

Under the bread smell was the workshop smell. Solder flux. Machine oil. Brass filings, which did not really have a smell, except that they did.

"Weather's turning," Hesk said, from behind the notice sheet.

"It says that?"

"It says the canal authority is raising the lock fees." He folded the sheet. "I can tell about the weather myself."

Hesk was fifty-four. He had a square, weathered face and hands that were bigger than his fine work seemed to need. He was Iron-tier, retired from active practice for longer than Cael had been alive. He made instruments now. Pressure gauges for the canal authority. Level meters for the survey office. Once, memorably, a very small clock for a man who wanted it inside a walking stick.

He looked at Cael over the folded sheet.

"You're not eating."

"I'm eating."

"You're holding bread."

Cael took a bite to prove something. Hesk watched him chew it with the patience of a man who had raised a boy and knew exactly how much a single bite proved.

"Workshop," Hesk said. "When you're done. I want your numbers on the lock gauge housing before the test."

That was a kindness. Cael knew it was a kindness, and he took it anyway.

---

The workshop was the back half of the house. It had a long bench under two windows, a stove, racks of brass stock sorted by gauge, and a wall of small drawers. Each drawer had a label in Hesk's square hand. *Springs, fine. Springs, coarse. Springs, do not use.* Cael had asked about that last drawer when he was six. Hesk had said, "Those are the ones that taught me something." Cael had not understood. He did now.

The lock gauge housing sat in the test rig. It was a brass case for a canal pressure gauge, about the size of two fists. It would bolt to a lock wall and hold its dial steady while tons of water leaned on the gate beside it. Hesk had built the case. Cael had done the bracket calculations three days ago, at this bench, with the city's appointment letter tucked in the back of his notebook so he would not see it.

He opened the notebook to the page.

"Walk me through it," Hesk said.

"Wall bracket, four bolts. Load is the housing's weight, plus vibration from the gate, plus a factor for when somebody bumps it with a boat hook." He ran his finger down the column. "Allowed load on each bolt comes from the stock sheet. I used the supplier's figure for the bracket alloy. With the margin you like, it holds at three times working load."

"Three times," Hesk said. "Good. Let's see."

The rig was simple. A lever arm, a hanging tray, iron weights in stacks of five. A dial gauge touched the bracket at its weakest point. If the bracket bent, the needle would move. If it bent too far and did not come back, it had failed.

Hesk added weights. One stack. Two. The needle stayed near zero.

Cael found he was holding his breath. He let it out.

Three stacks. That was working load. The needle twitched and settled.

Four stacks. Five.

At five and a half, the bracket made a small sound. It was not loud. It was a tick, like a knuckle. The needle swung, hung, and did not come back.

Hesk lifted the weights off, one stack at a time. The needle stayed where it was. The bracket had a slight new curve to it, as if it were thinking about bowing.

It had failed at less than twice working load. Not three times.

Cael stared at it. He felt his face go hot, which was ridiculous. It was a bracket. Nobody had been standing under it.

"Well," said Hesk, with interest. "That's a lie somewhere."

"My numbers were right."

"Then find out what was wrong."

Cael went back down his column. The arithmetic held. He did it twice. The weight of the housing was right; he had weighed it himself. The bolt spacing was right. The margin was right.

Then he came to the number he had not made.

*Allowed load, bracket alloy, per supplier stock sheet.*

He had copied it. He had not tested it. He had not even looked at where the supplier had got it from.

"The alloy," he said.

Hesk said nothing, which meant *go on.*

Cael took the bent bracket to the bench. He took a scrap off-cut of the same stock and clamped it, and he spent the next forty minutes doing the small, boring test that would have taken forty minutes three days ago. File a sample. Measure it. Load it. Read the needle. Do it again.

The real figure was about two-thirds of the supplier's.

"They printed the number for a different temper," Hesk said, when Cael showed him. "Softer stock, same name. It happens." He turned the off-cut over in his fingers. "It's a good calculation, Cael. Everything you made yourself was right."

"But I used their number."

"You did."

"Because it was printed."

"Because it was printed, and it looked like a number." Hesk set the off-cut down. "Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from."

Cael had heard him say it before. The first time, he had been eight, and a bracket had failed then too, and he had filed the sentence away with everything else Hesk said. He had not known yet how many things it would turn out to be about.

He wrote it down now anyway. He wrote it at the top of a fresh page, and under it he wrote the alloy figure he had measured himself, and the date.

"Redo the bracket," Hesk said. "Thicker stock. You know the number now."

"I know *a* number."

Hesk looked at him for a moment. Then he smiled, which he did not do often, and which changed his whole face.

"That," he said, "is the right answer."

Cael spent another hour on it. When the new bracket went into the rig, it held at three times working load, and at three and a half, and Hesk stopped there because there was no reason to break a good part to prove a point. Cael watched the needle sit still at the top of the stack. It was a small, specific pleasure. He had made something, and it held, and he knew why.

He did not think about tomorrow for nearly a whole minute.

---

He went out afterward, because the house had begun to feel small.

The Outer District of Denvash ran along the inside of the city's interior wall. It was wider in the northern quadrant, where the transit depots were, and narrower to the east, where the streets got tangled against the Inner District's wall. Cael had grown up in the northern quadrant. He knew its depots and its market, its community hall, its canal gate that sighed when the locks let water through. He knew which streets flooded and which dogs bit.

He walked north toward the market with no particular place to go. The morning was cold enough for his breath to show. He counted things as he went, which was what he did when his own thoughts were too loud.

Fourteen carts between Fen Street and the market road. Six stalls already open. Three still setting up. One man arguing with a supplier about the weight of a delivery, in the tone of an argument both of them had enjoyed every week for years.

At the end of Tallow Lane, the Inner District wall came into view over the rooftops.

It was taller than it needed to be. Cael had a notebook entry about that. It was five pages long. It covered the height of the wall, the grade of the road on each side of it, the number of street lamps per hundred paces on each side, and the gate.

The gate mattered most.

Every city in Valdris was built in rings, and the rings were built on rank. The Registry kept your classification, and your classification decided where you could go. Copper-tier got the outer districts and part of the markets. Iron-tier got general access, and guild districts with the right papers. Bronze got the whole city and the administrative core. Silver got every district and the right to travel between cities. Gold got everything else.

Hesk was Iron-tier. He went through the Inner gate twice a month to deliver gauges. He showed his registration and the guard waved him on. He never talked about it, the way people never talk about the doors that open for them.

Below Copper was Unranked. Unranked could not legally enter an inner district at all.

Most of the people Cael knew were Copper. About six in ten practitioners plateaued there, according to the Registry's public summary. Iron was another quarter. Bronze was perhaps one in ten. Silver was rarer still. Gold was the kind of thing people put on posters.

Each tier had ten ranks. You climbed them by use, by doing what your Path did, again and again, until a registered Arbiter station evaluated you and said you had climbed. Rank 10 was the doorway to the next tier. Most people never went through it. Some went through once.

He had read all of this. He had read the public summary of the Compact Registry three times, at the community hall, at the long table under the window. He had read the sections on tiers and ranks, on declarations, on district access, on guild enrollment.

And he had read the part near the end.

It was under a heading called *Non-standard designations and legacy classifications.* It was four lines long. Cael had read it once and had not needed to read it again. He knew it the way he knew the sound of the canal gate.

*[SHATTERED]: Classification indicating fractured Path architecture, historically associated with unstable practice and practitioner safety risk. Currently deprecated. Four (4) instances recorded in the history of the Compact registry.*

Four.

Not a percentage. Not one in a hundred thousand. Four people, in the whole history of the Registry.

He had not written it in his notebook. He wrote down everything. He had not written this.

There was a reason for that, and he knew what it was, and he was not going to think about it on Tallow Lane in the cold.

His preliminary assessment had been in spring. Every child in the district had one. You sat in a chair at the district office while an assessor laid two fingers on your wrist and the other hand flat on your chest, and frowned at nothing for a minute. Then she wrote something down. Most children got *standard coupling.* Cael's assessor had looked at him a moment longer than she looked at the boy before him.

*Irregular coupling patterns,* she had written. And under it, kindly: *Uncommon variation. No action required.*

He had looked up *irregular coupling patterns* that afternoon. There was not much. There was that four-line entry, in the same summary, a page further on.

He turned off Tallow Lane before the wall could fill the whole sky. He walked toward Weaver's Row, because his feet had decided to, and he let them.

---

The certification office on Weaver's Row had been a dye-house once. You could still smell it if the wind was wrong. Now it had grey stone facing, the Compact Registry seal over the door, and tall windows that let in light without seeming to want it.

Alis Trent was standing outside it with her mother.

Alis lived two streets over. She had turned fourteen early in the autumn and had Kindled three days ago. She was holding a certification document in both hands and reading it as though it were in a language she was still learning. Her mother was crying, quietly, into a handkerchief. Cael looked at the mother once and then did not look at her again, because people's crying was their own.

Alis looked up when his boots scuffed the step.

"Tide Path," she said. "Copper, Rank 1."

"Good healing potential," Cael said, because it was true.

"That's what everyone says."

"Everyone's right."

"My brother got Blade Path."

"Also good."

"He's Silver Rank 3 now."

There was a space after that. Cael tried several things in it, and none of them fit.

Tide Path was a real Path. It had real advancement and steady guild demand, and in ten years Alis could be an Iron-tier healer with her own rooms and a waiting list. But Copper Rank 1 at fourteen was not the same thing as a Silver-tier brother with travel papers and a guild sword, and Alis was clever enough to know it. Pretending otherwise would have been an insult.

"You're going to have more choices in two years than you think you do right now," he said.

Alis gave him a look. "You sound like a pamphlet."

"I'm quoting my grandfather."

"Is he right?"

Cael thought about the needle sitting still at three and a half times working load. "Usually," he said.

Her mother had gone inside to speak to the clerk about the guild enrollment inquiry. Cael knew the order of it. You received your certificate. You filed the family notification. You updated the residential registry. Then you went to the guild of your Path and asked whether they had room. He had read the procedural summary in the community hall three months ago, because he read everything in the community hall.

"What does it feel like?" he asked. "Having one."

Alis considered this, which he liked her for. Most people answered before they thought.

"Like being able to hear my own pulse in my hands," she said. "All the time. I touched my mother's arm yesterday, and I could tell she had a headache." She frowned at the document. "It's not very dramatic."

"You didn't want dramatic."

"I wanted—" She stopped. "I don't know what I wanted. I wanted it to be over." She looked at him properly. "Tomorrow's yours."

"Yes."

"Are you worried?"

It was a direct question. He respected it with a direct answer. "Yes."

She nodded once, as if he had confirmed something she already knew. "Me too. Before. Three days ago." She looked down at the paper again. "It's—I don't know. It's better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted."

Cael took out his notebook. He turned to page seven, below the eleven descriptions, and wrote it there, standing on the step of the certification office with the cold coming up through his boots.

*Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.*

He starred it.

Alis watched him do it. "Are you writing me down?"

"Yes."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's the second star on the page."

She almost smiled. It was a tired, real almost-smile. "Who's the first?"

"Hesk."

"Then I'll take it." She folded her certificate in half, and then, after a moment, unfolded it again and held it flat, as though folding it had been the wrong thing to do to it. "Good luck tomorrow, Cael."

"Congratulations," he said. "On the Path."

He meant it. He hoped she could tell.

He knew Joren's result because the whole northern quadrant knew it.

Joren had spent a year predicting his Kindling. He had started in a general way—*something fast*—and narrowed it down, week by week, until by midsummer he was telling people at the market *Blade Path, Silver-track, probably Rank 3 by sixteen.* He had told it to Garrik. He had told it to Ressa. He had told it, once, to the gate guard on Merchant Row, who had said *A door opening* and then *Good luck, son,* in a voice that made Cael wince.

The whole quadrant had followed Joren's certainty with the attention people give to certainty they suspect is wrong.

It had been wrong.

Stone Path. Copper, Rank 1.

It was not a bad Path. Cael had looked it up the day Joren Kindled, because he looked everything up. Stone rewarded patience. It built slowly and held well, and at the higher tiers it did things with ground and weight that the guilds paid for. Joren had patience, under the noise. In ten years, if he worked, he could be Bronze. That was a life. That was more of the city than most of the Outer District ever saw.

But it was not Silver. It was a beginning, not an arrival. Joren had spent a whole year telling everyone he would arrive.

Cael found him at the low wall.

The wall stood at the corner where Fen Street met the canal path. It was waist-high, made of old grey blocks, and it did not hold anything up. It had been part of a building once. The building was gone. The wall had stayed, the way some things do, because nobody could think of a reason to take it down. When they were seven, Cael and Joren had sat on it every afternoon and argued about which Paths they would get. Joren had always wanted Blade. Cael had wanted whatever would let him build things that lasted, and had never worked out which Path that was, and had at some point stopped wanting anything in particular.

Joren was standing in front of the wall, not sitting on it. His feet were planted wide. His arms were folded. His face had the fierce, private look of someone concentrating on something nobody else could see.

"You look like you're waiting for a cart to hit you," Cael said.

Joren did not move his feet. He turned only his head.

"Cael." He grinned, and then remembered not to grin too much, and then grinned anyway. "Good. Good timing. Come here. Push me."

"No."

"Push me. Hard as you can. I'll tell you why after."

"You'll tell me why before, or I'm going home."

Joren sighed the long, wounded sigh of a person whose friend had always been like this.

"I got my declaration," he said. Then, in a lower voice, glancing up the street: "My first one. It came with the word, the same morning. Rank 1." He paused. "You're not supposed to show anybody."

"I know."

Declarations were private. The Registry summary said so, and everybody knew it anyway. When your Path gave you an ability, the Arbiter showed it to you and only you. Sharing one was allowed. It was also the kind of thing you did with family, or with someone you would trust with a door key.

Joren held out his hand.

"Give me your notebook."

Cael hesitated for one heartbeat. Then he gave it over, open to a clean page near the back. Joren took the pencil from the spine and wrote slowly, his tongue at the corner of his mouth, the way he had written everything since they were small. When he handed it back, the page said:

```
PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1
[Rooted Stance] — Passive. While both feet are planted flat, your stance resists displacement by force. The effect ends when either foot lifts.
```

Cael read it twice.

"That's the exact wording?"

"Exact. I read it about four hundred times." Joren tapped his own temple. "It shows up right there. Not in your eyes. Sort of behind them. Like words written on the inside of your eyelids, except they aren't really words, and your eyelids are open." He scowled. "That's a terrible description."

"It's better than *Brief.*"

"Who said *Brief*?"

"The reference room."

"Of course she did." Joren unfolded his arms and set them on his hips. His feet did not move. "Rooted Stance. You know what that means?"

"It means you're hard to push over."

"It means I'm *impossible* to push over." He lifted his chin. "Go on. Before it gets dark."

"It's ten in the morning."

"Before it gets dark, Cael."

So Cael put the notebook on the wall, and he put both hands flat on Joren's chest, and he pushed.

It was like pushing a doorframe.

He had expected it to be hard. He had not expected it to feel like that. Joren's body still felt like Joren's body under his hands, warm and thin, a boy's shoulders under a wool coat. But behind it there was something else. It was as if the push went into Joren and then kept going down, through his legs, into the paving stones, and the paving stones were not interested.

Cael leaned. He put his weight into it. He dug in his heels.

Joren did not move.

Around Joren's boots, Cael saw, the loose grit on the paving shivered once and lay flat. Every grain. As though the ground had settled itself, the way a dog settles when it decides it is staying.

"Huh," said Cael.

"Right?" Joren was beaming. His face had gone a little red. "Right? I've been out here every morning. You have to do it to rank. Use is how you climb, that's what the Stone Guild woman said. An hour a day. Feet down. Let people push." His voice dropped. "Nobody else wants to push me. My mother won't. She says it feels like pushing the house."

Cael stepped back and looked at him. Not at his face. At his whole shape.

Joren's weight was low and wide. His knees were a little bent. Everything was set. There was a stillness in him that Cael had never seen in Joren in his life, because Joren had never in his life stood still for longer than it took to think of the next thing to say.

But he was talking now. He was talking about the Stone Guild woman and the hour a day and the Rank 2 threshold, which was nowhere close, and how she had told him that Copper Rank 1 to Rank 2 could take a year for Stone, and that was normal, and how normal was a word he was going to have to get used to—

And as he talked, his left heel came up.

Only a little. Only for a moment, at the end of each sentence, when he wanted the sentence to land. It was the bounce he had always had. At seven, telling Cael he would be a Blade Path champion, he had bounced on his toes so hard he had once fallen off this exact wall.

*The effect ends when either foot lifts.*

Cael did not decide anything. He simply waited until the end of the next sentence.

"—so it's not the Path I wanted, but I'm going to be the best Rooted Stance in the whole—"

The heel came up.

Cael put two fingers on Joren's shoulder and pushed, gently, the way you would close a door that was almost shut already.

Joren sat down in the street.

He sat down hard. His mouth was still open from the word he had been saying. For a second he looked so astonished that Cael felt a cold flash of worry that he had hurt him.

Then Joren began to laugh.

It was not his market laugh, the loud one he used when people were listening. It was the old laugh, from the wall, from when they were seven. It came out of him in a rush and kept coming, until he was lying flat on his back in the grit with his arms out, wheezing at the sky.

"You—" he gasped. "How did you—"

"Your heel."

"My *what?*"

"Your heel comes up when you talk. At the end of a sentence. You've always done it." Cael sat down on the wall. "The declaration says *either foot lifts.* A heel is part of a foot."

Joren stopped laughing. He raised his head off the ground and looked at his own boots as if they had betrayed him personally.

"That's not fair."

"It's in the wording."

"You read my declaration once."

"Twice."

Joren let his head fall back on the paving. He lay there and looked at the sky for a while. Some of the laugh was still in his face. Some of something else was coming in under it.

"So that's the whole Path," he said at last. "I'm very hard to move, as long as I don't move." His voice was light. It was very carefully light. "Great. Good. I can be a door."

Cael did not say anything clever. There was nothing clever that would not have been worse.

He got down off the wall and held out a hand. After a moment, Joren took it, and Cael pulled him up. It was easy. Joren weighed what a fourteen-year-old weighed. The ground did not hold him down at all when he did not ask it to.

"It's Rank 1," Cael said. "It's the first thing it does. It isn't all it does."

"You read that somewhere."

"I read it in the Stone section. Higher ranks build on the stance. Ground reading. Weight transfer. Things that make other people's footing bad." He brushed grit off Joren's sleeve. "And the heel thing is fixable. You just have to stop being you for an hour a day."

Joren snorted. Then he looked at Cael for a long moment, and the carefulness went out of his face, and what was left was just Joren.

"Nobody else would have seen that," he said. "The heel."

"You'd have seen it eventually."

"Not for about a year. And not before somebody at the guild sat me on my backside in front of everyone." He shook his head slowly. "You notice stuff. You've always noticed stuff. It's really annoying."

"Thank you."

"It's not a compliment." He grinned. "It's a little bit a compliment."

He set his feet again. Planted them flat. Cael watched the grit shiver and settle around his boots.

"Again," Joren said. "And this time, I'm not going to talk."

"You're always going to talk."

"Then I'll talk without my heel. Push."

They did it six more times. Joren talked through four of them and kept his heels down for three. The fourth time, Cael waited him out, and Joren started a sentence about the Stone Guild woman's terrible tea, and you could see the effort it took him to finish the sentence flat-footed, and the heel stayed down, and he stayed up. He crowed about it as if he had won a tournament.

The sixth time, Cael pushed until his arms shook. Joren did not move. His face went red from the effort of holding still, and sweat came out at his temples, and at last he said, "Stop, stop, it costs, it's like holding your breath in your legs," and Cael stopped, and Joren let go of whatever he had been holding, and nearly fell over anyway, from relief.

They sat on the wall afterward, side by side, the way they used to. Joren's calves were trembling. He rubbed them and pretended they were not.

"So," Joren said. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"What do you think you'll get?"

"I don't know."

"You'll get something good." He said it with all the old certainty, as though the certainty had never been wrong. "Something smart. You notice things. They have to give you something that goes with that."

Cael looked down the canal path. The lock-keeper was opening the upper gate. The water leaned, and the gate sighed, and the sound went out across the rooftops like a long breath.

He thought about four lines in a summary at the community hall.

"Maybe," he said.

Joren looked at him sideways. For once, he did not fill the silence. He sat in it, with his trembling calves, and let it be there. It was the most patient thing Cael had ever seen him do.

"Come by after," Joren said finally. "Tomorrow. Whatever it is. I'll show you how to fall down properly. I've had a lot of practice this week."

"I'll come by."

Joren slid off the wall. He set his feet on the paving and stood there, flat-footed and stubborn, in front of a wall that did not hold anything up. He was going to stand there for an hour. Cael could see it in his shoulders.

Cael picked up his notebook. He looked once at the page near the back, at the declaration in Joren's round, careful hand. Then he closed the book over it, gently, the way you close a door on someone's room.

He walked home the long way. He did not count anything at all.

# Chapter 2 — Tuesdays

Hesk found him on the roof in the afternoon.

The roof was where Cael went when he needed sight lines. That was what they called it, so that neither of them had to say what it really was. The access hatch was at the top of a ladder in the back hall. Beyond it was a flat stretch of tar and gravel, a chimney, a water tank, and a low brick lip around the southern edge where you could sit with your legs inside and look out over the northern quadrant.

He had been up there for two hours. The notebook was open on his knee. He had written nothing. It was mostly something to hold.

The hatch creaked. Hesk came up the ladder one-handed, which he could do, because in the other hand he had two cups of tea balanced on a saucer. He did this when he had something to say and was still choosing how to say it. Cael had worked that out at nine. He had never told Hesk he knew. It seemed like the kind of thing that would stop working if he did.

Hesk sat down next to him on the brick lip and handed him a cup.

They sat. Below them the afternoon moved through the streets. Carts. A cooper rolling a barrel along the gutter with a stick. Two practitioners in Copper armbands walking a delivery of lumber, one of them lifting his end with no visible effort, the other pretending she didn't need help with hers. A knot of children ran between houses shouting about nothing.

And the grey dog.

It lived somewhere on this block. Nobody knew where. It was large and grey and shaggy, with one ear that stood up and one that had given up, and it appeared on the street several times a day to bark at things only it could see. Cael had once spent a week trying to follow it home. He had lost it every time at the same corner.

It came out now, crossed the intersection below them at a steady trot, and barked twice at a drainpipe.

"Joren seems better," Hesk said.

"He is better. He's working at it." Cael turned his cup. "I sat him down in the street this morning."

Hesk's eyebrows went up.

"He asked me to," Cael said. "He's got a stance declaration. Rank 1, Stone. You can't move him while his feet are planted. But his heel comes up when he talks."

"His heel."

"At the end of sentences. When he wants them to land."

Hesk drank some tea and thought about this.

"He'll fix that," Hesk said.

"He fixed it twice before we stopped." Cael looked down at the street. "He's the right temperament for Stone, actually. Patient. He doesn't mind being pushed."

Hesk made a sound in his throat.

Cael knew that sound. He had grown up with it. Hesk had at least four versions, and this one was the one that meant *I have something specific, and I'm deciding where to set it down.*

"You're not Joren," Hesk said.

"No."

"Whatever happens tomorrow—"

"Hesk."

Hesk stopped. He looked at the street. Then he said, more quietly, "—we'll figure it out."

"And?" he said.

"It's manageable," Hesk said. "Whatever the result is. There are provisions."

Cael opened his notebook and wrote *there are provisions.* He did not yet know what it meant. But Hesk did not say things he did not mean, and Hesk had gone to the record office and asked. You did not ask about procedures for something you thought would never happen.

"How long have you been thinking about this?" Cael asked.

Hesk's face stayed level. "A while."

"Since my preliminary assessment."

"Before that."

Cael looked at him. Hesk looked at the street. The grey dog came back the other way, slower, as if it had forgotten something.

"You should have told me," Cael said. He was not angry. It was just the correct statement.

"I know."

"By myself."

Cael believed him.

"How long?" Cael said again.

Hesk walked three lamps before he answered.

"Some years."

"Why?"

"Because I like it." That was true. Cael could hear that it was true. Hesk walked one more lamp. "And because I wanted to understand how people move when they have a Path in them. How a body changes, once it's carrying something."

"Why?"

Hesk did not answer that.

Cael did not ask again. He had the feeling of a door he had not known was there, standing slightly open. He did not want to push it. He was afraid of what it would weigh.

They turned onto Fen Street. Their house was dark, the workshop windows black.

"Hesk."

"Yes."

"The thing you saw before. The irregular coupling. The person." Cael stopped walking. "How did it go for them?"

Hesk stopped too. He stood under the last lamp before their door, with his hands in his coat pockets, and he looked at the ground.

He was quiet long enough that Cael thought this was going to be one of the things he did not answer.

"It was complicated," he said. "I know how it started. I don't know how it ended." He lifted his head. "I've spent longer than you've been alive not knowing the rest of it."

He went in and lit the lamp in the kitchen.

After a moment, Cael followed him in.

---

He woke at two in the morning and did not know why.

The house was silent. Hesk had gone to bed an hour after him; Cael had heard the reading lamp click off in the other room. The street outside had the deep stillness of a district where most people worked an early shift. Somewhere far off, the canal gate sighed once, letting the night water through.

He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling.

There was a water stain in the northeast corner, from a winter four years ago when the gutter had frozen. It was shaped a little like a boot. He had looked at it for four years.

Tomorrow the Arbiter would come.

He did not know what that meant. Nobody could tell you, because it was different for everybody, and because the people who had done it could only tell you after. *Sunlight. A door opening. Like remembering something you never knew. Brief. Heavy.* Eleven people had tried. One of them had not even tried.

He thought about Alis on the step of the certification office, folding her certificate in half and then flattening it out again.

He thought about Joren lying on his back in the street, laughing at the sky, and then not laughing.

He thought about the woman on the sand, dropping her stance under a strike that was not coming. Rising out of it into a forearm. Touching her fist to her chest.

He thought about Hesk, who had gone to the record office and asked about provisions. Who had seen it once, a long time ago. Who had sat on a folding chair in the dark every Tuesday for some years, studying how a body moves when it carries a Path, and had never said why.

*I know how it started. I don't know how it ended.*

Cael sat up.

His notebook was on the shelf beside the bed, where it always was. He took it down. He could not see the page. It did not matter. He had written this before, in other words and other notebooks, in the margins of other things. He did not need to see the page to write it.

*whatever it is, we figure it out.*

He closed the book and put it back on the shelf.

Then he lay down again. The house made its small night sounds around him: a joint in the old timber easing, the tick of the cooling stove downstairs, the patient breathing of a place that thinks nobody is listening. He had grown up inside those sounds. He knew them the way he knew his own name.

He listened to them for a long time.

Somewhere in the listening, without deciding to, he slept.

# Chapter 3 — Eleven Seconds

The certification office opened at eight. Cael arrived at a quarter to.

Being early was not something he could stop doing. He had tried, once, at eleven, for a whole week. He had arrived at everything at the exact minute. It had made him so anxious that Hesk had asked if he was ill.

Hesk had offered to walk with him this morning. They had stood in the kitchen with the low sideways light lying across the table between them, and Hesk had said, "I'll get my coat," and Cael had said, "No."

He had not known he was going to say it until he said it.

"I want to walk there," he said. "By myself. Like Alis did. Like Joren did." He had felt his face going hot. "I want it to be ordinary. Until it isn't."

Hesk had looked at him for a long moment. Then he had nodded, once.

"I'll be in the workshop," he said. "They'll send for me if they need me."

He had not said *if anything happens.* He had said *if they need me.* Cael had noticed. He was not sure Hesk knew he had noticed.

So he walked to Weaver's Row alone. Past Ressa's side door, which was open, the ovens going. Past Garrik's shop, which was shut, because Garrik never opened before nine. Past the low wall at the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, where Joren was not standing yet. Along the canal, where the water lay flat and brown and the lock-keeper was drinking tea in his little hut with the window steamed.

The morning was cold. His breath showed. He did not count anything.

---

The waiting room had a long bench, a counter, and a window in the counter with an official behind it doing paperwork. A small brass machine on the counter gave out numbered tickets. Cael took one. He was seven.

Three people were already there.

Two girls sat close together at the far end of the bench. They were twins. They were his age, and they had the same dark hair tied back the same way. They were not talking. One of them was turning a thin bracelet on her wrist. Three turns, a pause. Three turns, a pause. Cael did not think she knew she was doing it.

At the near end sat a boy of about fourteen, very upright, holding his registration slip in both hands. He was reading it. He was not really reading it. His eyes had the glazed look of someone who has imagined a thing so many times that the real thing has become hard to get to.

The twins had numbers five and six. The glazed boy had eight.

Cael sat between them. He opened his notebook to a clean page. He wrote the date and the time. Then he sat with the pencil over the paper and did not write anything else.

That was not like him. He noticed it, and decided not to look at it too closely.

There was a clock above the counter. It had a big white face meant to be read from across a room. He found that he was watching it more than anything else. It was not that he needed the time. It was that the clock was the only thing in the room behaving predictably. It moved forward. It had no opinions. It was the calmest thing there.

At two minutes past eight, the official behind the window called five.

Both twins stood up.

The official looked at them over her glasses. "Same week?"

"Same day," said one twin. "We were born nine minutes apart."

"Together, then. It's allowed." The official went back to her paperwork. "Second door."

They went in together. The bracelet girl was still turning the bracelet. The door shut.

Cael looked at the clock. He looked at the glazed boy. The glazed boy was looking at nothing.

"Do you know what you'll get?" Cael asked him.

The boy blinked, slowly, as if he had been very far away.

"Force," he said. "My father's Force. My grandmother. My aunt." He looked down at his slip. "It's always Force."

"That's good," said Cael. "If you want it."

The boy thought about this. It seemed to be a new question.

"I don't know," he said, and went back to looking at nothing.

The twins came out twelve minutes later. Each held a certificate. Their faces were almost identical. Both had the stunned, loosened look of people who have been holding something heavy for a long time and have just put it down. One of them was crying a little, quietly, not as though anything was wrong. Their mother was waiting by the outer door. When she saw their faces, something in her own face answered a question Cael had not known he was asking.

*This is what it looks like,* he thought, *when it goes the way you hoped.*

"Eight," called the official.

The glazed boy stood. He went in. He came out after eight minutes with a Force Path certificate, and the look of someone who had expected Force and was now trying to feel something about it. At the door he paused and glanced back toward the examination room, as if to make sure it was still there. Then he left without looking at anyone.

Cael was alone in the waiting room.

He looked at the clock. He looked at the brass ticket machine. He looked at his own hands. They were not quite steady. He decided to stop looking at his hands.

"Seven," said the official.

He stood up.

The examination room was smaller than he had expected.

It held a desk, two chairs, a grey filing cabinet, and a shelf of identical grey folders. There was a framed copy of the Compact Registry charter on one wall, and one tall window. In the middle of the floor, set into the boards, was a circle of pale wood about as wide as his outstretched arms. It was worn smooth in two places, side by side, where years of feet had stood exactly there.

The room smelled more strongly of the old dye-works than the waiting room had. Under the paper and the ink there was something bitter and mineral, like the inside of a stone jar. It had been in the walls longer than the Registry had.

A woman sat at the desk, writing. She was about fifty, with short grey hair and a grey wool jacket and reading glasses on a cord. The nameplate on her desk said WARDEN-ADJUNCT PELLIN. She did not look up straight away. She finished her line first. Cael understood that this was not rudeness. She was giving him a few seconds to arrive in the room before she needed anything from him.

Then she looked up.

"Name and registration," she said.

"Caelen Hesk-ward. Registration 41-7843-V."

He had learned the number the week before. He had said it to himself in the dark, three times, so that it would come out right.

Instead, it gathered from inside him, from somewhere behind his breastbone, behind his sternum, as though it had always been there and had only now decided to be noticed.

It was small. A point of light that he felt more than saw.

It rose up through him slowly, and when it reached the edge of his sight it stayed there. It hung just off to one side. When he turned his eyes toward it, it was still just off to one side. It was like trying to look at the inside of your own eyelid. It was like the place a held breath sits in your chest, which you can feel exactly and never point to.

He glanced at Pellin. She was three feet away, watching the brass plate. Her face showed nothing. She could not see it.

Nobody could. It was his. It had always been his.

It was the sigil. His Arbiter.

It made a sound. It was less like a sound than the memory of one: a single low note that he felt at the bottom of his breastbone, not in his ears.

Then it was quiet.

He counted.

He had not meant to count. He found himself doing it anyway, the way you count during a needle at the healer's, without choosing to.

One. Two. Three.

He watched Pellin's face from the corner of his eye. Her face was information. She was not looking at him. She was looking at the brass plate.

He could feel his heart. He could feel the worn wood under the soles of his boots. He could feel the sigil sitting at the edge of everything, very still, very attentive. It was looking at him. He did not know how he knew that. He knew it the way you know someone is standing behind you in an empty room.

He thought of page seven. *Sunlight. A door opening. Like remembering something you never knew.* And before the count was finished, he knew none of them was going to be his.

Four. Five.

Pellin's face had the pleasant, patient blankness of a professional doing something routine, who does not want to look bored in front of the person she is doing it to.

Six. Seven.

The patience shifted.

It did not shift much. It moved a little way toward something that was not quite attention. The plate at her elbow, Cael understood without looking at it, had not lit when it should have.

Eight.

She sat up straighter. Half an inch. Perhaps less. It was small and it was real, and he saw it because small real things were what he saw.

Nine.

Her hand left the desk. It went to the filing cabinet beside her and rested on the handle of the bottom drawer. It did not open the drawer. It only rested there.

Ten.

The sigil did not move. It hung at the edge of his sight, steady as a nail.

Eleven.

He had been counting for eleven seconds. He was quite certain. He had counted every one.

Then the Arbiter spoke.

It was not a word, exactly. It was more like the shape of a word with everything warm taken out of it. He had heard Alis describe her classification as arriving in her chest before her ears. He had heard Joren call it heavy. What Cael heard was very far away and very close at the same time. It was clear and complete. It was certain the way a mountain is certain.

**[SHATTERED].**

The brass plate at Pellin's elbow lit.

It was eleven seconds late. Text rose up in it, pale and even, and settled into the same word he had just heard.

Pellin's face did not change.

That was the first thing Cael thought, in the half-second before he could think anything else. Her face did not change, and that was extraordinarily professional of her. Her hand on the drawer handle opened the drawer. Her fingers went to the back of it, past the thick front files, and brought out a single form from the very back. It had a coloured tab. The tab was clean and stiff. It had not been handled much.

She uncapped her pen and began to fill the form in, with the same small, quick strokes she had used for everything else.

Her voice, when she spoke, was perfectly level.

"Your classification has resolved," she said. "Please wait a moment."

She did not say the word. Cael understood why, even then, standing in the circle with eleven seconds of silence still sitting in his chest. It was not unkindness. The word had already arrived. They both knew it. Saying it aloud would only have been for form, and she was not a person who did things for form.

He stepped out of the circle. His legs worked. He noted that.

At the edge of his sight, the sigil dimmed. It did not go out. It settled, the way a cat settles into a chair it means to keep, and became something he could stop looking for.

Pellin finished the form. She lifted the telephone receiver on her desk and dialled three numbers. She waited.

"Pellin, Weaver's Row," she said. "Non-standard result." A pause. "Yes. I'll proceed." She hung up.

She looked at Cael properly then, for the first time since he had stepped into the circle. Her eyes were grey and tired and gave nothing away. But she looked at him for a full second longer than she needed to, and in that second he thought there was something in them that was not on the form.

"There's a secondary consultation room," she said. "I'll need you to wait there. Someone will come for you. It won't be long."

---

She walked him down a short corridor. It was longer than it should have been for a building that size, and lined with shelves, and the shelves were full of the same grey folders. He wondered as he walked how many of them held mornings like his. Then he made himself stop wondering.

The room at the end held two chairs and a window. That was all.

The window looked out on a small courtyard of grey flagstones. It had been left open an inch, and cold air came through the gap. In the middle of the courtyard stood a tree.

It had grown at an angle. Its trunk came up out of the flagstones leaning hard to the left, as though it had started out in one direction and thought better of it. Then, about halfway up, it bent and grew straight again. It looked like a question that had changed its mind partway through being asked.

Pellin left. The door closed. He heard her footsteps go back up the corridor at the same unhurried pace she had used coming down.

He sat.

He looked at the tree. He counted its branches. He counted the ones growing up toward the light, and the ones that had given up on the light and grown sideways instead. Eleven toward the light, he decided. He was not sure. He did not count again to check.

There was no clock in this room. Nothing to measure the minutes by except his own attention, which had never felt less reliable.

He was fourteen. He had, perhaps a minute ago, been given a classification that the Compact Registry called deprecated and associated with practitioner safety risk. One of four, in the whole history of the Registry.

He tried to feel something about that. He could not find the place where the feeling should go.

Instead he looked at the tree, and he thought: *that's what it is.* Not the word. The tree. Something that had started in one direction, and was now finding out whether there was another one.

He took out his notebook. He opened it to the page where he had written the date and the time. Under them he wrote:

*Eleven seconds.*

Then he closed it.

He waited thirty-seven minutes. He knew because he counted those too, though less carefully.

---

At the thirty-seventh minute, the door opened, and Hesk came in.

He was wearing his good coat. Not the workshop coat with the burn on the sleeve, but the dark wool one he wore to the Inner gate. His boots were clean. His hair had been combed. In his left hand he held a flat leather folder, and Cael knew without being told that it had his registration papers in it, and Cael's, and that it had been waiting by the front door. Possibly for days. Possibly for years.

His face was completely still.

It was not closed. Hesk never closed his face the way some people did, pulling something down over it like a shutter. It was still the way water is still before something comes up through it. And Cael could see, even then, even in that first moment, that the stillness had been prepared. It had been practised. It had been ready for a long time.

Hesk sat down in the other chair.

He looked at Cael. Then he said, "All right."

"All right," Cael said.

"You're not hurt."

"No."

Hesk nodded slowly. He was looking at Cael the way he looked at a housing he had just opened on the bench. Checking for cracks. Testing where the load went. It was not cold. It was care, expressed as attention.

"The officer told you," he said. It was not a question.

"She didn't say it. She didn't have to."

"What did it say?"

Cael said the word.

He had not planned to say it so plainly. It came out as what it was. One word, specific. He watched Hesk receive it.

"Yes," Hesk said, very quietly. Then: "You've read the registry entry."

"Yes."

"How much of it?"

"Enough."

Hesk was quiet. He was not surprised, Cael saw. Not the way you are surprised by something unexpected. He looked the way you look when something you prepared for finally arrives, and the preparing turns out to help less than you hoped.

"There are things I should have told you," Hesk said.

"Yes." Cael looked out at the crooked tree. "When did you know?"

"Before your preliminary assessment." A pause. "There were signs. Small ones. Before that."

"How much before?"

Hesk did not answer at once. When he did, he said: "A few years."

A few years.

Cael heard it, and something in him went click, like a part seating home.

*A while,* Hesk had said on the roof. *Some years,* he had said under the lamps on Chandler Street. *Because I wanted to understand how people move when they have a Path in them. How a body changes, once it's carrying something.*

Tuesdays.

Some years of Tuesdays, in a folding chair in the dark, studying what a Path does to a body. Because Hesk had been afraid that one day a Path would do something to Cael's.

"Tuesdays," Cael said aloud.

Hesk looked at him. Something moved in his still face, very briefly, and was put away again.

"Yes," he said.

"You were studying. For me."

"Partly for you. Partly because I like it. I didn't lie to you about that." Hesk set the leather folder on his knee. "I'm sorry, Cael. I made a choice that seemed right. It may have been wrong."

Cael let that sit. He watched the tree move a little in a wind he could not feel from inside the room.

"A few years," he said. "You've been watching me for a few years, and you never once—"

"Never once told you," Hesk said, before Cael could finish. "No. That's fair to be angry about. I'd rather you were angry about it out loud than carried it quiet, if it's all the same to you."

"I'm not—" Cael stopped. He did not know what he was, other than tired, and full, in a way that left no room over for anger even if he had wanted it. "I don't know what I am right now."

"That's a reasonable place to be," Hesk said. "You don't have to sort it out today."

Cael could see the shape of the choice now. He could build it, the way he built a calculation. *Don't tell him it might be [SHATTERED]. If it turns out normal, he'll never have had to carry it. Keep the preparing quiet. Keep the worry separate. Don't make a thing real before it is.*

He understood the logic. He did not feel good about the logic. But he understood it.

"Tell me now," he said. "What you know."

Hesk told him.

He told him that he did not know, for certain, what [SHATTERED] meant. That nobody living did, not really. Anyone who told Cael otherwise with confidence was telling him more than they knew.

He told him what he believed, for whatever a belief was worth against a word like that. That the name was registry language. That it had been chosen by people who were frightened of something they did not understand. That fear giving a thing a name did not make the fear right. The word might mean broken. It might mean something the system simply had no other word for yet. He did not know which. He was not going to pretend that he did.

He told him that abilities would come. That they might already be starting. But they would not come the way a standard Path's did. No declarations. No ranks. No clear markers telling him how far he had climbed. Whatever Cael could do, he would have to find by doing it, not by being told.

He told him that there would be practical problems. Registry problems. The Compact kept records. The record would follow Cael wherever he went, and people in certain positions would read it, and what they did then would depend on who they were.

He said nothing at all about the other four. Cael was too full to notice the gap. He would notice it later.

At the end, Hesk leaned forward with his forearms on his knees and looked at Cael from very close.

"Don't let anyone see you move. Not until you know what you're doing."

Cael frowned. "I don't know what that means."

"You will." Hesk said it the way he read a needle. Not drama. A measurement. "Your body is going to learn before your mind does. That's the nature of it, if it's what I think. When it happens—when you find yourself doing something you didn't decide to do—don't show it to anyone. Not yet."

"Has that already happened?"

Hesk held his eyes. "Has it?"

Cael thought.

He thought of the notebooks. Three of them, full of things he had never decided to start writing down. He thought of the hall last night. *There,* he had said, out loud, half a second before the strike, without knowing how he knew. He thought of Joren's heel coming up at the end of a sentence, and his own two fingers already moving to Joren's shoulder before he had finished thinking *now.*

But that was just watching. He had always watched.

"I don't know," he said. "Possibly."

Hesk nodded slowly, as though Cael had given him a number he could trace.

"Then start paying attention to what you don't decide."

---

They did not go back through the waiting room.

Hesk opened the window the rest of the way, looked out into the courtyard, and then closed it again. Then he opened the door and looked down the corridor. Then he said, "This way," and led Cael not left, toward the examination room, but right, toward a door at the other end with a bar across it.

"How do you know about this door?"

"I asked," said Hesk, "on the way in."

The bar was stiff. Hesk lifted it. The door opened onto the grey courtyard and the crooked tree. They crossed the flagstones. Cael passed so close to the tree that he could have touched its bark, and he almost did, and then didn't. On the far side was a wooden gate in the wall. Beyond it was an alley, and the back of a laundry, and the steam from the laundry's vents, and the smell of hot soap.

Nobody saw them go. Cael understood that this was the point.

They came out onto the canal path two streets east of Weaver's Row. The walk home was the walk it had always been. North along the canal. Left at the corner where the man sold transit schedules for cities Cael had never seen. The morning had warmed. People went about their business with the total indifference of a district where everyone was busy with something.

Two blocks from home, they passed Alis.

She was on the other side of the street, carrying a basket. She looked up the way you look up when something familiar moves at the edge of your eye. She saw Cael, and she saw Hesk in his good coat with the leather folder, and something in her face shifted.

It was not pity. Pity would have been worse. It was more like *I see what happened.* That was harder to know what to do with.

She did not cross the street. She did not call out. She lifted one hand. It was not a wave. It was just an open hand, raised, held there a moment.

*I see you.*

Cael raised his in return.

He did not know how much she knew. Kindling results travelled through a neighbourhood like water through a ditch. She probably knew that his had not gone well. She probably did not know the word. What she had given him was the same thing he had given her on the step: notice, and nothing asked in return.

He thought about what she had said. *Better when it's done. Even if it's not what you wanted.*

That evening, he would write under her starred line, in small letters: *She was right that it's done. It doesn't feel done.*

---

Hesk made tea that night.

There was dinner too, at some point. Something from the cold box. Neither of them paid it much attention. The tea was what mattered.

Hesk made it the careful way. Cael had seen him do it perhaps four times a year: on the first frost, on Cael's birthday, on the night Garrik's wife had died. He warmed the pot first. He measured the leaves with the little brass spoon instead of tipping them from the tin. He set the clock and waited the full time instead of the short time they used on ordinary nights. He did not talk while it steeped.

Cael watched him do it and understood, without anyone saying so, that this was how Hesk marked something.

They sat at the kitchen table with the lamp between them.

"There'll be a notice," Hesk said. "Two or three days. Registered post. It's the standard procedure for a non-standard result." He turned his cup. "It'll say what you can live in and where. And it'll say that my standing is affected, while you live here."

Cael had read that. He had read the residential provisions in the Registry summary, months ago, on the long table under the window. He had not needed to be told. Being told was different from reading it.

"Your guild standing," he said.

"My guild standing."

"Because you're Iron-tier and I'm not anything."

"Because I hold the residence," Hesk said. "And they like the people who hold residences to be easy to file."

He drank his tea.

"Ardenmere," he said after a while.

Cael looked up. "You said that on the roof once. Last spring. When I asked about other cities."

"Did I?"

"You said it like it was a word you'd been carrying around."

Hesk almost smiled. "Maybe I had." He set the cup down. "You looked it up."

"Six months ago. The reference room has a section on the eastern cities." Cael had made notes. He had told himself it was ordinary curiosity. "The Unranked District is large."

"Large enough to disappear into, if you need to."

"I don't want to disappear."

"No." Hesk looked at his tea. "That's not what I mean. I mean large enough that nobody checks your registration at the district gate. Anywhere the Compact enforces properly, they pull your record when you come in. Ardenmere's Unranked District is—unofficial. They put up with it because they think it keeps certain kinds of practitioner contained."

"Or they think they do."

Hesk almost smiled again. "Yes."

Cael opened his notebook and wrote *Ardenmere.* Under it he wrote *Unranked District. Large. No gate checks.* Under that he wrote *Hesk knows things about this he hasn't said.* He looked at it for a moment. Then he crossed it out. Not because it was untrue. Because written down it looked like an accusation, and he did not mean it as one. He meant it as a note. There was more to learn, and Hesk was not the only place to learn it.

When he looked up, Hesk was watching him write.

There was an expression on Hesk's face that Cael could not quite read. It was somewhere between pride and worry, with the two so tangled together that pulling them apart did not seem worth doing.

"You're already cataloguing it," Hesk said. "Good. Keep doing that. Write everything down, even the things that don't seem to matter. You won't know which ones matter until later."

"Is that going in the notebook you keep not giving me?"

Hesk's eyebrows rose.

"You said, *I'll give you some things before you go,*" Cael said. "On the roof, last week. When you thought I was asleep against the chimney."

"You were asleep against the chimney."

"I was resting my eyes against the chimney."

Hesk shook his head slowly. Something in his face loosened, just for a moment, the way it did on quiet mornings in the workshop when nothing was broken.

"Some version of it," he said. "Is in the notebook. I've had a long time to think about what I'd tell you, if it ever came to this. Turns out most of it fits on not very many pages." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "I'll give it to you before you go. And some practical things."

Before you go.

It was the first time either of them had said it. It sat on the table between them with the teapot.

"Hesk."

"Yes."

"Whatever you were preparing for. The provisions you asked about. The notes. The Tuesdays." Cael said it because it was true, and because the other thing would have been easier and less true. "It helped. I'm not telling you it was right. I'm telling you it helped."

Hesk was quiet for long enough that the evening came into the room between them. A cart on the stones outside. The grey dog, somewhere, announcing something to nobody. The long mechanical sigh of the canal gate to the north.

"All right," Hesk said at last. His voice was not quite steady. He did not try to make it steady. "Drink your tea. I made it properly."

Cael drank his tea. It was very good. It had been made properly.

They sat with the lamp and the pot until both cups were empty, and then a while longer, and neither of them got up to make more.

# Chapter 4 — The Record

The day after Kindling was the strangest day of all, because nothing happened in it.

No notice came. No officer knocked. The sun came up at the usual time and lay across the kitchen table for its usual twenty minutes. Ressa's ovens went on at the usual hour. The canal gate sighed on schedule. It was as though the whole city had agreed to pretend yesterday had not occurred, and was waiting to see whether Cael would play along.

Hesk went into the workshop after breakfast. Cael heard him in there all morning. He heard the vice open and close. He heard files on brass. He heard the rig's lever creak. Once, near eleven, he went to the workshop door and looked in. Hesk was sitting at the bench with a gauge face in one hand and a jeweller's loupe in the other, perfectly still, not looking through the loupe at all.

Cael went away again without saying anything. He was not sure Hesk had heard him come.

He spent the afternoon on the roof.

From the brick lip on the southern edge, if you leaned out a little, you could see the corner where Fen Street met the canal path. You could see the low wall.

Joren was standing in front of it.

His feet were planted. His arms were folded. Even from here, Cael could see how still he was holding himself, flat-footed, stubborn, in front of a wall that did not hold anything up. Every few minutes he unfolded his arms and looked up Fen Street, toward Cael's house. Then he folded them again.

*Come by after,* Joren had said. *Tomorrow. Whatever it is.*

Cael had said he would.

He sat on the roof with his notebook on his knee and watched Joren wait. He tried, several times, to work out what he would say if he went down. *Hello. I got [SHATTERED]. It means I have to leave. No, I don't know where. No, I don't know what it does. Show me how to fall down properly.* Every version he built fell apart under its own weight before it reached the end.

He knew the word was all over the quadrant by now. He did not know if Joren knew it. He found he could not stand the idea of watching Joren's face find out.

So he did not go down.

Joren stood for his hour. Then he stood for a little longer than his hour. Then he unfolded his arms, and looked up Fen Street one last time, and walked away along the canal with his hands in his pockets.

Cael sat on the roof until the light changed. He had broken a promise to his oldest friend. It was not a large promise. It was the first one he could remember breaking.

He wrote that down. He made himself.

---

The notice came on the second morning.

A courier brought it. He wore the grey-and-brass of the city administration, and he knocked once and waited exactly long enough to be polite. Hesk answered. Cael watched from the kitchen table through the open doorway to the front room.

He noticed the courier's satchel. It was worn pale along the strap, the way a strap wears when it goes out every morning and comes back every night. He noticed how Hesk signed the receipt slip, slowly, in his square hand, without hurrying and without stopping. He noticed the courier's eyes move once over Hesk's shoulder to find Cael at the table, and stay there for half a second, and then slide away. It was the look you give a thing you have been told about and do not know how to look at directly.

The courier left. His boots on the front step going down were quieter than they had been coming up.

It should not have meant anything. It did anyway.

Hesk brought the document into the kitchen and set it down in the middle of the table, between the bread and the teapot. He did not open it. He did not sit down straight away either. He stood and looked down at it, the way he looked at a block of brass stock before deciding where the first cut went.

"You should be the one to read it," he said.

The seal was dark wax. The Compact's mark was pressed into it, a hand holding a balance. The pressing was so clean and even that a machine must have done it. Cael thought, for no good reason, of the brass plate on Pellin's desk. All this care, poured into the outside of things.

He broke the seal and unfolded the paper.

The language was official in the way official language works. It held its own meaning at arm's length. Long sentences. Passive verbs. Everything true and nothing felt. He read it twice. The second time was to be sure he had understood it, because the first time had been like reading through water.

*By authority of the Denvash City Administration, acting under Compact Registry Statute 14, Section 9 (Non-Standard Classification, Residential Provisions):*

*The bearer of Registration 41-7843-V, classified [SHATTERED] as of this date, is hereby notified that residential access above Unranked District designation is revoked effective immediately. The bearer retains legal access to Unranked-designated residence.*

*Note: the registered residence at 14 Fen Street, Outer District, Denvash, is held under the standing of an Iron-tier practitioner (registration 22-1190-H). Continued cohabitation by a [SHATTERED]-classified individual may affect the standing-holder's guild status pending Compact review. The standing-holder is advised to consider residential arrangements accordingly.*

*This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer. It constitutes a residential and administrative provision only.*

He read the last part a third time.

*This notice does not constitute a criminal or civil finding against the bearer.*

It was a strange thing to put in writing. You only wrote that down if a great many people needed to be told it before they would believe it.

He set the paper on the table. He found his hands were not steady enough to write, so he did not take out his notebook. He noted that about his hands. Noting was easier than the other thing.

The paper was good. Heavier than the certificate. When the light from the window caught it, he could see a watermark, a little official flourish in the corner. Someone, somewhere, had decided that this document deserved nice paper. He almost laughed. It did not come out.

He read the note about the residence again. *The standing-holder is advised to consider residential arrangements accordingly.*

He understood it then. Not the words. The machine behind the words.

"They're not expelling me," he said. "They're expelling you. From having me."

Hesk had come round to read it over his shoulder. His face did what it had done in the side room yesterday. It went still, the prepared kind of still.

"That's the mechanism," he said. "It's built that way on purpose. It's easier to make a family choose than to be seen putting a fourteen-year-old out on the road."

"That's convenient."

"Very." Hesk set his hand flat on the table beside the notice. "It isn't a request, Cael. Don't spend yourself being angry at the choice. There isn't one."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway."

Cael looked at the line about guild status. *Pending Compact review.* He thought about the years Hesk had spent being what he was. A competent, quiet, reliable Iron-tier instrument-maker. Canal gauges. Survey levels. A clock inside a walking stick. A name on the guild roll that nobody had ever had a reason to look at twice.

Three lines of print could put all of that in question, over something Cael had not chosen and could not have stopped.

"You could refuse," he said.

It was not really a suggestion. He needed to hear how it sounded out loud. It sounded exactly as impossible as he had known it would.

"I could," Hesk said. "And in three months my registration gets reviewed. And in six I lose the workshop. And in a year we're both in some Unranked District with no income and no standing, and no way for me to help you that isn't worse than this." He said it without heat. It was a calculation, not an argument. "This isn't a fight I win by refusing. It's a fight I win by making sure you land somewhere with something in your hands."

"I don't want you losing anything because of me."

"I'm not losing anything because of you."

Hesk's voice did not rise. It went flatter and steadier, and that made it land harder than shouting would have.

"I'm doing exactly what I decided to do a long time ago," he said. "Which is whatever it takes. This is the *whatever.* Don't make it smaller by apologising for it."

Cael did not have an answer.

They sat. The notice lay between them on the table, and neither of them moved it. Outside, the ordinary sounds of the street went on without them. A cart. A vendor calling. Three doors down, the clatter of Ressa's flour delivery being unloaded off a wagon, sack by sack, with someone counting them in a loud bored voice.

Cael found the ordinariness of it almost unbearable. Then, a moment later, he found it steadying. The sacks would keep coming off the wagon. The count would keep going up. Whatever else happened, somebody would bake bread tomorrow.

"What happens to the workshop," he said at last, "if they actually review you?"

"Nothing today. Possibly nothing for months. The Compact moves slowly when slow suits it, which is most of the time." Hesk's voice had come back to its usual level, the one he used for load figures and hard truths alike. "I won't pretend there's no risk. There is. I've decided the risk is mine. I decided that a long time before this morning."

"When?"

"When I took you in."

He said it simply. As if it were the least interesting fact in the room.

"You don't raise a child," Hesk said, "and keep the right to change your mind about the cost later. That isn't raising. That's something else. I didn't do something else."

He went to the district record office in the afternoon.

It was three streets from the house. He had been there twice before, both times in the way of a curious boy researching something that might never apply to him. It applied to him now.

The building was older than the certification office and did not care how it looked. The stone was the original stone, never refaced. The front steps had worn into a shallow dip in the middle from centuries of feet. Inside it smelled of paper and dust and ink that had dried long ago and had no plans to fade.

A young woman sat at the front desk. She looked barely older than Alis's brother. When Cael gave her his registration number, her pen stopped moving.

It was only for a moment. She caught it and smoothed it away. But Cael saw it, and he saw something else go across her face just before she smoothed that away too. It looked very much like sympathy.

She did not ask why he had come. She stood, took a key from a hook, and led him down a passage to a door at the back.

"Historical classification archive," she said, unlocking it. "The full summary's on the second shelf from the window. Bound in green." She hesitated. "The reading table's by the window. The light's better there."

"Thank you."

She nodded and went back to her desk. She left the door open behind her.

It told him something about how fast information moved through a system that was supposed to be discreet. She had not needed him to ask for the archive. She had known his number. Yesterday morning his number had meant nothing to anyone.

The archive was one long room. The shelves went from floor to ceiling. Under the window stood a scarred reading table and one chair. The window faced north, the way archive windows always seemed to, as though direct sunlight might hurt something. Dust turned slowly in the pale light. He watched it for a moment. Then he made himself stop watching and start working.

The full summary was where she had said. It was thick and green and heavy. The spine cracked when he opened it, a small dry sound in the quiet room that seemed much too loud.

He found the section by working back from the index. It was not where he expected. It was filed near the very end, under a subheading he had not seen in the community hall's copy:

*Deprecated and Non-Standard Designations, Pre-Compact Era Holdovers.*

He read the subheading once. Then he turned the page.

The entry was longer here. Not four lines. Several paragraphs, in the same tight, careful print.

*[SHATTERED]: Designation assigned where Arbiter evaluation finds practitioner architecture that does not resolve to a single recognised Path. The coupling between practitioner and Path framework presents as broken or incomplete. Ability manifestation in such cases has historically been irregular and difficult to predict, and practitioner risk has been assessed as elevated.*

*Of the four (4) recorded instances, disposition is as follows: three (3) practitioners are recorded as deceased within weeks of classification, cause of death listed as "practice-related incident" in Compact records. One (1) practitioner's disposition is recorded as unknown, last registry update dated 32 years prior to the present record.*

He read the last paragraph again.

Then a third time. The sentence was doing something he needed time to take in.

Three of the four, dead within weeks. One not accounted for. This was not a policy. It was not a warning. It was a record of what had actually happened to the only other people who had ever stood where he had stood yesterday morning, with their feet in a worn circle, counting.

He had known the number four. He had known it the way you know a fact you read once and set on a shelf. He had not, until this moment, turned it into four people. Four people with names, who had stepped out of a circle somewhere and heard what he had heard.

Three had been dead within weeks.

*Practice-related incident.*

He looked at the phrase for a long time. It was doing a great deal of work. It could mean they had died trying to use something they did not understand. It could mean someone had made sure of it. He did not have the information to know which. He suspected, without any evidence yet, that the person who had written it down had cared which one was believed. And he suspected that was the only part of the sentence that was really true.

He took out his notebook. His hands were steady now. He copied the entry word for word, all of it, including the subheading. His handwriting came out neater than it needed to be. He noted that. It was its own kind of tell.

When he had finished, he sat with the green volume closed under his hands and did not read anything.

He thought about the clerk at the front desk, young and briefly sorry before she caught herself. He thought about how many people had sat in this chair over however many years the archive had stood, reading something that applied to someone they loved. The room gave no sign of remembering any of them. It had shelves, and dust, and the patience of paper, which outlasts everyone who ever needs it.

He put the book back exactly as he had found it, spine level with its neighbours.

At the front desk, the clerk looked up as he passed. She opened her mouth as if to say something. Then she closed it, and nodded, and he nodded back.

He walked out into a street that looked exactly the way it had looked two hours before. That was almost the worst part. The vendors were still calling. A cart was still creaking past with a load of new-cut lumber that smelled of resin. The world had once again declined to notice that his had moved.

He did not decide, on the walk home, how he felt about any of it.

He decided something else instead. He decided he was not going to be the fifth entry in that book.

It was very easy to decide. That worried him. Deciding things on a walk home was easy. Staying alive long enough to make them true was going to be the hard part.

---

Hesk was in the workshop when he got back, with a half-built housing clamped in the vice and a tray of small tools beside it. He looked up as Cael came in. He read something in Cael's face. He put the tools down.

"You went to the archive."

"Yes."

"You found the full entry."

"Yes." Cael sat on the workshop stool across the bench from him. It was the stool he had sat on since his feet did not reach the floor. They reached now. "Three dead within weeks. One not accounted for."

Hesk was quiet a moment. Then: "I know."

"You knew before."

"Yes."

"You didn't tell me."

"No."

Hesk met his eyes and did not look away from it.

"I told you what you needed to make decisions," he said. "I didn't tell you the thing that would only make you afraid, without helping you do anything about it. I won't pretend that was a neutral choice. It wasn't. I made a judgement. You're allowed to think it was the wrong one."

Cael sat with that.

He found, a little to his surprise, that he did not think it was wrong. Not completely. It was the choice of someone trying to hold two things at once. One was the job of getting Cael ready for what was coming. The other was the instinct not to hand him more weight than the day required. Those two things did not fit together neatly. They hardly ever did.

He watched Hesk's hands while he thought. They had gone back to the housing in the vice. They moved through the work without hurry, as precise as ever, the hands of a craftsman who had done this motion ten thousand times and could keep doing it while the rest of him held a conversation that mattered far more than the instrument. Cael had always found that steadiness a comfort. He found it a comfort now. It took him a moment to notice that the comfort and the anger were both there at once, side by side, and neither one was cancelling the other out.

"I need you to tell me things now," Cael said. "As they come. Not managed. Not timed for when you think I'm ready."

"All right." Hesk set down the file. "Then I need to tell you something now. While we're being plain."

"Okay."

Hesk looked at the housing. Then he looked back at Cael.

"The fourth record," he said. "The one with no disposition. Thirty-two years ago."

He stopped. He was choosing the next words carefully, the way a man chooses words he has rehearsed for years without knowing when he would have to say them.

"I was twenty-two," he said. "I was doing two years with the Compact's regional logistics division. Not fighting. Supply routing. The kind of posting they give to competent Iron-tiers who don't ask questions." He paused. "I was there when that file was opened. I saw the beginning of it. I didn't see the end."

Cael had gone very still.

"What happened to them?"

"I don't know."

He said it plainly, and it had the weight of a true answer that had cost something to give.

"That isn't an evasion," Hesk said. "I mean I don't know. The case was reassigned. It was pulled out of regional oversight into something higher, something I didn't have clearance for. The file went quiet. I asked about it twice. Both times I was told it wasn't my concern. In the tone that means *stop asking.*"

He let out a long breath.

"I left the division eighteen months later," he said. "I've wondered about it for thirty-two years. I don't have an answer. I have a name I've never told anyone. And a promise I made myself the day I mustered out. I think you should know both of those exist. Even though I can't finish the story for you yet."

"Will you tell me the name?"

"When you're older."

Cael opened his mouth.

Hesk lifted a hand.

"Not because I don't trust you," he said. "Because a name like that is a thread. And I want you strong enough to pull it without it pulling you first." He lowered his hand. "That isn't a managed timeline, Cael. It's a real condition. When you meet it, you'll know."

Cael did not write any of it down. Not yet. Not in front of him. He held it instead. He turned it over in his mind the way he had turned over the third exchange on the sheet in the community hall. The shape of it mattered more right now than what was inside.

A name never told. A file opened, and then taken somewhere higher. A promise.

"Okay," he said at last. "I believe you."

Hesk nodded once. He picked his tools back up.

For a while they sat in the workshop together and did not talk. Hesk filed. Cael watched the brass dust gather in a little gold drift on the bench. It was, as it had always been, a way of saying several things at once.

---

That evening, after the light had gone out of the window and Hesk had gone quiet over his book in the front room, Cael packed.

It did not take long. He did not own much. Clothes. Three notebooks. A handful of small tools he had made himself in the workshop, which were more for remembering than for using. A file with a handle he had turned on Hesk's lathe at ten. A pair of fine tweezers he had ground from spring steel and got slightly wrong, so that they closed a hair off true. He packed them anyway.

He held each notebook before he put it in the bag.

The first was three years old. Its spine was cracked and its cover had gone soft from handling. Most of it was a younger boy's observations, the kind he would have been embarrassed by if they had not been so earnest. *Garrik says a bearing is happiest when it is doing nothing.* *The grey dog does not like the colour blue.*

The second carried him through the year he had first started noticing the gap between the Outer District and the Inner one. The five pages about the wall were in it. They had felt like an essay when he wrote them. Now they felt more like a warning he had written to himself without knowing who it was for.

The third was the current one. It held eleven descriptions of Kindling, and two stars, and a Stone Path declaration in Joren's round hand near the back. And now, folded into the back pages, a copy of a registry entry about four people he had never met and was never going to stop thinking about.

He packed them in order. Oldest at the bottom. The way you pack something you mean to keep adding to.

Then he sat on the bed and took a single loose page from the back of the current notebook, and he wrote a note to Joren.

It took a long time. He wrote three versions and tore up two. The one he kept was short.

*Joren —*

*I didn't come by. I said I would and I didn't. That was wrong of me and I'm sorry. I couldn't work out what to say.*

*It was [SHATTERED]. You can look it up in the hall. Don't read the green book in the record office. It isn't worth it.*

*I have to leave Denvash. Hesk's standing depends on it. I'm going east. I'll write when I know where I am.*

*Keep your heels down.*

*— C.*

He folded it in four and put it in his coat pocket.

Hesk came in as he was tying the bag.

He was carrying three things. He set them down on the bed one at a time, with the care of a man who had planned this moment for a long time, and would not admit for how long.

The first was a cloth pouch, heavy, tied at the neck. Cael lifted it. It clinked softly. It held more than he had expected. When he looked inside, the coins were all small ones, worn marks and half-marks, the kind that are saved one or two at a time rather than drawn out all at once.

The second was a book.

It was small, a pocket-sized book bound by hand in leather gone soft at the corners. A cord was wound around it to keep it shut. The cord was worn smooth in the place where it was always tied.

Cael reached for it. Hesk did not stop him. But Hesk did not let go of it right away either, and for a moment they both had a hand on it.

"Not tonight," Hesk said. "Read it on the road. It'll make more sense with some distance between you and this house."

Cael nodded. Hesk let go. Cael put the book in the bag without opening it.

The third thing was not an object.

Hesk sat on the edge of the bed. He sat the way a man sits when he is about to say something he has rehearsed, and has decided to say plainly anyway.

"Ardenmere," he said. "Two days east on the merchant road. The Unranked District there is bigger than anything you've seen. Big enough that nobody checks registration at the district gate. It isn't perfect. It isn't safe the way a home is safe. But it's a place where your classification doesn't announce itself, and that buys you room. Room to work out what you're dealing with before anyone with authority over you gets a vote in it."

"You've been there."

"Once. A long time ago."

He did not say more. Cael remembered the workshop, and the name, and did not push.

"Go there first," Hesk said. "Find your feet. Everything else, you'll work out as it comes. Same as everyone does." He looked at Cael directly. "You're not behind, Cael. You're early. There's a difference."

Cael looked at the bag. Money. Notebooks. A few crooked tools. A leather book he was not allowed to open. It was a strange kind of complete. Everything he owned that mattered. None of it told him what he was going to become.

"What was Ardenmere like?" he asked. "When you were there."

Hesk was quiet long enough that Cael thought the question had gone somewhere he was not going to follow.

"Loud," Hesk said finally. "Bigger than I expected. I was younger than you'd think, and I didn't understand half of what I was looking at." A pause. "I understand more of it now, looking back, than I did standing in it. That's most places, I think. You don't see the shape of somewhere until you're far enough away to see all of it."

"Will I understand it while I'm there? Or only after?"

"Both, probably. At different speeds. For different parts of it." Hesk almost smiled. It was the almost-smile he used for a question that was fair and had no clean answer. "You've always understood things faster than most people your age. That isn't going to stop just because the thing in front of you is bigger than anything you've had to understand before."

Cael turned that over.

It was the closest thing to reassurance Hesk had offered all day. He had delivered it the way he delivered most things. Sideways. Tucked inside the answer to a different question. Trusting Cael to find it.

"I'll write," Cael said.

"You'd better."

Hesk's voice caught on the second word. It was very slight. He covered it by getting up and going to the window and fussing with the latch, which did not need fussing with. He worked it open and shut twice. Then he stood with his back to the room and his hand on the frame.

"Long walk tomorrow," he said, to the window. "Get some sleep."

He went out. He left the door open a hand's width, the way he had left it when Cael was small and afraid of the dark, and had not been for years.

Cael did not close it.

# Chapter 5 — The Six O'Clock Light

He woke before it was light and lay still, doing what he always did on days that mattered. He took inventory.

Ceiling. The boot-shaped stain in the northeast corner. The bag by the door, packed and tied, waiting with the patience of an object that knows what it is for. The door to the hall, still open a hand's width.

From the kitchen came the sound of Hesk already up. The kettle being filled. Not banged down on the stove; set there. It was the sound of a man being careful not to hurry something he did not want to end.

Cael stayed in bed a little longer than the inventory needed.

He listened to the house. The second floorboard from the kitchen door creaked when Hesk crossed it. The tap ran and stopped. A cupboard closed with a soft, deliberate click. That was the cupboard by the stove; its latch had worked loose two winters ago, and if you shut it firmly it rattled for a full minute afterward, so Hesk always closed it gently, with two fingers. Cael knew this house's sounds the way he knew the streets outside it. Completely. Without ever having had to think about it.

He was thinking about it now. He supposed that was what leaving was. You noticed what you knew because you were about to stop being able to use it.

He got up.

Dressing took longer than it should have. There was nothing to decide. Everything else was packed. But his hands kept doing small unnecessary things. Smoothing a sleeve. Re-tying a lace that was tied. He let them. They seemed to know something he did not want to say out loud, which was that he would like this part to last.

---

Hesk had made the kind of breakfast that was not about food.

Bread from Ressa's, still warm, which meant Hesk had been to the bakery before it was properly open. The good preserves. The plum ones, from the jar on the high shelf that only came down for birthdays and first frosts. And tea made the careful way, with the brass spoon, steeped the full time.

Neither of them said anything about it. They did not need to.

"Merchant road runs east from the north depot," Hesk said, over the tea. He used the voice he used for practical information, because practical information was easier to say than the other kind. "Two days on foot at a steady pace. Less if a cart takes you partway for a fare. There's a waystation about a day out. Low timber building, south side of the road, a lamp over the door. Sleep there."

"I read the road notes at the record office."

"I know you did. I'm telling you anyway." A small dry almost-smile. "Some things go in better heard than read."

"Ardenmere's gate opens at dawn and closes at full dark," Cael said. "Don't arrive after dark on the first night."

"There. You see? Heard it, and now you can say it back."

"I could say it back before."

"Now you can say it back in my voice."

Cael looked at him. "Is that going in the notebook? Under *things I told you that you already knew*?"

"It might." Hesk did not quite smile, but something in his face loosened. "There's a way of doing this morning where I say all the practical things and none of the other things. Because I've had more practice with the practical ones. I know that's what I'm doing. I'm doing it anyway. The practical things are true too, and you'll need them on the road whether I manage the rest or not."

"You don't have to manage the rest."

"I know." He looked into his tea. "I'm going to try anyway."

Cael ate the bread. It tasted exactly the way it always tasted. That seemed wrong somehow. Everything else had changed. The bread had not even noticed. He chewed more slowly than usual. He did not admit to himself why.

When they had finished, Hesk got up and went into the workshop. He was gone for a minute. When he came back, he had something small in his closed hand.

He put it on the table in front of Cael and took his hand away.

It was the bracket. The lock gauge bracket, the first one. The one that had failed at less than twice working load with that small sound like a knuckle. It still had its faint new curve, as if it were considering a bow.

Cael looked at it. Then he looked up.

"The drawer," he said.

*Springs, do not use.* The ones that taught him something.

"Everyone needs one," Hesk said. "You'll have a bench of your own someday. You'll want something in the drawer." He sat back down. "Start with that."

Cael picked it up. It was cold and small and slightly bent. He turned it over in his fingers. He thought of the needle swinging and not coming back. *That's a lie somewhere.* He thought of forty minutes of filing and loading and reading a dial, to find a number that had been printed and never traced.

He put it in his coat pocket, next to the folded note for Joren.

"Thank you," he said.

It did not seem like enough. Hesk nodded as though it were.

---

They walked to the depot together.

It was not strictly necessary. Cael knew the way. He had known it since before he could remember not knowing it. Neither of them suggested skipping it.

The Outer District at six in the morning had its own texture. Cael had soaked it up so completely over fourteen years that he had never looked straight at it. He looked now.

Ressa's side door was open. The ovens were going. The smell of the first loaves carried down the street the way it always did, half a block in every direction. Ressa herself was out on the front step with a broom, sweeping. She was a broad, quiet woman with flour to the elbows. She had sold Hesk bread every morning of Cael's life.

She looked up as they came by.

Her eyes went to the bag on Cael's shoulder. Then to his face. Something in her own face eased into an understanding that did not need any explaining.

"Safe travels," she said.

That was all. She did not ask where. She did not ask why. She had heard something. Everyone within four streets had heard something. News in the Outer District moved the way water moved through the canal locks, finding every low place. And Ressa had decided for herself that the right answer was two words and nothing else.

"Thank you," Cael said.

She nodded, and went back to her sweeping. Then, as they passed, without looking up, she said, "The one by the window's still warm. I put an extra in the bag your grandfather took."

Cael looked at Hesk. Hesk looked at the sky.

"She put an extra in," Hesk said, "and wouldn't take for it."

Cael turned to say something to Ressa. She had already gone inside. The side door stood open, and the smell came out of it, and that was her answer.

---

Three doors down, Garrik was out in front of his shop.

He had a cart wheel up on the stand and was turning it slowly with one hand, listening to the hub, his head tilted. It was what he did every morning, the first wheel of the day, before he had his tea. He said a wheel told you everything if you were quiet enough to hear it.

He did not look up as they passed.

He did not look up in the particular way of a man choosing not to. His hand kept turning the wheel. His head kept its tilt. His eyes stayed fixed on the hub with a steadiness that took more effort than looking would have.

Cael walked by. He did not say anything.

Two nights ago, in the warm little room behind the curtain, Garrik had said *Well. Finally,* and patted the projector, and said *he's one of us.* Two nights ago, Cael had not had a classification. Now he had one, and it had a word in it that belonged to four people in a green book.

It hurt more than he expected. That surprised him. He had thought he had used up all the hurt there was.

He made himself think about it properly, walking, because thinking about things properly was the one tool he knew he had. Some people met a hard thing by looking straight at it. Some people found it easier to look somewhere else. Neither had very much to do with him. Garrik's hand on the wheel was not saying *I think less of you.* It was saying *I don't know how to do this.* Those were different. He was nearly sure they were different.

He filed it. It cost something to understand that at fourteen. He understood it anyway.

Behind them, the wheel stopped turning.

Cael did not look back. He thought perhaps Garrik had looked up after all, once they were past, when it was safe. He thought perhaps that was the best Garrik could manage. He decided to count it.

---

They passed the community hall. The front was shuttered. The reference room was dark behind its windows. Down the side of the building, the green door with the worn brass handle was shut.

Cael thought about the long table under the window, and the summary on its shelf, and the four lines near the end of it that had started all this before it had any right to start. He felt an odd gratitude toward that room. Denvash was taking a great deal from him this morning. But it had never hidden the truth from him. It had left it on a shelf, in a public room, for any boy stubborn enough to read that far.

"Will you still go?" he said. "On Tuesdays."

Hesk walked several paces before he answered.

"Yes," he said. "Someone has to tell Garrik when he's threading it backward."

"He threads it backward?"

"About one week in four. The biscuit woman says it's the only time the films make sense."

Cael laughed. It surprised both of them. It came out of him short and rough, and it hurt a little, and he was glad of it.

---

At the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, he stopped.

"One minute," he said.

Hesk stopped too, and waited, and did not ask.

The low wall stood where it always stood. Waist-high, grey, holding nothing up. There was nobody in front of it. Joren would not come for his hour until after breakfast. The grit on the paving lay loose and ordinary, the way it lay when nobody was standing there asking it to settle.

When they were eight, they had found a gap between two of the top blocks, at the end nearest the canal. The mortar had crumbled out of it. It was just wide enough to slide a folded paper into. They had used it for a whole summer to leave each other messages. Most of them were maps to places that did not exist. One of them, Cael remembered, had been a list of every word Joren knew that rhymed with *canal.* There were four. Two were made up.

He found the gap. It was still there.

He took the folded note out of his pocket and slid it in, deep, until only a white corner showed. Joren would see it. Joren would stand right in front of it for an hour, flat-footed, with nothing to look at but the wall.

Cael put his hand flat on the cold top of the wall for a moment.

Then he went back to Hesk.

"Joren," Hesk said. It was not a question.

"I didn't go and see him. Yesterday. I said I would."

Hesk nodded slowly. "And now you've written."

"It's not the same."

"No," Hesk said. "But it's honest. And honest lasts longer." He started walking again. "He'll be all right. He's got a stance declaration."

"He's got a heel problem."

"He'll fix it," Hesk said. "You showed him where it was."

---

Two streets on, a woman Cael half knew nodded to Hesk as they passed. She was somebody's aunt, a face from the market rather than a name. It was the ordinary nod of one neighbour to another. Then her eyes found Cael's bag, and whatever Hesk was carrying underneath his steady face, and her nod slowed.

It softened into something more like a held breath.

She did not say anything. Nobody, it seemed, had the right words for this. Cael was beginning to think that was not a failure on the street's part. It was an honest admission. There were no right words. People who said none were telling the truth about it.

Then they came out onto Lantern Street, where it ran east toward the depot, and the light came in.

It was six o'clock. The sun was just over the rooftops at the far end of the street. It came down the whole length of Lantern Street at a low, flat angle, and it was gold. Not yellow. Gold, the deep warm gold of brass that has just been polished. It caught the dust from the bakeries and the smoke from the first chimneys and lit them, so that the whole street seemed to be full of slow, bright, drifting specks. It caught the edges of the cobbles. It caught the wet on a horse trough. It caught the side of Hesk's face and turned his grey beard briefly to the colour of wheat.

Cael stopped walking.

He looked at it. All of it. He looked deliberately, the way he would look at a page he meant to copy.

*This,* he thought. *This exact colour. I am going to keep this.*

He did not take out his notebook. Some things did not go in the notebook. Some things you wrote somewhere else.

Hesk had stopped beside him. He did not ask what Cael was looking at. After a moment, Cael saw, he had turned to look down the street too, into the same light, with the same face.

They stood there until the sun cleared the rooftop and the gold thinned to ordinary morning.

Then they walked on.

---

The north transit depot was louder than the streets.

Merchants were loading carts. A family with far too much luggage was arguing, quietly and fiercely, about whether they had too much luggage. A team of draught horses stood steaming in their traces while their driver fed them from a nosebag and talked to them about his sister. A toll-taker in a little booth was disputing a charge with a carter. Both of them had clearly had this exact dispute many times before and seemed to find it relaxing.

Two Bronze-tier practitioners in guild colours walked through the crowd. They moved with the unhurried ease of people who had never once in their lives had to wait in a queue. The crowd opened for them without being asked. Cael watched their feet. Heel, flat. Heel, flat. The same measured walk as the Iron fighter on the sheet.

He thought: *I'm going to watch everyone's feet for the rest of my life now.*

Hesk walked him to the far edge of the depot, where the paved yard ended and the merchant road began. The cobbles stopped there in a ragged line, as if they had simply run out of interest. Beyond them was packed earth, pale and hard, running east between low walls toward the farmland.

They stood at the line.

Cael had imagined this moment many times over the past two days. He had imagined it a dozen different ways. None of them had prepared him for how ordinary it felt. It was not smaller than he had imagined, exactly. It was quieter. It felt less like a scene and more like a moment, the kind that turns out to have mattered much more than it seemed to while it was happening.

Around them, the depot carried on. The toll-taker and the carter reached their weekly agreement. The horses' driver finished his story about his sister. The family with too much luggage left one bag behind on purpose and walked off without looking at it.

Cael found it almost offensive that the depot did not know. That nobody in the city knew this morning was different from all the others. Then, a moment later, he found it steadying. The world was going to keep doing what it did, whatever he did next. There was something almost generous in that.

"You have the money," Hesk said.

It was not a question. It was a checklist, said aloud. Hesk checked every load calculation twice, even when he was sure.

"Yes."

"The notebook. Not opened until the road."

"Yes."

"The bread Ressa put in."

"Yes."

"Ardenmere. The Unranked District. Find your feet before you find anything else."

"I remember."

"Don't trust the first person who's kind to you completely. Don't distrust them completely either. Watch. The way you already watch everything. Let the watching tell you which way to lean." Hesk stopped. Something shifted in his shoulders, the way it shifted at the bench when a repair turned out to be more delicate than it looked. "I know I'm repeating myself. I've had two days to say everything I wanted to say. It turns out there isn't a version of two days that's enough."

"You could have another two."

"I could. And then another two after that. And I'd still be standing here finding things I hadn't said." The almost-smile came back, worn thinner now by holding it. "That isn't a problem two more days fixes. At some point I have to trust that what you're carrying is enough. Not because it's complete. Because it's what there is. And you're good at building on incomplete things. You've been doing it all your life."

Cael did not have an answer to that. So he did not try to make one.

Hesk nodded, as if the silence had answered him anyway.

He looked, for a moment, as though he were going to say something else. Something large. Something the size of what was actually happening. Cael watched him gather it. Then he watched him decide against it, visibly, in favour of something smaller and truer.

"Write to me," Hesk said. "Even if there's nothing to say. Especially then."

"I will."

Hesk reached out and put his hand on Cael's shoulder.

"It's enough," Hesk said quietly. "It's more than enough. Go on, then. Before I decide it isn't."

Cael did not trust his voice to answer that.

"I'll write," he said instead. It was not the same thing. Hesk seemed to understand it as close enough.

Hesk let go. He stepped back.

He stepped off the last cobble onto the packed earth.

He went.

Cael lifted a hand.

The shape lifted one back.

Then Cael turned east and did not look back again. Looking back a second time would have made it harder. He needed it to stay possible.

The district gate was a quarter mile on.

Mostly it existed so that someone could check merchant manifests. Carts queued in front of it. He flagged Cael down out of habit, not suspicion.

"Registration," the guard said. He was already reaching in his mind for the next cart's papers.

Cael handed his over.

The guard glanced at it the way he must have glanced at a hundred papers already that morning. Name. Number. Classification.

Then he didn't.

His eyes stopped on the classification line. Cael watched it happen. There was a half-second in which the man's face went from routine to something it had not expected, and had to decide, in that half-second, what to do about it.

The guard's face did nothing dramatic. It did something smaller, and Cael thought, more honest. It drew back. Not his body. Just his face. It took on the shape of a man deciding that the quickest way out of an uncomfortable thing was to make it as short as possible.

"Go on," the guard said.

He handed the papers back without meeting Cael's eyes. He waved him through the arch faster than he had waved the cart in front of him.

Cael walked through. He did not say anything.

He did not look back at the guard, though he wanted to. He wanted to see whether the man's face had already gone back to boredom. Whether the whole thing had stopped mattering to everyone except Cael the moment his back was turned.

He suspected it had. And that, he thought, was the most useful part of the whole exchange. He was going to matter enormously to himself for a long time to come. To almost everyone else, he would matter for exactly as long as it took them to finish the sentence.

Twenty paces past the arch, on the open road, with the wall falling away behind him, he stopped.

He took out his notebook. He wrote it down, because it was information, and information went in the notebook whether he liked how it felt or not.

*Gate guard, Denvash Outer gate. Saw [SHATTERED]. Didn't ask questions. Waved me through faster than routine.*

*People don't engage with what they're afraid of. They get you through the interaction as quickly as possible so they don't have to keep looking at it.*

*This is useful. Fear that wants to look away is fear that isn't paying close attention. Remember this.*

He read it over. He started to close the book.

Then he stopped, and opened it again, and added one more line below the others.

*Corollary, added a moment later, because it felt important enough to earn its own line: this will not always be true. Somewhere, eventually, I am going to meet the kind of fear that does the opposite — that looks harder, not away. I don't know yet what that looks like. I should.*

# Chapter 6 — The Rise

For the first few hours, the merchant road ran through farmland.

Fields on both sides. Low stone walls. A farmhouse every mile or so, with smoke going up straight from the morning fire. The traffic was light. A few carts went the other way, into Denvash, loaded with cabbages and firewood. A family went by on foot, pulling a hand cart with a sleeping baby tucked in among the sacks. Two merchants on horseback passed him at a trot and did not look down.

Cael had walked a great deal in his life. He had never walked like this.

In Denvash, walking had always meant moving through something he already understood. Streets had names. Distances had ends. There was always a corner coming, and he always knew what was round it. Out here, the road simply went on. It did not care about him. His own footsteps were the only steady rhythm in a landscape that otherwise offered him the odd bird and, very far off, the slow work of someone in a field he would never see up close.

He noticed things anyway. Noticing was the one skill he trusted himself to have, wherever he was.

Mist lay in the low places between the fields. It burned off unevenly as the sun climbed. The high ground cleared first. The hollows stayed white and ghostly long after, as though they were holding on to the night on purpose.

A farmer two fields over raised a hand to him without stopping his work. He was mending a wall, lifting stones from a heap and setting each one with a little twist, and he raised his hand between one stone and the next. Cael raised his back. For the space of that small exchange, he felt less alone than the empty road had been making him feel. It surprised him how much less.

Around the second hour, a dog joined him.

It came out of a gateway without any fuss. It was a thin brown dog with a white chest and one torn ear, and it fell into step beside him as though they had agreed to meet. It did not look at him. It did not ask for anything. It trotted along at his left side for most of an hour, matching his pace, with the loose, easy indifference of an animal that belonged to the road itself rather than to anyone on it.

"You're not the grey dog," Cael told it.

The dog did not deny this.

At a crossroads with a milestone, it peeled off toward a farmhouse without a backward glance, the same way it had come. Cael watched it go. He found he minded more than he would have expected to.

By midmorning his legs had started to complain. It was not pain, exactly. It was a dull, insistent awareness of themselves that he had never had to think about in the city, where distances were short and full of things to look at. The road notes had said to find a pace you could hold all day rather than one that felt brave. He slowed down. He found he could breathe properly again.

There was no reason to hurry. Ardenmere would be there in two days whether he arrived worn out or only tired.

He stopped late in the morning at a low wall beside the road.

It was waist-high and made of grey stone. It held back a field of stubble. He sat on it, and looked at it, and thought of the wall at the corner of Fen Street and the canal path, with a folded paper in the gap between two blocks.

Joren would have found it by now. He would have stood in front of it for his hour. Cael hoped he had kept his heels down.

He ate the bread Ressa had put in the bag. It was still faintly soft. It tasted of the corner, of the side door, of every morning of his life. He ate all of it, slowly, and brushed the crumbs off his knees for the sparrows.

Then he did the thing he had been telling himself he would wait for the road to do properly.

He took out the leather book.

It was smaller than he had thought, now that he had it in his hands with nobody watching. It fitted in his palm. The leather was soft with handling. The cord was worn smooth in one place, where it had been tied and untied a great many times, over what must have been years.

He untied it. He opened it to the first page.

It was Hesk's handwriting. But it was not the square, careful hand he used for load figures on the workshop slate. It was rougher, and it changed as the pages went on. Different inks. Different pressures. Some entries were pale and brownish with age. Some were still dark. It looked like something written in many sittings across a long stretch of time, by a man who had kept coming back to it.

The first page had only a few lines on it.

*If you have this, then it was what I thought it was. I am sorry. I would have given a great deal to be wrong. I can't change the word. What I can give you is what I've worked out, mostly by watching, because there was nobody I could ask without being asked why.*

*None of it is certain. Read it anyway.*

He turned the page.

*First: I don't know what [SHATTERED] actually means, and I want to be honest with you about that before I tell you anything else, because everything after this sentence is a guess dressed up as advice. What I believe, for whatever a belief is worth against a word like that: the name was chosen by people who were frightened, and fear naming a thing doesn't make the fear correct. It might mean broken. It might mean something outside the map entirely — not off it, just not yet drawn. I don't know which. I've had years to sit with not knowing, and I still don't have a better answer than that. Anyone who tells you they're certain is telling you more than they actually know.*

Cael read it twice.

He had been expecting something. He realised it only now, sitting on the wall, with the page under his thumb. Somewhere underneath the parts of himself he had looked at closely, he had expected that a man who had spent years preparing for this would have reached an answer. That the book would open, and the answer would be in it, in Hesk's handwriting, the way the answer to a load problem was always at the bottom of the slate.

There was no answer. There was a grown man saying he did not have one.

He sat with that for a while. And slowly he found that it was more use to him than an answer would have been. An answer could be wrong. An answer could be a number printed on a stock sheet that nobody had ever traced. This was not that. This was the true figure, measured by hand. *I don't know.* You could build on a figure like that. You knew exactly how much weight it would hold.

He turned the page.

*Second: your body is going to learn before your mind does. This isn't poetry, it's the most practical thing I can tell you. Whatever you can do, it won't arrive as a declaration in a formatted box the way it does for standard Paths. It will arrive as instinct — a moment where you do something before you've decided to do it, and only afterward do you understand what just happened. When that happens, don't perform it for an audience. Watch it. Learn what your body already knows before you try to explain it to anyone else, including yourself.*

*Third: don't perform weakness, and don't perform strength. Perform competence. People read confidence as a threat and helplessness as an opportunity. Competence, quiet and undramatic, is the only posture that buys you room to actually figure out what you are before someone else decides for you.*

He thought about Joren's declaration, in Joren's round hand at the back of the current notebook. *PATH DECLARATION — COPPER RANK 1.* A heading. A bracketed name. A sentence telling you exactly what you could do and exactly where it stopped. Joren had read it four hundred times. Joren knew where the edges were. Joren's whole Path was a set of edges, written down for him.

Cael would not get one. Not at Rank 1, not at Rank 10. There would be no box with his abilities in it, no line telling him what ended the effect. He would have to find the edges by walking into them.

He looked down the road. It ran east between its low walls, pale and empty.

*Perform competence.*

He did not know yet what he was competent at. But he knew what it felt like to be competent at a bracket. He knew the needle sitting still at three and a half times working load. He supposed it would feel something like that. He supposed he would know it when it happened.

He turned the page.

The ink here was different. Paler. Older, or newer; it was hard to tell. The handwriting was more careful, as though the man writing it had slowed down.

*Fourth: if the abilities do come, they will not come labeled. You will not be told what you have or where it came from. This is the part I have the least practical advice for, because I have never watched it happen to someone and I am working from theory rather than observation. My best guess: pay attention to what you find yourself able to do only under real pressure, not in practice. Whatever this is, I suspect it does not show itself for an audience of one, calm and unthreatened. It shows itself when something forces it to.*

*Fifth, and this one matters more than the others, so read it twice: you are allowed to be afraid and competent at the same time. I have watched people convince themselves those two things are opposites, that fear is proof you're not ready. It isn't. Fear that keeps you paying attention is a tool, not a flaw. The only fear worth worrying about is the kind that makes you stop watching. Yours won't. I've known you fourteen years. You don't know how to stop watching. It's the thing about you I'm least worried will fail you.*

He read the fifth one twice, because it told him to.

Then he read it a third time, because he wanted to.

He thought about the eleven seconds, counting in the worn circle while the sigil hung at the edge of his sight. He thought about his own hands on the kitchen table the morning the notice came, and how he had looked at them and named what they were doing instead of feeling it. He had thought, all his life, that the watching was a kind of hiding. A way of standing a little outside things so that they could not quite reach him. He had thought it was something he would have to grow out of.

Hesk did not think so. Hesk thought it was the tool.

Cael sat on the wall and let that settle. It did not settle all the way. But it went a good distance down.

*When you don't know what you can do yet, watch someone who does. Your body will learn before your mind does.*

There was no number on it. It had been written recently. Months ago at most. Perhaps weeks.

He thought about a folding chair in the dark behind a curtain. A white sheet on a frame. A projector ticking. A woman dropping her stance under a strike that did not come, and rising into a forearm. *Watch his hips. The third exchange is where they tell you the truth.*

The only lesson Hesk had been sure enough of to give. Hesk had taken him to the hall because he wanted Cael to know how to watch before he needed to.

It helped. Not all the way. But it helped, the way true things help more than comforting ones, even when the true thing is *I don't know.*

He put the book in the inside pocket of his coat, where the bracket was. Then he got up, because Ardenmere was still most of two days east, and the afternoon was not going to last forever.

Ardenmere lay below him in a wide river valley, and it was enormous.

He had read about it. He had read the reference section on the eastern cities three times. He had known it was larger than Denvash. He had not known what *larger* meant until he stood on the rise and looked.

The river came in from the north, wide and slow and copper-coloured in the low sun. It bent through the middle of the valley like an arm. On the far bank, in the crook of that arm, rose the Ranked core. It was walled, and the walls were stepped, ring inside ring, each ring higher than the last. He could see the districts climbing toward the centre. Copper, he thought. Iron. Bronze. At the very top, catching the last of the light, something white with towers.

He had five pages in a notebook about one wall in Denvash. He could see six walls from here.

And around them, outside them, spilling past every one of them, was the rest.

The Unranked District.

It was not a district. It was a city. It ran along both banks of the river and up the slopes on either side and out across the flat land to the south. It had no walls at all. The sun went down while he watched.

The gold went out of the river. The white towers on the Ranked hill held the light a little longer than everything else, and then let it go too. The sky turned grey-gold, and then a deep, clear blue.

And the fires came out.

They came out one by one at first, and then all at once. Cookfires. Hundreds of them. More than hundreds. All across the Unranked District, in yards and on roofs and in doorways and along the river, little orange points of light, each with a thin grey thread of smoke going up from it and leaning east in the wind. Each one was somebody's dinner. Somebody who was not Ranked enough for the walls. Somebody making do.

He thought: *a lot of those people are still finding out.*

He stood on the rise with the wind at his back. He was very tired. His legs ached all the way down. He had a turnip in his bag and a bent bracket in his pocket and a book against his chest that said *I don't know* in his grandfather's handwriting.

He thought about the green book in the archive. Four entries. Three ended in a few weeks. One ended in a line that said nobody knew.

He was the fifth.

He had told himself on the walk home from the record office that he would not be the fifth entry in that book. It had been easy to say. He had said it the way you say things on a walk home. He said it again now, to himself, and it was not easy anymore. He could see now how big the thing was that he would have to say it to.

He took out his notebook. He opened it to a clean page, and wrote, in the last of the light:

*Three of them were dead within weeks. They didn't get time to find their feet. Whatever happened, it happened before they knew what they were.*

*So that's the first job. Not to find out what [SHATTERED] means. Not yet. First, get past the weeks.*

*Find somewhere to sleep. Find work. Watch everything. Show nobody anything. Be dull. Be competent. Be still here.*

*Then find out.*

He looked at it. Then he underlined *still here*, once, firmly.

He did not know what was down there. He did not know where he would sleep tonight, or who the first person to be kind to him would be, or whether he would be right to trust them. He did not know what the Unranked District did to people like him. He did not know what he was.

He knew he was not going to be an entry somebody else wrote.

He decided that. Standing on the rise, at the end of the second day, with a thousand fires coming up out of the dark below him, he decided it properly, the way you set a part into a rig and load it to see if it holds.

He put the notebook away.

Down in the valley, the road ran on toward the river, pale in the dusk, and where it met the first houses there was no gate at all. Only a wide space in the dark, with fires on either side of it, standing open.

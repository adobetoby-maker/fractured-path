# Chapter 14 — Claim, Evidence, Ruling

He woke because one of the brothers dropped a boot, and for a moment, before he moved, nothing hurt at all.

Then he moved. The right side of his chest reported first, the hot band from under his arm to the bottom of his ribs, and the left shoulder joined in a breath later, stiff and thick, as if somebody had packed the joint with wet sand while he slept. Last of all the scrape down his left forearm stung where the blanket dragged across it, the scrape he still could not account for. He lay still and let all three finish what they had to say. The window over his cot had gone from black to grey. The brother whispered an apology to the boot, and then the door closed on both of them, and the stairs creaked their way down to the river.

Last night he had stood under Torvin's pump in the dark with his shirt off and counted, because Lira had told him to count. He had stopped at sixty, because sixty was what his ribs would stand. He could not tell whether it had helped. He could breathe, and every breath went carefully round the edge of something on its way in and out, and that would have to do.

He sat up in three stages, like a folding chair.

Yeni was awake on the next cot. She was lying on her side under her blanket with one eye open, watching him do it.

"Renn," she said.

"Renn."

"How many?"

"Four exchanges."

The eye closed again. "Torvin's wife had you at two," Yeni said into her pillow. "Now she owes the one in the boots a bun."

He had not known that anybody in this house had had him at anything. He sat on the edge of the cot with his shirt in his hands and thought about that, about a bet made over a kitchen table on a boy nobody there had seen fight, and he was not sure whether it made him feel better or worse. Both, probably. He was getting used to things being both.

Putting the shirt on took longer than it should have. He did the right arm first, keeping the elbow tight against his side, and then worked the left shoulder into its sleeve a little at a time, like threading a stiff wire through a hole that was slightly too small. When he had finished, Yeni's eye was open again.

"Left's the shoulder," she said. "Right's the ribs."

"Yes."

"Don't sleep on either." She rolled over to face the wall. "And don't let him tell you to rest. He'll tell you to rest. Rest is for people with savings."

He did not know whether she meant Torvin or somebody else, and she did not say.

In the kitchen the range was lit and the oats were on. Torvin's wife stood over the pot with her wooden spoon. The woman who slept in her boots was at the end of the table eating a sweet bun with great slowness and pleasure, and she raised it to Cael when he came in, as somebody might raise a cup.

"Four," she said. "I said three or better. I'm a generous judge of people."

"You said three because you'd heard about the Wind girl," said Torvin's wife, without turning round. "Sit down before you fall down, boy. There's a bowl."

He sat. The bowl came, and with it a spoon and a hard look at the way he lowered himself onto the bench, and then the woman went back to her pot and said nothing else about it. He ate slowly. Turning his head to the left was fine. Turning his body was not. He worked out, by the bottom of the bowl, exactly which movements cost what, and he put the list away in his head beside the other lists.

Torvin came in from the yard with a bucket and stopped in the doorway.

"Pump handle was wet at midnight," he said.

"I used it."

"Lira tell you to?"

"Yes."

"She's right." Torvin set the bucket down by the range. He looked at Cael for a moment in the way he looked at coins, slowly, checking his own count. "I said fight in the street."

"It wasn't in your house."

"No." Torvin seemed to consider this from several sides, and to find nothing wrong with any of them. "Four, they're saying."

"Four."

Torvin grunted. It was not quite approval, and it was not nothing. Then he picked up the oats pot from the range with a cloth round the handle and set it down on the table at Cael's elbow, so that there would be a second bowl if he wanted one, and went out again without another word.

Cael had a second bowl. It seemed rude not to.

The Cinder House yard was half in shadow when he got there.

The sun was up somewhere behind the east wall, and the wall threw a long, clean shadow across the packed dirt, so that the yard was split down the middle into a grey half and a bright half, with the circle's groove running through both. There was mist in the low corner by the drain. The crows were working last night's leavings along the rope line, where the pie boy had dropped crusts and someone had dropped a whole half pie and not come back for it.

Lira was at the post in the far corner, in the grey half. She did not stop when she saw him. She watched him walk across the yard to her between one strike and the next, and he understood partway across that she was not watching his face.

"Right side," she said, when he reached her. "And the left shoulder. You walk like a man carrying a tray of glasses."

"You were at the rope. You saw where he hit me."

"I'm not telling you what I saw. I'm telling you what I see." She struck the post, low, and let her hand rest against the canvas. "You're not sparring today."

"I didn't come to spar."

"Then what did you come for?"

"You said you'd see me at the yard."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she turned back to the post so that he could not see her face, which told him more than her face would have, and struck it twice more, a little harder than before.

"All right," she said. "Then you can stand there and learn something with one arm. Right arm. Hold it up. No, like this." She took his wrist and set his forearm upright in front of him, elbow down, palm toward his own face, as if she were propping up a fence post. "This part. The outside. From the wrist to the elbow. That's what you catch with."

She showed him the deflection.

It was the one she had shown him at the board on the second day, the one every Wind student learned in their first month. Catch, reset, send. She went through it slowly, so slowly that it stopped looking like fighting and began to look like a craftsman showing an apprentice how to hold a plane. Her strike came in toward his face at the speed of somebody reaching for a cup. His forearm met it on the outside and caught it. That was the first part.

"Now stop," she said. "Feel where you are."

He felt where he was. The strike had pushed his arm a little across his own body, and his weight had gone with it, onto the wrong foot.

"That's why there's a middle," said Lira. "Catching it moves you. Reset puts you back. Weight in the centre, arm back on its line. Then you're ready to do something with what you caught." She took her hand away and brought it in again, just as slowly. "Catch. Reset. Send."

On *send* she turned his forearm for him, rolling it outward against her wrist. Her strike went away past his shoulder into the empty air, as if it had changed its mind about where it wanted to go.

They did it twenty times before the sun reached the post. Then forty. The reset was where his ribs complained, because the reset meant a small turn of the body back to centre, and the turn went right through the bruise. The third time he flinched in it, she saw. After that she brought her strikes in from a slightly different angle, so that the reset needed less turn, and she did not say anything about why.

"Why not teach me the other way?" he said, around the fiftieth. "Your way. Without the middle."

"Because you can't skip a step you don't own." Catch. Reset. Send. "When I skipped it, I'd done all three parts ten thousand times. I knew exactly what I was leaving out, and what it cost, and why I could afford it. You'd only be leaving out something you never had." She lowered her hand. "That's not a skip. That's a hole."

He thought about that through the next ten repetitions, and he found nothing in it to argue with, which was annoying.

He had brought bread. He had stopped at the top of the second row of the market on the way and asked the bread woman for two from underneath, and watched her face, as the nut boy had told him, and she had given him two from underneath with a small sour look that he took as respect. He held one out to Lira now.

She took it, tore it in half, put one half on top of the post and ate the other in four bites. Then she set her feet.

"Breakfast's between," she said. "Not before. Before, you're slow. After, you're late." She brought her hand up. "Catch."

So he ate his roll in the gaps, in pieces, between sets of ten. When the sun finally came over the east wall and the shadow began to shrink back toward it, Lira moved their work with the shadow, a step at a time, keeping them both in the grey half until there was no grey half left. He noticed it, and he noticed that she did it without looking, as she did the things she had done so often that she no longer needed to decide them.

He did not ask about it. He thought there was probably a reason, and he thought that if she wanted him to have it she would give it to him, and that until then it was hers.

She did not mention the third exchange. Not once, all morning. He kept waiting for it, between the catches and the resets, and it did not come, and somewhere around the hundredth repetition he understood that she was not going to ask today, and that the not asking was a thing she was doing on purpose, as deliberately as she kept them in the shade.

The third notebook from Denvash had nine pages left in it.

He counted them that afternoon, sitting on the edge of the low wall in Torvin's yard beside the pump, and then counted them again, because nine was not many. The front of the book was full of Ardenmere: the nut boy's map, the board decoded, the rules, six pages on two strangers' bouts, Marrow's numbers. The back of it held the things he had written from the other end, as he always did, in the smaller hand. The archive entry. The gate entry. And on its own page, near the end, the dip between the hedges and the turnip and *I don't think I did.*

He closed it on that page without reading it again.

The paper stall was at the bottom of the market, near the fruit woman with no name, and it was run by a man with ink on his cuffs who sold everything from single sheets to bound ledgers with marbled edges that cost more than a week at Torvin's. Cael looked at the ledgers for some time. Then he bought the cheapest thing on the table that still had a proper sewn spine: a thick, plain notebook with a grey board cover and paper so thin that the ink of one page would show faintly through the next. It was a good deal more than he wanted to pay, and he paid it, and wrote the figure in the back of the old book under the line he had drawn that morning.

He took it back to the wall by the pump and opened it to the first page and sat there for a while with the pencil.

He had kept notebooks since he was eight, and every one of them had been the same kind of book. Things went into it as they came, in order. A bearing, a dog, a wall, a word on page seven. That had been enough, for a long time, because the things he wrote down were mostly things that would still be there tomorrow.

This was different. He could feel the difference in his hands. The bout with Renn was already less clear than it had been last night. The second probe, the one across the body, had it come off the front foot or had he only expected it to? He had been sure on the walk home. He was nearly sure now. By next week he would be sure again, in the wrong way, the way you become sure of a thing you have told yourself often enough.

He wrote, at the top of the first page, *Log*, and underlined it.

Then he wrote the first entry the way he had always written things, in sentences, and it came out like this:

*Renn. Copper Rank 3, Blade. He opens with two probes, high and across, off the front foot. The true strike loads in his back heel about a quarter of a second first. I read the heel in the second exchange and he answered with the sweep. In the third I got out of the whole figure—*

He stopped.

He read the line back. *In the third I got out of the whole figure.* It was true. He had been standing outside the figure at the end of it, and Vell had seen it, and Renn had seen it, and Lira had seen it from the rope. And the sentence sat there on the page next to the heel and the probes as though it belonged to them. It was in the same hand. It was in the same voice. Anybody reading it a year from now would think he had done the third exchange the way he had done the second, by reading and moving, and he had not. He did not know how he had done it. The sentence did not know either. It simply did not say so.

That was what sentences did, he thought. They ran on. You put a true thing in one end, and a guess came out the other in the same coat, and nobody could tell them apart.

He tore the page out. He did it carefully, along the spine, so that the binding would not loosen, and folded the page and put it in his pocket.

Then he took the straightedge from his tool roll, the little steel one he had made at twelve, and ruled the next page into three columns.

He thought about what to call them for some time. Hesk's slates in the workshop had had columns: *load*, *figure*, *result*. Those were close but not right, because nobody here was hanging weights on a rig. What he wanted was something that made each line prove itself before he let it stay. He thought about Vell at her table, and the way the yard went quiet when she spoke, and the one word she said at the end of a bout that made it finished.

He headed the columns *Claim*, *Evidence*, and *Ruling*.

A claim was what he thought was true. Evidence was what he had seen with his own eyes that made him think so, and when, and how many times. A ruling was what he would do about it next time, and it was only allowed to exist if the evidence was there. If he had not seen a thing himself, if somebody had told it to him, it could go in, but it went in with a small *h* beside it, for *heard*, so that he would always know it was a figure somebody else had traced and he had not.

*Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.*

He began again.

*1. Renn. Copper R3, Blade Path (Vell's rating). Eleven bouts, eight wins (Vell, h).*

*Claim: the probes carry no weight. Evidence: off the front foot, high then across, every exchange but the last. Ruling: spend nothing on them.*

*Claim: the true strike loads in the back heel a quarter second before the blade. Evidence: first exchange, paid for with the shoulder; second exchange, moved on it and was right. Ruling: move on the heel, not on the blade.*

*Claim: he rolls his shoulders when he changes his plan. Evidence: before the first exchange, after the sweep, before the fourth. Ruling: shoulders mean a new plan. Stop expecting the old one.*

*Claim: the sweep is kept for people who have learned the heel. Evidence: second exchange, once. Ruling: one is not enough. Watch for it again.*

*Claim: the blade reaches eight inches past the fist. Evidence: Lira, h. My eye says less. Ruling: add eight until I have measured it myself.*

It took him most of the afternoon. When he had finished, he had a page and a half on a man he had fought for perhaps three minutes in all, and every line on it would hold weight. He had tried each one. He could tell exactly where each one came from.

There was nothing on it about the third exchange.

He had not left it out by deciding to. It simply had nowhere to go. He had a claim, *I got out of the figure*, and he had no evidence, because he had not seen himself do it, and he could not make a ruling, because he did not know what he would do next time. The columns would not take it. He looked at the empty place where it would have been and felt something that was partly relief and partly shame, and he could not have said which was larger.

He turned the notebook over and opened it from the back, as he always did, to the last page.

This would be the other kind of page. He knew that already. Things would go here that did not fit the columns. He made himself a rule about it, sitting on the wall with the pump dripping beside him, and the rule was this: nothing went in the back until the front was finished for the day. The facts first. Then the rest. He thought that if he could make himself do it in that order, the rest might stay a sensible size.

He wrote only one line, at the top of the last page.

*There are things that won't go in the columns. I know where they are. I am not counting them yet.*

Then he closed the Log and sat with it on his knee, and listened to the pump drip, and did not open the old notebook to the page near the back, though he knew exactly which page it was.

The pouch said he had until the eleventh day.

He did the sum on the morning of the tenth, at the kitchen counter, in small stacks of coins, twice. Torvin's week ran out tomorrow. After that the pouch would pay for three more weeks if he ate the way he had been eating, and four if he ate the way the brothers ate, which was mostly bread and whatever the river gave them. It would not pay for five.

*Find work,* he had written on the rise. He had written it second, after *find somewhere to sleep*, and he had done the first and not the second, and now the second had come round to find him.

He went down to the river workshops in the late morning, with his left arm held close, past the cobbler and the lantern woman and the cooper, to the doorway at the end of the row with no sign over it.

The mender was there. He was not straightening a mangle today. He had the clock with no hands open on his bench, its works laid out on a cloth in neat rows like a surgeon's instruments, and he was looking at them through a loupe, and he did not look up.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE*, said the doorframe.

"Something of yours broken?" said the mender, to the clock.

"Not today," said Cael. "I wanted to ask if you need anybody."

That made the man look up. He took the loupe out of his eye and looked at Cael, the same checking look he had given him over the mangle frame, and his gaze went to the left arm Cael was holding close, and then came back.

"For what?"

"Sorting. Cleaning. Truing small things." Cael thought about how to say the next part so that it would be true and not sound like boasting, and decided he could not, and said it anyway. "My grandfather makes instruments. I've worked at his bench since I was six."

The mender did not say anything to that. He reached under the bench, felt about without looking, and came up with a little brass balance of the kind a spice seller uses, with a beam no longer than a hand. The beam was bent. It was not bent much. It dipped a hair to the left at rest, so that anything weighed on it would weigh light in one pan and heavy in the other, and you would have to hold it up to the light and squint along it to see why.

He put it on the bench, and laid his straightedge beside it, and went back to his clock.

Cael looked at the balance for a long time before he touched it.

Then he did what he had watched the mender do with the mangle, and what Hesk had done at the rig for as long as he could remember. He did not guess. He laid the straightedge along the beam and found the bend, which was not in the middle, as he had expected, but a little way out along the left arm, where somebody had probably caught it on the edge of a stall. He clamped the beam lightly in the bench vice with a fold of leather between the jaws. He pressed. Not much. Then he took it out and laid the straightedge along it again, and looked, and it was better, and not right. He pressed again, less. He looked. He had gone over, very slightly. He turned it and pressed back the other way, with the pad of his thumb this time and no vice at all.

The fourth time he laid the straightedge along it, he could see no light underneath.

He hung the beam on its pivot and let it settle. The pointer swung, and slowed, and came to rest dead on the mark.

"There," he said.

The mender put down his loupe. He came round the bench and looked at the balance, and then, without saying anything, took two identical brass weights out of a drawer and set one in each pan. The beam dipped and rose and came back to the mark and stayed there.

He took the weights off. He looked at Cael's left arm again.

"Two afternoons," he said. "Not mornings. I don't talk in the mornings. By the piece, not by the hour, because I don't pay for people standing about thinking." He put the weights back in their drawer. "You'll sort what comes in, and sweep, and true the small things. You won't touch the clocks."

"I won't touch the clocks."

"And if you break something, you'll pay for it out of what I owe you, and then you'll go." He went back round the bench. "Tomorrow. After noon."

That was all. Cael stood in the doorway a moment longer and then understood that he was being allowed to leave, and left.

On the fish steps below the carriers' hut, a barge was unloading sacks of meal onto the landing, and three men were carrying them up the steps to a cart one at a time, on their shoulders. The second man up was Renn. He was in a sleeveless shirt with a sack on his right shoulder, taking the slick steps two at a time as though they were level ground, and when he reached the top he swung the sack onto the cart without breaking stride and turned to go down for the next one.

He saw Cael on the street above. He stopped on the top step.

For a moment he did not say anything, and Cael did not either. Renn looked at him the way he had looked at him across the circle in the third exchange, briefly, carefully, a man checking a part he had not cut.

"Healing?" Renn said.

"Slowly."

"Good." He went down a step, and then stopped and looked back over his shoulder. "A month," he said, and went on down to the barge.

He went to Vell's table on the fourth morning after the bout, when he could lift his left arm as high as his shoulder without his face doing anything about it.

The yard was empty except for Vell and the crows. She was at the table in the shade with her ledger squared and her tea at her elbow, exactly as she had been the morning of the terms, and when he stopped in front of her she looked at him for a while without speaking.

"Lift your left arm," she said.

He lifted it to the height of his shoulder.

"Higher."

He lifted it higher. He kept his face still. He was nearly sure he kept his face still.

Vell watched the arm, and then the face, and then the arm again. "Thursday," she said, and picked up her pen. "Not before. Renn's return stays where I put it, at the month. Between now and then you'll fight who I give you."

"Who?"

"Brenna." She wrote, a short line, without looking up. "Copper Rank 2, Shield Path. She lost to Dessa a fortnight ago in three exchanges and she hasn't stopped talking about it. She wants somebody she can finish quickly, so that people will talk about that instead." The pen stopped. Vell looked up. "If you're any use, you'll be somebody she can't finish quickly. That'll be good for both of you, and she won't thank me for it."

He nodded. He was watching the pen.

It had written a line beneath the line from four nights ago. He could see the shape of the old entry, the long one that had run well past two lines and on down the page. He could not read any of it upside down. But he could see where a name would go, at the start of each entry, and he could see that at the start of his there was no name. There was something shorter.

"You haven't written my name," he said.

Vell did not look down at the page. She seemed to have known exactly where he was looking.

"No."

"Is that because I'm unrated?"

"It's because the book doesn't need it yet." She laid the pen across the ledger. "You're in the book as what you are. Unrated. Fourteen. Vouched. Lost to Renn in four exchanges, by the flat, called. That's everything anybody coming to this book will need to know about that night. A name goes in when the yard comes to see it."

"When will that be?"

"For most of them, never," said Vell. "Most of them come and fight and go home, and the book holds what they did, and nobody ever needs to know what they were called. It's no slight. It's what a book's for." She picked up her tea. "When the yard comes to see you, I'll write it. Not before. And not because you asked."

He found, standing there, that he did not mind. He had thought he would. But he was in a book, in somebody else's careful hand, as exactly what he was and what he had done, and every word of it was something Vell had seen herself from this stool. It was a ledger he could check. It had no word in square brackets in it.

"Thursday," he said.

"After the lamps." Vell drank her tea. "Third bout. Don't be late, and don't be early either. Being early makes people think you're frightened."

He went out through the gate in the wall. On the board at the crossroads, on his way back down, he found the old result again, still pinned near the bottom, gone pale in the rain. *Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), third exchange, called.* He read it twice. He remembered two men arguing behind him in the triangle on his first afternoon, and one of them saying that Brenna could not stand that it went to three.

He took out the Log and turned to a clean page and wrote *2. Brenna*, and under it, in the first column, *wants it over quickly*, and in the second, *two men at the board, h; Vell, h*, and in the third column nothing at all.

The ruling would have to wait until Thursday. He was learning that most of them would.

# Chapter 15 — Never Resets

On the third morning Lira let him use both hands.

"Open hands," she said. "Touch only. If you hit me, I'll hit you back, and I've got two good shoulders and you haven't." She set her feet in the grey half of the yard, where the east wall's shadow still lay long across the dirt. "I'm going to come in slow. You're going to catch, and reset, and send, and if you get the send right you can try to touch me afterward. Anywhere. A finger on my sleeve counts."

"And if I don't get the send right?"

"Then I touch you," said Lira. "And I'm not slow afterward."

She was not. He found that out in the first minute. Her strikes came in at the speed she had promised, the speed of somebody reaching for a cup, and he caught them and reset and sent them away, and then, in the half-breath after each send, while he was still working out where his own hand should go next, her other hand would arrive from somewhere he had not been looking. It touched his collarbone. It touched his ear. Once it touched the very end of his nose, lightly, with one finger, and she said "Boop," in a perfectly flat voice, and he was so surprised that he laughed and his ribs made him regret it.

"You're thinking after the send," she said. "Like the send's the end of something. It isn't. Nothing's the end of something until Vell says so."

They went again. He stopped thinking after the send. He stopped thinking about anything, as far as he could tell, except the place her shoulders went before her hands did. He caught, reset, sent, and in the half-breath after the send he put his left hand out, not far, only as far as the shoulder would allow, toward the place he thought she was going to be.

His fingertips touched her sleeve.

Lira stopped. She looked down at her own sleeve as if a moth had landed on it.

"Well," she said.

"You're getting slower."

"I am absolutely not getting slower." She set her feet again, and there was a brightness in her face that he had begun to recognize, a morning brightness that did not come out later in the day, when the yard filled up and she had to be the girl who had lost her first four. "You're getting faster. There's a difference. Mostly to my pride."

"Or you're getting predictable."

Lira looked at him with deep and dignified offence.

"I am never predictable," she said. "I'm *generous.* I let you have that one, because you've got one shoulder and a face like a kicked dog, and I thought you could do with some encouragement."

"You didn't let me have it. Your weight went onto your left foot before the strike started. I watched it go."

"That," said Lira, "is exactly the kind of thing a person says when they want to feel better about losing a sparring round."

"I didn't lose it. I touched your sleeve."

"You touched my sleeve because I was being generous." She brought her hands up. "Again. And this time I'm going to be a great deal less generous, and you're going to stop watching my left foot, because I've just noticed I've been doing that for four months and I'm going to stop."

She did stop. He watched for it for the rest of the morning, and it did not come back once, and he wrote that down that night in the Log, in the column for evidence, with something very close to admiration. *Told her the tell. She removed it in an hour. Ruling: never tell anybody their tell unless I'm sure I don't need it.*

He looked at the ruling for a while after he had written it, and did not like it much, and left it in. It was what he had seen.

---

On Tuesday there was an afternoon card at the Cinder House, three bouts, and Lira took him to the barrel by the drain to watch a man called Baro.

"Just Baro," she said, before Cael could ask. "No other name, or none he uses. Nobody's asked. He turned up at the start of the summer and put himself down for an afternoon card, unrated, and he's been on the afternoon cards ever since."

"Unrated since the summer?"

"He's never asked Vell for a rating, and she's never offered one." Lira hitched herself up onto the barrel lid beside him. "People have opinions about why. Some of them say he's somebody who used to be somebody, across the river, and doesn't want the book to find out what. Some of them say he's just strange." She looked across the yard. "I think he's the most careful fighter in Ardenmere. I've watched him eleven times and I've never once seen him get hit anywhere he didn't choose."

Baro was standing by the far wall. He was perhaps thirty, lean and long in the arm, with close-cut dark hair and a face that did not seem to be doing anything at all. He was not talking to anybody. When he moved, to take a cup of water from the bucket, Cael saw that he turned his whole body to do it instead of turning at the waist. There was a stiffness on his left side, low in the back, that he carried as if it were part of his clothing.

*Old injury,* Cael thought. *Bad one. He's built round it.*

Vell gave Baro out as unrated, Blade Path, against a Copper Rank 4, Force Path, and the yard made a low, interested noise that was not like the noise it made for a debut.

The bout took two exchanges.

In the first, Baro brought his blade up, a pale, steady edge along his right forearm, and then simply stood with it. He let it hang low. He let his guard drift a little open on the left side, the stiff side, so that anybody looking at him would see the gap and see the stiffness behind it and put the two together. The Force fighter saw it. Cael watched him see it. The man's whole frame turned toward the gap like a dog turning toward a sound, and he came in hard on the left, with a short driving strike that would have folded Baro over his own bad back.

Baro was not there.

He had not moved fast. That was the strange thing, and Cael made himself see it clearly. He had moved early, and only a little, a single pivot on his right foot that took the stiff side away and brought his blade arm round, and the Force fighter's strike went into the air where the gap had been. In the same movement the flat of Baro's blade laid itself along the man's ribs, lightly, almost politely. It was not a blow. It was a note left on a table: *I could have.*

The second exchange was the same. Baro offered the left side again, more openly this time. The Force fighter hesitated, and then took it anyway, because it was so open, and because he could not believe that a man would show the same weakness twice unless it was real.

"Called," said Vell. "Blade Path."

The yard clapped, not loudly. Baro touched fists with the Force fighter and walked to the table and stood there while Vell wrote, with his weight on his right foot and his face doing nothing at all.

He fought again in the third bout, against another Copper Rank 4, Iron Path this time, who had watched the first bout from the rope and had plainly decided not to make the same mistake. The Iron fighter did not go for the left side. He went for the right, the good side, where Baro had shown nothing. He went patiently and carefully, giving the gap on the left a wide and sensible berth, and in the third exchange Baro let his right foot slide, as if the packed dirt had given under it. For one heartbeat his right side was as open as a door.

The Iron fighter took the door.

And Baro, whose left side had been the bait all along and was still the bait, turned on the bad back as if there were nothing wrong with it at all, and put the flat of his blade across the man's chest, and that was that.

Cael sat on the barrel with the Log open on his knee and found he had not written anything since the first exchange.

"He beat two Copper Rank 4s in an afternoon," he said, "and they never touched him."

"He does it most weeks." Lira was watching Baro walk back to the wall. "People say the trick is the left side. It isn't. The trick is that every opening he gives you is one he's put there. If you can see it, he wants you to see it." She was quiet a moment. "You remember Renn's second exchange? After the sweep? When I said you almost had him?"

"Yes."

"Baro would have had you." She said it without any weight at all. "If that had been Baro, crouched and twisted with his hips open, and you'd stepped in, he'd have been somewhere else before your hand got there, and you'd have been on the ground wondering how. Renn was really open. Baro never is."

Cael thought about that for a long time.

"Then how do you tell?" he said. "Between a real one and bait?"

"You fight a great many people," said Lira, "and you get it wrong a great many times, and after a while you get it wrong less." She slid off the barrel. "That's the only way I know. If you find a quicker one, tell me."

He wrote it in the Log that evening, as entry three.

*3. Baro. Unrated, Blade Path. Watched, two bouts. Old injury, low left back, carried stiff.*

*Claim: every opening he shows is bait. Evidence: four exchanges, two opponents, the left side offered three times and the right foot once; every one taken; every one empty. Ruling: until I can tell bait from a real opening, take only the openings I make myself. Take nothing I'm given.*

He looked at that ruling for some time. It meant that against anybody good, for a long while yet, he would be taking nothing at all.

He left it in. It was what he had seen.

---

"Shield," said Lira, on Wednesday morning, sitting on the low wall at the side of the yard with the bread. "You've seen one?"

"The gate guard on Merchant Row, in Denvash. He never used it. It was on his badge."

"Then you haven't seen one." She tore her roll in half. "At Copper it isn't much to look at. The air goes thick in front of the forearm, about a hand off the skin. You can see it if you know where to look, a sort of shine, like looking through the side of a bucket of water. It's about so big." She held her hands apart, a little wider than her shoulders. "It goes where her arm goes. It doesn't move on its own and it isn't a thing she holds. It's more like the arm's got longer and flatter and harder, all at once."

"What does it do when you hit it?"

"Nothing. That's what it does. You hit it and you stop." Lira ate half of her half. "Hitting it is like hitting a door somebody's leaning on from the other side. Brenna knows that. So she doesn't block with it, exactly. She walks it at you. She puts it in your face and keeps walking, and hits you with the edge when you run out of room, and then walks some more."

"Does it cost her anything? Holding it up?"

"Breath, mostly. Shield's like Joren's stance, the way you told me about it." He had told her about Joren on the second morning, and the heel, and she had laughed until she had to sit down. "It's on, and holding it isn't free. But Brenna's strong, and she's stubborn, and she's never once in her life let it down in a bout until the bout was over."

"Never?"

"Not once." Lira finished the bread. "She doesn't reset. That's the thing about her. Most people come back to a guard between strikes. Even Renn does, a bit, between the probes and the real one. Brenna doesn't. She hits you and steps into the next one and hits you again, and she never comes back to where she started, because with that thing up she doesn't think she needs to."

"Then when does it come down?"

Lira looked at him sideways and smiled, the narrow smile she used when he had asked the right question and she did not intend to answer it.

"That's your homework," she said.

---

On Thursday night the yard was full, and Marrow was working the rope.

Cael did not watch the first two bouts. He sat on the barrel at the corner, on the shaded side, and watched Brenna.

She was standing by the water bucket with two other women, both bigger than her, and she was talking. She talked with her hands, in short chopping movements, and Cael could not hear the words over the crowd, but he did not need to. Every few sentences she chopped the air with the edge of her left hand, low and flat, as a woman might bring a cleaver down on a block. Her friends nodded. One of them laughed. Brenna did not laugh. She was about twenty, square-built, with cropped fair hair and a face that went red easily, and it was red now, though she had not done anything yet but talk.

*She's still fighting Dessa,* he thought. *She's been fighting Dessa for a fortnight, and Dessa isn't here.*

Marrow came past the barrel. Cael read the slate without moving his head. *Brenna*, and a short price. *Unrated*, and a long one. Underneath, on its own line, *Over/under: 2 exch.*

"He's moved your line," said Lira quietly, beside him.

"It was one, last week."

"It was one for Renn because you were nobody. It's two for Brenna because you lasted four against somebody better." She did not look at him. "That's the closest thing to a rating you've got right now, that number. It isn't Vell's. It's what a few hundred people with money think, and Marrow's shaved it. But it moved."

He looked at the number on the slate. It was chalk, and Marrow would wipe it with his sleeve in a minute and write something else. It was the first figure in Ardenmere that had changed because of something he had done.

"Third bout," said Vell.

He stepped over the rope.

Brenna was already on her mark. She did not roll her shoulders, or stretch, or look at the crowd. She stood with her left arm up and bent in front of her body and her right fist cocked at her hip, and she looked at Cael with an expression of great impatience, as though he were a queue she had been made to wait in.

"Brenna," said Vell. "Copper Rank 2, Shield Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The small pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Brenna was ready before Vell had finished the word.

The air in front of her left forearm thickened. He saw it happen. It was exactly as Lira had said, a shine in the lamplight, a little wider than her shoulders, standing a hand's breadth off her arm like the side of a bucket of water stood on end. Through it her face wobbled slightly, the way a face wobbles through old window glass. Then she came at him.

She did not amble, as Renn had. She did not probe. She walked the shield straight at his face and kept walking, and the first exchange was over almost before he understood that it had begun.

He gave ground. He had decided to give ground, sitting on the barrel, and it was a good thing he had decided, because there was no time to decide anything now. He went back along the curve of the groove, left, and she followed. She swung the edge of the shield at him, flat and low, the same chopping movement she had made with her hand by the water bucket. He got his right forearm up and caught it on the outside, as Lira had taught him.

It was like catching a falling door. The blow went through his forearm into his shoulder and through his shoulder into his ribs, and the ribs, which had not quite finished with Renn, told him about it at length. He reset, badly, and did not try to send. There was nothing to send. The shield was not a strike that could be turned away. It was a wall that was walking.

He went back three more steps. She came three more steps. And then he was at the edge of the circle with the groove under his heel, and she brought her right fist round the outside of the shield toward his ear, and he ducked under it and slipped sideways along the groove, and she turned to follow without ever once bringing the shield back in toward her body.

At the rope somebody said "Rabbit," and somebody laughed.

Brenna stepped back to her mark. Cael went to his. His forearm was ringing and he had learned nothing at all, except that Lira had been right about the door.

*Fine,* he thought. *The first exchange is a handshake. You find out their distance.*

In the second exchange, he watched her feet.

She came in the same way, straight, with the shield up and walking, and he went back the same way, along the curve. But he was not looking at the shield now. He was looking under it, at her boots on the packed dirt, and he saw that she stepped into every strike off the same foot. Left foot forward, shield forward, chop. Left foot forward, shield forward, chop. She hit his forearm with the edge, and the jolt went through him again, and as it landed her right foot came up off the ground to bring her into the next one.

And the shield dipped.

It was not much. A hand's breadth, perhaps less. For the space of half a beat, while she was stepping through, the bottom edge of the shine sank toward her hip, as if the arm under it had relaxed for a moment to let her body catch up. Then the foot came down and the shield came back up and she chopped at him again.

He went back. He watched for it. He saw it again. Strike lands, she steps through, the shield dips. Strike lands, step, dip.

*She doesn't reset,* he thought. *She doesn't come back to a guard. But she has to move her feet. And when she moves her feet, the arm lets down a little, because it's tired of being up, and she doesn't know.*

He did not do anything with it. He went back along the groove, and let her walk him round half the circle, and got out from under her right fist twice, and at the end of the exchange he had a second bruise coming up on his forearm beside the first, and Brenna's face was redder than it had been at the start.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

In the third exchange he gave her nothing at all.

He went back. He went sideways. He let the shield come and caught it and gave ground, and let it come again. He did not try to touch her or to get round it. He watched the dip, and counted it. Four times in that exchange it dipped, always on the same step, always for the same half-beat.

And he watched something else begin.

Brenna was breathing differently. She had been breathing hard since the first exchange, because walking a shield at somebody was work, but now there was a sound at the top of each breath, a short hard huff through her nose that came just before she stepped. It was louder every time. Her face had gone from red to a darker red, and her mouth had set in a line, and halfway through the exchange she said something through her teeth. He did not catch it the first time. He caught it the second time.

"*Stand.*"

He did not stand. He went back along the curve.

"Stand and *fight.*"

At the rope, the noise had changed. There was laughter in it still, but there was something else now, an edge of real interest, the sound of people who had come for a quick finish and were beginning to wonder whether they would get one. Somewhere on the sunny side a man shouted "Hit him, Brenna," as if she had not been trying, and she heard it, and her face went darker still.

Vell called the end of the exchange. Brenna went back to her mark breathing like a bellows. She did not look at the rope. She looked at Cael, and her look had stopped being impatient.

The fourth exchange told him the thing he had come for.

Brenna came off her mark faster than before, and harder, and her first chop was so big that he saw it coming from her shoulder long before it reached him, and stepped away from it with room to spare. The huff came before the second one. He heard it, and moved, and the second chop missed him too. The third was the same. They were not faster. That was what he saw, with a kind of cold surprise. Her strikes had grown bigger and louder, but they had not grown faster. They had grown slower, because she was putting everything into them, and everything took time to gather.

*Frustration's a tell,* he thought. *It's the loudest one there is. It's louder than the heel, louder than the dip. She's telling the whole yard what she's going to do, a full breath before she does it.*

He did not know whether that was in Lira's homework. He thought probably it was not. He thought it was something he had found on his own, in the dirt, with his forearm aching, and he held on to it as if somebody might take it off him.

He also thought, as he went back along the curve for what must have been the fortieth time that night, that his ribs had begun to hurt in a new way. It was not the bright, exact hurt of a fresh blow. It was a tiredness under the bruise, as if the bruise itself were getting heavy. Giving ground was not free either. He had been paying for every step.

Vell called the end of the fourth.

He stood on his mark and looked across at Brenna, and Brenna looked back, and he decided.

He had watched for four exchanges. He had found the dip and counted it. He had found the huff, and he knew what it meant. If he went on giving ground he would go on learning nothing new, and sooner or later his ribs would give out before her breath did, and he would end up on his back having never once found out whether the dip was a real opening or only a thing he had seen.

*Take only the openings I make myself,* the Log said. He had written it two nights ago. But he had not made this one. He had found it. He did not know which column that belonged in.

"Begin," said Vell.

Brenna came. The shield came. He caught the first chop on his forearm, deliberately this time, and held his ground under it for the first time all night, and felt it go through his shoulder and his ribs and down into his heels. He heard the huff. Her right foot came up to step into the next one.

The shield dipped.

He went in.

He went in low and to his left, under the sinking edge of the shine and past it, toward her right side, where there was nothing but a cocked fist and an open shirt. He was inside the shield's reach. For half a beat he was exactly where he had meant to be, and he put his hand out to touch her, anywhere, a finger on her sleeve.

And Brenna, who was twice his weight and had been walking a door at people for years, simply turned.

She did not bring the shield back up. She did not need to. She turned her whole body toward him on the foot she had just set down, and the edge of the shine came round with her like a gate swinging shut. It took him across the chest. It did not hit him so much as collect him. It lifted him off the one foot he had left on the ground, and walked him backward two steps, three, and then there was the groove under his heel and nothing under the other one. He went down flat on his back across the edge of the circle, with the thick bright air of the shield standing over his chest a hand's breadth from his shirt, and Brenna's red face behind it, and her right fist cocked.

She did not throw it.

"Called," said Vell. "Shield Path."

Brenna stood over him a moment longer. Then the shine went out of the air, all at once, like a breath let go. She was breathing so hard her shoulders went up and down with it. She looked down at him with an expression he could not read at first, and then could.

It was not triumph. It was relief, and something else underneath the relief that looked a great deal like anger at herself.

---

He reported to the table, as you did, win or lose.

Vell was writing. It took longer than two lines. It took about as long as the long entry had taken on the night of Renn, and once, without lifting her head, she raised her eyes to him and then lowered them again.

"Five," she said, when she had blotted it.

That was all she said. He understood that it was a number for the record, and he understood also, from the way she said it, that she had been counting too.

Brenna was at the water bucket when he went past. She had a cup in her hand and had not drunk from it. She looked at him over the rim.

"You ran me round for four exchanges," she said.

"Yes."

"On purpose."

"Yes."

She drank half the cup, in one swallow, and looked at him again over it. Her face was still red, but it had begun to go back toward its ordinary colour, and he could see her working out whether to be angry, and deciding something else.

"You found the step," she said. "In the fifth. You came in on the step."

He did not say anything. He did not know whether he was supposed to.

"Dessa found it in the third," said Brenna. She put the cup back on the bucket's edge with a click. "Took her two exchanges. Took you four." She looked at him for a moment longer, a hard, considering look, and then something in her square face eased, not much. "Next time stand and fight. It's dull, the running."

"Next time I'll have a better reason to stand."

Brenna snorted. It was nearly a laugh. She went off toward the gate with her friends, and he heard one of them say something, and Brenna said, quite loudly, "He's fourteen," as if that answered whatever the question had been.

Lira found him at the rope. She walked him out through the gate and down the lane and did not say anything for two streets, and he could feel her saving it.

"Well?" he said, at last.

"You went in on the dip."

"You saw it."

"I've fought her twice," said Lira. "It took me the second time to find it, and when I went in on it she did exactly what she did to you." She was quiet for a few steps. "The dip's real. It's not bait. She doesn't know she does it. But you can't go in on it unless you're quick enough to be through before she turns, or heavy enough that she can't turn you. You were neither."

"I know."

"I know you know." She looked at him sideways in the dark. "That wasn't the homework, though."

"What was the homework?"

"Whether you'd find it," said Lira. "Not whether you could use it. Nobody uses it the first time. I wanted to know if you'd see it." She looked ahead again. "You saw it in the second exchange. I watched your eyes go down."

He did not answer that. He was thinking about her breath, Brenna's, the huff at the top of it, getting louder.

"She got angry," he said.

"She always gets angry. That's why she lost to Dessa."

"It made her slower. Not faster. Slower. Everything got bigger and slower and louder, and I could hear it coming." He tried to put it into words that would hold. "It was a better tell than any of the others. It was the whole of her telling me."

Lira walked a little way without speaking.

"Yes," she said at last, in the voice she used for things she had not expected him to know yet. "That's true. That's one of the truest things there is in that yard. And almost nobody learns it from the losing side."

---

That night, at the end of the cot, with the brothers asleep and the wheezing man whistling and Yeni's lamp turned low, he finished entry two.

*2. Brenna. Copper R2, Shield Path. Lost, called, fifth exchange.*

*Claim: she never resets. Evidence: five exchanges; not once came back to a guard. Ruling: don't wait for her to reset. She won't.*

*Claim: the shield dips on the step through, half a beat, every time. Evidence: counted four in the third exchange alone; went in on it in the fifth and it was there. Ruling: the opening is real. I am not quick enough or heavy enough to use it yet. Not yet is a ruling.*

*Claim: frustration makes her bigger, slower and louder. Evidence: third and fourth exchanges; the breath before each strike; the strikes themselves got slower. Ruling: if somebody gets angry, they will tell me everything. Let them.*

He read it through. Then he closed the front of the book, because the front was finished for the day, and opened it from the back.

He sat with the pencil for a while. It was harder to write in the back. The columns did not help there. There was nothing to hold the words up.

*Four exchanges I let her think I was running. I wasn't running. I was watching. But she didn't know that, and she got angry because of it, and that was how I learned the thing about anger.*

*I don't know whether that's a trick or a lie. It felt like both.*

He stopped. He thought about the fifth exchange, and the moment he had decided, and the hot, tight feeling in his chest when he had made up his mind to stand under the shield and go in. He had been angry too. Not at Brenna. At the running. At being, every single time, the boy who went backward.

*I think I was angry in the fifth,* he wrote. *I think that's why I went in. I'm not sure it was a decision. I'm not sure it wasn't her tell and mine at the same time.*

*Find out what mine sounds like.*

He closed the book and put it under his pillow, and lay on his back, and felt his forearm ache and his ribs ache and the new bruise across his chest where the edge of the shield had collected him. Five exchanges. One more than Renn.

He had lost again. He wrote that down, too, in his head, where the Log could not see it, and he found that it weighed less than he had expected it to, and he did not yet know whether that was good.

# Chapter 16 — The Air Before the Heat

Lira caught him trying the skip on the second morning after Brenna.

He had been practising it at night. He had not meant to tell anybody that, and he had not meant to be caught, and he was caught anyway, because she came round the corner of the public house earlier than usual and found him alone at the post in the grey half of the yard, going from catch to send with no middle, over and over, with his face screwed up.

She stood by the gate and watched him do it four times. Then she said, "Stop."

He stopped.

"Do it again. Slowly. I'll come in."

She came in, at the cup-reaching speed. He caught her strike on the outside of his forearm and went straight on to the send without resetting, as he had seen her do on the second day at the board, the whole thing in one movement. Her strike went away past his shoulder. Then her other hand came round from nowhere and rested, very gently, on the side of his neck.

"That's where you are," said Lira. "You caught it, and it moved you, and you didn't come back, and now your weight's on your left foot with nothing behind it. You sent my right hand away, and my left hand's come in through the door you left open." She took the hand away. "That's not a skip. That's the hole I told you about."

"It worked for you."

"It works for me because I know where my weight goes when I catch. I know it without looking. I've known it for years." She stepped back. "You've known it a week. Again. The long way."

He did it the long way. Catch, reset, send. She came in again and again, and the third time he let his arm go round in the send without coming properly back to centre first, and her left hand was on his neck again before he knew he had done it.

"Again."

He did not do it a fourth time. He felt the moment in the catch when his weight went across, and he let the reset bring it home before he sent, and when her left hand came in he had a forearm in its way.

They went on until the shadow left the post. He made the hole twice more, early on, and then not again. He found the place where it happened, which was not in the send at all but at the very end of the catch, when the strike had pushed his arm across and he wanted to be finished with it. Once he knew the place, it was a matter of not wanting to be finished.

She noticed. He saw her notice. She did not say anything until they had stopped, and she had sat down on the low wall at the side of the yard with the bread, and he had sat beside her, and she had eaten half her half.

"You're not a natural," she said.

He waited.

"I'm saying it plainly, because if I said you were one you'd believe me, and it would be a lie, and I won't tell lies to anybody I train." She brushed a crumb off her knee. "A natural gets it right and couldn't tell you how. You get it wrong. You get it wrong the first time, every time, and then you see exactly why it was wrong, and then you don't do it again." She looked at him sideways. "Ever. I've been watching for it. I've made the same mistake a hundred times in my life. You make a mistake once and then it's gone, as though you've crossed it out."

"Is that worse than being a natural?"

"It's better," said Lira, after a moment. "Naturals coast. They go a long way on whatever they were born with, and then one day it runs out, and they're stuck there, because nobody ever made them learn how anything is put together." She looked at the post, where the sun had just reached the canvas. "You're the most efficient learner I've ever stood across from. Whatever you end up being, you'll have built it yourself, and you'll know where every piece of it came from. I'd take that over a natural every time."

He did not answer at once. He was trying to find somewhere to put it.

"Nobody's ever said that," he said. "About the classification. That it might be a thing you build, and not a thing that happens to you."

"I wasn't talking about the classification." She stood up and held a hand out to pull him off the wall. "I was talking about you. They're not the same." She hauled him up, carefully, on his good side. "Up. We're not finished."

He wrote it in the back of the Log that night, after the front was finished, and it was the first line in the back that was not a worry.

*Lira says I build. She says it's better than being born with it. I think she might be right, and I think I want her to be right more than I can afford to, so I'm writing it here and not in the columns.*

---

The mender paid him on the third afternoon.

It was not much. It was a handful of small coins pushed across the bench without comment, for two afternoons of sorting a tea chest full of hinges, hasps and screws into trays by size and kind, and sweeping, and truing the spoke of a child's wheel and the arm of a pair of spectacles. Cael counted it on the walk back along the river. It would not pay a week at Torvin's. It would pay three days of one.

*Slows the pouch,* he wrote, in the back of the old notebook, under the figure. *Doesn't stop it.*

In the market, on the way home, a man at the fish stall looked up from his board as Cael went past.

He was the bearded cousin, the one who knew about fish. Cael had never bought anything from him. He had never spoken to him at all, except once, to ask what a long silver fish was called, and the man had told him and gone back to his knife. Now he looked up from the board with the knife in his hand and caught Cael's eye, and nodded.

That was all. It was a small nod, a downward tip of the chin, the kind men in Denvash gave each other across the depot yard. Then he went back to his fish.

Cael walked on down the row with the coins in his pocket and his forearm aching, and did not understand for half a street what had happened. Then he did. The fish man had been at the rope. Or somebody who bought from the fish man had been at the rope, and had told him, and now a man Cael had never spoken to knew who he was, and had decided that this was worth a nod.

He found he did not know what to do with that. He told himself it was only a nod. He put it in the Log that night anyway, in the front, because it was a fact, though he could not think what column it went in, and in the end he wrote it in the margin with no column at all.

*Fish man, bearded. Nodded. I've never spoken to him.*

The letter came on the nineteenth day.

He knew it was the nineteenth because he had been counting. The letter to Hesk had gone in the west bag on the fifth day, and the clerk had said about a week, and Hesk would have needed a day, or two, or three, to write back, and then it would be another week coming. He had worked it out on the road that the earliest a letter could come was about the nineteenth, and the latest was about whenever Hesk decided, and he had tried very hard not to think about the second figure.

Yeni had it. She was sitting on her cot with her sewing kit open beside her when he came up the stairs, and she held it out to him without looking up from her needle. It was folded and sealed with a dab of plain wax, and on the outside it said *Cael, care of Torvin's, ROOMS (the O leans), Unranked District, Ardenmere*, in Hesk's square hand. Below that, in another hand, somebody had written *Torvin's — runner*.

"Came at noon," said Yeni. "West bag."

"Thank you."

"Mm." The needle went in and out. "People who get letters back stay longer still."

He sat on his cot by the window and turned the letter over twice before he opened it, because he could not quite believe it was not going to say something terrible. Then he broke the wax.

It was two sheets, written on both sides. The first lines were older than the rest. He could see that from the ink, which was a shade paler, and from the hand, which had been steadier and slower when it began.

*Cael,*

*Ressa had two loaves wrapped the morning after. She put one back. She wouldn't take money for the one she put in your bag; she says it was yours. I told her you'd have eaten it on a wall somewhere about the first milestone. Tell me if I was right.*

He had eaten it on a wall. It had been the second wall, not the first milestone, but it had been a wall, and he sat on the cot and laughed once, quietly, and Yeni did not look up.

*Joren came to the workshop door with your note. He's read it standing up, flat-footed, the whole way through, and he wanted you told. He says he's working on it. He wasn't angry with you. He wanted you to know that most of all.*

*I went on Tuesday. Garrik's kept your washer in the arm and he says he isn't going to take it out. He wanted you told that too. The woman in the knitted hat says they won't know what they've got in Ardenmere. I'm inclined to agree with her.*

He read that paragraph twice. He could see the back room, the folding chairs and the sheet on its frame, and Garrik with his back to the room, threading. He could not see Garrik's face. He thought that was probably how Garrik would have wanted it.

The next part was in the same pale ink, and it was about the guild.

*The guild has opened a review of my standing, because of the notice. They want a written account of who lives here, and I've sent it. It's routine, they say, and slow, and it will most likely come to nothing. I'll tell you how it goes, as it goes. I'm telling you now because you asked me to tell you things as they come, and I said I would, and I'm finding out that's harder to keep than I expected. Don't worry about it. I know you will. Worry about it a little, then, and then stop.*

He stopped reading.

He sat with the sheet on his knee and looked at the window, at the grey square of it with the rooftops in it and a line of somebody's washing, and he did exactly what Hesk had told him he would do. He worried about it. He worried about the guild letter with its polite paragraphs, and the workshop, and the drawer marked *Springs, do not use* with a bent bracket's space in it, and the canal gauges and the survey levels and the clock inside the walking stick. He worried that the review would not come to nothing. He worried that it would, slowly, over a year, while Hesk sat in the workshop waiting for it.

Then he made himself look at the other thing, which was harder.

Hesk had told him. Hesk had not had to. He could have written about bread and washers and Joren's heels and left the guild out, and Cael would never have known until it was over, one way or the other. And Hesk had sat at the kitchen table, Cael could see it, with the pen over the paper and the lamp lit, and had decided to put it in, because Cael had stood in a workshop and asked him to.

*As they come,* Cael had said. *Not managed.*

And in the letter he had written at the waystation, sitting at the end of a table with a stew he had not eaten yet, he had left out a turnip cart.

He read on. The ink went darker, and the hand went quicker. This part had been written later, after his own letter had come.

*Your letter came on the twelfth day, so the clerk was right about the week and I'm writing back the same evening. I read it at the table. I read the part about the fifth entry three times, which seems to be the family number.*

*I'm glad you rode the last miles. The carter sounds a decent man, and a Stone Path with turnips is a fine thing to have met on your first day. I'm glad of the waystation woman's coins and of the bread you didn't eat. I'm glad you're tired and your legs hurt and you're all right.*

*Write and tell me what Ardenmere is like. Not what the reference room said. What it's like. Who you've met. What you're doing with your days. Whether you've found anything you're good at that nobody there knew about. I'll be here.*

*— H.*

He folded the letter along its creases and held it.

*Whether you've found anything you're good at that nobody there knew about.*

He thought about the circuit. He thought about Vell's book with no name in it, and Marrow's slate, and Brenna's shield coming round like a gate. He thought about the third exchange against Renn, lying in its corner with the cart.

---

He wrote back that night, at the kitchen table, after the others had gone up.

He told Hesk about Torvin's, and the O, and Yeni, and the nut boy and the sister who flicked his ear. He told him about the board and the crossroads, and Lira, and how she had stood behind him for eleven minutes. He told him about the circuit. He did not dress it up and he did not make it smaller. He wrote that it was a ladder that did not ask to see a card, and that he had put himself on the bottom rung of it, and that he had lost twice now, to a Blade Path man and to a Shield Path woman, and that each time he had lasted longer than the time before.

*I'm not trying to win yet,* he wrote. *I'm trying to learn how people move. You taught me where to look. I'm looking.*

He told him about the shoulder and the ribs, because Hesk would want the truth about those, and because they were healing. He told him about the mender and the spice balance and the two afternoons. He told him about the fish man's nod.

He did not tell him about the third exchange.

He held the pencil over the page for a long time before he decided that. He made himself say the reasons, one at a time, in his head, the way you call a load figure. It would be a week getting there. Hesk would read it at the kitchen table and there would be nothing he could do about it except sit with it for another week, waiting for the next letter. And there was nothing to tell. He did not know what had happened. He had a claim and no evidence and no ruling. If he wrote it, he would be writing a guess in the same hand as the facts, and Hesk would read it as a fact, because it came from Cael.

All of that was true. He knew, while he was saying it, that it was also exactly what Hesk had told himself for years.

*Don't make a thing real before it is.*

He wrote one more line instead, at the bottom, and he meant it.

*Thank you for telling me about the guild. I know it was harder to put in than to leave out. I'll worry about it a little, and then stop.*

Then he signed it, and folded it, and put it inside the leather book to keep it flat, and went up to bed with the feeling of a man who has paid a debt in one currency and taken on another in a different one.

He was older than Brenna, older than Renn, a heavy-shouldered man somewhere near forty with a shaved head and a short grey beard, and forearms that were shiny and hairless in patches, the way a baker's forearms go after years of reaching into ovens. He sat on an upturned crate by the wall through the first two bouts and drank water and spoke to nobody, and when a boy tripped over his outstretched boots on the way to the pie tray, he caught the boy by the collar before he fell, set him on his feet, and gave him back the coin he had dropped.

"He cooks," Lira said, on the barrel. "At the eating house by the lower bridge. Has done for years. He fights twice a month because he says it's restful."

"Restful."

"He says nobody shouts at him in the circle."

On Marrow's slate, the line had moved again. *Over/under: 3 exch.*

"He's an honest fighter," Lira said. "He doesn't feint much. He doesn't need to. But I'll tell you one thing, because you'll find it out in about four seconds anyway." She did not look at him. "He doesn't miss with the heat by much. And at Copper, Ember isn't fire, mostly. It's the feeling of standing too near an open oven. Until it's not."

"Third bout," said Vell.

Amrit stood up from his crate as if he had been called to a table, and stepped over the rope, and nodded to Cael across the circle with grave courtesy, and set his feet.

"Amrit Sole. Copper Rank 4, Ember Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause. "Begin when you're ready."

Amrit raised his hands. He held them loose and open in front of his chest, the palms turned a little toward Cael, as if he were warming them at a fire.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the air in front of his hands began to move.

It was very slight. It was like the air above a road at midday, or above the top of a stove when the iron has just begun to tick. The lamplight behind his hands wavered. The rope behind him seemed to ripple, though the rope was perfectly still. Cael saw it, and wondered what it was, and was still wondering when Amrit stepped in and opened his hands.

Heat came out of them.

It was not flame. Lira had been right. It was the breath of an oven with its door flung open, a wall of dry, hard heat that struck his face and his chest and his raised forearm all at once, and drove the air out of his mouth, and made his eyes water so hard that for a moment he could not see. He went back. He went back three steps without deciding to. When his eyes cleared there was a smell of singed wool, and he looked down and saw that the cuff of his right sleeve had gone brown at the edge.

Amrit had not followed him. He was standing in the middle of the circle with his hands lowered, waiting, with the patience of a man who knew how long a loaf took.

The first exchange was over. It had taught him one thing, and he had nearly missed it, and then he had not. The air had moved before the heat came. Not much before. Half a beat.

He went back to his mark and watched Amrit's hands.

In the second exchange he moved on the shimmer.

Amrit came in slowly, with his hands up and open, and Cael watched the air in front of them, and the moment it began to waver he went sideways, left, along the groove. The heat came out of Amrit's palms into the place where he had been standing. It brushed his right cheek and his ear in passing, hot and dry, and did no more than that.

Amrit turned to face him. The shimmer came again. Cael went again, right this time, and again the heat went past him.

Without the heat, Amrit's hands were only hands. They were big, and they were quick enough, but they were a cook's hands and not a fighter's, and when he swung a plain fist at Cael's shoulder it came round so wide that Cael could have walked under it. He did. He came out on the far side of Amrit with his heart going hard and a grin trying to get onto his face that he did not let onto it.

At the rope the noise had changed again. It was the same change he had heard in Brenna's third exchange, the interest coming in under the laughter.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

In the third exchange he read the shimmer every time, and he began to feel something he had not felt in the circle before, which was that he knew what was going to happen.

The air moved. He went. The heat came where he had been. The air moved. He went. He was not trying to touch Amrit, any more than he had tried to touch Brenna. He was only trying to be somewhere else when the heat came, and he was managing it, exchange after exchange, and every time he managed it the knowing got a little surer and a little warmer.

That should have warned him. He wrote that afterward, in the back of the Log. It did not warn him at the time.

He was watching Amrit's hands. He was watching nothing else. And across the circle Amrit had begun to watch him watching.

Cael saw it, at the very end of the third exchange, and did not understand what he was seeing. Amrit's eyes went to Cael's eyes, and followed them down to his own hands, and came back up. It was quick, and grave, and almost kind. It was the look of a cook who has noticed that the boy at the back door is not watching the pot but the hand that lifts the lid.

In the fourth exchange the air in front of Amrit's hands began to waver, and Cael went sideways, left, along the groove, and nothing came out of Amrit's hands at all.

The heat was not there. The shimmer was there, wobbling the lamplight, as clear as it had been every time before, but Amrit was holding it, keeping it in his palms like a breath held in the chest, and Cael had already gone. He was committed to the step, his weight going left, his eyes on the shimmer still. And Amrit, who had not moved his feet at all, simply stepped after him and swung a plain closed fist into the meat of his right arm above the elbow.

It was not a heat strike. It was only a fist. It was a cook's fist, behind forty years of lifting stockpots, and it turned Cael half round and nearly put him down.

He stayed up. He stood on the edge of the groove with his right arm gone dead from the shoulder to the fingers, and looked at Amrit, and Amrit looked back at him, and there was nothing in Amrit's face but the grave courtesy he had had from the start.

*He showed me the shimmer,* Cael thought. *He showed it to me on purpose. Like Baro's left side. He's put it there for me to see.*

Vell called the end of the exchange. He went back to his mark with his right arm hanging and the feeling coming back into it in hot pins.

In the fifth exchange he tried to tell them apart.

He watched the shimmer and he watched Amrit's breath, because Brenna had taught him that the breath could talk. When the shimmer came the first time, Amrit's chest stopped moving. His shoulders went still, as a man's do when he braces to lift something. Cael went, and the heat came, and passed him.

The second time the shimmer came, Amrit was breathing. His chest went up and down under his shirt. Cael held. He held his ground for the length of a heartbeat, against everything his feet wanted, and the heat did not come, and Amrit's plain fist came instead, and he slipped it.

The third time, he could not tell.

The shimmer came and he looked at Amrit's chest and he could not see it moving or not moving. The lamp was behind Amrit now, and his shirt was dark, and Cael had a half-heartbeat to choose. He chose wrong. He held, and the heat came, full, from a pace away, and he got his left forearm up in front of his face, the scraped one, and the heat struck it like the side of a kettle.

He did not cry out. He went back four steps with his teeth shut and his forearm held away from his body as if it belonged to somebody else, and the sixth exchange came, and he knew before it began that it was going to be the last one.

He knew it with great clearness. He knew it the way he knew the bracket would fail when the needle swung.

Amrit came in, unhurried. He had seen the forearm. He was not going to chase it, and he was not going to spare it either; that was not how this yard worked. He was simply going to walk Cael to the edge of the circle, where there was nowhere left to step, and open his hands from a pace away. Cael had been watching him do it for five exchanges. He could see the whole of it before it happened: the groove at his heel, the shimmer, no room left to the left or to the right, the full open heat at a distance from which there was no getting the arm up in time.

He would be burned. Not badly, perhaps. Badly enough. He would be burned in front of sixty people on Lira's vouch, and he would go down into the groove, and he would learn nothing from it at all that he did not already know.

The groove was at his heel.

The shimmer began.

He raised his hand.

He held it up, open, the palm toward Vell, the way the grey-templed Stone woman had held hers up in the second bout on his first afternoon in this yard. He held it there, breathing hard, with the heel of his boot on the groove.

Amrit stopped. The shimmer in front of his hands wavered once and went out, like a stove door closing.

"Called," said Vell. "Conceded. Ember Path."

---

The yard did not applaud him the way it had applauded the Stone woman. He had not expected it to. But there was some clapping, here and there along the rope. It was not loud, and it came mostly from the shaded side, and one of the people clapping was a girl on a barrel by the drain who did not stop until he had walked all the way to the table.

Vell wrote. It took a long time. She blotted it and looked up at him, and her eyes went to his forearm, which had come up red and shiny from the wrist halfway to the elbow, over the old scrape, and then back to his face.

"Six," she said. Then, after a moment, in exactly the same voice: "That was the right breath. A breath later and you'd have been at a healer's in the morning."

"I know."

"I know you know. That's why it was the right one." She closed the ledger. "Go and put that arm in cold water. Not butter. People will tell you butter. They're wrong."

Amrit was waiting at the water bucket. He had a clean cloth in his hand, wet, and he held it out without a word. Cael took it and laid it over his forearm, and the cold went into the burn with a feeling so close to pain that for a moment he could not tell which it was.

"Keep it wet," said Amrit. His voice was low and unhurried and surprisingly gentle. "A long time. Longer than you'd think. Don't break the blister if one comes up. Let it go down by itself." He looked at the arm. "It'll be red for a week and it'll itch for two. It won't mark you."

"Thank you."

"You were watching my hands," said Amrit. "From the second exchange."

"Yes."

"In the fourth I let you."

"I know," said Cael. "I saw it after."

Amrit looked at him for a moment, with his grave cook's face.

"Most people don't see it after either," he said. He picked up his own cup and drank and set it down. "The breath. In the fifth. You were watching my breath."

"Yes."

"It was a good thing to watch." Amrit nodded, as if he had tasted something and found it nearly done. "Not good enough yet. But good." Then he went off through the gate, into the dark, toward the lower bridge and the eating house, and did not look back.

Lira walked him home with the cloth still on his arm. She had wet it again at the pump by the gate, and she said nothing for the first street.

"Yes."

"Before he burned you."

"I know you did. I watched you see it." Lira kicked a stone and watched it skitter down the lane ahead of them into a doorway. "You remember the Stone woman? The second bout, your first afternoon? How everyone clapped for her?"

"How you lose matters." She was quiet for a moment. "They didn't clap for you as much as they clapped for her. But the right people clapped. And Vell said the right breath. I've never heard her say that to anybody before."

He did not say anything. His arm hurt, and the cold cloth hurt, and something in his chest that was not a bruise had gone warm and tight and he did not trust it.

# Chapter 17 — About Speed

"There's a wager on you," Lira said.

It was the morning after Amrit, and she was sitting on the low wall with her knees drawn up, waiting for the shadow to reach the post, and she said it in the voice of somebody who has been saving a piece of news since the night before and has finally decided she cannot hold it any longer.

He looked at his forearm. It had come up in a long red patch over the old scrape, shiny and tight, and there was a blister the size of a thumbnail near the wrist that he was not breaking, because everybody had told him not to. He had slept with it outside the blanket.

"Marrow's always got a wager on me."

"Not Marrow." Lira's mouth was doing something at one corner that she was plainly trying to stop it doing. "You know the two errand boys who work the east lanes? One's got a red cap. The other one's got a brother in the carters'?"

He had seen them. Everybody had seen them. They ran messages and small parcels from the river up to the yards and down again, and they ran them in competition with each other, all day, at a dead sprint, shouting abuse at each other across whatever street they happened to be crossing. He had seen the red-capped one leap a handcart rather than go round it, because the other one was going round it.

"They hate each other," said Lira. "Properly. Since they were about six. And now they've got a bet on how many more bouts you'll need before your first win. Red Cap says two. The other one says never." She looked at him. "They've each put half a mark on it. That's more than either of them earns in a day. They told the pie boy and the pie boy told the woman at the water bucket and the woman at the water bucket told me."

"Which of them's going to win?"

"Red Cap. I think. He hates the other one more than he loves money, so he picked the opposite of whatever the other one said." She considered. "That's not a good reason to back somebody. But it's not the worst reason anybody's ever backed you."

Cael looked at the post for a moment.

"I don't like it," he said.

"I didn't think you would."

"I didn't come here to be talked about."

"You came here to fight in a yard in front of sixty people every week, and you keep lasting longer than anybody thought you would." She said it quite gently. "That's what being talked about is made of. You can't do one without the other. Not here."

He had known that. He had not let himself know it all the way. Not three weeks ago, standing on the rise above this valley, he had written *Show nobody anything*, and underlined *still here*. Since then he had walked into a circle four times with a crowd round it. He noticed it when he climbed onto the barrel. The rope was two deep on the sunny side and three deep in the shade, and there were faces in the crowd he had begun to know. The fish man was there, with his beard. The two errand boys were there, standing as far apart from each other as the yard would allow, both glaring. The woman who slept in her boots was there with a pie. Torvin was not there. Torvin's wife was, near the gate, with her arms folded.

He found he did not want to know how many of them had come for the second bout.

On Marrow's slate the line had moved again. *Over/under: 4 exch.*

And Marrow, for the first time, looked at him.

It was only a glance. Marrow was going along the rope with his chalk and his pleasant voice and his clean hands, taking coins, and as he came level with the barrel his eyes went up from the slate to Cael's face and rested there for the length of a breath. It was a perfectly friendly look. It was the look a man gives a horse that has begun, for reasons nobody can explain, to cost him money.

Then Marrow smiled and went on.

"Don't," said Lira quietly.

"I didn't do anything."

"I know. Keep not doing it."

"Second bout," said Vell.

He stepped over the rope.

Petra was already on her mark. She was not looking at the crowd or at Vell. She was looking at him, steadily, with a calm, narrow interest, and she was standing very lightly, with her weight forward on the balls of her feet and her hands up in front of her face, loose. She looked like somebody waiting for a door to open so that she could go through it first.

"Petra Voss. Copper Rank 3, Force Path. Against unrated. Vouched." The pause. "Begin when you're ready."

The first exchange taught him how little he knew.

She came off her mark so fast that he was still deciding which way to go when she arrived. A short straight punch came at his face. He got his right forearm up in time, barely, and caught it on the outside, the way Lira had taught him a hundred times in the grey half of the yard.

The catch held. Then the shove came.

It came a heartbeat after her fist had stopped, out of nowhere, as if somebody behind his arm had put a shoulder into it, and it drove his forearm back into his own face and threw him off his feet. He went back three skidding steps and sat down hard on the packed dirt inside the groove.

He was up again before he had finished thinking about it. That was the one thing he did right in the first exchange. The rope laughed. Petra did not. She was already back on her mark, waiting, with her hands loose in front of her face.

*There's no reset,* he thought, on his own mark, with his forearm ringing. *There's no time for one. You catch it and the shove comes before you've finished catching.*

In the second exchange he saw the lean.

It was very small. He would have missed it if he had not spent an hour in the lane watching her hit a sand-bag on a chain. Before a punch she meant, a punch she was putting the shove into, her shoulders tipped forward. Not her head. Not her hands. The shoulders, both together, perhaps the width of a finger, as if she were leaning into a wind only she could feel. Her jabs did not have it. Her feints did not have it. Only the ones she meant.

He saw it for the first time halfway through the second exchange. He saw the shoulders tip, and he knew what was coming, and he started to move.

The punch hit him anyway.

It caught him on the left shoulder, the Renn shoulder, because he had moved, and moving had taken his face out of the way. But it had not taken the rest of him. The shove came after the fist and spun him half round, and he stayed up, and stood there with the shoulder singing and understood something that he did not like at all.

He had seen it. He had seen it clearly, a long way off, the way he had seen the dip in Brenna's shield and the shimmer in front of Amrit's hands. And the seeing had bought him almost nothing. It had bought him the difference between being hit in the face and being hit in the shoulder. She was simply faster than his seeing.

He saw the lean three more times before the end of the exchange. Each time he started to move the moment he saw it. Each time he was hit, or nearly hit, or hit a little less hard. Once he got all the way clear and heard the shove go past his ear like a slammed door in the next room. Once he did not get clear at all.

At the table, Vell's pen moved.

In the third exchange he tried moving earlier.

He reasoned it as he stood on his mark. If the lean came and he moved and she was still quicker, then he had to move before the lean finished. He had to move on the very start of it, the first tilt of the shoulders, before it had become a lean at all.

She came in. Jab, jab, nothing in them. Then the shoulders began to tip.

He went. He went on the very first tilt, so early that he was moving before he was sure, and her real punch went past his cheek into the empty air where his face had been. It was the first time all night she had missed with one she meant.

The rope noticed. He heard it notice.

So did Petra.

In the fourth exchange she did something new. She leaned, and he went on the lean, and her punch was not where his face had been. It was where his face was going. It met him coming the other way. It caught him on the side of the jaw, not hard, a glancing blow, and the shove came after it, and he went down on one knee and his teeth clicked together and he tasted blood where he had caught the inside of his lip.

He got up. He looked at her across the circle, and she looked back at him, with the same calm, narrow interest she had had at the start.

She did not know about the lean. He was almost sure of that. Her face had no idea that her shoulders were telling him anything. But she knew that he was moving before her punches came. She had seen it in the third exchange. And she had done what Renn had done with the sweep. She had not tried to find out how he knew. She had simply stopped hitting the place he was and started hitting the place he would be.

*She's learning faster than I am,* he thought. *She's learning me while I'm learning her, and she's quicker at that too.*

The fifth exchange he tried everything he owned.

He tried Brenna. He gave ground along the groove and let her follow, and waited for her to get angry, so that she would get bigger and slower and louder. She did not get angry. The longer he ran, the calmer she became, and the calmer she became the quicker she was.

He tried Amrit. He watched her breath for the moment it stopped. It never stopped. She breathed in short easy puffs through her nose all the way through every punch, like a girl running for a cart she knew she would catch.

He tried the catch. He caught one punch she meant, square on the forearm, and held, and the shove came, and he leaned into it with everything he had, and it moved him anyway, a full pace backward, his heels plowing two short furrows in the dirt.

Nothing he owned was quick enough. Every piece of it worked. Every piece of it was a half-beat too slow.

The sixth exchange he lost by inches, over and over. He saw the lean and moved and was nearly clear. He saw the lean and moved and was clipped. He saw the lean and moved and she was waiting where he moved to, and he saw her waiting and changed his mind and moved again, and her second punch, which had no lean in it at all, only a jab, caught him under the ribs on the right side, the Renn side, and stopped his breath for a moment.

He was tiring. He could feel it. His feet were slower than they had been in the first exchange, and his hands were lower. She was not tiring at all.

At the rope the noise had gone strange. Some of it was the noise for a beating, the low pleased murmur of people watching what they had paid for. But under it there was something else. He heard somebody say "Seven," and somebody else say "He won't," and the first say "He will," and he understood that they were betting on whether he would last another exchange.

Vell called the end of the sixth.

He stood on his mark and breathed. He did not look at the rope. He looked at Petra's shoulders.

*I can see it,* he thought. *I can see all of it. I just can't get there.*

"Begin," said Vell.

The seventh exchange was very short.

She came. She jabbed, twice, nothing in either one. He let them go past and watched the shoulders. They began to tip. He went, on the first tilt, as early as he had ever gone. He went left, not right, because she had been waiting on his right for two exchanges. He did everything right. He would go over it afterward, line by line, and he would not find a single thing he had done wrong.

Her palm was there anyway.

It was not a fist. It was the heel of her open hand, flat, driven straight into his middle, below the ribs, and it arrived before he had finished going left. It arrived as if she had known he would go left before he did. And then the shove came after it, and it was the biggest shove of the night, and it lifted him.

He was in the air. Then he was not. He came down on his left hip across the groove, half in the circle and half out, and his face hit the packed dirt, and he lay there with his mouth full of the taste of earth and blood and no air in him anywhere.

"Called," said Vell. "Force Path."

---

He reported to the table with his lip swelling and his hip a single deep ache and his breath still coming back to him in pieces.

Vell wrote for a long time. When she had finished she blotted it, and looked at him, and looked at the swelling lip, and the way he was standing on one leg.

"Seven," she said.

He nodded. He could not have said anything just then without his voice doing something he did not want it to do.

"Four," said Vell. "Five. Six. Seven." She said each number flatly, as if reading them out of the book, which perhaps she was. "Every time one more." She closed the ledger and laid her hand on the cover. "You'll come to the end of that sooner or later. Everybody does."

"What happens then?"

"Then you'll have to do something besides last," said Vell. "Go and get your lip seen to."

Petra was at the water bucket. She had a cup in each hand, and she held one of them out to him, which surprised him so much that he took it without thinking.

"What were you looking at?" she said.

He drank. The water stung his lip.

"You," he said.

"No." Petra shook her head, once, so that her braid swung. "Everybody looks at me. You were looking at something. From the second exchange. You kept moving before I'd started." She frowned at him, not angrily. It was the frown of somebody looking at a lock she had not seen before. "What was it?"

He thought about the Log. *Never tell anybody their tell unless I'm sure I don't need it.* He had written it the night he told Lira about her left foot, and watched her take it away from him in an hour.

He was not sure he did not need it. He was fairly sure he would need it very badly, one day, in this circle, against this girl.

"I don't know," he said. "I was just watching."

Petra looked at him for a moment longer, the narrow, considering look. He could see she did not believe him. He could see her decide that it did not matter yet.

"You're quick at it," she said. "The watching. Quicker than anybody I've fought." She drank her own water. "Not quick enough."

"No," he agreed.

"That's all right," said Petra Voss, quite seriously. "Nobody is, the first time." And she put her cup back on the bucket's edge, and walked off to her friends at the gate, and did not look back.

Lira had a wet cloth for his lip and nothing to say about the bout, and he was grateful for both. She walked him home through the dark by the long way, round the market, where it was quieter. She let him go at his own pace, which was slow, because of the hip.

At the well with the iron cover, she stopped.

"You saw her shoulders," she said.

He looked at her.

"From the second exchange. Before every real one. Your eyes went to her shoulders." She was not asking. "I've fought her twice. I never saw it."

"She doesn't know she does it."

"No. I don't think she does." Lira looked at him in the dark. "You didn't tell her."

"No."

"Good," said Lira, and then, after a moment, as if it had surprised her to say so: "Good." She turned toward the four lanes. "Cold on the hip. Not long. Count it."

# Chapter 18 — Someone Else's Coat

"How do you make a body faster?" he asked her, the morning after Petra.

Lira was at the post, in the grey half, and she did not stop. She struck it twice more, low, and stepped round it, and struck it again. He had learned by now that this meant she was thinking, and that the strikes were where she put her hands while she did it.

"You don't," she said at last.

"Petra's faster than me."

"Petra's been doing it for a year and a half, and she was probably quick to begin with, and she's seventeen and you're fourteen." Strike. "Some of it's just that. Some of it's years, and you can't buy years. But that isn't what you're asking."

"What am I asking?"

"You're asking why you saw everything and got there late." She stopped with her palm flat on the canvas and looked at him over her shoulder. "Aren't you."

He did not answer. He did not need to.

Lira came away from the post and stood in front of him in the grey half with her hands loose at her sides.

"When she leaned," she said, "what did you do?"

"I saw it."

"And then?"

"And then I knew what it meant, and then I moved."

"Three things." She held up three fingers. "You saw. You knew. You moved. Three steps. And Petra only had one. She leaned, and hit you." She folded two of the fingers down. "That's the speed. Not your legs. Your legs are fine. It's the knowing in the middle. It takes you a quarter of a breath to look at what you've seen and say to yourself, *that's the lean, that means a punch, I should go left.* She doesn't wait for you to say it."

"Then how do I stop saying it?"

"You do it so many times that the saying wears out." Lira shrugged, the easy shrug that never quite looked as easy as she meant it to. "You remember the gap? The move you were taught, and the move your body makes when there's no time to remember the lesson? Everybody who's fast lives in the gap. You don't live there yet. You live in the lesson. You see a thing and you look it up." She tapped his forehead with one finger, lightly. "In here. Every time. Like that notebook of yours."

He thought about the columns. *Claim. Evidence. Ruling.* He thought about how long it took him to rule on anything.

"I don't know how to stop looking things up," he said.

"I know you don't." She stepped back and brought her hands up. "So we're going to do one thing, over and over, until you stop. I'm going to show you something new today, and I'm going to hit you with it, and I'm going to keep hitting you with it until one morning you don't need to think about where it's coming from. It might take a month."

"A month?"

"It might take a year. I'm being generous." She set her feet. "It's one of mine. It's a bit unfair. That's why I like it."

It was a combination of three parts, and the first two were nothing.

She came in with a touch, high, toward his face, quick and light and empty, a tap on a door to see who was home. Then another, just like it, from the other hand. They brought his forearms up without his permission, the way a flinch brings your hands up when something moves near your eyes. Then she stepped.

And the step was the unfair part. She was two paces from him when she started it and she was inside his reach when it finished, and he did not see it cover the distance. It was as if the ground between them had folded in the middle and let her across. She had told him on the first day at the board that at Copper, Wind did not make you fast like a thrown stone. It made the world slightly more negotiable for a second at a time. He had not understood her then. He understood her now, with her already standing where she should not yet have been and his forearms up in the wrong place, guarding his face from two touches that were long gone.

Her right hand came round from his left side in a backhand, flat, at the height of his head, and stopped a finger's width from his ear.

"There," said Lira.

He had not moved at all.

"Again."

They did it again. He got his feet moving this time, and the backhand stopped a finger's width from the back of his head instead of from his ear. They did it a dozen times. On the last few he began to build a counter. If the two touches came, he would not raise his arms for them. He would step back and outside instead, away from where the step would land, and bring his right forearm up for the backhand when it came, and catch it, and reset, and send. Lira let him build it. She watched him build it with her head on one side.

It worked once, out of a dozen. The rest of the time the step got there first.

"That's the one you'll use," she said, when the shadow had gone off the post. "The backhand. When I want to finish something in the morning and you're being annoying. I'll use it on you every day until you're not annoying about it."

She did. She used it twice the next morning, early on, while he was still loosening his hip, and both times the backhand stopped beside his ear before his counter was half built. He wrote it in the Log that night as if it were an opponent.

*Lira, the step. Claim: two empty touches, then the ground folds and she's inside. Evidence: thirty times, two mornings. Ruling: step back and out on the second touch. Catch the backhand. It works one time in ten. That's one more than none.*

---

The morning it happened was an ordinary one, and he would go over it afterward again and again, looking for whatever had made it different, and he would not find it.

He brought the bread. He had asked the bread woman for two from underneath, as he always did now, and she had given him two from underneath without the sour look, as she always did now, and he took that as a sign that the market had stopped thinking of him as new. The yard was cool and still. The crows were along the eaves of the public house, watching the dirt with the settled patience of birds who had worked out years ago that nothing that happened down there was food. The east wall's shadow lay across the yard in its long grey bar, the way it lay every morning, and Lira was in it.

They warmed up with the deflection. Catch, reset, send. His left arm could do it now, nearly as well as the right, though the burn on it still pulled when he turned the wrist. Then she moved him about the grey half with a drill for the feet that she had introduced the week before, a box of four steps forward and back and across, with her calling the corners faster and faster until he lost the count and she laughed at him. Then the bread, torn in half, eaten between sets.

They had been at it perhaps twenty minutes when she changed the pace.

He felt it change. It was not a thing she said. It was in her shoulders and her feet, a gathering, as if the morning had been a walk and now she meant to run. Her strikes came in quicker. They stopped stopping short. A touch on his collarbone was a real tap now, enough to sting, and a slap on his forearm when he caught it late was a real slap. He felt himself come up to meet it, all of him, the comfortable half-attention of the warm-up burning off like the mist in the low corner by the drain.

*She's going to use the step,* he thought. *She's working up to it.*

She came in with a touch, high. Another, from the other hand. He did not raise his arms for them. He had built that much. He began to step back and out, as he had planned, to bring his right forearm up for the backhand when it came.

And his body did something else.

There was a feeling attached to it, and he would spend days afterward trying to set the feeling down in the back of the Log and failing every time. It was not a thought. It was not a flinch either, the kind a hand makes from a hot pan. It arrived in the space between those two, quicker than the counter he had planned and somehow surer than any flinch. It did not feel as though his body had overruled him. It felt as though his body had made a different decision on his behalf, in some room he had no key to, out of information he did not remember having been given.

He dropped.

His weight went down and sideways all at once. It went lower than he had ever put it, lower than Lira had ever asked of him, his left leg sweeping out behind him in a long arc across the dirt and his right knee folding under him and his whole body turning low beneath the line of the strike. He had never been taught it. He had never seen anybody do it, not quite like this. He had not chosen it. It arrived exactly in time, so exactly that it could not have been timed on purpose, and Lira's backhand went over him.

He felt it pass. He felt the wind of it on the back of his neck and in his hair, where his head had been a half-breath before. It was so close that the air itself seemed to drag at him as it went by.

He came up out of it on the far side of her, and he was off balance.

Not clumsy. That was not the word. He was off balance in a way his body had never been off balance before. All his weight was in a place he did not know how to carry it. His feet were set wider than he set them, and his hips were turned further than he turned them, and his arms had ended somewhere he did not recognize, low and out to the sides. He had once borrowed Garrik's coat in the rain, when he was ten, and the sleeves had ended halfway down his hands and the shoulders had sat on him wrong, and for the whole walk home he had felt like somebody else wearing his own body. It was like that. It was like standing in somebody else's coat, and finding that the sleeves ended somewhere he did not expect.

Lira stopped.

She did not stop the way she stopped at the end of a set. She stopped completely, all of her, the way the yard had stopped in the third exchange against Renn, when he could hear the lanterns creak.

"How did you know that was coming?"

He looked down at himself. He looked at where his feet were, and his hands, as if they belonged to somebody who had just handed them back to him.

"I don't know."

"That wasn't your counter." She came round him slowly, a half-circle, the way she had walked round him at the board on the first day, the way a buyer walks round a horse. She was looking at the place where his feet had been, and the long scuffed arc his left foot had drawn across the dirt. "That wasn't your read. You were going back and out. I watched you start to go back and out. And then you didn't." She stopped in front of him again. "I've never seen you move like that."

"I don't know," he said again.

It came out quieter. He heard how much quieter. Under the honest not-knowing, which he had been carrying for weeks now and had got used to, there was something new, and he could feel it. It was not a puzzle any more. It was small and precise and cold, and it sat just under his breastbone, and it was fear.

Lira looked at him for a long time.

He found himself watching her face while she did it, the way he watched everybody's face, and the watching felt different, aimed at her. It felt like turning a gauge on something it had not been built to measure. He was looking for one thing. He knew exactly what it was. He had seen it on the face of the gate guard at Denvash, and the clerk at the record office, and Pellin, just for a second, behind the professional blankness. He was looking for the moment when somebody's face found out something about him and drew back from it.

It was not there.

He looked for it carefully, because its absence was information too, and he did not know yet where to file it. What he saw instead was the face she wore on the barrel when somebody in the circle did a thing she had not seen before. It was not alarmed. It was not arranging itself into calm for his sake. It was only working something out, quickly and completely, the way a person adds a new figure to a sum she thought she had finished.

Then she let out her breath, and she was Lira again.

"All right," she said. "Strange. Let's keep going."

She ran the deflection with him again, and the box drill, and she called the corners at the same speed as before, and nothing she did was different. But he could feel her watching him in a new way, and he could feel himself being watched, and the two feelings rubbed against each other. Neither of them could get back into the easy rhythm of the first twenty minutes. They were both careful now. It was like being two people who had found a door in a room they thought they knew by heart, and had shut it again, and were both now very aware of where it was.

Both times he reached for it.

He did not mean to. He knew it was useless before he did it, and he did it anyway. When the two touches came he felt for the thing that had dropped him, the way he had felt for it in the fourth exchange against Renn, like a man patting his coat for a key he had been holding a moment ago. He reached into the room he had no key to.

There was nothing there.

There was only his own counter, the one he had built himself, back and out with the forearm up. It was late. It was late both times. The first time Lira's backhand stopped beside his ear, and the second time it did not quite stop, and clipped him, and she said "Sorry," and did not sound sorry, and did not sound pleased either.

He filed that too. Whatever it was, it did not come because he wanted it. It did not come because the same strike came again, at the same speed, from the same girl, in the same grey half of the same yard. It had come once, for one exact moment, and then it had gone back into its room and shut the door.

"Cael."

He went to the mender in the afternoon, because it was a Wednesday and Wednesday was one of his afternoons, and because he could not think of anywhere else he wanted to be.

The mender had a tray of small keys for him, a hundred or more, all of them for locks that had long ago been thrown away, and he wanted them sorted by the shape of their bits so that somebody coming in with a lock could be matched to a key without trying every one. Cael sat on the stool at the end of the bench and sorted keys.

His hands were not right. He dropped one, and then another. He picked up a key and looked at it and could not see its bit for a moment, only a key, as though his eyes had forgotten what they were for.

The mender looked up from his clock once, and looked at Cael's hands, and looked back at his clock, and said nothing.

Cael put the keys in rows. He made himself do it slowly. He laid each one down beside the last and checked that its bit matched before he let go of it, and if it did not match he moved it, and checked again. It did not matter that it was only keys. He needed something in front of him that would do exactly what it was supposed to do when he did exactly the right thing to it. He needed something to obey.

By the end of the afternoon there were nine rows of keys on the bench, straight as a ruled page, and his hands had stopped.

The mender came and looked at the rows. He picked one key up out of the sixth row and looked at its bit, and put it back exactly where it had been.

"Something broken?" he said.

It was the same question as always. It was not the same question.

"I don't know yet," said Cael.

The mender nodded slowly, as if that were an answer he had heard before and respected, and went back to his clock.

There was no card on a Wednesday, and the public house's back door was shut, and the only people about were the crows and the pie boy, who raked the circle on the days there was no card and had finished raking it, and was asleep on a sack in the shade with his mouth open. She did it slowly at first. She stood with her weight back and out, the way he had been standing when he started to build his counter, and she imagined her own backhand coming round from the left at the height of her head. Then she dropped. She let her weight go down and sideways, and swept her left leg out behind her in an arc across the dirt, and folded her right knee under her, and turned.

It was easy. That was the first thing she found. She could do it. Her body knew how to put its weight that low, and how to turn under a line, because that was what Wind did best at Copper. It took you out of the place where things landed. She had been dropping under strikes since her first month at the academy.

It was not hard to do.

It was impossible to do in time.

She tried it again, faster. She stood in the empty yard and imagined her own step folding the ground, and the backhand coming round, and she went down. She was late. She tried it a third time and went early, on the second touch, before the step had even begun, and she came up on the far side of an empty yard, grinning at nothing, perfectly safe from a strike that would have changed its line the moment it saw her go.

She tried it eleven times. She counted, because she had caught counting from him, and was annoyed about it.

There was no moment to go. That was what she found. If you went before the step, the backhand followed you down. If you went after the step, it had already gone past where your head was. The only moment that worked was the exact one, the half-breath when the backhand was committed and could not change and had not yet arrived. She knew from every hour she had ever spent fighting that nobody could choose that moment. You could not see it coming and then decide to go. There was no time to decide anything in it.

You could only already be going.

She sat down on the dirt in the middle of the empty yard with her arms round her knees.

He had not decided it. She was sure of that now. She had watched his face. She had watched him start his own counter, back and out, the one he had been building for three days, and she had watched it stop halfway, as if somebody had taken it out of his hands. And then he had gone down into that drop, and come up out of it with his feet wide and his hips too far round and his arms low and strange. He had stood there looking at his own body as though it had come back to him from somebody else, slightly the wrong size.

It had not been his.

She knew how he moved by now. She had been watching him move every morning for nearly three weeks. He moved like somebody who had read about moving and was checking the book against the world. He was neat. He was careful. He was always a quarter of a breath behind his own eyes. She had been using that quarter-breath against him every morning, and she knew exactly how wide it was.

That drop had not been a quarter-breath behind anything. It had been on time. It had been on time with her, with her own step and her own strike, as though it had known them as well as she did.

And it had had a shape.

She sat in the dirt and tried to see the shape again, and could not quite. It had been low and sweeping, and the left leg had gone out behind in that long arc, and the turn had come from the hip and not the shoulders. It was not a move she had taught him. It was not anything anybody at the academy had taught her. And yet, when she had done it herself just now, slowly, her body had understood it at once, as though it were a word in a language she spoke but had never heard said in quite that accent.

She did not like that. She did not know what to do with it. So she set it down, carefully, beside the other things she had not known what to do with, and looked at the little row of them.

There were three now.

The first had been on the second day, at the board. She had been talking about the gap, and about moving before you decided to, and his hand had gone to his hip. It had gone to the strap of a bag he was not carrying, and stayed there, and his face had gone somewhere a long way off, down a road she could not see. She had not asked. She had wanted to. She had stood there and watched him come back from wherever it was, and she had decided it was his.

The second had been Renn's third exchange.

She had been at the rope. She had seen the heel settle, as she had seen it settle in her own bout in her second month. She had known what was coming, because she had been on the end of it. The first cut, the second, the third up the lane. She had known exactly where the third was going to go, because she had once lain on her back looking at the lamps from the place where it went. And he had not been there. His eyes had still been on the heel. His feet had already gone. She had bitten her own knuckle so hard there was a mark on it for two days.

*Instinct,* he had said, in the lane, under the lantern, with his hand on his ribs.

She had written it down, in the place in her head where she kept such things, as tonight's answer. She had meant it when she said it. It was the kind of answer you gave when you did not have a better one, and she had decided to let him keep it until he did.

And now this. The third. In daylight, in the grey half of the yard, against her own strike, with nobody watching but the crows.

She had not pushed. She had stood in front of him with the question right there in her mouth, the next question, the one that came after *I don't know*. She had been about to say it. *Then what was it? Do it again. Show me.*

She had not said it, because of his face.

She had seen him look for something in her face. She had seen him search it, quickly and thoroughly, as he searched everybody, and she had known exactly what he was searching for. She had seen it in other people's faces when she told them she had been sent away from the academy. It was the drawing back. The small polite step away that people's eyes took when they found out you were not what they had expected.

He had been waiting for her to do it. He had been bracing for it.

And she had found that she did not want to give him anything he could mistake for it. Not a question, not a frown, not an *again, show me* in the voice of somebody who needed an explanation before she would decide what to think of him. She had had enough of desks in her life. She had stood in front of one once and been told that what she had done did not count because nobody had approved it first, and she had asked *then what were you testing?* and been given silence. She was not going to be a desk.

So she had said *strange*, and *let's keep going*, and she had meant both. Then she had watched him reach for it twice more and come back empty both times. She had watched that be worse for him than the drop itself.

At the gate she had very nearly asked him anyway. She had turned round to do it. And then she had told him about his left foot instead, which was true, and which was something he could fix.

She got up off the dirt and dusted her hands on her trousers.

Out of habit, without deciding to, she reached for her Arbiter.

It was the most ordinary thing she did. She did it most mornings when she woke, and most evenings, and sometimes in the middle of the day for no reason at all, the way some people glanced up at the sky. You reached for it, which was not a movement of the body, more like remembering something on purpose. And it was there, small and steady at the edge of everything, and it opened for you. *Copper. Rank 2. Wind Path.* Her two declarations underneath, in the order they had come, the first one from Kindling and the second from the spring, which had come with its own odd small rush of pleasure that she still remembered. No new line. No change. It had looked exactly like this for a long time now, and she was used to it, and only very occasionally did it annoy her.

She let it close.

She wondered, for the first time, what his said about this morning.

She had never asked him. You did not ask. Declarations were private, and an Arbiter was the most private thing a person had, and she had not asked anybody what their Arbiter said since the week of her own Kindling. She did know the word. She had known it since the night of the first day. She had gone to the other market in the dark and found the man with the crate of old books with their covers torn off, and bought a public summary of the registry from him for less than a pie. She had sat up with it by a candle stub until she found the four lines near the end.

*Four (4) instances recorded in the history of the Compact registry.*

Four. And he was one of them. She had read it twice by candlelight, and closed the book, and lain awake for a while thinking that he had stood in front of a board for eleven minutes and then told her. He had said the word himself, knowing what the four lines said, before he knew whether she would draw back from him.

She had not told him she had looked it up. She did not know why. Perhaps it had felt like reading somebody's letters.

She walked to the gate. The pie boy was still asleep on his sack with his mouth open.

What she wanted out of the mornings, she thought, was not complicated. She had known it for some time, and she let herself look at it now, because she was alone and there was nobody to see her face.

She wanted to be right about the skipped middle.

She did not need the academy to say so. The academy had said what it said, and the desk had won, and she had stopped expecting desks to change their minds. She wanted to be right about it in front of somebody who would understand why she was right. Somebody who would learn the three parts properly, the long way, and then learn why the middle could go, and then do it, and have it work. She wanted to teach it the way she wished somebody had taught it to her. Not *this is the sequence, there are diagrams*, but *this is what the middle is for, and this is what it costs, and here is when you can afford not to pay*. She wanted to see her own way go into somebody else's body and come out working. Then it would not only be hers. Then it would be true.

He was the first person she had met in Ardenmere who might do it. He learned everything the long way, and never made the same mistake twice, and asked *why* about every single thing, and wrote the answers down.

And there was another thing she wanted, smaller, which she did not much like admitting even in an empty yard.

For four months she had trained alone, at a post, in the grey half of a borrowed yard, before anybody was awake. She had liked it. She had told herself she liked it. It was quiet, and nobody watched her, and she could make her mistakes where nobody would remember them. And then a boy with a notebook had sat down on a crate and started writing down how she hit a post. Three weeks later he had told her about her own left foot, a thing she had been doing for four months that nobody had ever seen. She had taken it away in an hour. She had been fighting better since. She was fairly sure she would beat Brenna now, if they put her in with Brenna again, and she had not been sure of that before.

She was better because he watched her. She had not expected that. She did not know quite what to do with it either. But it was not a thing she was going to give up because he had done something strange in the grey half on a Wednesday.

The shade, she thought, was the last thing.

She had never told him why she always began in the grey half. He had never asked. She had watched him notice it on the first morning after Renn, and file it, and not ask, and she had liked him for that more than for almost anything else. The reason was not a secret, exactly. It was only hers. In the shade the light did not lie as much. In the sun you could see everything, and so you trusted your eyes, and your eyes were the slowest thing you had. In the shade you could not quite trust them, and so you learned to trust your feet. You began where it was hard to see and finished where it was easy, and by the time the sun reached you, you did not need it.

She thought he would probably understand that, if she told him. She thought she might tell him one day.

Not yet.

At the gate of the Cinder House yard she stopped and looked back at the grey half, which was not grey any more, and at the long scuffed arc in the dirt where his left foot had gone out behind him. The pie boy had raked the rest of the circle that afternoon. He had not raked over there, because there was no reason to, and so the arc was still there, a long curved line across the dirt, already blurring at its edges.

She looked at it for a while.

*How did you know that was coming?*

*I don't know.*

She believed him. That was the strangest part. He was not a liar, and he was not hiding it from her, and he was frightened. And she had a row of three things now, and no word to put above them. She was not going to make one up. She was not going to be the kind of person who made one up.

She would watch. If he asked her anything, she would answer straight. And if it came again, she would be standing exactly where she was standing this morning, looking at him, without drawing back an inch.

She went out through the gate and down the lane toward wherever the rest of her afternoon was going, and by the time she reached the bottom of it she was walking quickly, the way she walked when she had made up her mind, with her weight a little forward, on the front of her feet.

# Chapter 19 — Absence

Torvin was at the kitchen table again that evening, with his thin ledger and its cracked spine, adding columns with his lips moving.

It had become a thing he did on certain evenings, Cael had worked out, though not on any evenings Cael could predict. He would come in from wherever he went in the afternoons and sit at the end of the table with the ledger and a stub of pencil, and his wife would put a bowl at his elbow without asking, and nobody would speak to him until he reached the bottom of a column. Tonight the whole house seemed to be at the table. Yeni had a shirt across her knee. The woman who slept in her boots was eating soup in her boots. The brothers had come in early, because the river was high and nobody was moving barges on it, and they were arguing in low voices about whether the river was high or only looked high.

Cael ate his soup. He did not taste much of it. He kept feeling the drop in his legs, in the long muscles at the backs of his thighs, where it had stretched something he did not normally stretch. It was not pain. It was more like the memory of having been somewhere.

Torvin reached the bottom of a column, wrote a figure under it, and drew a line.

"Man came by this afternoon," he said, to the ledger. "Asking after the circuit kid staying here."

The brothers stopped arguing.

Cael put his spoon down. He did it carefully, so that it did not click against the bowl.

"What did you tell him?"

"Nothing." Torvin turned the page. He seemed perfectly satisfied about this, in the way a man is satisfied with a wall he has built that has not fallen down. "Said I don't keep a list of who fights. Said I don't want to see anybody's registration and I don't. Said if he wanted to know about circuit kids, he could go and ask the circuit." He started down the next column. "He went."

"What did he look like?"

Torvin's pencil paused. He seemed to consider the question from several sides, as he considered most things.

"A man," he said. "Polite. Not from round here, I'd say, by his boots. Didn't give a name and I didn't ask for one." The pencil went on. "Didn't say why. I didn't ask that either."

That was all. He did not seem to think it was very important. He seemed to think, as far as Cael could tell, that the important part of the story was his own discretion, and that he had told it mainly so that the table would know how well he had done.

"People ask about the fighters," said Yeni, without looking up from her shirt. "They always have. Somebody sees a bout and wants to know where the boy lives, so they can tell their friends they saw him buying bread." Her needle went in and out. "It means you're worth a bet. That's all it usually means."

"Usually," said Cael.

Yeni's needle paused, for half a stitch. Then it went on.

"I'd have told him you were seven foot tall," said the woman in the boots, through her soup. "And lived on the roof. And ate nails."

"I didn't tell him anything," said Torvin, mildly. "That's the point of not telling people things." He reached the bottom of the column, and wrote, and drew his line. "Doesn't work if you tell them nails."

The brothers laughed. Torvin's wife said something about the soup getting cold, and the table went back to its ordinary noise, and after a while Cael picked up his spoon again.

He thought about it all through the rest of the meal. He thought about a polite man with boots from somewhere else, standing at the door with the leaning O over it, asking for *the circuit kid*. Not for a name. Torvin had not said that the man knew his name. Only for what he was, the way Vell's book knew him. *Unrated. Fourteen. Vouched.*

He thought about the fish man's nod, and the two errand boys with their half-marks, and Marrow's eyes going up from his slate. He thought about Lira on the low wall saying *the boy who's dangerous, and nobody knows why*.

It was probably nothing. Yeni was probably right. It was probably somebody who had stood at the rope on Sunday and wanted to tell his friends where the boy lived.

He wrote it down that night anyway, in the front of the Log, in the margin with no column, under the fish man.

*Torvin: a man asked at the door after "the circuit kid". Polite. Not local, by his boots (Torvin, h). No name asked, none given. Torvin told him nothing.*

He looked at it for a while. He did not know what the ruling was. He left the third column empty.

---

The front of the book came first. That was the rule, and tonight he kept it even though his hands wanted to go straight to the back.

He wrote the morning's drill as an entry, because it had been a bout of a kind. *Lira, the step. Third morning on it.* He wrote the two touches, and the step, and his own counter, back and out, and the times it had worked and the times it had not. He was honest about the numbers. His counter had not worked once that morning.

Then he came to the drop, and tried to put it in the columns.

*Claim: the first time she used the step this morning, I dropped under the backhand.*

He looked at that. It was true. He went on.

*Evidence: Lira saw it. I felt the strike go over. Left foot left a mark in the dirt.*

That was true too. He had looked at the mark himself, on the way out through the gate, a long curved scuff across the grey half, already blurring.

*Ruling:*

He held the pencil over the third column for a long time.

There was nothing to put there. A ruling was what he would do next time, and he did not know what he would do next time, because he had not done anything this time. He had not decided to drop. He had not known he was going to drop. He had reached for it twice afterward, in front of the same strike, and it had not come. A ruling that said *drop under the backhand* would be a lie. He could not drop under the backhand. He had tried.

It was the third exchange against Renn all over again. The columns would not take it. He had a claim and a witness and a mark in the dirt, and he had nothing he could do with any of it.

He crossed out the line. He did not tear the page out, this time. He drew one line through it, neatly, so that the words could still be read underneath, and beside it in the margin he wrote *back*.

Then he closed the front of the book and turned it over.

---

He had been carrying two things in a corner for four weeks.

He let himself look at them now. He had not done that before. He had known they were there, the way you know a stone is in your boot without stopping to take it out. He had walked a long way on them.

The first was the cart.

It was written down. It was in the back of the old notebook, the third one from Denvash, on a page near the end, in pencil, in the dark. *I told the carter I heard it coming. I don't think I did.* He had written it the night of Lira's question at the board, and had not drawn any arrow from it, and had not counted it. It was a fact by itself on a page, and he had shut the book on it.

The second was Renn.

That one was not written down at all. It was in Vell's book, in her hand, in whatever she had written in those eight extra lines. It was in Lira's head, *tonight's answer*. It was in Renn's, *it doesn't miss, it missed*. It was in nobody's book of his own. He had tried to write it once, at the pump, and torn the page out, and folded it, and the folded page was still in the pocket of his coat. He had not unfolded it since.

And now there was this.

He sat on the end of his cot with the Log open on his knee at its last page, and Yeni's lamp turned low, and the wheezing man whistling by the door, and he thought very clearly: *there are three.*

He did not like the thought. It went down into him and stayed there and was cold.

Three was not one. One was a thing that happened. One could be the wheels he had not noticed hearing, or the watching that had soaked into him without his knowing, or a figure that had come off its lever wrong by luck. Two was harder, but two could still be a coincidence, if you wanted it to be badly enough. He had wanted it to be badly enough. He had wanted it so badly that he had not let himself count to two.

Three was a column.

He thought about counting all three. He could do it now, here, tonight. He could turn to the back of the old notebook and copy the cart into this one, and unfold the page from his coat, and sit down and write the whole of the third exchange as honestly as he could, and then write this morning under it, and number them. *One, two, three.* That would be honest arithmetic. Hesk would have done it that way. Hesk would have said that you did not get to choose where a column started, that it started with the first figure, and if you left the first figure out because it frightened you, then every total under it would be wrong.

He knew that. He sat with it for a long time.

And then he decided, with the pencil in his hand, that he would not.

Not yet. He would not count the cart, because the cart had been on a road with nobody to see it, and he still could not be sure, not all the way, that it was not his own ears. He would not count Renn, because Renn was in the middle of a bout, and bouts were full of things, and he had been frightened and fast and watching harder than he had ever watched anything. He could still tell himself, if he tried, that the watching had done it. He did not believe that any more. But he could still tell himself.

This one he could not tell himself anything about.

This one had happened in daylight, in the grey half of a yard he knew, against a strike he had seen thirty times, with somebody standing in front of him who knew exactly how he moved. It had not been hearing. It had not been watching. He had been watching, and building his counter, and it had taken the counter out of his hands. Lira had seen it. Lira had said so. There was a mark in the dirt.

This one he would count.

He wrote, at the top of the clean back page:

*First instance.*

Then he sat and looked at the two words. He knew they were not true. He had known it before he wrote them. It was not the first. It was the first he was counting, which was a different thing, and he knew exactly how different, and he was doing it anyway. A column had to start somewhere. He had found that he could not yet make himself start it further back, and he would rather start it here, on purpose, knowing what he had left out, than not start it at all.

He was not proud of that. He wrote under it anyway.

*Morning, Cinder House yard, the grey half. Lira's step, the two touches and the backhand. I was going back and out, my own counter. I didn't. I dropped. Low, sideways, left leg out behind, right knee under, turned from the hip. I have never been taught that. I have never done it. It was exactly on time. The backhand went over the back of my head.*

*Afterward I was standing wrong. Feet wide, hips too far round, arms low. As if I had put on somebody else's coat and the sleeves were in the wrong place.*

*I tried to find it again twice. Nothing there. It doesn't come when I want it to. It doesn't come because the same strike comes again.*

*She asked me how I knew. I don't know.*

He stopped. His hand was cold. He went on.

*Hesk wrote: your body is going to learn before your mind does. On the wall by the road I thought he meant that I would do things before I understood them. Like a reflex you grow into. I don't think that's what this was. My body didn't learn this. It didn't have it to learn. It wasn't mine.*

He read that back, and his stomach turned over, and he nearly crossed it out. He did not cross it out. He drew a small box round *It wasn't mine*, which was not a thing he had ever done in a notebook before, so that he would know when he came back to it that he had meant it, and been frightened by it, and left it there.

He thought about writing to Hesk.

He got as far as taking a clean sheet out of the back of the old notebook. He sat with it on his knee and the pencil over it, the way he had sat at the waystation table and the kitchen table, and he thought about what he would say.

*Something happened today.*

It would be a week getting there. Hesk would read it at the kitchen table in the twenty minutes of sideways light, and then he would have nothing to do about it but sit in the workshop with a guild review on the shelf and his grandson's fear in a letter. And whatever Hesk wrote back would come a week after that. By then the morning in the grey half would be two weeks old, and whatever was happening to him would have gone on happening, or stopped, and the answer would be to a question he had stopped asking.

That was true. It was a good reason. He knew it was a good reason, and he knew it was not the whole one.

The whole one was smaller, and he was less proud of it. He wanted to understand this himself first.

Everything else about the word had come to him from somebody else. The four lines in the summary. The green book in the archive, and its three entries ending in weeks. Pellin's form from the back of a drawer. The notice. Hesk's book, with its *I don't know* in faded ink and its fifth entry he had read three times. All of it had been handed to him, already written, by people who had got there first and were warning him. Even Hesk, who loved him, had been living alongside the word for years before Cael had ever heard it.

This was the first thing about it that had happened to him and to nobody else. Nobody had warned him about it. Nobody had a page on it. He did not know what it was, but it was his, and he wanted, for a little while, to be the one who found out.

He knew it was the same choice. He knew it exactly. *Don't make a thing real before it is.* He had been angry at Hesk for making it, in a room with a crooked tree in the window. And here he was making it again, with a cart, and with Renn, and now with this.

He put the clean sheet back in the old notebook.

Then, in the back of the Log, under the box, he wrote one more line, because he had decided that if he was going to keep things from Hesk he was at least going to keep a record of keeping them.

*Didn't write to Hesk about it. That's the third thing I haven't told him. I'll have to answer for all of them at once one day. Know that.*

And under that, smaller:

*It happened in her yard, against her strike. Be careful with that. For her sake.*

He sat there afterward with the Log closed on his knee.

He thought about ledgers. He had been thinking about ledgers for weeks without noticing that he was. There was Vell's book, which knew him as what he was and what he had done, without a name. There was Marrow's slate, which knew him as a number that moved, *one*, *two*, *three*, *four*, chalked up and wiped off with a sleeve. There was the Log on his knee, which knew him better than either of them and was still full of empty third columns.

And there was one more. Everybody else in Ardenmere had it. Joren had it. Alis had it. Renn and Brenna and Amrit Sole and Petra Voss had it. Lira had it, and he had watched her eyes go somewhere for a moment, now and then, between drills, in a way he had never asked about. It was the one ledger that was supposed to tell you what you were. Tier. Rank. Path. The declarations, one after another, as they came. You could check it whenever you liked, and it would tell you, and you could believe it.

He had not checked his in four weeks.

He sat on his cot and made himself say it plainly, in his head. Not four days. Not since he had settled in, or found his feet, or been too busy. Four weeks. He had looked for it twice in the side room at Weaver's Row, with the crooked tree in the window, and it had not been there, and he had not looked since.

He had told himself all sorts of things about why. That there was no point, because he knew what he would find. That there was no time, between the yard and the mender and the circle. That he had more important things to measure. He had said each of them to himself at some point in the last four weeks, and each of them had sounded perfectly reasonable while he was saying it.

He knew what the truth was. He had known it for some time. He had been doing it with the cart, and with Renn, and now he could see himself doing it with this too.

He was afraid of the answer.

He was afraid that he would reach for it and nothing would happen, and then he would know. He was also afraid, in a way he had not let himself look at at all, that something would happen. That it would open, and show him something, and he would not understand it.

An honest log used every instrument it had. He had made that rule himself, at the pump, with the straightedge. *Never trust a number you can't trace.* And there was one instrument he had not touched once, because it frightened him. Which meant that every page in the Log, every claim and every piece of evidence and every ruling, had been written by somebody who was not looking at the one gauge everybody else looked at first.

He got up.

It was past midnight. The house had gone quiet round him: the wheezing man, the brothers breathing in their long river-tired rhythm, Yeni turned to the wall. He went down the turning stairs in his socks, carrying his boots, and put them on at the bottom, and let himself out through the kitchen into the yard.

The yard at night was a different place.

In the day it was where the pump was, and the washing, and Torvin's wife's buckets, and once a week somebody boiling sheets over a low fire in the corner. At night it was only a square of packed dirt between high walls, with the pump standing up in the middle of it like a thin dark man, and a stunted pear tree in the corner by the wall that had given up trying to grow tall and had grown wide instead. There were no lamps. The only light came from one shuttered window high up in the next house, a thin bar of yellow that fell across the top of the far wall and did not reach the ground.

It was cold. He could see his breath.

He went to the middle of the yard, to a spot that seemed to him, for no reason at all, to be the centre of something, and stood there.

He thought about the circle in Pellin's office. The pale wood worn smooth in two places side by side. He thought about putting his feet where the wood was worn, and the thing that had gathered behind his breastbone, small, and risen, and come to rest at the edge of his sight, just off to one side. *Every practitioner's Arbiter is their own, dormant since birth.* The procedural summary had said that you reached it by attending to the place behind the breastbone. He had read that a dozen times at the long table under the window. He had never once had to do it.

He attended to the place behind his breastbone.

Nothing happened.

He tried to remember exactly how it had felt. Not the word. Before the word. The point of light rising through him slowly, like a held breath. The low note at the bottom of his chest that was less a sound than the memory of one. He held all of it in his mind, carefully, the way he would hold a measurement he did not want to lose, and he reached for the place where it had been.

Nothing.

He moved his feet. That was foolish. Nothing about the circle had needed a particular stance, and he knew it, and he set his feet a little wider anyway, and squared his shoulders, as a man leans harder on a stuck door when what it wants is the handle turned the other way. Then he shut his eyes. Joren had said *it shows up right there, not in your eyes, sort of behind them, like words written on the inside of your eyelids.* He looked at the inside of his eyelids.

They were dark.

He opened his eyes and looked to the edge of his sight, left, where it had hung, steady as a nail, for eleven seconds. Then right. Then left again, quickly, the way you turn your head for a sound you are sure you heard.

The pump. The wall. The bar of yellow light. The pear tree.

He tried again. And again. He counted. He had always counted, and he counted now, though he would have given a good deal not to. Four. Five. He held the reach longer each time, past the point where it felt reasonable, the way you hold your breath past comfort to find out how long you can, daring the dark behind his breastbone to prove him wrong. Six. Seven.

It did not prove him wrong.

And it was not silence. He knew that now, standing in the yard, as clearly as he had known it in the side room. Silence would have been something. Silence would have been a thing that was there and was choosing not to answer, the way Pellin's brass plate had sat dark for eleven seconds and then lit. He could have waited out a silence. He could have knocked louder.

This was absence. It was not a door that would not open. It was the place where he had been told a door would be, and there was no door there. There was not even the shape of one. There was nothing to knock on.

Eight. Nine.

Somewhere after that he stopped counting.

He noticed himself stop. He noticed the number go, the way you notice a clock stop ticking in another room. And he understood that for him, of all people, that was its own kind of answer, and he stopped reaching.

He stood in the middle of the yard with his hands at his sides.

The cold had got into his fingers and was working its way up his wrists. The burn on his left forearm, which had been only an itch for days, had begun to ache again in the cold. Somewhere a long way off a dog barked, twice, and was told to be quiet, and was quiet. The bar of yellow light on the far wall went out, and then the yard was quite dark, and he could see only the shape of the pump and the shape of the tree.

He thought about the people who had it.

He thought about Joren reading his declaration four hundred times. About Alis touching her mother's arm and knowing she had a headache. About the twins coming out of the examination room together with their certificates and their loosened faces, and the glazed boy who had got Force and did not know whether he wanted it. He thought about Lira's eyes going somewhere for a moment between drills, and coming back, as though she had glanced at a clock. He had never asked her about it. He had not wanted to know how easy it was.

Every one of them could reach into the dark behind their breastbone and find a light there, and the light would tell them what they were. Not all of it. Not the whole of a person. But the part that the city cared about. The part that decided which side of the bridge you slept on. Tier. Rank. Path. Whether you were getting better.

And he had stood in the circle and been told one word, and then the light had gone out, and he had been standing in the dark ever since and calling it a classification.

He found that he wanted to cry.

He noticed it the way he noticed everything, from a little way off. There was a tightness at the back of his throat and a heat behind his eyes, and his breath had gone uneven, and he stood very still in the middle of the yard and looked at it. He did not cry. He did not think it would have been wrong to. He thought, quite clearly, that it would have been a perfectly reasonable thing to do in a dark yard at midnight after finding out that there was nothing there. But he did not, and he noted that he did not, and he noted that he wanted to. He noted that the wanting was information too, and that he did not yet know what it measured, and he put it away with the rest.

Then he went back across the yard, and let himself in, and took his boots off at the bottom of the stairs, and went up in his socks.

---

He did not write anything. He had thought he would. He lay on his cot with the Log under his pillow and the pencil in the spine, and he did not take it out.

He lay a long while in the dark and listened to the room.

At some point he slept, because at some point he dreamed. It was not much of a dream. He was standing in the circle at Weaver's Row, with his feet in the worn places, and nothing was happening, the same nothing as in the yard. But in the dream the nothing had a texture to it, almost a weight, like standing in a doorway that went somewhere and had decided not to open for him and for nobody else.

He woke once, in the black part of the night, and did not know where he was. Then he heard the wheezing man, and knew. *Torvin's. The window cot. Ardenmere.* And then he slept again.

When he woke for good the window had gone grey and the brothers were dropping their boots.

He lay still and did what he did most mornings now. He took stock. What he had learned. What he still did not know. What the day was going to ask of him.

The yard was not an open question any more. That was the first thing. He had tried, properly, many times, and there had been nothing, and now he knew. It was not the knowing he had wanted. But it was a fact, and you could build on a fact, and he had not had this one before.

And there was one other thing he knew, lying there in the grey light with the boots dropping, and it surprised him by how sure it was.

Standing still with this had not helped. He had stood still with it for four weeks. He had stood still with it in a yard at midnight until he stopped counting.

He would try moving instead.

He got up before the others, and dressed under his coat, and went down through the kitchen and out of the front door under the leaning O, toward the Cinder House, because Lira would be there, in the grey half, before anybody else was awake. And because there was a question he had never asked her, and he thought that he was going to ask it now.

# Chapter 20 — What the Registry Says

He was early, even for him. The sun was not up. There was mist lying in the yard of the Cinder House, low and white over the packed dirt, so thick in the corner by the drain that the barrel stood up out of it like a post in a flood. The crows had not arrived yet. The circle's groove ran away into the mist and disappeared, and the east wall had not yet begun to throw its shadow, because there was not yet any sun to throw it.

Lira was there anyway.

She was at the post, stretching, with one foot up on the canvas wrapping and her body folded down over the leg. She did not look surprised to see him. He was beginning to think she was never surprised to see anybody, that she simply arrived everywhere first and waited.

"You're early," she said, into her knee.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. You're early for you." She changed legs. "You look as though you slept in a ditch."

He had walked all the way from Torvin's with the question, turning it over and over, trying to find a way into it that was two steps away from the real one. He had wanted something he could back out of if her face did the wrong thing. He had built three or four. He had walked them up and down the lanes and knocked the corners off them. And standing in front of her in the mist, with her folded over her leg and not looking at him, he found that he could not remember any of them.

"What does it look like," he said, "when you check your Arbiter?"

It came out much too fast. It cut straight across whatever she had been about to say about ditches. She did not seem to notice. She was still stretching, and she answered the way you answer a question about the weather.

"Like checking a ledger. More or less." She straightened, and put her hands on her hips, and leaned back until her spine cracked. "You reach for it. That's hard to describe if you've never done it. It's not a thing you do with your body. It's more like remembering something on purpose. And it opens, and it shows you your page. Tier, rank, Path. Your declarations, in the order they came. Sometimes a line at the bottom, if something's shifted, if you've been building toward a rank and you're close." She shrugged. "Takes about a breath. I do it most mornings without thinking. Why? Are you wondering what yours'll look like, once it stops sticking?"

She said it lightly. She was still loosening her shoulders. She had no idea what she was standing on.

"Does it always answer?" he said.

"Of course it always answers. It always—"

She stopped.

She stopped in the middle of the word, and her hands came down off her hips, and she looked at him. He watched her whole body change in the space of that one broken sentence. It was not alarm. It was the thing he had seen her do on the barrel when somebody in the circle did something she had not trained against, and in the grey half yesterday morning. Everything in her went still and quick at the same time and began to add.

"Why are you asking?" she said.

He had known this would come. He had not known it would come like this, blurted at dawn, without any of the slow care he usually took for weeks before he let himself act. He had imagined something careful. He had imagined being ready.

"What does it look like," he said, "when it doesn't?"

"It doesn't not. It always answers. It's an Arbiter, it's—" She stopped again. "Unless."

He watched her get there. He watched her take a word she had read on a page by candlelight and turn it into a person standing in front of her in the mist, the way he had done in the archive in Denvash with the number four.

"You know what I am," he said. It was not quite a question.

"[SHATTERED]." She said it carefully, as though she were picking up something she had read about and never held. "I looked it up. After the first day."

He had not expected that. It must have shown.

"There's a man in the other market," Lira said, "with a crate of old books with the covers torn off. I bought a registry summary off him that night, for less than a pie. I sat up with it till I found the four lines." She looked at the post, not at him. "I didn't tell you. I don't know why. It felt like reading somebody's letters. You'd told me the word yourself. I didn't want you to think I'd gone looking behind it."

"What did it say?"

"What you already know it says." She was quiet for a moment. "Four instances. Deprecated. Safety risk. That's all it says. There wasn't anything about an Arbiter in it." She looked back at him. "So that's it. It isn't that you drew a poor Path. You don't get one you can check. You don't get a page."

"I got one word," Cael said. "Then it went out."

"Went out."

"It came up the way everybody says. From behind here." He touched his breastbone. "It sat at the edge of my eye for eleven seconds. Then it said the word, and then it went out, like a lamp when you pinch the wick. And it hasn't come back." He made himself say the next part. "I didn't look for it again for four weeks. I was afraid to. Last night I went down into Torvin's yard at midnight and tried. Properly. I don't know how many times. I stopped counting."

Lira did not say anything for a long moment.

"You stopped counting," she said.

"Yes."

He watched her understand what that meant, coming from him. He saw it go into her face and stay there.

"That's why you don't know things," she said slowly. "Things everybody else just knows. Your rank. Whether you're getting better. Whether you're close to anything." She let out a breath. "I check mine every morning. I don't even think about it. It's like looking up to see what the weather's doing. I never once thought what it'd be like to reach for it and have it not be there." She shook her head. "That's worse than I thought the word meant. I thought it meant a bad draw. That's not a bad draw. That's no draw."

"Is it worse?"

"I don't know." She looked at him straight. "Is it?"

She was not saying it to be kind. She wanted to know. It was the same thing she had done at the board on the second day, when she had looked at the word as if it were a question.

He thought about it. He had not thought about it this way before. He had been too busy being afraid of it.

"It's worse," he said, "because I don't have a map. Everybody else gets told, more or less, what they are and where they've got to. I have to make the map myself, out of evidence, and I don't know if I'm reading the evidence right, and there's nobody to check my working against." He stopped. "But it's better, too. Nobody can tell me where I stop. Because nobody knows. There isn't a page somewhere with a number already on it, waiting for me to catch up to it."

"That's either very freeing," said Lira, "or completely terrifying."

"Both. Most hours of most days."

She almost laughed. She did not, quite. She sat down on the low wall at the side of the yard, in the mist, and after a moment he sat down beside her.

"Can I ask you a thing," she said, "that might be stupid?"

"You've never asked me a stupid thing."

"There's always a first." She picked at a loose thread on the cuff of her jacket, where it had been turned back and stitched. She was not looking at him. He had learned that this was how she chose words when they mattered. "When it happened. In the office. When it said the word. Did it feel like something was wrong with you? Like something broke? Or did it just feel like nothing, like a door not opening?"

He thought about it honestly, because that was what her question deserved. He went back to the circle of pale wood, and the light rising, and the low note in his chest.

"Neither," he said. "That's the strange part. It felt finished. It didn't feel like a mistake. It felt like an answer, a real one, only nobody had a drawer to put it in." He looked at the mist. "Nothing about it felt broken from the inside. It was other people's faces that made it broken. Pellin's hand going to the back of the drawer. The guard at the gate. That's when it broke. Not before."

Lira was quiet.

"That wasn't a stupid question," she said at last. "I was wrong. Nothing you've told me about it has been more use than that." She turned and looked at him. "Because that's the part that counts, isn't it? Not what the registry thinks it is. What it's like from where you're standing."

"I don't know yet what it's like from where I'm standing. That's most of what I'm doing. Trying to find out from the inside what everybody else keeps telling me from the outside."

"Then we're doing the same thing," said Lira. "We started from different ends. That's all."

She did not make anything more of it than that. She said it simply, and looked away, and he found that because she did not try to make it land, it landed.

---

They sat on the wall a while longer. The mist had begun to thin. Somewhere above it the sun was coming, and the top of the east wall had turned pale gold, and the first crow came down onto the eaves of the public house and shook itself and looked at them.

"Yesterday," Lira said.

He waited.

"I'm not going to ask you again. Not until you've got something you want to tell me." She was looking at the crow. "But I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it, either. I'm not going to pretend it was nothing, and I'm not going to pretend it frightened me, because it didn't." She looked at him. "I want you to know that. It didn't."

"I looked for it," he said. "In your face. Afterward."

"I know you did. I watched you look." She almost smiled. "It wasn't there."

"No."

"It won't be." She said it flatly, as though she were giving him a measurement. Then, quite differently, practically, the way she said *catch, reset, send*: "So you haven't got a page. You've got Vell's book."

"Which doesn't have my name in it."

"Which has what you did in it. And Marrow's slate."

"Which is a number he wipes off with his sleeve."

"Which went from one to four in three weeks. And you've got that." She nodded at the bulge of the Log in his coat. "And you've got me."

He looked at her.

"I've been watching you for three weeks," Lira said. "Every morning. I know what you were like on the first one. So I can tell you." She held up a hand and counted on it. "You catch with the left now, as well as the right, and you didn't, a fortnight ago. You don't make the hole in the deflection any more. You found it and it's gone. You turn your back foot out on the box drill, still, but less. And against Petra you went on her shoulders in the seventh exchange a good quarter-breath sooner than you went on them in the second. I watched it." She put her hand down. "That's not a page. It's not the one everybody else has. But it's not nothing, and it's true, and somebody's checking it."

He did not say anything for some time. He did not trust what his voice would do.

"Every morning," he said, at last.

"Every morning. You write it down. I'll tell you what I saw, and you'll put it in the columns, and you'll have your evidence." She stood up. "It's a worse Arbiter than the real one. It's grumpy before breakfast and it's got opinions about your feet. But it's yours."

He did not answer at once. He was not unwilling. It was only that it was the one piece of the whole thing he had in writing, and handing somebody a paper like that felt like handing them a share of the weight. He had carried it alone so long that the carrying had become a habit, and he did not know how you set a habit down.

"You don't have to show me," Lira said. She had seen him hesitate. She seemed to see most things. "It's not something you owe me."

"I know." He took the old notebook out of his coat, the third one from Denvash, and opened it from the back. The notice was folded small and tucked inside the back cover, where it had been since Fen Street. Its creases had gone soft from being opened and shut more often than any paper needed to be. "I think I want you to. I've just never shown it to anybody who wasn't in the kitchen when it came."

He gave it to her.

She read it standing in the middle of the box drill, in the last of the grey half. She read it all the way through without any expression at all. Then, before she handed it back, she read it again, more slowly, the way he had read it himself at the kitchen table with the teapot beside him. The way you read a thing a second time when some part of you is hoping that you misunderstood it the first.

She read one part aloud, under her breath, as if she were checking it.

"*Continued cohabitation by a [SHATTERED]-classified individual may affect the standing-holder's guild status pending Compact review.*"

"They've started it," said Cael. "The review. Hesk wrote. It's routine, he says. It'll probably come to nothing."

Lira looked up from the paper.

"This is built," she said. "Do you see? Somebody built this, the way you'd build a bracket. Nobody in here says you have to leave. Nobody orders your grandfather to put you out. They just wrote down, very exactly, what it would cost him if you stayed. And then they gave it to him to add up for himself." She tapped the line with one finger. "So when you went, it was his choice. And yours. Nobody at the registry ever had to be the one. That's not an accident. Somebody's written a great many of these and knows exactly how much weight a sentence can carry without ever looking like a threat."

"That's what Hesk said. Nearly."

"He's right." She folded it again along its soft creases and held it out. For a moment she looked older than fifteen. She did not look tired. She looked as if she were carrying a thing of the same shape as his, and had been for a while, and had just recognized it.

"The circuit doesn't care what the registry says," Lira said.

He took the notice from her hand.

"I'm not saying that to be kind," she went on. "It's just true. Vell doesn't look at registrations. She never has. Nobody at that table's ever asked to see a card. The book doesn't know what tier the Compact stamped on you at fourteen and it doesn't want to. It knows what it's seen you do in that circle, and nothing else." He had been given a great many things since Weaver's Row. He had been given Pellin's level voice, and Hesk's careful truths, and the green book's three entries ending in weeks. He had been given a guard's face drawing back, and the archive's cold, exact print, and a leather book with *I don't know* in it in faded ink. Most of them had been true. Some of them had been kind. Nearly all of them had been somebody telling him what the word made him.

This was the first thing anybody had said that did not change the word at all. It did not change the registry, or the notice, or the review, or the four entries in the green book. It only pointed, quite exactly, at the one place where none of those things counted. And it turned out he had been standing in it for four weeks.

"Thank you," he said.

He meant more than that. The two words were all he could find, and they did not hold all of it, and he said them anyway.

Last night he had stopped counting because counting had stopped being any use. This morning he had stopped because his feet had stopped needing him to. They were going to the corners by themselves. "Better?"

He gave the question the honest answer it was asking for.

"Not solved. Better."

"That's all training ever does," said Lira. "Don't trust anybody who tells you it does more."

And the question. *Did it feel like something was wrong with you?* Nobody had ever asked him that. Hesk had not asked it, because Hesk had been too busy getting ready for it. Pellin had not, because it was not on the form. He had not even asked it himself.

He did the front first. That was the rule.

*Lira's report, first morning. Catches with the left now. The hole's gone. Back foot still turns out on the box, less. Against Petra, moved on the shoulders a quarter-breath sooner in the seventh than the second. (Lira, h. But she saw it, and I didn't, and that's what h is for.)*

He looked at that a long time. Then he wrote, in the third column, the first ruling in weeks that he did not have to make up.

*Ruling: getting better. Somebody's checking.*

Then he turned the book over.

*Tried to reach the Arbiter last night, in Torvin's yard. Nothing. Not silence. Nothing there to be silent. I stopped counting, and I'm writing down that I stopped, because for me that's the measurement.*

*I don't think it's going to come back the way hers does. A page you check. A number you read in the morning. I think whatever this is, it doesn't work like that, and I have to stop waiting for it to.*

*Lira knows. She knew the word from the first night and didn't tell me, because it felt like reading my letters. She didn't flinch this morning. She didn't arrange her face. She added it up, the way she adds up somebody new in the circle, and then she kept going. I didn't know until this morning how much I'd been carrying just from not knowing whether she would.*

*She asked me what it felt like. Not what the registry says. What it felt like, from where I was standing. I didn't have a whole answer. But the question moved something. It shifted the whole thing over by an inch. It used to be something done to me. Now it's something I'm allowed to have an opinion about.*

He stopped. He held the pencil over the page. Then he wrote the line he had been carrying since the morning, in his own hand, so that he would have it.

*The circuit doesn't care what the registry says.*

*I'm going to build whatever this is inside that. It's the only solid ground anybody's offered me since Weaver's Row. Everything else has been somebody telling me what I am. The circuit's the first place that's asked me to show it.*

He read it back once. He did not cross anything out.

He put the Log under his pillow, beside the leather book with its cord and its *I don't know*, so that the two of them lay together. One was in his grandfather's hand, written over years. The other was in his own, written over four weeks, and was still working out what it was.

The house settled round him. The brothers murmured something about the river that neither of them would remember in the morning. The wheezing man whistled at the top of every breath. Somewhere below, the woman who slept in her boots shifted once, and went still, and Torvin's voice came up through the floorboards with its one word, and the last line of light under the door went out.

It was all still there. The absence was still there behind his breastbone, exactly where it had been at midnight. The three things were still in their corner, one of them counted. The review was still on a shelf in Denvash, propped against a clock. Somewhere a polite man with boots from somewhere else still knew there was a circuit kid at Torvin's.

None of it had gone.

But for the first time since the circle at Weaver's Row, he was not the only person in the world who knew the whole shape of it. Somebody else had stood in it with him this morning, in the grey half of a yard, and read the notice twice, and not drawn back an inch.

He had not known how heavy it was to be the only one until he was not.

He slept, that night, without keeping watch.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## The Architect

**SECRET:** The Architect's error was categorical, not motivational. They believed the problem was [UNBOUND] potential. ## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). ## Continuity Rules

6. **Cael's ability integration has limits.** He cannot integrate an ability he has not witnessed in use. Seeing a written description does not qualify. The ability must be performed in his presence.

7. **The found family is permanent.** No companion exits the series without narrative justification. The loss in Book 12 is a choice, not a death — the companion is still alive, their relationship with Cael changed.

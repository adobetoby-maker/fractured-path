# Chapter 14 — Claim, Evidence, Ruling

He woke because one of the brothers dropped a boot, and for a moment, before he moved, nothing hurt at all.

Then he moved. The right side of his chest reported first, the hot band from under his arm to the bottom of his ribs, and the left shoulder joined in a breath later, stiff and thick, as if somebody had packed the joint with wet sand while he slept. Last of all the scrape down his left forearm stung where the blanket dragged across it, the scrape he still could not account for. He lay still and let all three finish what they had to say. The window over his cot had gone from black to grey. The brother whispered an apology to the boot, and then the door closed on both of them, and the stairs creaked their way down to the river.

Last night he had stood under Torvin's pump in the dark with his shirt off and counted, because Lira had told him to count. He had stopped at sixty, because sixty was what his ribs would stand. He could not tell whether it had helped. He could breathe, and every breath went carefully round the edge of something on its way in and out, and that would have to do.

He sat up in three stages, like a folding chair.

Yeni was awake on the next cot. She was lying on her side under her blanket with one eye open, watching him do it.

"Renn," she said.

"Renn."

"How many?"

"Four exchanges."

The eye closed again. "Torvin's wife had you at two," Yeni said into her pillow. "Now she owes the one in the boots a bun."

He had not known that anybody in this house had had him at anything. He sat on the edge of the cot with his shirt in his hands and thought about that, about a bet made over a kitchen table on a boy nobody there had seen fight, and he was not sure whether it made him feel better or worse. Both, probably. He was getting used to things being both.

Putting the shirt on took longer than it should have. He did the right arm first, keeping the elbow tight against his side, and then worked the left shoulder into its sleeve a little at a time, like threading a stiff wire through a hole that was slightly too small. When he had finished, Yeni's eye was open again.

"Left's the shoulder," she said. "Right's the ribs."

"Yes."

"Don't sleep on either." She rolled over to face the wall. "And don't let him tell you to rest. He'll tell you to rest. Rest is for people with savings."

He did not know whether she meant Torvin or somebody else, and she did not say.

---

In the kitchen the range was lit and the oats were on. Torvin's wife stood over the pot with her wooden spoon. The woman who slept in her boots was at the end of the table eating a sweet bun with great slowness and pleasure, and she raised it to Cael when he came in, as somebody might raise a cup.

"Four," she said. "I said three or better. I'm a generous judge of people."

"You said three because you'd heard about the Wind girl," said Torvin's wife, without turning round. "Sit down before you fall down, boy. There's a bowl."

He sat. The bowl came, and with it a spoon and a hard look at the way he lowered himself onto the bench, and then the woman went back to her pot and said nothing else about it. He ate slowly. Turning his head to the left was fine. Turning his body was not. He worked out, by the bottom of the bowl, exactly which movements cost what, and he put the list away in his head beside the other lists.

Torvin came in from the yard with a bucket and stopped in the doorway.

"Pump handle was wet at midnight," he said.

"I used it."

"Lira tell you to?"

"Yes."

"She's right." Torvin set the bucket down by the range. He looked at Cael for a moment in the way he looked at coins, slowly, checking his own count. "I said fight in the street."

"It wasn't in your house."

"No." Torvin seemed to consider this from several sides, and to find nothing wrong with any of them. "Four, they're saying."

"Four."

Torvin grunted. It was not quite approval, and it was not nothing. Then he picked up the oats pot from the range with a cloth round the handle and set it down on the table at Cael's elbow, so that there would be a second bowl if he wanted one, and went out again without another word.

Cael had a second bowl. It seemed rude not to.

---

The Cinder House yard was half in shadow when he got there.

The sun was up somewhere behind the east wall, and the wall threw a long, clean shadow across the packed dirt, so that the yard was split down the middle into a grey half and a bright half, with the circle's groove running through both. There was mist in the low corner by the drain. The crows were working last night's leavings along the rope line, where the pie boy had dropped crusts and someone had dropped a whole half pie and not come back for it.

Lira was at the post in the far corner, in the grey half. She did not stop when she saw him. She watched him walk across the yard to her between one strike and the next, and he understood partway across that she was not watching his face.

"Right side," she said, when he reached her. "And the left shoulder. You walk like a man carrying a tray of glasses."

"You were at the rope. You saw where he hit me."

"I'm not telling you what I saw. I'm telling you what I see." She struck the post, low, and let her hand rest against the canvas. "You're not sparring today."

"I didn't come to spar."

"Then what did you come for?"

"You said you'd see me at the yard."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she turned back to the post so that he could not see her face, which told him more than her face would have, and struck it twice more, a little harder than before.

"All right," she said. "Then you can stand there and learn something with one arm. Right arm. Hold it up. No, like this." She took his wrist and set his forearm upright in front of him, elbow down, palm toward his own face, as if she were propping up a fence post. "This part. The outside. From the wrist to the elbow. That's what you catch with."

She showed him the deflection.

It was the one she had shown him at the board on the second day, the one every Wind student learned in their first month. Catch, reset, send. She went through it slowly, so slowly that it stopped looking like fighting and began to look like a craftsman showing an apprentice how to hold a plane. Her strike came in toward his face at the speed of somebody reaching for a cup. His forearm met it on the outside and caught it. That was the first part.

"Now stop," she said. "Feel where you are."

He felt where he was. The strike had pushed his arm a little across his own body, and his weight had gone with it, onto the wrong foot.

"That's why there's a middle," said Lira. "Catching it moves you. Reset puts you back. Weight in the centre, arm back on its line. Then you're ready to do something with what you caught." She took her hand away and brought it in again, just as slowly. "Catch. Reset. Send."

On *send* she turned his forearm for him, rolling it outward against her wrist. Her strike went away past his shoulder into the empty air, as if it had changed its mind about where it wanted to go.

They did it twenty times before the sun reached the post. Then forty. The reset was where his ribs complained, because the reset meant a small turn of the body back to centre, and the turn went right through the bruise. The third time he flinched in it, she saw. After that she brought her strikes in from a slightly different angle, so that the reset needed less turn, and she did not say anything about why.

"Why not teach me the other way?" he said, around the fiftieth. "Your way. Without the middle."

"Because you can't skip a step you don't own." Catch. Reset. Send. "When I skipped it, I'd done all three parts ten thousand times. I knew exactly what I was leaving out, and what it cost, and why I could afford it. You'd only be leaving out something you never had." She lowered her hand. "That's not a skip. That's a hole."

He thought about that through the next ten repetitions, and he found nothing in it to argue with, which was annoying.

He had brought bread. He had stopped at the top of the second row of the market on the way and asked the bread woman for two from underneath, and watched her face, as the nut boy had told him, and she had given him two from underneath with a small sour look that he took as respect. He held one out to Lira now.

She took it, tore it in half, put one half on top of the post and ate the other in four bites. Then she set her feet.

"Breakfast's between," she said. "Not before. Before, you're slow. After, you're late." She brought her hand up. "Catch."

So he ate his roll in the gaps, in pieces, between sets of ten. When the sun finally came over the east wall and the shadow began to shrink back toward it, Lira moved their work with the shadow, a step at a time, keeping them both in the grey half until there was no grey half left. He noticed it, and he noticed that she did it without looking, as she did the things she had done so often that she no longer needed to decide them.

He did not ask about it. He thought there was probably a reason, and he thought that if she wanted him to have it she would give it to him, and that until then it was hers.

She did not mention the third exchange. Not once, all morning. He kept waiting for it, between the catches and the resets, and it did not come, and somewhere around the hundredth repetition he understood that she was not going to ask today, and that the not asking was a thing she was doing on purpose, as deliberately as she kept them in the shade.

The third notebook from Denvash had nine pages left in it.

He counted them that afternoon, sitting on the edge of the low wall in Torvin's yard beside the pump, and then counted them again, because nine was not many. The front of the book was full of Ardenmere: the nut boy's map, the board decoded, the rules, six pages on two strangers' bouts, Marrow's numbers. The back of it held the things he had written from the other end, as he always did, in the smaller hand. The archive entry. The gate entry. And on its own page, near the end, the dip between the hedges and the turnip and *I don't think I did.*

He closed it on that page without reading it again.

The paper stall was at the bottom of the market, near the fruit woman with no name, and it was run by a man with ink on his cuffs who sold everything from single sheets to bound ledgers with marbled edges that cost more than a week at Torvin's. Cael looked at the ledgers for some time. Then he bought the cheapest thing on the table that still had a proper sewn spine: a thick, plain notebook with a grey board cover and paper so thin that the ink of one page would show faintly through the next. It was a good deal more than he wanted to pay, and he paid it, and wrote the figure in the back of the old book under the line he had drawn that morning.

He took it back to the wall by the pump and opened it to the first page and sat there for a while with the pencil.

He had kept notebooks since he was eight, and every one of them had been the same kind of book. Things went into it as they came, in order. A bearing, a dog, a wall, a word on page seven. That had been enough, for a long time, because the things he wrote down were mostly things that would still be there tomorrow.

This was different. He could feel the difference in his hands. The bout with Renn was already less clear than it had been last night. The second probe, the one across the body, had it come off the front foot or had he only expected it to? He had been sure on the walk home. He was nearly sure now. By next week he would be sure again, in the wrong way, the way you become sure of a thing you have told yourself often enough.

He wrote, at the top of the first page, *Log*, and underlined it.

Then he wrote the first entry the way he had always written things, in sentences, and it came out like this:

*Renn. Copper Rank 3, Blade. He opens with two probes, high and across, off the front foot. The true strike loads in his back heel about a quarter of a second first. I read the heel in the second exchange and he answered with the sweep. In the third I got out of the whole figure—*

He stopped.

He read the line back. *In the third I got out of the whole figure.* It was true. He had been standing outside the figure at the end of it, and Vell had seen it, and Renn had seen it, and Lira had seen it from the rope. And the sentence sat there on the page next to the heel and the probes as though it belonged to them. It was in the same hand. It was in the same voice. Anybody reading it a year from now would think he had done the third exchange the way he had done the second, by reading and moving, and he had not. He did not know how he had done it. The sentence did not know either. It simply did not say so.

That was what sentences did, he thought. They ran on. You put a true thing in one end, and a guess came out the other in the same coat, and nobody could tell them apart.

He tore the page out. He did it carefully, along the spine, so that the binding would not loosen, and folded the page and put it in his pocket.

Then he took the straightedge from his tool roll, the little steel one he had made at twelve, and ruled the next page into three columns.

He thought about what to call them for some time. Hesk's slates in the workshop had had columns: *load*, *figure*, *result*. Those were close but not right, because nobody here was hanging weights on a rig. What he wanted was something that made each line prove itself before he let it stay. He thought about Vell at her table, and the way the yard went quiet when she spoke, and the one word she said at the end of a bout that made it finished.

He headed the columns *Claim*, *Evidence*, and *Ruling*.

A claim was what he thought was true. Evidence was what he had seen with his own eyes that made him think so, and when, and how many times. A ruling was what he would do about it next time, and it was only allowed to exist if the evidence was there. If he had not seen a thing himself, if somebody had told it to him, it could go in, but it went in with a small *h* beside it, for *heard*, so that he would always know it was a figure somebody else had traced and he had not.

*Never trust a number you can't trace back to where it came from.*

He began again.

*1. Renn. Copper R3, Blade Path (Vell's rating). Eleven bouts, eight wins (Vell, h).*

*Claim: the probes carry no weight. Evidence: off the front foot, high then across, every exchange but the last. Ruling: spend nothing on them.*

*Claim: the true strike loads in the back heel a quarter second before the blade. Evidence: first exchange, paid for with the shoulder; second exchange, moved on it and was right. Ruling: move on the heel, not on the blade.*

*Claim: he rolls his shoulders when he changes his plan. Evidence: before the first exchange, after the sweep, before the fourth. Ruling: shoulders mean a new plan. Stop expecting the old one.*

*Claim: the sweep is kept for people who have learned the heel. Evidence: second exchange, once. Ruling: one is not enough. Watch for it again.*

*Claim: the blade reaches eight inches past the fist. Evidence: Lira, h. My eye says less. Ruling: add eight until I have measured it myself.*

It took him most of the afternoon. When he had finished, he had a page and a half on a man he had fought for perhaps three minutes in all, and every line on it would hold weight. He had tried each one. He could tell exactly where each one came from.

There was nothing on it about the third exchange.

He had not left it out by deciding to. It simply had nowhere to go. He had a claim, *I got out of the figure*, and he had no evidence, because he had not seen himself do it, and he could not make a ruling, because he did not know what he would do next time. The columns would not take it. He looked at the empty place where it would have been and felt something that was partly relief and partly shame, and he could not have said which was larger.

He turned the notebook over and opened it from the back, as he always did, to the last page.

This would be the other kind of page. He knew that already. Things would go here that did not fit the columns. He made himself a rule about it, sitting on the wall with the pump dripping beside him, and the rule was this: nothing went in the back until the front was finished for the day. The facts first. Then the rest. He thought that if he could make himself do it in that order, the rest might stay a sensible size.

He wrote only one line, at the top of the last page.

*There are things that won't go in the columns. I know where they are. I am not counting them yet.*

Then he closed the Log and sat with it on his knee, and listened to the pump drip, and did not open the old notebook to the page near the back, though he knew exactly which page it was.

The pouch said he had until the eleventh day.

He did the sum on the morning of the tenth, at the kitchen counter, in small stacks of coins, twice. Torvin's week ran out tomorrow. After that the pouch would pay for three more weeks if he ate the way he had been eating, and four if he ate the way the brothers ate, which was mostly bread and whatever the river gave them. It would not pay for five.

*Find work,* he had written on the rise. He had written it second, after *find somewhere to sleep*, and he had done the first and not the second, and now the second had come round to find him.

He went down to the river workshops in the late morning, with his left arm held close, past the cobbler and the lantern woman and the cooper, to the doorway at the end of the row with no sign over it.

The mender was there. He was not straightening a mangle today. He had the clock with no hands open on his bench, its works laid out on a cloth in neat rows like a surgeon's instruments, and he was looking at them through a loupe, and he did not look up.

*IF IT'S STILL HERE*, said the doorframe.

"Something of yours broken?" said the mender, to the clock.

"Not today," said Cael. "I wanted to ask if you need anybody."

That made the man look up. He took the loupe out of his eye and looked at Cael, the same checking look he had given him over the mangle frame, and his gaze went to the left arm Cael was holding close, and then came back.

"For what?"

"Sorting. Cleaning. Truing small things." Cael thought about how to say the next part so that it would be true and not sound like boasting, and decided he could not, and said it anyway. "My grandfather makes instruments. I've worked at his bench since I was six."

The mender did not say anything to that. He reached under the bench, felt about without looking, and came up with a little brass balance of the kind a spice seller uses, with a beam no longer than a hand. The beam was bent. It was not bent much. It dipped a hair to the left at rest, so that anything weighed on it would weigh light in one pan and heavy in the other, and you would have to hold it up to the light and squint along it to see why.

He put it on the bench, and laid his straightedge beside it, and went back to his clock.

Cael looked at the balance for a long time before he touched it.

Then he did what he had watched the mender do with the mangle, and what Hesk had done at the rig for as long as he could remember. He did not guess. He laid the straightedge along the beam and found the bend, which was not in the middle, as he had expected, but a little way out along the left arm, where somebody had probably caught it on the edge of a stall. He clamped the beam lightly in the bench vice with a fold of leather between the jaws. He pressed. Not much. Then he took it out and laid the straightedge along it again, and looked, and it was better, and not right. He pressed again, less. He looked. He had gone over, very slightly. He turned it and pressed back the other way, with the pad of his thumb this time and no vice at all.

The fourth time he laid the straightedge along it, he could see no light underneath.

He hung the beam on its pivot and let it settle. The pointer swung, and slowed, and came to rest dead on the mark.

"There," he said.

The mender put down his loupe. He came round the bench and looked at the balance, and then, without saying anything, took two identical brass weights out of a drawer and set one in each pan. The beam dipped and rose and came back to the mark and stayed there.

He took the weights off. He looked at Cael's left arm again.

"Two afternoons," he said. "Not mornings. I don't talk in the mornings. By the piece, not by the hour, because I don't pay for people standing about thinking." He put the weights back in their drawer. "You'll sort what comes in, and sweep, and true the small things. You won't touch the clocks."

"I won't touch the clocks."

"And if you break something, you'll pay for it out of what I owe you, and then you'll go." He went back round the bench. "Tomorrow. After noon."

That was all. Cael stood in the doorway a moment longer and then understood that he was being allowed to leave, and left.

On the fish steps below the carriers' hut, a barge was unloading sacks of meal onto the landing, and three men were carrying them up the steps to a cart one at a time, on their shoulders. The second man up was Renn. He was in a sleeveless shirt with a sack on his right shoulder, taking the slick steps two at a time as though they were level ground, and when he reached the top he swung the sack onto the cart without breaking stride and turned to go down for the next one.

He saw Cael on the street above. He stopped on the top step.

For a moment he did not say anything, and Cael did not either. Renn looked at him the way he had looked at him across the circle in the third exchange, briefly, carefully, a man checking a part he had not cut.

"Healing?" Renn said.

"Slowly."

"Good." He went down a step, and then stopped and looked back over his shoulder. "A month," he said, and went on down to the barge.

He went to Vell's table on the fourth morning after the bout, when he could lift his left arm as high as his shoulder without his face doing anything about it.

The yard was empty except for Vell and the crows. She was at the table in the shade with her ledger squared and her tea at her elbow, exactly as she had been the morning of the terms, and when he stopped in front of her she looked at him for a while without speaking.

"Lift your left arm," she said.

He lifted it to the height of his shoulder.

"Higher."

He lifted it higher. He kept his face still. He was nearly sure he kept his face still.

Vell watched the arm, and then the face, and then the arm again. "Thursday," she said, and picked up her pen. "Not before. Renn's return stays where I put it, at the month. Between now and then you'll fight who I give you."

"Who?"

"Brenna." She wrote, a short line, without looking up. "Copper Rank 2, Shield Path. She lost to Dessa a fortnight ago in three exchanges and she hasn't stopped talking about it. She wants somebody she can finish quickly, so that people will talk about that instead." The pen stopped. Vell looked up. "If you're any use, you'll be somebody she can't finish quickly. That'll be good for both of you, and she won't thank me for it."

He nodded. He was watching the pen.

It had written a line beneath the line from four nights ago. He could see the shape of the old entry, the long one that had run well past two lines and on down the page. He could not read any of it upside down. But he could see where a name would go, at the start of each entry, and he could see that at the start of his there was no name. There was something shorter.

"You haven't written my name," he said.

Vell did not look down at the page. She seemed to have known exactly where he was looking.

"No."

"Is that because I'm unrated?"

"It's because the book doesn't need it yet." She laid the pen across the ledger. "You're in the book as what you are. Unrated. Fourteen. Vouched. Lost to Renn in four exchanges, by the flat, called. That's everything anybody coming to this book will need to know about that night. A name goes in when the yard comes to see it."

"When will that be?"

"For most of them, never," said Vell. "Most of them come and fight and go home, and the book holds what they did, and nobody ever needs to know what they were called. It's no slight. It's what a book's for." She picked up her tea. "When the yard comes to see you, I'll write it. Not before. And not because you asked."

He found, standing there, that he did not mind. He had thought he would. But he was in a book, in somebody else's careful hand, as exactly what he was and what he had done, and every word of it was something Vell had seen herself from this stool. It was a ledger he could check. It had no word in square brackets in it.

"Thursday," he said.

"After the lamps." Vell drank her tea. "Third bout. Don't be late, and don't be early either. Being early makes people think you're frightened."

He went out through the gate in the wall. On the board at the crossroads, on his way back down, he found the old result again, still pinned near the bottom, gone pale in the rain. *Dessa (Cu 5, Stone) over Brenna (Cu 2, Shield), third exchange, called.* He read it twice. He remembered two men arguing behind him in the triangle on his first afternoon, and one of them saying that Brenna could not stand that it went to three.

He took out the Log and turned to a clean page and wrote *2. Brenna*, and under it, in the first column, *wants it over quickly*, and in the second, *two men at the board, h; Vell, h*, and in the third column nothing at all.

The ruling would have to wait until Thursday. He was learning that most of them would.

# O'Connor seat 1.3.0 — Monroe Jackson

Internal seat ID: `oconnor`. Public byline: Monroe Jackson unless the owner chooses

## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,

- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —

- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 13 — Tonight's Answer

His breath came back to him slowly, a mouthful at a time, as if somebody were passing it to him through a gap in a door.

The first piece was very small and hurt all the way down. The second was larger. By the fourth he could get an elbow under himself, and then a knee. He stayed like that on his elbow and his knee in the dirt with his head hanging, and made an inventory, because an inventory was a thing to do that was not lying in the dirt.

Ribs, on the right side. Bright and deep, a hot wide band from under his arm to the bottom of his chest, and every breath went round the edge of it instead of through it. But the breaths went, and nothing grated when they did, so probably nothing was broken. Probably.

Shoulder, on the left. Duller now. Warming up, slowly, toward the kind of ache that would be worse in the morning than it was tonight.

And his left forearm. He turned it over and looked at it in the lamplight. There was a long scrape down the outside of it from wrist to elbow, crusted with dirt from the circle, stinging now that he had noticed it. He had no memory at all of getting it. He looked at it with a kind of distant, clerkly annoyance, as he might have looked at a figure in a column that he had not written and could not trace. He did not know whether he had got it in the fall at the end, or in the sweep, or somewhere in the middle of the third exchange, where his feet had been and he had not.

He did not want to think about the third exchange. He put the forearm down.

A shadow came across the lamplight. Renn was standing over him. The blade was gone, and his right hand was held out, open, at the height of Cael's face.

Cael took it. Renn pulled him up off his knee in one easy movement, with the plain strength of a man who had spent years lifting things heavier than a fourteen-year-old and did not notice the difference, and held on to his hand a moment longer than he needed to, until Cael's feet had decided to stay under him.

"Four exchanges," Renn said.

He said it flatly, as a fact for the record, the way Vell might have said it. But he was looking at Cael's face while he said it, closely.

"You know what an unrated debut usually gives me?" he said. "One. If they're brave, two. I've never had four." He glanced down at Cael's ribs, at the way Cael was standing round them, and then back up. "And that third one."

He stopped. He shook his head, once, and it was not a shake of disbelief. It was the shake of a man putting something in a drawer to take out later, when he had time to look at it properly.

"That was a real figure I threw at you," Renn said. "It doesn't miss. It missed."

Cael did not say anything, because there was nothing accurate available.

"What's your Path?"

Renn asked it straight, without any suspicion in it at all. It was a craftsman's question, one tradesman to another across a bench: *what tool did that?*

"Nothing to register," Cael said.

Renn studied him. If he thought anything about that, he kept it, except that one corner of his mouth lifted a little, as though nothing said in the yard all night had pleased him more.

"Tell Vell you want me again," he said. "Give it a month. You'll be more worth the trouble by then." He let go of Cael's hand at last. "And I want the second look."

He did not say it the way a winner says something kind to a loser. He said it plainly, and meant it plainly. It was an appointment, made and kept in his head. Then he patted Cael once on the right shoulder, the one that had not been hit, and nodded toward Vell's table, half a courtesy to Vell and half a direction to Cael, and then walked off to the rope. The crowd opened to let him through and closed again behind him and gave him back its ordinary noise.

You went to the table afterward. You reported, win or lose, and you stood there until she had finished with you. Lira had told him that on the barrel, and he went.

Vell was writing.

He stood in front of the table and waited, holding his right arm against his side, and watched the pen. He had seen her record the end of a bout four times now, from the barrel and the rope: the names, the result, the exchange, anything owed. Two lines, mostly. Three for the Bronze pair, because of the concession.

Her pen was well past two lines. It moved, and stopped, and moved again. It did not move the way a pen moves when someone is copying down something they already know. It moved the way Cael's own pencil moved when he was trying to put down something he had seen before it faded, choosing each word, crossing nothing out because there was nothing yet to cross out.

Once, without lifting her head, she raised her eyes and looked at him.

It was a flat look, and unreadable, and very thorough. It was the look of someone going over a sum she did not yet trust. It stayed on him for a breath, and then it went back to the page, and the pen wrote a little more.

She blotted the page and closed the ledger on it. Whatever she had written stayed inside.

"Result stands as called," said Vell. "Renn by the flat, fourth exchange." She laid her hand on the cover of the book. "He's asked for a return at a month. I'm inclined to give it him. Come and see me when you've healed, and we'll talk about terms."

"Yes."

She looked at him for a moment, and it was very nearly something other than the flat look, and then it was the flat look again.

"You lasted longer than the book said you would," she said.

Over by the rope, Marrow was wiping his slate clean with the side of his hand, without any expression at all.

"Is that good?" Cael said.

"It's what happened," said Vell, and she squared the ledger to the edge of the table, and looked past him at whatever came next, and he understood that he had been dismissed.

Halfway back across the yard it came to him that three people had watched him move tonight, Renn across the circle and Vell from her stool and Lira at the rope, and that he had not decided to show any of them anything. It had happened in front of them anyway.

In the Cinder House yard, Vell did not open the ledger again until the last of the crowd had gone.

That took most of an hour. The crowd went first, in a long slow drain out through the gate, still arguing about the second bout, which had gone the way the book said it would and had therefore been a disappointment to nobody but the loser. The pie boy went next, with his tray empty and his pockets heavy.

Marrow came and stood at the end of her table for a minute, as he did every night, and told her what the evening had taken, and she wrote the figure where she always wrote it, in the back, and did not ask him how he had come by it, because a long time ago she had decided that Marrow's book was Marrow's business and the circuit's book was hers, and that the two must never be allowed to touch.

"The debut," Marrow said pleasantly. "That cost me."

"Did it."

"Everybody had him at one." He smiled. He had a very good smile and he knew exactly how much it was worth. "Over pays long, on a debut. I didn't lay it off."

"Then you'll lay it off next time," said Vell, and did not look up, and after a moment Marrow went away.

The boy from the public house came out with a pole and began taking the lanterns down from their hooks, one at a time, starting at the far wall, so that the yard went dark in stages, from the outside in. Vell sat on her stool and let him. When there were only three lanterns left, the ones nearest her, she opened the ledger at the page and read what she had written.

*Renn (Cu 3, Blade) over unrated debut (14; nothing to register; vouched, L.), fourth exchange, by the flat. Called.*

That was the result. There was no name in it. The book did not carry the names of unrated debuts, only what they were; a name went into the book when the yard had a reason to need it, and most debuts never gave it one. It was two lines. It was all the book required, and it was all anybody coming to the book in a year, or ten years, would need in order to know what had happened in the yard tonight and who owed whom what.

Under it she had kept writing.

*First exchange: debut gave ground on the curve, did not answer. Took the flat on the left shoulder off the true strike. Stayed up.*

*Second exchange: moved on R.'s rear heel, before the strike. Early. Read it. R. answered with the sweep; debut cleared it, was offered the opening after, saw it (I watched his eyes), did not take it.*

*Third exchange: R. threw the full figure.*

She stopped there, as she had stopped when she was writing it, with the yard full and the boy standing in front of her holding his ribs.

She had kept this book, or one very like it, for longer than some of her fighters had been alive. Before that she had fought in yards like this one, and in better ones, for long enough to reach a rating nobody gave lightly and a body that told her about it every wet morning. She had seen, at a rough count she had once made on a winter night with nothing better to do, something over four thousand bouts. She had called nearly all of them. She did not remember most of them, and that was as it should be. The book remembered them. That was what the book was for.

She remembered Renn's figure.

She had watched him build it, over two seasons, a piece at a time. The first time he threw it, it had not been finished, and a woman with a Shield Path had walked through the middle of it and put him down. The second time it had been nearly finished. By the end of last season it had been finished, and she had watched him throw it nine times since, and she had written it down nine times.

Nine times the third strike had come up the lane, and nine times somebody had been standing in the lane, because that was where the first two strikes put you. That was the whole of its design. It did not need you to guess wrong. It caught you where guessing right had sent you.

She had written it down on a night in the summer against the name of the Wind girl, the one who had stood in front of her table tonight with her chin up and said *I do.* The girl had read the first two strikes as cleanly as Vell had ever seen anyone read them, and the third had put her in the dirt, and she had got up, and come back the next week, and the week after that.

Tonight it had not put anybody in the dirt.

Vell had been looking straight at it. She looked at every third exchange straight, because the third was where fighters stopped asking questions and told you what they meant, and it was where things happened that the book needed to know about. She had watched the boy's eyes, because a reader's eyes went to the place he was reading, and she had seen him go to Renn's heel in the second exchange like a man going to a door he knows. And in the third exchange she had watched his eyes go to the heel again. She had watched the heel settle.

And then the boy had not been where the strike was. And his eyes had still been on the heel. He had moved before they had left it.

She did not know what she had seen. She had sat at the table with the pen in her hand and the boy standing in front of her, and she had known that she did not know, and she had known that the book did not care whether she knew. The book cared what had happened.

There was a rule she kept for herself, which was not written down anywhere, because there was nowhere it belonged. She had made it on the night she called the Ash Path man for the third time, and he did not stop, and a woman had been carried out of this yard to wait for a healer from across the river.

Vell had written that entry afterward, and it had run to a page and a half, because she had written down everything she had seen, in order, including the two calls before and how she had made them and what she had seen in his face when he did not stop. And when people came to the table in the weeks after, wanting to know whether she had called it too late, or too early, or whether she had a grudge, she had opened the book and turned it round and let them read.

Nobody had argued with the page and a half. The circuit had survived it, and so had she.

*Write what you saw,* the rule went. *Not what you understood. If you only write what you understand, the book is only as honest as you are clever, and nobody is that clever.*

She dipped the pen.

*Defender off the line before R.'s strike began. Not a read. I watched for the read and there was none. Eyes still on the heel. Off the second strike the same. Was not in the lane for the third. A hand's width.*

*Debut threw one punch, untrained. R. turned it with the forearm.*

*Fourth exchange: R. threw the figure without probes. Debut read the first fold, part of the second, and nothing after. Down by the flat, right side. Got up.*

She read it back. It was eight lines more than the book needed, and every one of them was a thing she had seen with her own eyes from this stool, and not one of them said what any of it meant.

That was correct. She did not know what it meant. Neither, she thought, did the boy. She had looked at him while she wrote, with the yard still noisy round them, and she had seen a fourteen-year-old holding his ribs and waiting, as he had been told to wait, with nothing in his face at all.

Most of them had something in their faces at the table, after a first bout: shame, or fury, or a grin they could not stop, or the dazed look of somebody who had not known until tonight what it was to be hit by a person who meant it. He had had nothing. He had had the look of somebody who was keeping very still around something, so that it would not spill.

She thought about the margin, where she had written *nothing to register* that morning. She had heard that phrase before, now and then, from people who did not want to say their Path out loud in a yard, and it usually meant a bad draw they were ashamed of. It usually did not mean anything she needed to know.

She thought about him saying the terms back to her.

She had made a great many people say the terms back to her, over the years. Most of them said them in her words, in her order, as fast as they could, like children saying a lesson. He had said them in his own, slowly, as if he were checking each one before he set it down, and when he had finished she had known that he had heard every word and weighed it, and had decided to come in anyway. That was rarer than talent. She had seen a great deal of talent.

And she thought about the Wind girl.

Four months. Four bouts lost and then a long run that had made people start betting on her, and in all that time she had not vouched for anybody, though Vell knew for a fact she had been asked. Tonight she had stood at the table and said *I do* as if she were afraid somebody would take the words out of her mouth before she could get them said. *Because he stood in front of the board for eleven minutes. And when I asked him what he was, he told me.*

Vell had not asked what he was. She had decided, in the morning, that she did not need to know.

She thought now that she had been right not to ask, and that she would not ask, and that she was going to watch very carefully all the same.

There was one more line to write, and it was not one the book required, and she wrote it anyway, at the bottom of the entry, smaller, in the space she used for things that belonged to her and not to the circuit.

*Lasted four. Marrow's slate said one. Match him with care. Not soft. Against people who'll teach him something.*

She blotted it, and closed the ledger, and sat for a moment with her hand flat on the cover in the light of the last three lanterns.

The boy from the public house was waiting with his pole.

"Go on," Vell said, and he took the last lanterns down, one, two, three, and the yard went dark, and she picked up the book and her stool and went in.

---

An hour earlier, while Vell's yard was still loud round her, Lira had come off the rope and fallen in beside Cael at the gate without saying anything, and they had gone out together into the dark of the district.

The lanterns in the lanes were few and far apart, and between them the dark was real dark. By the time they had gone two corners the noise of the yard had fallen away, and the only sounds were their own boots and somebody's dog and, far off, the river.

Cael walked carefully, round his ribs. Lira walked beside him at exactly his pace, which was slower than hers, and did not remark on it.

She managed a block and a half.

"You almost had him in the second exchange." The words went out into the dark ahead of them, not to him. "After the sweep went past you. He was down and twisted round and facing you square, for half a beat. Put a hand on him there and it counts. Even with arms as short as yours. I nearly bit through my knuckle."

"I wasn't trying to win. I was trying to learn how he moves."

Lira went quiet for three steps. He could feel her changing her mind beside him; it had a kind of temperature, like walking next to a stove that has just been damped down.

"Oh. That's actually smarter." It came out in a different voice altogether.

"He opens everything with the same two probes." The bout was still in him, laid down in clean layers like the pages of a notebook, and saying it to her was also saying it to himself. "High, then across. Off the front foot. There's no weight in them. They're for looking. The real strike loads off the back heel first. He sits into it. A quarter of a second, maybe less by the end. He rolls his shoulders when he changes his plan; once after the sweep, and once before the end. And the sweep's what he throws at someone who's just learned to read the heel. He was waiting for me to get clever."

"He was," Lira agreed.

"And when he stops testing you and just comes," said Cael, "that's the one to be afraid of. That was the last exchange."

"That," said Lira, with some feeling, "was the last exchange."

She had watched it from the rope. He could hear in her voice that she had found it hard to watch.

Then she stopped walking.

He went on two more steps before he noticed, and stopped too, and turned round. She was standing under one of the few lanterns, in the middle of the lane, with her hands at her sides. She had stopped in the way people stop when the conversation they have been having has finally caught up with the one they wanted.

"The third exchange," Lira said.

He waited.

"I told you on the roof that I wouldn't tell you what it looked like. That figure. Because knowing wouldn't help." She did not move. "I'll tell you now. The whole thing exists for the third cut. The first two don't care whether you read them. They're not trying to hit you. They're herding you. If you read the first one right, you go where it sends you. If you read the second one right, you go where *that* sends you. And the place they send you is the lane the third one comes up." She said it with no particular feeling, as if she were reading out a measurement. "I read the first two perfectly, my second month. I've never read anything better. And the third one put me on my back, looking at the lamps. Everybody I've ever seen him throw it at has ended up looking at the lamps."

Her eyes were on his face. He could see them catching the lantern light.

"Tonight it went past a boy on his first night in the circle. By a hand." She tilted her head. "Somewhere after the second cut, you went where that figure doesn't send people. So I've got one question, and it's small, and I think it's fair."

He knew what it was before she asked it. He had known since the table.

"How?"

He had walked all the way from the yard without getting an answer ready, and he knew that had been on purpose. Getting one ready would have meant turning round to face the thing and measuring it, and here in the dark he could admit, a little, that he did not want to. There was nothing to measure it with. In the fourth exchange he had felt for it and his hand had closed on air.

He could not have shown it to her. He could not have done it again here, between these walls, because she asked, or told her what it was built from, or promised her it would ever come back. It was one stone in a heap he could otherwise count, and it would not let itself be counted, and whenever his thoughts drifted toward it they discovered urgent business elsewhere.

"Instinct," Cael said.

It was true, as far as it went. It stopped about a hand's width short of the truth.

Lira looked at him for a long moment. He was learning that she had a very exact sense of when somebody had given her a real answer, or a false one, or a true answer with its legs sawn short. He suspected she had another sense besides, a rarer one, for when to send the bill for the difference and when to let it wait.

"All right," she said. "Instinct."

She started walking again, and he fell in beside her. She let three streets go by, the lanterns coming and going over them, before she said anything else, and when she did it was lightly, in the voice of someone making a note in a book of her own.

"That's tonight's answer," said Lira. "I'm writing down that it was tonight's."

---

They walked.

The district went about its late business round them: shutters going up, lamplight under doors, somebody in a window two streets over arguing cheerfully about the odds on something. A cart went by with its lamp swinging. A woman on a doorstep called a cat in by a name Cael did not catch, and the cat did not come.

His body had sorted its pains into their long arrangements by then, ribs and shoulder and the stinging forearm, so that every breath was a small negotiation.

He had lost, and it was in the book in two lines, and he had not touched Renn once. But he had walked out of the circle owning the working of one man, and bruises healed, and what he had bought with them would not. A month from now, at Vell's table, he meant to have every piece of it ready.

Nearly all of it.

There was one thing from the evening he did not sort. He did not even pick it up. He let it lie where it had fallen, whole and unexamined, in a corner of himself where he would know it was there without having to look at it. He had been doing that with a turnip cart since the road, and now there were two things in the corner, and he did not let himself count them, because counting them would have been the first step toward a word.

So he went home at Lira's side through the cooling dark: fourteen, beaten in four exchanges, his shoulder and his ribs charging him for every step. And he was certain, quietly and against all the evidence of the book, that he had won something tonight that nobody on the rope had been watching for.

At the corner by the well with the iron cover, Lira stopped.

"I go this way," she said, with a nod at the dark beyond the well that could have meant any of four lanes. Then she looked at him, at the way he was holding his side. "Torvin's has a yard with a pump. Put the cold on the ribs before you lie down, or you won't get up in the morning. Not too long. Count it."

"How long?"

"You'll know how long." She almost smiled. "You count everything."

"Lira."

She had already turned. She turned back.

"You didn't lose your name tonight," he said. "Did you?"

Lira looked at him in the dark for a moment. Then she laughed, short and real, the laugh from the yard in the morning that had surprised her.

"No," she said. "Marrow did. He'd laid nothing off on the over." She walked backward a few steps down the lane, without looking behind her, without hitting anything. "Go and put your ribs under the pump, Cael. I'll see you at the yard."

And she was gone into the dark, and he stood at the corner by the well for a moment longer, holding his side, and then went home.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

  reader-facing preference, or when the owner states the preference directly.
- Do not learn voice from continuity fixes, factual corrections, typographical
  repairs, rejected suggestions, reverted edits, or untouched AI prose.

  movement. Learn the difference between WAS and NOW, not the example's subject.

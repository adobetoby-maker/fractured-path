# Chapter 34 — Maps with Holes

It was that same night, the ninety-sixth going into the ninety-seventh, and Seln had the south.

He stood forty yards from the lamp, in grass to his hip, where its light did not reach him and his own eyes were not spoiled by it. He had the line on his left hand. He could not see it. He knew where it was by a strip of burnt stems that began ten yards from his boot, and by a thing behind his breastbone that had gone out at a stone on the way in, and had been there again at the same stone on the way out, exactly where he had left it.

He had called a word up a hill tonight, at the top of his voice, and he could not remember when he had last raised it.

The camp was quiet behind him. He listened to it as he listened to any room that he had his back to, and sorted what he heard: a pencil, and then no pencil; a big man washing his hands without a sound; and the healer's breathing, which was slow and went all the way down.

"Seln. Say yours."

The boy had asked it an hour ago, across the lamp.

He turned that over in the dark, since he had the leisure. He had said once, at a fire in a meadow, that he kept waiting for someone to ask for his report. For fifteen years his reports had been asked for by a slot in a door; for a hundred days after that they had been asked for by nobody, and he had gone on walking the first hour of every road, alone, and saying what he found or not as it bore.

Tonight he had been asked, by name, for the first time. And the whole of what he had to hand in was one word.

It was an accurate word. He had submitted a great many accurate reports, and few of them had sat so ill.

He knew why. He was the cut; he had told a girl so in a loft, in so many words. The cut is the man placed where the subject will run, and he is never where the blow falls. At a ford he had lain in a pool for an hour and a quarter while four people were bled on a bar of gravel thirty yards from his face. And tonight, while a hound took a girl's arm on a white floor, he had been two hundred yards away with his fingers in the lashings of a mule's pack.

It had been right. A bow at the lip had been worth more than a second knife, and he would have gone again. He noted only that it came out the same each time, and that somebody else always paid. He had no column for that, and he found, a little to his surprise, that he would have liked one.

There was the other thing, and he came to it when he had finished with himself, as he came to the difficult page of any file.

He had been looking straight back up the lane. That was his place; the man on point looks behind him as often as before. He had seen the small one go down, and a grey animal step out between her and his knife, and beyond those the two women, and the box. Behind the box there had been a boy with a blade in his hand.

Then there had been the lane, and the grass, and the box.

Seln had not seen him go. Nobody sees that. It is the whole of the craft that there is no instant at which a person is seen to leave; the eye passes over the place and finds it not worth resting on, and goes to something with more in it. He had learned it on a back stair when he was younger than any of them. It had taken him a year to do on purpose, and three more to stop doing by accident whenever a door slammed.

He had known it this time from the outside, by two signs. A big man's eye had slid over a piece of air and come back to it. And along the south edge of the lane the grass had leaned, stem by stem, with nothing leaning on it.

It had gone on for ten strides. Then there had been a boy with both hands on a hound.

Seln had done the sum himself, in the half-breath before. It was the first thing that a person of his trade did on entering any room, and he had done it without choosing to: who is here, and what can each of them see, and where will each of them be tomorrow. There were four who had known for years and had been tried. There was a healer, who was owed everything and knew nothing, and who walked seven roads. She had her back turned and a hundredweight of copied stone between.

He would have said *go*. He had not needed to. The boy had stood there for as long as a jaw takes to shut, doing the same sum, and had got the same answer; and he had lifted it, and carried it exactly as far as it was wanted, and put it down with his hands already full.

It was correct. Seln looked for a better word and did not find one.

He had wondered, since a night when the boy told him the whole of it in order, what it would be to see his own stair in another person's feet. He had supposed that he would feel robbed. He considered the matter now with some care, standing in frost with a locked case against his ribs, and what he found was nearer to what a mason feels who passes a wall and sees that somebody has laid a course as he would have laid it.

He did not propose to say so to anyone.

The seal was a stranger matter. It guarded the place where a boy had learned that craft, and the place was a man. Seln had spent fifteen years keeping other people's secrets in drawers; he had not looked to end by being one himself, kept shut on a hillside by a person of eighteen who had weighed a friend's throat against it and had still stopped to count who was looking.

---

The stars went over, and the frost came down, and toward the end of the watch he let himself think about the iron.

He had been keeping off it since noon. It had stood at the back of everything that he did that day, as a figure stands at the back of a room: a square bar, driven at the far end of a measure, with a stamp on its neck that he had seen on every door he had walked through for fifteen years.

Thirty-one.

He had told the boy that he did not know what the number counted, and that he would not guess. Both of those were true. There was a third thing which he had not said, because it was not yet a thing: something in him had answered to that iron, and it was not the Shadow.

He carried what he had carried as a careful clerk carries it, in drawers, each one shut. It was how a man could bear fifteen years of other people's paper and still sleep. But one of them, at the stake, had shifted on its runners, and all day it had stood a finger's breadth open, with nothing to be seen in it but dark.

The big man came down out of the north when the east had begun to grey, walking as a person walks who has stood since midnight, with a bow in his two hands and a rag round one of them.

"Yours is over," said Brom. "Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Same. I'd sooner there'd been something. I'd know where it was."

"That is very well put," said Seln, and meant it.

He did not go to his blanket. He went along the line to the north, with the burnt strip on his right hand and then the unburnt grass, counting Brom's turfs as he came to them. They lay grass downward, each a cord from the last, and the frost had made them grey. At the third he stopped.

It stood where it had stood. He crouched over it without touching it, as he had crouched at noon, and waited for the light.

It came slowly. First there was a bar, and then the head of it, spread by a mallet. Then there was the stamp.

He did not read the stamp; he knew what it said. He let his eye rest on it as one lets a hand rest on a latch. A man answers for what he takes: he had said that at noon. Iron of that pattern is counted out across a board by a storekeeper who does not look up, and signed for in a book with ruled columns, and carried away in a case with a strap.

A case with a strap.

The drawer came open.

It was a tin, as long as his forearm, with a cap at one end and a lead tag crimped through the hasp. He had it under his arm. He was young, in the memory, with a satchel and one key, and he was carrying the tin from one room to another; that was the whole of the errand. A junior was permitted to break the tag in the second room, before a senior, and to draw out what was in the tin and count it against a docket. He was to count. He was not to read.

There had been eight sheets. They were fair copies, on paper backed with linen, and they were of the east.

He had not read them. But nobody can count eight sheets, corner by corner, and see nothing. He had seen a ruled stroke down the left of each that he took for the Line. He had seen roads beyond it, drawn in dashes, as they were drawn on the printed sheet that lay in a box at the camp; only these had run on much farther before they stopped.

And on some of the sheets, out in the white, there had been a round.

It was a small exact circle, ruled with a compass. He remembered the prick of the point at its middle, where the paper had been dented. There was nothing inside it at all; a dashed road came up to one and went about it and on. At the foot of each sheet there had been a little table of signs, a ford, a hold, a hill, and no sign among them for a round with nothing in it. On one sheet there were two.

He had counted eight, and said *eight*, and the senior had initialled the docket; and a young man with a satchel had gone down a stair thinking about his dinner.

He had taken those rounds for what a junior takes everything for, which is a thing that he has not been told. He had supposed a post, or a store, with a guard on it. It had never once occurred to him that a place might be left off a map because of what the ground itself did there.

Seln stayed on his heels by the iron a long while, and went over what he had, strictly, as he would have gone over it for somebody else's signature. He had eight sheets, seen once, by a boy who was counting. He had rounds, ruled, with a road going about one of them. He had a stake under his eyes, of the pattern that is signed for, driven on the edge of a circle that a researcher had paced with her feet.

He did not have a leaf to put beside the sheets. He had never seen one. He knew the weight of a tin and the number of its sheets, and he knew that he would be asked for more than that before the sun was high, by a small person with a pencil and no voice.

He could not tell, from memory, where on those sheets a round had fallen. He had the look of one: a water crossed, and dashes running on east of it, and then the white place. To say more than that he needed a ford drawn by somebody who had put a wheel through it.

He stood up, and his knees cracked in the cold.

The camp was stirring when he came down the line. The healer lay as she had fallen. The girl was awake, with her eyes on the sky, and the boy sat beside her with a book shut on his knee and one arm held in his lap like a thing that belonged to somebody else. Karis was sitting up against the box. She had her hand flat on its lid, and he knew that she had not yet opened it to look at eleven sheets, and why.

He went down on one knee in front of her, so that she should not have to lift her chin.

"I should like the carter's map," said Seln. "And the charcoal, if it came east in that box."

---

By the time the sun stood straight overhead, Karis had a horn cup in both hands and was finding out whether she could drink.

She did it a mouthful at a time, with her chin down and a look of sharp inquiry, as she would have tried a new pen. Across the front of her throat, where there had been a mark the colour of a plum at first light, there was something yellow and old.

The healer had named the hour herself. She had woken with the sun and eaten the sixth of a loaf that lay by her head, and slept again, on purpose, for two hours more; then she had taped Lira's foot without a word, and sat down against a pannier with her eyes on the top of the sky. She would do her two when it was straight up, she said. They had all told her that it was the hour at which those animals had never been seen about, and if she was to shout twice more she would do it then.

The throat had taken her a quarter of an hour. She had sat back from it on her heels, with both hands hanging, and given her judgment to the grass.

"Days. Hoarse before it's anything."

Karis had reached for her pencil at once.

"No. Don't write it. I can see what you want to know. I've taken the swelling down and started the rest, and that's all there was to do. The voice comes back by itself, rough first, and you'll leave it be until it does. You don't whisper. A whisper's harder on it than talking." She had flexed her fingers. "That's one."

Brom was carrying the pan back to the panniers now. He had it by the rim, in the hand that she had done, and he held it out in front of him and watched four of his own fingers under it as he went, as a farmer watches a new man at a job. That had been her second. He had come to her on his knees straight after the throat, with the rag already unwound, and Cael had seen the whole of it from the grass.

The hand was the pads of four fingers laid open by a string in the dark, bled into the linen and dried there, and she soaked the linen off with the pan's water and a corner of the lantern's oil, slowly, while Brom looked at the scrub. When it was off she turned the hand over in the light and said, "String," as a verdict, and put her palms round it.

Shorter than the throat; less than half, by the floor. The fingers, when she opened her hands, had four dark lines across the pads where there had been four open mouths, and Brom closed the hand and opened it.

"Nothing on the string for three days," Oryn said. "Span it with the stirrup and your foot, like a man who's had his fingers opened once, which you are now. You'll not lose the lines. That's two."

"The hundredth," said Brom. "I'll know when."

"I know you will. I've met you."

That had been a little while ago. She had been grey when she woke, and she was grey again; a Tide on a road had two in her, and she had spent the whole of yesterday's on one arm.

She did not get up. She looked across the grass at Cael's left leg, where a strip of grey blanket had gone dark in the night, and jerked her chin at it.

"Who tied that?"

Karis lifted one skinned hand.

"It's a good knot, and it's the wrong cloth, and it'll serve. Don't take it off. Lift the edge for me."

He lifted the edge. She leaned, with her hands on her own knees, and looked under the wool from a foot away.

"That opened last night. It's half shut. I can see it drawing together from both ends, clean, with nothing doing it, the same as those ribs." She sat back. "You've got what a carter gets, and you've got it on already."

"You've not read it," said Lira.

She had come up on her good elbow. The healer turned her head.

"I've read him three times."

"Not since the hill. You've had a night's sleep, and you've seen more of him than you had. Put your hands on him."

"I've seen him run up a hill. That's not the same as reading him."

She came over all the same, on her knees, since she had been asked plainly. She took his right wrist, which had nothing wrong with it but the cold, and laid two fingers on the side of his neck, and her lips began to move.

He counted with her, as he had counted on a mountain, and twice on a scrubbed table. *One. Two.* He knew now what he had not known on any of those days. He had stood on a hill in the dark with every one of the nine going out of him at once, and she had been standing on a lane below him with her eyes open.

*Five. Six.*

At six her lips stopped. He saw the old crease come between her brows; she had the look of a person trying a door that she knows to be locked, for the sake of having tried it.

"I can't mend what I can't read. That's the rule."

"Again," said Lira.

"No." Oryn let go of him, and wiped her hands on her breeches. "And I don't say a finding four times to a patient and change it on the fifth."

Her eyes came up to his, level and tired and a little hungry, as they had come up once over three broken ribs. She put no question to him of any kind. She pressed the edge of the blanket strip down with her thumb, as she pressed the strip on her own heel, and left Karis's knot where it was.

---

Seln came for them in the afternoon.

He had been at the stake since first light, by himself, with a calfskin and a stick of charcoal and a sheet of Karis's clean paper. Cael had watched him from the camp: a narrow dark figure three cords up the line, sitting on its heels, that drew a little and then sat for a long while.

"I have something to show," he said. "I should like it shown where the iron is. It is three cords, and she may take my arm."

They went at Lira's pace. She walked it flat, in the great boot, with her right hand on Seln's sleeve and her left tied across her breast; and Cael came behind on a leg that gave him eleven strides and then shut like a fist, as it had done once before, on a green rock in a river. He stood until it let go. It was a count that he knew.

The healer had come too. She had not been asked. She looked at the square brown bar in the grass, and at the stamp on its neck, for about as long as she had looked at five people under a rock; and then she let herself down beside it, by stages, and sat with her knees up and the iron against the small of her back.

"That's cold," she said. She did not move.

"Three in two days," Oryn said, after a while, to the country.

He waited.

"The arm. That was last night, and it was one, and it was the size of two. The throat. The hand. Three mendings in two days." She turned her hands over on her knees and looked at them, and let them lie. "That's a route. Near enough, that's what I carry between a hold and the next one, for the people who feed me and shoe the mule. Three serious hurts and a road's worth of small ones. And I've spent it on a road two days from the nearest roof, on a crew that's not on my circuit, sitting against a piece of iron somebody drove where they'd measured to."

Cael sat across from her, with his leg out, and listened.

"The top gate of my round's eleven days north of here," said Oryn. "That's by the road, at her pace." She tipped her head toward the camp, and the mule. "I'm not telling you for thanks. I've watched you. You count everything that's put in front of you, and that's a figure nobody would have put there."

He had been counting, since a far lip, what the day had cost, and he had supposed the count complete. He had not had this. A woman had added a stop to the only thing she owned, for two reasons of her own, and seven gates that had never been asked were paying for it.

"I'd not have had it," he said. "I have it now."

"Good. Don't do anything with it. Just have it."

She put her head back against nothing, and shut her eyes.

---

Seln had laid the calfskin on the turf, with a stone on each corner, where the stake's shadow did not fall across it.

"I shall say first what this is worth," he said. "It is one man's memory of a thing that he was told not to read. I carried a case once, between two rooms. There were eight sheets in it, of the country east of the Line. I counted them. I saw what a person sees who is counting."

He took up the sheet of paper. It was thin stuff, the kind that Karis had rubbed stones with, and when he laid it over the hide the carter's brown ink showed through it faintly.

"I have traced three things from his map, and no more. Here is the first water; those are his three strokes. Here is his square with a dot, which is the hold we slept at. And here is his day's measure, a tick to a day, so that what I draw shall be on his scale and not on mine."

He lifted the sheet away and laid it on his knee.

"The rest I have put in from memory, with the paper off the skin, so that I could not be led. On the sheets that I counted, a road went out from the Line in dashes. It crossed a water. It ran on east. And at a certain distance past the crossing, the dashes went round a white place, ruled with a compass, that had nothing in it."

He had drawn it in charcoal. Cael saw a row of short strokes going across the thin paper from left to right, and two days beyond a small square they bent, and went about a neat black ring, and came back.

Seln laid the paper down on the calfskin again, and fitted the three wavy strokes to the three beneath.

Nobody spoke.

There was a pencilled ring on the hide, where Karis had drawn it on the ninety-second evening, round a patch of bare skin that a carter had left when he bent his road. The charcoal ring lay on it. It was a little the larger. They had one middle.

"They surveyed it," Seln said.

His voice was as mild as it had ever been, and Cael had known him two years and had never heard it so cold.

"Five years ago by the stake, maybe six. They paced the circles and stamped the stakes and drew the maps with holes where the circles are. That's not ignorance. Ignorance doesn't stamp iron."

"Circles," said Lira. "You said circles. More than this."

"There was a sheet with two. I cannot tell you where the second lay, or the third, if there was a third. I counted corners." His eyes went to the two small rings on the hide beside which Karis had written *guess*, and came away. "I shall not promote a guess of hers on a memory of mine."

Brom was looking at the iron, behind the healer's back.

"Who's *they*? You keep saying it. The registry's a room full of clerks. A clerk didn't walk two days past a fence and drive that in."

There was a word that not one of the five had said aloud since they crossed the Line, and Cael saw him decide to say it.

"The Compact knows what quiet ground does."

It lay on the afternoon like the iron itself. Cael looked at the stamp, a thumb's breadth below the beaten head.

"And it's filed somewhere we can't reach."

Karis had the notebook on her knee. She wrote one word, large, and turned it to him.

*Where*

"No."

He said it gently.

"I carried the maps. I never saw the file. I'm not going to draw you one."

She looked at him for a long breath. Cael knew that look as well as he knew his own hand: she was weighing a source. Then she turned the leaf back toward herself and wrote under her question, and held it up again for all of them.

*Entered as said*

She was not finished. The pencil stayed over the paper, and came down, and it made three words, each with a stop after it, one beneath another, as she set down the columns of a table.

*Blank. Stamped. Filed.*

Cael read them twice. They were the whole of it, and there was not a verb in them that reached past what six people had seen with their eyes. A map had a blank where this place was. A bar of iron had a stamp.

He had been ruling a line since a wash, through a query and a posting and a stake. He had it now with a fourth point on it, and for the first time he could see which way along it he was looking; and it ran west.

He took the cream sheet out of Hesk's book.

It had ridden there since the ninety-first morning, folded once, between a letter in a narrow upright hand and a leaf out of a ledger. He opened it on his knee, and they heard it again as Pike had given it to a meadow: the recovery, and the instruments, and the rise where a road left its line. A hundred marks, in words, and again in a bracket.

"There weren't any," said Brom. "Instruments. We looked."

"We looked very thoroughly," said Seln. "I have been asking myself since last night what a paper is for that sends a crew to fetch a thing which is not there."

"Somebody got it wrong," said Lira. "Somebody thought they'd left them."

"A person who writes a sum twice, so that it cannot be altered, does not forget where he has put a brass level." Seln held out his hand for the sheet, and when he had it he held it up unread to the afternoon, as he had held it to a lamp, with the light behind. "I said in that inn that it had been written by somebody who learned to write inside. I said that it was not a reason against going. I should have gone on, and asked what it was a reason for."

"And what is it?"

"It is a question. It is put to this hillside, and the hillside cannot write. So it is put to whoever will walk here for a hundred marks; and there is a clerk at a landing, with ink on his cuffs, who has been told to see what is brought down and send word of it west by the next boat." He lowered the paper. "The word that he sends does not need to have a brass level in it. It needs only to say what became of the crew."

Cael saw it then, as he saw a seam in a bout when it was already past mending. A paper had asked whether anybody who went into that ring would come out of it. He had carried the answer down a hill himself, with a light.

"We just told them," Seln said. "The report goes west on the next ferry. It'll say we came out."

"It'll say what Pike writes," said Brom slowly. "He writes what was done. Went in, came out, brought nothing. No price."

"That is all that it needs to say."

"It'll not say how," said Lira. She was looking at Cael, and then she was looking at the grass. "Nobody saw how but us."

"No. That is the one thing in this that I like."

The healer had opened her eyes. She had sat through all of it against a bar of cold iron, and she had the face of a person who has listened to a family argue about a will.

"What does he write? The one-armed man. How many?"

"Six went in," said Cael.

"Six is wrong. I'm a stop."

"They'll write six," Seln said.

She thought about that. Then she shut her eyes again, and whatever she made of it she kept.

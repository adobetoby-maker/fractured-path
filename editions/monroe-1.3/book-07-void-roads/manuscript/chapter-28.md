# Chapter 28 — Sixty-Two Cords

That same night, in the second watch, Karis went back to the stone.

She did not go alone, since Seln had made a rule and she had lost no time in seeing the sense of it. Lira had the watch with her. Lira sat down in the grass ten yards short of the place, with her back turned and her knife across her knees, to look at everything that was not a ring of stones; and Karis went on the last ten yards by herself, with a notebook, and a pencil, and no lamp.

There could be no lamp. She had lit one in a yard two evenings ago and been absurdly glad of it, and she would not have set a point here for all the paper in Ternhall. She knelt down in the frozen grass on the western side of the stone and laid the notebook open on her knee. The stars were out. She could see the white of the page and not much else, and she resolved to write large, one line to a leaf, and sort it by daylight.

First she called her sigil.

She had been taught to do it at fourteen, by attending to a place behind the breastbone, and it had been years since she had needed to do it on purpose; it was simply there when she wanted it, as a clerk is there behind a counter. It came now. It hung at the edge of her sight where it had always hung, small and steady and yellow-white, and it had about it the particular quality of attention that she had never been able to describe to anybody who did not have one.

She looked at it for a good while. Then she stood up, and stepped over the stone.

It went out.

There was no flicker in it, and no fading. She had half expected a lamp turned down, and it was a lamp that had never been in the room. She stood a stride inside the line with her heart going hard, and looked for the place behind her breastbone, and found the place. There was nothing in it.

She stepped back, and it was there. It hung where it always hung, with the same steadiness, as though it had been waiting behind a door and had not noticed that the door was shut.

Karis did it nine times.

She counted them, and at each crossing she made herself attend to one thing only, as she would have made a student attend. The third time she watched for the instant, and it had no width that she could find. The fifth time she went over slowly, a hand's breadth at a time, to learn whether it dimmed; it did not, and then it was gone.

The seventh time she tried to hold it. She fixed the whole of her will on that small bright mark and carried it forward as one carries a full cup, and it went out between one heartbeat and the next without the least sense that anything had been taken from her. That was the part she liked least. She had not been robbed. She had merely looked down and found her hands empty.

After the ninth she knelt again, on the near side, and thought about emptiness, since she had lately become something of an authority.

She had been empty for nine days. She had spent every point she owned on a mountain in eight seconds, and afterward she had not been able to strike a flint. But in all those nine days the sigil had been there. She had called it on the first night under the pale rock, and it had come, faithful as ever, and when she asked it for a point it had simply had nothing to give her. That had been a counter with a clerk behind it and an empty till.

This was a different thing altogether. A stride beyond that stone she was three parts full; a healer had told her so with two fingers on her wrist. The till was heavy. There was no clerk, and no counter, and no room for them to stand in.

*Empty: sigil present, reserve gone.* She wrote it large, by feel. *Here: reserve present, sigil gone.* She turned the leaf. *These are not the same condition and must not be entered under one word.*

Then she set about the stride.

It was the only measurement that she could take tonight, and she took it with the stubbornness of a person who has been given one tool. From the turn of the ruts to the stone it was eighty of Cael's strides, by his count, and his were a yard. She paced it herself, there and back, and her own count was a good deal larger, as it always was. She wrote both. She paced it a second time and was one stride out, and wrote that too, and the mean of the pair.

She tried next to make the line finer than a stride, and could not.

A hand held out across the stone did nothing at all; the sigil hung where it was. She had watched the healer stand so at dusk, for a long while, and had not asked her what she felt. An arm did nothing. But when she stood astride the stone itself, with one boot on either side, she had the sigil or had it not according to how she leaned, and Karis would not write down a lean. She put: *Narrower than one stride of mine. No finer figure tonight. Wants daylight, and a second person to watch my feet.*

She sat back on her heels. The grass was stiff with frost, and beyond the stone the ground went down and up again to eleven shapes that she could only just tell from the sky.

It was very tidy, and she made herself look at that, because it was what she was afraid of.

Five people had walked up to this place in a row, an arm's length apart. She was an Ember, and Lira a Wind, and Brom an Iron Skin; there was a Shadow among them of a higher tier than any of the rest, and a Tide who had been trained in a healing house. Karis had sat at lectures on what an Arbiter was. She had copied out, in a restricted room, a good many pages on the subject, and on one point every page agreed: it was a person's own. It woke for that one person, on one morning, and named a single gift.

And five of them had gone out on the same stride.

Her pencil had come down on the page. She looked at the point of it, resting on white paper in the starlight, and she knew exactly what kind of sentence it was about to begin. It would have had *as though* in it. It would have said what the thing was like; and a likeness, set down in a notebook at midnight, is a hypothesis that has not yet had the honesty to call itself one.

She lifted the pencil.

*Five rows,* she wrote instead, and ruled them by touch. *K. L. B. S. O. Each: gone at the stone, going east. Back at the stone, coming west. One stride. Observed by all five at once on the 95th; by me alone, nine times, that night.* And under that, because she kept that sort of account as well: *I am frightened by how well it agrees. Entered.*

There were six of them on that road, and she had ruled five rows. She considered that. It was right as far as it went; one could hardly enter the stopping of a thing that had stopped years ago, on a brass plate, in front of witnesses. She left a space at the foot of the leaf all the same. She had no idea what she expected to put in it.

Behind her, Lira shifted in the grass.

"Well?" It was hardly a whisper. "Is it still there?"

"It is in exactly the same place," said Karis. "To the stride. I have never in my life measured anything that kept so still."

---

She gave them the procedure at first light, with the frost smoking off the grass, and she gave it once.

"We are going to walk round it," she said. "I want to know what shape it is and how far about, and I can't see it, so I shall have to find it sixty times with my feet. Lira goes a stride inside, the whole way. She is the instrument. She says nothing unless it comes back to her, and then she says *out*, and steps in again."

"Why me?" said Lira.

"Because yours sits nearest the skin of any of us. You had your hand up before the rest of us had finished the stride." Karis shook out the long cord, with its scraps of red wool. "I walk on the line itself, with this end. Brom has the other, at the last mark. When the cord comes tight between us I stop, and find the line under me, and that is a station. Brom cuts a turf there with the bill and turns it over, so that we leave a black mark in the grass at every cord. Then he comes up to me, and we do it again."

"And I'm in front," said Seln.

"You are in front, reading whatever is on the ground, and you will stand where I wave you. He writes." Her pencil pointed at Cael. "The number of the station, and one figure more, which I'll give him."

"What do I do?" said Oryn.

"You keep the stone. Somebody must be standing at the place where we began, so that we know it when we come round to it from the other side. And I should like one of us to be outside the whole time, with the mule, where an Arbiter is an Arbiter."

The healer nodded. She had expected nothing else, Cael thought, and she took up her post beside the grey stone with the rein in her hand, as she would have stood at the head of a bench.

They set off northward, with the ring on their right hands and the ruts some forty yards away on their left.

It went slowly at first. Lira walked with her arms a little out from her sides and a look of deep concentration, like a person carrying a bowl filled to the brim; twice in the first cord she said "Out," sharply, and stepped to her right. Karis felt her way along beside her. At the cord's end she stopped, and shuffled a hand's breadth inward, and a hand's breadth out, and stood still.

"Here."

Brom came up, and drove the hooked point of the bill into the turf, and levered out a square of it as big as a plate. He laid it grass downward. It showed black on the frost.

"One," said Cael, and wrote it.

At the second station Karis did a new thing. She turned round and looked back along the cord to the first turf, and beyond it to the stone; and then she waved Seln forward along that same line, until he stood a full cord ahead of her with a peeled wand held upright.

"That is where the next mark would fall if the line ran straight," she said. "It won't. Watch."

They walked the third cord. Lira kept to her stride inside, and Karis to the line, and when the cord came tight Karis was standing well to the right of Seln's wand. She had her short cord out already, and she laid it on the grass from the wand to her own boots and read the knot.

"Six feet and a half. Inward. Write that beside the three."

He wrote it.

It was the third line of a new table, and it was of another kind from the sixty-three that he had written on a mountain. There a cord and a clock had shown what anybody could check. Here the only instruments were two people's Arbiters, going out and coming back; he had none to offer, and so he held the pencil for the people who had, and it surprised him a little that he did not grudge it.

---

It was Brom who found the iron.

He had come up to cut the third turf, and he set the bill's point where Karis's heels had been and leaned on the haft; and there was a sound that did not belong on that hillside, steel on iron, a short dull clink. Brom took the bill away as if it had burnt him.

"There's a thing in the ground," he said.

It stood a boot's width from the place where she had stopped, and the grass had hidden it. When Brom parted the stems with the side of his hand they all saw it: a square bar of iron, as thick as two fingers, driven straight down into the turf so that a hand's length of it stood clear. It was brown with rust and flaking, and its head had been beaten a little flat, and spread at the edges, by something heavy.

Lira had come out across the line to look, and she crouched and tipped her head.

"There's a mark on it. On this side. And figures."

The mark was stamped into the iron, a thumb's breadth below the head, and Cael knew it before the rust let him see the whole of its shape. He had seen it painted over a hearth in a wool-factors' counting room, and cut in a lintel at the last station on a western road; it was printed in the corner of the sheet that lay rolled in the middle box, back at the turn of the ruts. Under it somebody had punched two numerals.

"Thirty-one," said Karis.

She had gone very still, with the cord's end in her fist.

"That's the registry's stamp," said Lira. "That's theirs. What's it doing in a field two days past a fence that isn't on their map?"

Seln had come back down the line with his wand, and he stood over the stake and looked at it without bending. His mild face told Cael nothing whatever, and by now Cael knew that for a kind of news.

Something in Cael had turned toward the thing as well. Anchor asked now, more quietly than it had ever asked, as one craftsman takes note of another's work. *There.* It was not a question. Here was a point that somebody else had fixed, and told to stay, and it had stayed.

He put that down, and looked at what was left.

He had laid two things side by side at a wash four days ago, and had refused to rule a line between them for want of a third. A query had come down to a registry from somewhere above it, asking which way certain people had gone. A paper had come over a river with a hundred marks, in the hand of somebody who had learned to write at a ruled desk. And here, on the edge of a place where a carter would not put a wheel, was a bar of iron with a registry's stamp on its neck.

That made three. He could rule the line now, and it would be straight. What he could not do was say which way along it he was looking.

"Don't touch it," said Karis. Her voice shook a little, and then steadied. "Nobody is to pull at it, or clean it. I have three stations and I want sixty. It has stood there a good while without us and it will stand until we come round." She turned her back on it with a visible effort. "Brom. Cut the turf a foot short, and I'll allow for the foot."

"You're leaving it," said Lira.

"I am finishing a count. If I stop at every wonder on this hillside we shall be here at dusk with half a shape, and I had rather have a whole one."

They went on. Cael walked the fourth cord with his head turned back over his shoulder. The bar stood in the grass where they had left it, brown and small, beside a square of black turf; and he thought that it was a strange thing to have crossed a Line, and a mountain, and ninety-six days of road, and to be met at the far end of all of it by two numerals that somebody at a counter had signed for.

---

By the tenth station she no longer needed the wand.

"It is in my wrist," she said. She was standing with the cord's end held out before her, and as Cael watched she swung it to the right, without looking down, and stopped. "There. Lira?"

"In," said Lira, a stride beyond her.

He laid the short cord down all the same, since a wrist is not an instrument, and wrote what the knot said. He had written that figure nine times already.

They went round the north side with the ruts beside them. He could see where the carts had gone, forty yards off, keeping the same distance from the line the whole way, as exactly as if the carters had had a cord of their own; and he supposed that in a sense they had. A man who walked in front of a mule here for twenty-two years, with a Path in him, would have learned to the yard where he ceased to be comfortable.

From the north the ring looked different. The eleven uprights had closed up and overlapped, and between two of the tallest he had his first clear sight of what lay among them. It was a floor, grey and pale and perfectly level, raised a little above the grass like the top of a low table; and the morning light lay on it without a shadow.

The ruts came back to their old line on the eastern side, and went away from them over the next swell. Seln was waiting there, a cord ahead, and he was down on one knee.

"Here," he said, when they came up. "And there, and beyond that tussock. I should like all of you to see it before it is trodden."

There were paths in the grass, and Cael would not have found them. They were no wider than his two hands, and the stems had not been broken, only pressed aside, low down, by something that went under them rather than through. Three of them crossed the line within twenty yards of one another. Where the nearest ran over a patch of bare ground there was a print in the frost-softened earth, as broad as a palm, with no mark of a claw.

"Going in?" said Lira, very low. "Or coming out?"

"Both. That one is this morning's, and it points inward. This is older, and it goes out. They use these as a hold uses its gate." Seln had not risen. "I cannot give you a number from a path. I can say that it is not one animal, or two. And I can say that I have gone the whole of the north side and not found a single run. They come and go by the east."

"He told us they lived in them," said Brom.

"He did. I had hoped that he was repeating something." Seln stood, and dusted his knee. "I have seen no animal. I should like that entered exactly. I have seen where they walk."

Cael looked at the print, which was the breadth of the ones beside a white stone in a valley, and of the ones on a gravel bar; and then he looked at the sun, which was a third of the way up the sky.

"We go in at noon," he said. "Not before; it's the hour they've never once been seen abroad, on a stretch or at a ford. And we're out again and on the west side of that stone with the sun two hands above the hills. Whatever isn't done by then isn't done."

"Two hands," said Seln. "I shall hold you to it."

They took the south side at a better pace, since by now each of them had the work by heart. Lira drifted out twice more and said so. Brom's turfs lay behind them in a long curve, black on the pale grass; and when Cael looked back along them from the fiftieth, he could see the curve closing on itself like a hoop.

From the south he had the whole of it in one view, for the first and only time. The grey stone was away on his left hand, with a small brown mule beside it. Beyond it the ruts swung out round the north in their wide curve. The floor lay in the middle of everything, with its eleven broken stones, and on the far side, where no turf had been cut, there was the low place in the grass where Seln had knelt.

Nothing moved in the whole of it but themselves. It looked like the plainest piece of country in the world, and five people had just walked the edge of it with their Arbiters going out and coming back at every stumble.

---

The last cord fell short.

They had come up from the south, with the grey stone in plain sight ahead and a square brown figure standing beside it with a mule. Karis reached the end of the sixty-second cord, and stopped, and found the line, and Brom cut his turf; and the stone was still some way off.

She coiled the cord over her shoulder, and walked the rest on the line, in her own short steps, counting under her breath, until she set her last foot down against the stone itself.

"Sixty-two cords and twenty strides," said Karis.

She stood there with her eyes shut, and her lips moved for some time. Cael knew what she was doing, since she had the length of that cord in feet, to the knot, in a notebook that nobody else had read, and had paced her own stride twice against his in the dark. He would have given a good deal to check the sum; he had only her figures for the half of it, and so he waited.

"One thousand three hundred and seventy-four yards, and a piece."

"Round a field," said Brom.

"Round a field, and home to the stone that it started from." She opened her eyes. "I have one offset written sixty-two times in his hand. There is only one shape that will do that."

She turned her head and looked across the grass to the east, where eleven broken stones stood about a pale floor.

"From any mark on it to the middle is ten cords. I can work that without a table. Ten cords from this stone, on the old line of the road, and one would be standing in the centre of those."

Then she said one thing more, without any weight.

"A circle is a made shape."

It lay in the air a breath. Lira was looking at the stones, and Brom had the bill over his shoulder and was counting his turfs. Oryn, who had stood at that stone since first light, was watching five people come toward her along a line that she could not see. Nobody took the words up, and Karis did not seem to look for anyone to do so. She had entered a property of the figure, and she bent to tie the cord.

Cael heard it. He put it with the three other things, a little apart from them, since it was of another kind.

Seln had already started back along the black marks toward the third.

Cael went after him, and they stood over the iron together. In the full light it was plainer than it had been: the square bar, and the spread head, and the stamp. Seln crouched now, without laying a finger on it, and looked at the rust, where it had lifted in leaves, and at the turf round the bar's foot, which had grown up close against the metal on every side.

"Five years in the weather. A cold term either side."

"You can tell that from rust?"

"From rust, and from the grass. It has had time to forget that anything was done to it." He tilted his head to bring the stamp into the light. "I have seen iron of this pattern given out across a counter, and signed for. A man answers for what he takes."

"Thirty-one."

"I do not know what the number counts, and I shall not guess." Seln rose. He looked from the stake to the ring, and back along the line of turfs to the stone, as he would have looked over a room that somebody had left in a hurry. "It stands a boot's width from where she stopped. She found that place with her feet, this morning, knowing nothing. Whoever drove this found the same place to the same boot's width."

"With a cord," said Cael.

"With a cord, or a chain. That is what I should like you to carry away." He nodded at the iron. "You drive them where you've measured to. Not where you've measured from."

Cael looked at it. A stake marked the far end of a measure; somebody had stood at another place, with the other end of a line in his hand, and had paid it out until it reached exactly here.

He looked along the only line there was. It ran from the stake's foot, across two hundred yards and more of pale grass, to the middle of eleven stones.

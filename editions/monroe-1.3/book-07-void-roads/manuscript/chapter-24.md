# Chapter 24 — Under the Stone

The ferryman would not take silver for his rope.

He said so on the western landing, with the scow rocking at his back and the far drum already groaning. He was the same long sour man who liked animals and nobody else, and he had stood with his arms folded through the whole of Brom's account: the wash, and the well, and a peeled log on a hill of plates, and at the last a gorge with a cart in it. Brom told it as he would have told a debt, in order, leaving nothing out.

"So it's under a mountain," said the ferryman.

"With a cart on top of it. I'd sooner say so here than have you find it out from the wall."

"Sixty yards of four-strand. I laid that rope up myself, in the cold term, with him turning the hook." He jerked his chin at his boy. Then he looked at Karis, who had the purse out on her knee, and his long face soured further. "Put that away. What'd I do with it? There's not a rope-walk nearer than the turf fire, and I can't draw a wagon out of a river with marks."

"Then what do you want for it?" said Lira.

"A rope. When you've one. I'll not put a figure on it, for a figure's a thing you can pay and walk off from."

He turned his back on them and clicked his tongue at the far bank.

Karis wrote it down as he had said it. *Owed: one rope, sixty yards, four-strand, to the ferryman. No price.* She put the purse away uncounted, and Cael thought that it was the first debt she had ever entered without a number beside it, and that she did not like the look of the line.

They went over with the Oxhollow cart, a copper a wheel and a copper a beast out of the carter's own bag. Halfway across Brom lifted his head. In the round shed on the eastern bank something brown was walking its circle, steadily, with one white foot coming round and round into the light, and Brom watched that foot for the rest of the crossing with the face of a man who has seen a child of his doing well at a trade.

The meadow had thinned, for the cold had sent most of the tents home, and there were five fires where there had been a dozen; but the long eave was where it had always been, and under it twelve feet of tarred oak, and behind the oak a bald brown head bent over a book.

Pike saw them from the landing road. He looked at a strange cart with three boxes on it, and at a girl in one boot of her own and one the size of a bucket, and at how Cael held his left arm; and then he looked at all of it a second time, as he did.

"You're down," he said.

"We're down."

"You'd a cart when you went up."

"It's in a gorge," said Brom.

"So I'd heard, from a man who came in last night." Pike set his pen in its pot. "I'll say your line when I've read your paper. Read mine first."

Lira was already reading. She had gone to the left-hand post, from habit, and she was standing in front of two slips that had not been there on the seventieth morning. One was bark and one was a shingle, and on each of them, under a hold's mark and a line of words, stood a word in the square black hand, in capitals, which Cael had seen on this plank only once before.

*HOLD.*

It had had one name after it, that time. Under each of these there were five, set down in the order in which they had been spoken at the plank on the ninth day, and his own was the last.

"That's us," said Lira. Her voice had gone up. "That's our names. Who asked you to keep these?"

"Holds came to the ferry. Asked who. I said you."

Pike had not raised his head, and he said it level, as he would have told her the price of standing-money.

"Asked who what?"

"Who they ought to have. One's a bull wants bringing down off the top grazing before the snow shuts it, and he's killed a dog. The other's a ford that wants staking. They'd each of them heard a line said in this meadow about a river and five dead hounds." He blotted something. "I told them you were six days off on a hill. They said they'd wait a fortnight. After that they'll have Teague."

Cael stood and looked at his own name in another man's capitals.

He had been written on a good many boards. A woman in Ardenmere had chalked him up with a question mark, and a panel had posted him at a rate that it had scattered on purpose, and for two years a house had put his name on a card every week because a schedule told it to. Nobody had ever before gone to the keeper of a book and asked who was good, and been given him.

He had stood in an inn, forty days ago, and heard Pike say that he could not send a hold a crew that had never yet had anything go wrong. Something had gone wrong for them twice since then, and they had come back from it both times, and here it was on a shingle.

"A fortnight," said Lira. "They'd wait a fortnight, for us, with a bull loose." She looked round at the others, and her eyes were very bright. "He didn't say fast. Nobody said fast. They said *who*."

---

Karis had the paper out of her coat.

It had come down a mountain inside her shirt and six days along a valley, and it was soft at the folds. Pike laid it on the oak and put two fingers on it, and read it to himself from the careful writing at the head to the foot, where five strokes stood side by side over a horned head and three wavy lines; and then he read it again.

"He's marked it," he said. "And paid you there, by the paper."

"Seventy," said Karis. "In silver, counted onto his own board."

"And the map?"

"He has it. He followed it with his finger from end to end, and then he said that his carts would go round, and went to his hearth for the bag."

Pike nodded slowly. He opened the book at the leaf with five names, and set the flat stone on its upper corner, and turned his shoulder to the meadow. There were perhaps thirty people within hearing, and Cael saw the hide-woman among them, with her knife on her thigh, and under the ash an old carter who lifted his chin.

"High traverse, Oxhollow to Thornwater. Mapped. Delivered at the ford. Carts go round."

He stopped there, with his mouth a little open, as a man stops on a stair to feel for the next tread. Cael knew what the next one was, for he had heard it on a river bank at dusk, from a man with a band of leather on his wrist.

"Cart lost. Crew of five, three hurt, none lost. Seventy."

It went down the meadow in that level carrying voice, past five fires. A woman looked at Lira's boot. A man by the nearest tent said something to his neighbour, low, and the neighbour looked at the strange cart, and then at the empty place beside the smith's shed where a hired one had stood. The Oxhollow carter had halted his mules at the edge of the grass to listen. At *carts go round* he had nodded slowly, like a person who hears his own errand read out to strangers; and at *cart lost* he looked at his boots.

"Is it wrong?" said Pike.

He put it to all five, as he put it to anybody. Cael went through the line a word at a time, for the place where he could say so. *Mapped* was true, and *delivered*; carts would go round, for an old man with a tally-stick had said it in those words. There had been a cart, and there was none, and three of them had been hurt.

He looked for the thing that the line left out, and found it at once, and it was everything: a ledge that had shown no crack, and a page marked derived, and eight seconds of fire. None of that was in Pike's column, and he had known since a river bank that it would not be.

"No," he said.

Pike wrote, and the pen went to the horn pot and came back four times. When the line was on the page he did a thing that Cael had not seen him do before; he dropped the nib to the space beneath it and wrote four words more, smaller, and drew a short rule under them.

*less six, the cart.*

"Seventy's what the ford paid. Sixty-four's what the job was worth. That's what goes on the wall."

"Fair."

It was Brom who said it. He had come up to the oak, with his two hands hanging open, and he was looking at the small words under the line as he had looked at a smith's cut axle.

"It was his cart, and we brought him back a story about it. I'd have written it the same."

Pike straightened, and gave the whole of it to the meadow a second time, from the first word to the last, with the six taken off at the end; and this time nobody along the fires murmured. He did not go to the iron box, since there was nothing in it of theirs. He drove the soft paper down onto the spike beneath the plank, among the dead slips, and that was the end of a mountain.

Cael found that he did not mind it.

He turned that over, since he had expected to. On the bank at Oxhollow he had written that he kept waiting to mind, and he had gone on waiting for six days of road. The line said that they had lost a cart, and it said so to thirty strangers, twice. And he stood under an eave with five bands of linen round his chest and felt only that a thing had been counted, by a person who counted everything alike; it was the seventh line under their names, and it was worth sixty-four, and both of those were so.

Karis had the purse in her hand, and she had not opened it.

"I want one thing straight, for my own page," she said. "The seventy is in here. I counted it at a ford, and it hasn't altered on the road. Earned stands at a hundred and thirty-two. What you have written under that line is another figure, and I don't believe that it is money."

"It's what I'd tell a hold you were worth for that job, if a hold came and asked me," said Pike. "The six you'll count out to a smith yourselves, before dark, if I know you. I only take it off where the meadow can see it go."

"Seven," said Lira, under her breath. She was counting on her fingers. "A boat, a fence, a wagon, a mile behind a mule. A ford. A well. A mountain."

"And a bull, if we want it," said Brom.

---

It was while the pen was back in its pot that Cael saw the paper.

He had been reading the plank, as he read it every time, from the left-hand post to the right, and there were nine slips besides the two with their names on. He had sorted them and found nothing among them that weighed much, and his eye had come to rest, for want of anything better, on Pike's flat stone.

It was a grey river stone the size of a loaf, and it lay on the upper corner of the open book, as it always lay. Something was under its far edge: a corner showed there, toward Pike's side of the oak, no wider than three fingers, and it was neither bark nor hide. It was paper, of a good cream weight, such as a clerk writes on at a desk.

There was a curve of dust across the corner, and he looked at that for a breath before he understood it. The dust followed the rim of the stone exactly, a fine pale line, as a tide leaves its line on a beach; so the paper had not been slid there this morning. It had lain under that stone, in that one place, for long enough to take the stone's shape.

Every other slip that Pike had was on a tack, and he read them all out to anybody who was frying dough.

Cael leaned a little over the oak. The writing ran away from him, toward the man with the book, and he read it upside down, as he had once read a bailiff's list on a counter in Denvash.

*Recovery. Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Fallow road. East of Thornwater; the second day; where the road leaves its line at the rise and comes back to it. One hundred marks (one hundred).*

There was no hold's mark at its foot. There was a seal: a hook and a bar, in dark wax.

He read it a second time, to be sure of the figure. Then he reached across the oak and drew the paper out from under the stone by its corner, and the dust-line broke. He turned the sheet round and held it out to Pike, in his fingers, saying nothing, as any crew holds out a slip that it wishes to hear said.

Pike's face stopped.

Cael had no other word for it. He had watched that face for eighty days, and it was never still; it looked at a thing and then looked again, and something in it went on working like a mill between the two looks. Now the one arm lay on the book and the brown eyes rested on a sheet of cream paper, and nothing in the face moved at all.

He counted, since he could no more have helped it than breathing, and it was nine before the man spoke.

"That's the Fallow Ring."

He said it to the paper. Lira had turned round from her two slips, and Brom from the spike. None of them answered him, since the name meant nothing to any of the five, and Pike raised his eyes and saw that it meant nothing.

"That's quiet ground."

Beside Cael's shoulder a notebook shut with a small clap.

He did not need to look, for he knew whose it was, and he knew what was folded in the middle box, forty yards off, with two lines ruled under two of its words. Karis had heard those two words once before, dropped by a woman with a sack of barley on her hip, and she had asked nobody what they meant for thirty-two days.

"I have that noun," she said. Her voice was very careful. "I have had it since the fifty-eighth day, from a carter's wife, in three sentences, and I have had nobody to put a question to. You have just said it to me yourself, at your own plank. I think that I may ask now. What is it?"

Pike laid the cream sheet down on the oak, face upward, and put his hand on it.

"Quiet ground's where the sigil goes to sleep. Yours. Everyone's. You walk a road, and there's a place on it where your Arbiter stops — not slows; stops — and you walk on past and it's back. There's a handful of them. Along the void roads, all of them. Carters walk round them, and have as long as there've been carters. The dogs live in them. Stillhounds. Nobody's asked them why. Why any of it, nobody knows and nobody's needed to, because the rule's *don't go in*, and the rule's never once failed anybody who kept it."

It was the longest thing that Cael had ever heard him say, and he had said it once. He let it stand, without going back over a word of it, and that was stranger than what was in it.

"That's what a carter knows. That's what I know. There's not a man on the ferry knows a word more."

Under the eave there was no sound, and out in the meadow a mule shook its harness.

Cael had heard *yours* and *everyone's*, and he had thought of a place behind his own breastbone, where a small bright thing had gathered once on a morning in a windowless room, and looked at him for eleven seconds, and gone out. It had been dark ever since, and he kept that thought where it was.

"You've seen it?" said Lira. "This ring."

"I've walked past the Fallow Ring two hundred times. Never once stepped over the line. Thirty years on the roads and I've never met a practitioner who did it twice."

"What's the line?"

It was Karis, and it was the second of the four questions that she had swallowed in a cart-yard.

"You'll know," he said. "Your Arbiter tells you. It stops."

---

Karis went to the cart.

She walked the forty yards without hurrying, and climbed the wheel, and had the lid off the middle box; and when she came back she had a folded leaf in one hand and a roll of calfskin under her arm. She laid the leaf on the oak beside the cream paper. It was in Cael's own writing, three sentences with a question mark after the first, and under two of the words there were two ruled lines.

Then she unrolled the hide, and held it open with her wrists, and put one finger down.

Cael knew the place. East of a square with a dot in it, two days of ticks out, a double line left its straight and went out to the left in a curve and came back to itself, as a path goes round a pond; and inside the curve a carter had drawn nothing.

She read the paper's third sentence over to herself, with her lips, and her finger had not moved.

"He has drawn that. A road that leaves its line and comes back. He drew it for his own wheels, and never drew what was in the middle, since he had no business there. And she said that everybody knew it, and that nobody went in." She took the finger away. "I have had those two for a month and could do nothing with either. This is the third, and it has a price on it."

She looked up at Pike.

"A location. And a contract."

"It's a paper with a hundred on it," said Pike. "I'd not call it more."

Cael looked at the three things lying side by side on the tarred oak. There was a leaf in his own hand that held a stranger's passing word, and a calfskin that a carter had drawn for his wheels over twenty-two years, and a cream sheet under a seal that he had first seen burned into the end of a cask.

None of them had been meant for the others' company. The woman with the barley had never looked at her husband's map with a pencil in her hand, and the carter had certainly never read a broker's paper; and yet all three were speaking of one patch of grass, two days beyond a fence, as though they had agreed on it beforehand.

"Why's it under your stone?" said Lira. "Everything else you've got is on a tack. You shout it at people."

"I said it. I'm bound to say it; twice, the day it came, to the whole grass, with the price." He turned the cream sheet a little with one finger. "The broker's clerk brought it up from the landing the morning after the last sack, sealed as you see it, with a hundred marks in a bag, counted. I've had that seal on bales for thirty years. I never had it before on a slip that sends a crew two days past Thornwater."

"And nobody took it."

"Nobody scratched. There were forty in this meadow that day, and when I'd done you could have heard the river. So I put the stone on it. A thing on a tack is a thing I'm offering."

He said that last quite mildly, and Cael understood that he had been told something about the man. Pike wrote down whatever was put in front of him and read it to anyone; but he had found, in thirty years, one paper that he would not hang up between two fences and a wheel, and he had kept it where a person must go looking.

Cael had gone looking, and he weighed that, with the oak under his hands.

A hundred marks was more than everything that the board had paid them in ninety days, the mountain included. It was to walk two days east of a hold where they were known, and pick up something that somebody had left lying, and carry it home. He could find no opponent in it. There was a ring on a road, and a rule with no reason given, and instruments, which were only things; and he heard himself thinking that, and distrusted it, since he had thought the same about a shelf.

"What's a surveyor want out there?" said Brom. "On ground a carter won't put a wheel on."

"That I couldn't tell you," said Pike. "It's not a trade I've ever seen come over my ferry."

"Then who left the things?"

"The paper doesn't say. I've read it more than twice."

Seln had not spoken at all. He stood at the plank's end with his hands behind him, and he had not yet troubled with the words; it was the sheet itself that he studied, where it lay, as a joiner studies a board.

"I'll not mark it tonight," said Pike. "That's not a thing I'd say of a fence. Take it in with you, and put it on your boards, and look at it with a lamp. If you bring it me in the morning I'll say it and write your strokes." He pushed the paper across the oak with one finger, until it lay in front of Cael. "And if you don't, it goes back where it was."

---

They paid the smith before the light went.

He was at his door, as he had been on the seventy-first morning, and he looked past them at the place under his lean-to where a cart with a cut axle ought to have been standing. Brom told it again, and it was shorter this time, since a smith needed no telling what loose stone did to a wheel.

"Floated," the smith said, when it was done.

"To the edge, and over, with her shafts up."

"I'd cut that axle true."

"You had. She never once ran crooked. It wasn't the cart." Brom was quiet a little. "You kept my seat board, and I'm sorry for it."

The smith held out his hand, and Karis counted six thick coins into it, and he shut his fist on them without looking down.

"A hundred and eight," she said.

It was done. Cael had carried that six since the seventy-first morning, as one carries a stone in a boot, and he waited to feel lighter and did not. The smith had gone back into his shed already, and presently the rasp began again. A cart was only a cart, and the man had been paid for it twice over; all the same there would be a clean place under that lean-to for a good while, and Brom stood looking at it with his hands hanging until Karis took him by the sleeve.

The account was square, as far as coin could square it. There remained a rope with no figure against it, and a line in a book that said *cart lost*, and neither of those could be paid off; they could only be added to, by whatever the five of them did next.

He came on Lira at the roof's edge.

She was standing where the eave's shadow stopped, with her toes on the line of it, her own boot and the great one side by side, and she was looking east. The saddle road went up there, pale in the last light, toward a wash, and a ford, and a fence; and beyond the fence there was a road that none of them had walked, with a bend in it. The cold was coming up off the river behind them. Along the meadow the fires had drawn in to red, and somebody was calling a child home.

He stood beside her. The others had gone in, and through the open door he could hear a boy being told to hold a cloth taut.

"Two hundred times. He counted."

He had not meant to say it aloud. It had been going round in him since the plank, a thing that he could not sort. A man had walked a road for thirty years, and each time that he came to a certain place he had gone round, and each time he had made a mark in his head. He had never once put his foot over, and he knew the number of his own refusals to the unit.

Lira did not turn her head.

"So do you. I've never once heard you say never."

He looked at her.

"He counts what he hasn't done," she said. "That's all that is. Two hundred times he didn't. You'd have gone round it the first time, and the second, and you'd have written both down. And the third time you'd have stood there with your book shut, working out what it would cost to look." She scuffed the great boot along the shadow's edge. "I've watched you price things since a lane in Ardenmere. You've said *not yet* to me, and *not here*, and *no* a hundred times. You don't say *never*. I don't think you know how."

It was true, and he had not known it, and she had. He had refused nine things a stride at a time for ninety days, and thought himself a careful person; but a refusal that is made again at every stride is the opposite of a rule, for it is a door that somebody is holding shut with his hand.

"You want to see it," said Lira.

"Yes."

"So do I." She said it low, and she was still looking at the road. "He said your Arbiter tells you. It stops. I've spent three months not doing the one thing I'm good at, on purpose, a stride at a time, and it went off anyway when a ledge fell. I'd like to stand somewhere that I couldn't. I'd like to know what's left when it isn't me doing the *not*."

He thought of a question that stood in his book under the fifty-ninth day, in her words, with nothing written beneath it. She had asked what she was becoming if nobody was measuring, and he had not answered, and nobody had. He did not suppose that a ring on a road would answer it either. But it would tell her one thing plainly, at one stride, without a sheet or a panel or a coach; and he saw that she had heard that in the old man's three short sentences before any of the rest of them.

Inside, a voice had begun to count lines.

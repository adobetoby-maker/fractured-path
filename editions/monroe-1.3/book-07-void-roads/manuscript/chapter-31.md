# Chapter 31 — Nine on the White

Seln stood up, and the floor went white.

The sun had gone under the hills while Cael's book was shutting. He had seen the pale thing hold the light on the evening before, from three hundred yards, and had taken it for water; now he stood below its eastern lip, with the rolled edge at the height of his thigh, and saw what it did close to. The long shadows of the stones were gone, and the whole round of it lay level with his hip and gave back the sky, as milk in a pan gives back a window.

He went over the ground once in his head, since six people were about to walk it with the light going.

They stood on the east, at the mouth of the den, and everything that they wanted was on the west. The floor lay between, twenty-six strides across. From its western lip the lane that the cord had trodden ran down through the grass, as straight as a rule, for two hundred and seven yards; and at the end of the lane there was a grey stone and a mule, and beyond the stone a Path was a Path again and a healer's hands were hands. That made two hundred and thirty-three yards from where his boots were.

He had one other mark on that slope. North of the lane a rut came down from under the floor's north-western lip, knee-deep and a stride across, where the rain off the rim had cut itself a road. He had marked the head of it from the ninth cord, and Seln had crossed it on both his rounds in the grass.

"Cael," said Lira.

She had not raised her voice. She was looking between two of the uprights, with the knife held low.

Something grey had come onto the white.

It came over the western lip, where the lane ended, and it came without any sound at all, as everything went on that floor. It was low and long in the back, and it carried its head level, and he had seen that shape beside a white stone in a valley and on a bar of gravel with a river round it. A second came up behind the first, and a third; and then they were coming too quickly to be watched singly, and he counted them as he would have counted fighters onto a yard.

The first was pale, nearly white along the spine, so that against that floor he could see little of it but the legs. The heaviest had a dark stripe from its neck to the root of its tail. Two smaller ones came over shoulder to shoulder and stayed so. There was one long in the leg, which moved like a thing built for running, and one grey to the eyes, which walked a little way onto the white and looked about it and lay down. Three more he could not have told from each other.

"Nine," he said.

"Nine," said Seln. "I am glad to have the figure. I could not get it from their feet."

They did not hurry, and it was that which he liked least. They spread out among the eleven stones as a crew spreads out in its own yard at the end of a day. One sat and scratched. The pale one stood at the middle of the round, by the place where a cord had ended, with its nose down on the grey.

They had gone out by the east at first light, as Seln had read off bare earth, and they had come home by the west, up the one track on the whole of that hillside which six people had made since noon. He thought of a healer at the far end of that track, counting to ten on a wrist, twice, with her Path running; he did not know that it bore, and he put it by for whoever kept findings.

"The road's cut."

It was Seln, and he said it as he would have read out a line from a bill of lading.

"What road?" said Brom. "There's not a road in the place."

"The grass. Where the cord went."

Brom looked at the lane then, and at what stood between themselves and the head of it, and said nothing more.

Cael had the ruling, and he made himself look at both ways before he gave it.

Behind him the slope fell away to the east, and the line on that side was nearer by twenty-six yards. It was also the hounds' own door. There were three runs across it and not a stride of it that any of the six had walked; the grass stood hip-high the whole way down, the ground was steep, and in a quarter of an hour it would be dark. If they came out alive on that side, they would be half a circle of open country from a mule. The west was a lane trodden flat by their own feet, with a stone at the end of it that five of them could find with their eyes shut.

"There's a bow on that mule," said Oryn.

She had finished with her boot. She stood up, and for the first time since he had known her she did not seem to know where to put her hands.

"I looked at it at noon and left it lying. I'd come to find out about my hands, and I didn't want anything in them." Her mouth went hard. "That was a healer's reason. Six bolts, in a sleeve on the stock."

"West, then," said Cael.

Nobody asked him why. He saw Seln take up the strap of the locked case by two holes, as he had taken it up at a ford.

"I shall go south about, at forty yards from the lip," said Seln. "I have walked that round twice today. Then down outside the lane, and up it with the thing in my hand. I should not be looked for before three hundred."

"Three hundred. I'll count."

"I know that you will."

He went down off the trodden earth into the grass, stooping, and at his tenth stride there was grass and no Seln. He had done as much at noon, to amuse them, for a count of three.

*One*, thought Cael. He priced it while he counted, since it was done. It took one of two knives off the hill, and the only one of the six who had nothing wrong with him, and against that it put a bow at the head of the lane. There was a third figure in the sum, which he looked at and did not say: something in his own feet could have had that bow on the floor in forty.

"Round by the south," he said. "On the turf, close in under the lip, and nobody runs. Lira's first, with the knife. Then Karis, and Oryn. I'm behind you. Brom's last, and he's on the floor side." He looked at the hundredweight of paper on Brom's back. "Leave the box."

"No."

"Brom."

"It's eleven sheets and her whole afternoon. It's the only thing we're bringing down off this hill." Brom shifted the plaited strap an inch across his chest, and took the bill in both hands like a quarterstaff, with the hook uppermost. "It weighs what it weighed at noon. I tried it."

Karis did not thank him. She put one small hand flat against the box as she went by, as a person touches a horse.

Cael opened the read.

It came as it had come beside a square of black turf: a pace and a half of ground on every side, and the weight of everything that stood on it. He had the trodden earth, and the hollow going back under the floor, and five people's boots; and something went out of him into the cold, as small as a breath.

On the white, the old one lifted its chin from its paws.

It looked east, at six shapes under a lip, and for the length of one breath it looked at one of them. Then it laid its chin down again.

That was the price of the quietest thing he owned, with the width of a floor between: one head, once. He did not have to work out what the other eight would cost. Any one of them, used here, would bring nine animals to the place where everybody he had in the world was standing within reach of his arm.

---

They went along under the lip with the floor on their right hands.

For the first few yards it stood over them like a shelf. Then the turf came up to meet it, and by the south-east it was only a kerb, a hand high, with the white running away level beyond it to the feet of the stones. Lira went in front on the great boot, flat-footed, with the knife out. Karis kept one hand on the breast of her coat, where a roll of paper was, and the healer walked behind her at the carter's pace, since it was the only one she had.

Cael had his eyes on the white and the rest of his mind in the ground.

He was at a hundred and ten, and the pack had begun to move with them. It did not come on as seven had come on at a ford, in a line, behind one that went first. It drifted. A grey shape would get up and walk a few yards toward the southern rim and stand, and another would pass it and stand; and so, by pieces, without any one of them appearing to decide anything, the nine were always a little nearer.

The pale one came to the rim first.

It stood on the rolled edge a yard from Brom's right hip, with its head no higher than his belt, and it looked him over as a man looks over a cart that he may buy. Cael had it inside the read from the instant its forefeet touched the lip, and he felt the weight of it come forward, slowly, off the haunches and onto the forelegs.

"Now," he said.

Brom did not swing. He set the butt of the haft against his own hip and lifted the other end, and the animal brought its throat down onto the ash.

It ought to have had him. Cael saw that as it came, and saw why it had not. A hound springs off its hind feet. On that rim its hind feet had nothing square to push from; they were set on a thing as round as a log, with the edge falling away in front, and so it came over the lip reaching down, with all its weight on its forelegs and its jaw stretched out. The haft took it under the chin. Brom turned his hips, and it went past his knee and onto its side on the turf with a grunt.

It got up. It shook itself from the head to the tail, like a dog out of a pond, and looked at Brom with no ill will whatever. Then it walked off along the foot of the lip, the way that they had come, and jumped up onto the white again.

"It's only a hand," said Brom, hoarsely. He was looking at the rim. "A hand's drop. I'd not have thought it."

"It can't get its legs under it. Keep close to the edge. If you give it a yard of ground it'll have its spring."

"How'd you know it was coming?"

"I was watching its feet."

That was true, as far as it went. Brom took it for eyes, and Cael let him.

The dark-backed one came next, forty yards farther round. It was the heaviest of the nine, and it came to the very same kind of place, at Brom's right hand, and stood, and brought its weight forward in the very same way; and Cael said "Now," and the haft was there.

This one went down harder, for Brom had hit it as he would have hit a post, with both arms and the hundredweight on his back behind them. It lay a breath on the turf with its legs working. Then it too stood up, and shook, and went home along the lip.

"They don't mind me," said Brom.

He sounded more troubled by that than by the teeth.

"I dropped that first one hard enough to stun a calf. And this one walked up to the same place as if I'd never been born. It watched me do it. It was standing there on the floor."

"Nothing told it."

"I told it. I've an ash pole."

"You didn't make the kind of noise they listen for." Cael had the count at a hundred and sixty, and his eyes on a long-legged shape that had begun to pace them along the inside of the rim. "You're a man with a stick, and you can't declare. So far as that floor's concerned you haven't said a word since you came over the stone."

Brom thought about it for three strides.

"Well," he said. "That's a thing I've been wanting to be, by and large. I'd not looked to be it here."

He grounded the haft and walked on, a foot from the lip, with a hundredweight of copied stone on his shoulders. Cael looked at the broad back in front of the grey, and at the wrists, which were the colour of wrists. Brom had stood on a rock in a river once and been the loudest thing for half a mile, on purpose, for three breaths; he was doing a harder thing now, which was to be nobody, with a pole, in the right place.

---

It was the two small ones that got in front.

Cael did not see them go. He had the whole of his attention in a pace and a half of turf, feeling for the next weight to come onto the rim above Brom, and he had let his eyes follow it down; and when he lifted them the file had stopped.

They were at the south. Fifteen yards on along the kerb two grey shapes were standing on the grass, shoulder to shoulder, with their heads level. They had gone ahead on the white and dropped off the rim where there was nobody under it with a pole, and they stood now on turf, where a hound has all the spring that it was born with.

"Two," said Lira. "In front. On the ground."

She had set her feet. He saw the right one go down whole-soled, a beat behind the left, inside a boot that she had said was like a bucket, and he saw her know it. She had killed one of these with that knife, and she had done it at the end of twenty-five yards of running, on two good feet.

The pair began to walk.

They did not come at Lira. They had seen a knife, and they came wide of it, one on either hand, toward the small pale person behind her and the square one behind that. It was how a thing would come that had learned to take the easiest first, and it was the first sign of sense that he had seen from any of the nine.

"Brom," said Cael. "Front."

"I'm coming."

He was coming at the best pace that a man can make under a box, and it would not be enough, since there were twelve yards of turf between Brom's pole and Karis.

Lira did not turn her head.

"I can't stop two on this foot," she said. "I can't stop one. I can be what they go after."

"You're not to run on it," said Oryn. Her voice was very low. "I told you a month."

"You told me why, too. It's new, and it wants time. It's had sixteen days." Lira had her eyes on the nearer of the pair. "If it goes, mend it at the stone."

She did not wait to be given leave, and he had none to give that would have reached her in time. She took two steps to her right and put her left boot on the rolled edge and went up.

Then she ran.

She ran along the rim itself. It was a thing no wider than a roof-ridge, round on top, with the white a finger below it on one side and the turf a hand below on the other, and she went along it as he had watched her go along a ledge under a mountain, close and quick, with her arms a little out. The great boot came down every second stride with a slap. There was no burst in it and no Path, since there could be none; it was a girl of thirteen on the lanes at Fenmark, who had only legs.

Wind stood up in his own feet to go with her. He put it down, and it was like putting down his own heart.

The pair turned as she passed them. He saw both heads go round together, and both bodies after, and they left Karis as a dog leaves a bone for a thrown stick and went loping along the foot of the kerb under her heels. On the white the pale one had seen her too, and it swung in beside her on the inside, easily, with its head at the height of her knee.

"Go," said Cael to the others. "Now. Round."

The turf in front of them was empty, and Karis went along it at a stumbling trot with the healer's hand in the middle of her back.

Lira had run a quarter of the round. He counted it afterward at twenty yards. She was two strides short of the place where the lane came up to the lip, and she had three hounds strung out after her and nothing whatever in front, when the great boot came down on the outer curve of the roll and not on its top.

He saw the foot go over. It went outward, past the place where an ankle stops, as it had gone on round stones at a river and under a plate of shale; and that made three times.

She fell inward, onto the floor. The knife left her hand and slid away over the grey, spinning, without the least sound.

The pale one took her by the left forearm.

It did not snap. It closed on the arm between the wrist and the elbow, as a door closes on a hand, and its jaws stayed shut. She was on her back, and he saw her do then what he had seen her do at a stone at noon: she gathered the whole of herself, heels and hips and shoulders, and asked.

At noon it had bought her a step. It bought her a yard of floor now, sliding, with the animal's forefeet sliding after her and her arm in its mouth.

"Lira!"

It was Karis, and Karis had never in his hearing shouted anything.

---

He was on the floor before he had decided to go.

It was twenty yards, and the leg ran every one of them. He went over the lip without feeling it, and the pair, which had come up onto the white at Lira's feet, turned their heads to watch him arrive.

Brom was a stride behind, and he brought the haft down across the pale one's skull with both hands, in a blow that would have broken a door.

The jaws did not open. The hound's eyes shut and opened again, and it settled its forefeet a little wider on the grey; that was all. It had what it had come for. It was not biting to be answered, as seven had bitten at a ford, since nothing on this floor had ever answered; it was only keeping hold, and presently it would do what a dog does with a thing that it is holding.

"Don't let it shake her."

That was Oryn. She had reached the lip with Karis and had not come over, and she stood on the turf with both fists shut.

"Whatever else you do. If it shakes that arm there'll be nothing for me to mend."

Cael came down on the animal with both knees, behind the skull, and put both hands on its head.

It was the size of a calf. He bore the long head down until the jaw lay on the floor, with Lira's forearm lying on the floor between the teeth, and he put the whole of his weight on it and kept it there. He had the thing inside the read, every pound of it; and each time that it gathered itself to wrench he had the gathering a breath before the wrench came, in the neck and the shoulders, and leaned his own weight to the place where it meant to go.

It could hold. It could not shake.

Her face was a foot from his, as white as the floor, and she did not cry out, and she kept her eyes on him.

"Knife," she said, through her teeth. "By your knee."

"I can't let go of its head."

"No. I can see that."

Everything that he carried had come up, as he had not felt it come since a mountain. Compression showed him the skull under his palms and how little it would take. Wind wanted his feet, and hers. And Ember had the place already, as it had had it once in a ford: he was looking into a yellow eye from a foot away, and it would set a point on anything that he looked at for long. *There.*

He could have ended it in a breath, three ways, and he went through what came after as quickly as he would have gone through a bout. Whichever he chose would go out of him on that floor a great deal louder than a healer's count, and eight heads would come round. There were two hounds within a yard of Brom's legs already, and Karis and the healer stood at the lip with nothing in their hands. He could take one animal, perhaps two; he could not take eight off four people who were standing inside the length of a cart.

It needed room. It needed him a long way from everybody he loved, and them walking the other way; he saw that much and no further, and he had no room at all.

*No*, he told the three of them. He had said it ten thousand times since a shelf above a valley, and he had never before said it with his hands on the thing that it would have stopped. He was not sure that he was right.

"Two hundred and forty," he said aloud. He had not known that he was still counting.

"What's that?" said Brom. One of the pair had him by the skirt of the coat and was tugging. He let it tug.

"Seln."

The other one tried Cael. Its teeth met in the tail of his coat, and in the cloth of his breeches behind the knee, and it pulled, and stood back to see what he would do. He did nothing whatever except keep a head on a floor, and count.

Seln came up the lane at two hundred and seventy.

He was thirty inside his time. He came over the lip with a long thing under one arm, out of its cover, and no breath to speak with. He gave the bow to Brom, and stood holding his own knees.

"There is a hook for the string," he said, when he could. "A healer does not draw that with her fingers. I did not stay to look for it."

"Pannier," said Oryn. "Near side. It doesn't matter. He's the arms."

Brom had never held one, as Cael saw from how he turned it over; but he had drawn carts and hauled rope all his life, and he found the iron stirrup at its nose, and put his boot in that, and bent and took the string in the fingers of both hands.

He straightened his back, and the string came up the stock and dropped behind its catch with a sound like a gate-latch. When he took his hands away there was a dark line across the pads of four fingers, and he wiped them down his breeches.

Seln drew a bolt from the sleeve on the stock, and laid it in the groove.

"Not from there," said Cael. "You'll have it jump. Get down outside, and lay it on the lip."

It was the lip a third time. A hand's height had spoiled the spring of two hounds, and now it gave a man who had never shot a bow something to rest one on. Brom went back over it and knelt on the turf, box and all, and set the stock on the rolled edge and his cheek against the stock. There were four strides and a foot of white between the iron head of that bolt and the eye that Cael was looking into, and one foot more between the eye and Cael's own face.

"Thirteen feet," said Karis. Her voice was a thread. She could no more have helped it than he could.

"She throws low," said Oryn. "A hand, at that. Lay it on the top of the eye."

"I've never shot one," said Brom.

"I know you haven't. Hold your breath out, and squeeze it as you'd milk a goat."

Cael did not move his hands. He had made the thing as still as he knew how to make it, and that was the whole of what he had to give. Nine fragments lay under his refusal, and not one of them could do what was wanted, which was to let a friend try. He looked across thirteen feet at one grey eye over a bow-stock, narrowed, and at a face that would have done anything on earth sooner than loose that string.

"When you like," he said.

There was a slap, and something went by his wrists with a wind on it.

The head under his hands stopped pulling. There was no struggle in it: it was a weight that had been leaning against him, and then it was only a weight, and he was pressing a dead thing to the floor with all his strength.

"Eight," said Karis.

The pair stood. They had let go of two coats, and they stood as two had stood in the shallows of a river when the one in front of them died, with their heads level, waiting for something to follow.

The jaws had not opened. Seln knelt, and put the flat of the narrow dull knife between the teeth at the hinge, and Cael took the upper jaw in both hands, and it was done as a trap is opened. Lira made one sound while they did it, and it was not loud.

He had her knife in his hand when he stood up. He did not remember reaching for it.

---

They got her over the lip to the head of the lane.

Brom carried her the four strides, on one arm, with the bow in the other hand, and Oryn was on her knees in the grass before Lira's back had touched it. Both her hands went round the arm, above the place and below it, as they had gone round a shin on a mountain, and her eyes shut.

"Nothing," Oryn said, to the arm. Her hands were to the wrist in what was coming out of it. "Nothing in my hands."

She took her hands away as though the arm had burnt them.

Then she was a different person, and one that he had not met. She had a roll of linen out of her coat, the stiff kind that smelled of a pine wood, and her right knee on the inside of Lira's arm above the elbow, with all her weight on the knee.

There was a good deal of blood, and it was black in that light. The teeth had met through the arm; he knew what that was, from the inside of his own calf, and he knew that his calf had been the smaller matter. He had seen her hurt in a dozen ways since a lane in Ardenmere, and he had never before seen so much of her on the outside.

"You," said Oryn, to Karis. "Both hands. There, where my thumb is. Harder than that. You'll not hurt her worse than she's hurt."

Karis put both hands where the thumb was, and leaned.

The linen went on from the wrist upward, turn over turn, pulled with the healer's teeth at every third. Her lips kept no count, for there was nothing in her hands to count. She worked as the steading's woman had worked in a barn by two lanterns, quickly and without tenderness, and when the roll was done she tore the tail of it down the middle and knotted it.

"Up. That stays above her heart." She took Lira's left wrist and laid it against Lira's own right collarbone, and shut Lira's right hand over it. "You hold that there. You don't let it down to balance, and you don't put it out if you fall. If you fall, you fall on your face."

"The foot's gone," said Lira. Her teeth were knocking. "The right. I heard it."

"I heard it too. It'll carry you flat. Put the whole sole down, every step, like a duck." Oryn got her own shoulder under Lira's right arm, and stood up with her. She was the shorter by half a head, and she took the weight as she would have taken a pannier.

Then she looked at Cael. In that light her face was all planes, and there was a thing in it that he had not seen there at noon, when a count stopped in her mouth.

"Move," Oryn said. "I can't fix this here. Move."

He looked back once. The white lay behind them, a hand higher than the turf that he stood on, and it had not dimmed. One shape was stretched on it by the lip, four strides in, pale on pale, and eight others stood or sat among the feet of the stones. None of them had come a yard nearer while a bow was drawn and a girl was bound; they had watched it as the pale one had watched Brom from the rim.

The lane went down in front of him into the dusk, a thin pale stroke with the grass standing dark on either hand, and he could not see the stone at the end of it, or the mule.

"Seln in front," he said. "Karis behind him. Then you two. Brom, wind that thing again and keep it up. I'm last."

*Two hundred and seven*, he thought, and they began to walk.

# Chapter 15 — Ground

The paper was on the oak before the mist had left the river, and Lira read it with one boot on and the other in her hand.

She had gone up to the eave on purpose to be first. Cael came after her at his own pace, which on level grass was anybody's pace now, and he took his inventory on the way, as he took it at the start of anything. The calf was a leg again on the flat; he had asked it the evening before, on the saddle road, and it had told him nine strides uphill and then shut. Brom's forearm was closed along the whole of its seam and wrapped by Brom's own choice, and it was to pull nothing. Lira had wound a strip of linen round her right ankle, over the stocking, and she walked on the whole sole of that foot. Karis had two pink lines across each palm and held a pencil for a quarter of an hour at a time. The nine fragments lay in him as they had lain since the ford, idle, each with its rent paid.

There were fifty-two marks in the purse, and the name at the top of the sheet was still up the north water on two holds' slips.

"It's a mountain," said Lira.

She did not turn round to say it. She stood before the left-hand post with her head a little on one side, and her lips moved at the figure.

"It isn't a ford at all. The ford's only where they pay you." She put her boot on at last, hopping. "He let me say I'd had enough of fords, and he knew what was on it the whole time."

"I told you to read it first," said Pike.

He had come out with the book under his arm and his coat on, and he set the flat stone on the page before he so much as glanced at the new slip. He had read it by lamplight when the carter brought it, and had no need to look.

It was real paper, which was rare on that plank, and the hand was a careful one that did not write often. Beside the words somebody had drawn a horned head, broad between the horns, over three wavy strokes. Cael knew the strokes. A carter had drawn every ford on a calfskin with those three lines, and Karis had them by heart.

*The high traverse, Oxhollow to Thornwater. To be walked with a cart, and mapped. Paid at Oxhollow, on the map: seventy marks.*

Pike read it out for the meadow, and then he read it again from the first word, with his shoulder turned to the tents. There were not many to hear. The dry roads had emptied the grass to seven fires, and at most of those people were still asleep.

"Seventy," said Brom. He had come up behind them without a sound, as a large man can who has spent sixteen days with nothing in his hands. "That's more than the six lines we've got. All of them put together."

"By eight," said Karis.

"What's a high traverse?" said Lira. "In a carter's mouth. I know what it is on a hill."

"It's the same thing with a wheel on it." Pike laid his one hand on the paper. "Oxhollow's six days north-west of this plank, and Thornwater's two days east of it. A cart from the one that wants grain from the other comes round by my ferry, and it's eight days, and eight home. There's high ground between the two holds. The salt crews say there's a shelf along the top of it, a bench, with a gorge down one side. A mule string went over it once, before I'd this book, and came down in three days." He looked at the horned head, twice. "Somebody's told the ford so. The ford wants to know whether it's true before it sends a wheel."

"The ford," said Lira.

"Oxhollow's a ford with a fence round it. The keeper takes a copper a wheel, the same as my ferryman, and it's the keeper's paper. He'd sooner his wheels came back."

"And he pays at his end," said Karis. "He's left nothing in your box."

"It's six days of road, and he'd not trust seventy marks to the inside of a carter's hat. I'd not, either. That's what *on the map* means. You put the map in his hand, and he puts seventy in yours, and his mark goes on that paper under my strokes. Then you bring the paper here, and I say it."

Karis had her notebook out, and her pencil was already turning in her fingers.

"What does he call a map?" she said. "I've a registry sheet in the middle box with the whole continent on it, printed, and a calfskin that a carter drew for his own wheels. They aren't the same kind of thing. Which does he want?"

"He wants to know where a wheel goes and where it doesn't. He's a ford-keeper. He'll have no more letters than he needs to count a toll." Pike thought about it. "Draw it so a man can follow it with his finger. Put the numbers where he can find them if he's a mind to."

"A strip, then, with the shelf drawn down the middle of it, and a table beside. A line to every stretch." She was writing as she spoke. "I can rule that tonight."

Cael read the slip through once more, for the thing that was not in it. There was no name on it but the hold's. Nobody had asked to have it kept.

---

He went along the whole plank afterward, from the left-hand post to the right.

There were fourteen slips. He gave each the same attention, the bark and the hide and the two shingles, and he sorted them as he read. There was a fence to be set for a steading on the north water, at five marks, and a wheel to be fetched for a carter on the south road, and hay to be got in at the upper inn for a mark and the loft to sleep in. A string of mules wanted walking westward to the turf fire, and that slip he passed over without stopping.

When he had done, the paper with the horned head was at the top of the heap in his mind, and it had got there so quickly that he distrusted it. He made himself say why, in items, as he would have made a stranger say it.

The first was the plainest: it paid. Seventy marks was more than Pike's book had counted out to them in six lines, and the crew was still four hurts short of whole.

The second was that nobody's finger was on it. The last large slip they took had carried a captain's name in capitals. A one-armed man had struck that name with his own pen before a meadow, and they had gone down to a river under the weight of it. This one had been posted for anyone at all, with no hold on it. If they took it they would take it off nobody, and the captain at the top of the sheet could read it when he came down and find nothing in it that had been his.

He was longest over the third item, since it was not a reason of the same kind.

It was *ground*. Nothing in those three lines of careful writing had teeth; he looked for the opponent in it, from a habit as old as his reading, and found only a shelf, and a gorge, and a question about a wheel. Somebody wanted a mountain looked at closely and written down, with numbers, by people who would say what they had counted and no more. The paper named no tier and no rank. It asked for a cart and a count.

He had done that for nothing since before he could remember. He had catalogued a harness room and a painted floor and sixty feet of sprung oak, and two years of other people's fights from the east tier of a yard; and all of it had been held against him at one time or another, as the habit of a person who watched because he could do nothing else. Now a stranger with a ford was offering seventy marks for it.

It was work, besides, that four half-mended people could do. A man with a bitten calf could pace a level shelf and write. A girl with a stiff ankle could tell him whether a hundred yards of it would bear her, and a researcher who could hold a pencil for a quarter of an hour had a cord, and a clock, and a head that kept numbers as a jar keeps water. Nobody would have to be fast, or hard, or to set anything alight; the job asked for no Path from any of them, and for no fragment of his.

He wanted that job more than he had wanted the forty. It surprised him, and he did not hide it from himself, since Hesk had taught him that a figure left out of a sum did not thereby cease to be owed.

"Does it need the cart?" he said. "We've none. We could walk your bench and measure it. She has a cord, and I can pace a yard."

Pike considered him in his double fashion.

"You want to tell a ford it holds a cart, you'd better have had one on it."

He said it level, and then, because he said everything of weight twice, he gave it again a little differently.

"A cord'll tell him how wide it is. It'll not tell him what a wheel does on it. The paper says *with a cart*, and he'll ask you at his gate where yours is, and I'd have the answer ready."

"What's up there?" said Lira. "Besides a shelf."

"Weather. It's cold on that top a month before it's cold here." Pike dipped his pen. "And shale-backs. The salt crews see them from the cold road, across the valley, on the high ledges. Bigger than a cart, and they eat moss."

"Do they eat anything else?"

"Not that I've heard."

"Then why did you say it like that?"

"I said it as I say everything." But he had not, quite, and Cael saw Seln's mild eyes rest on the bald brown head for a breath longer than the answer needed.

"Hands," said Cael.

Lira's went up, and Brom's, and Karis's with the pencil in it. Seln lifted two fingers from his folded arms. Cael put up his own and took the paper off its tack, and Pike wrote five short strokes at its foot, side by side, and said the whole of it over to the grass with *taken* at the end.

---

The other four went down the meadow to find the smith, and Seln did not go with them.

Cael looked back from the ash. The eave was sixty yards behind him, and under it two men stood at the far end of the plank, where the oak ran out past the right-hand post. One of them had a book under his arm. The other stood with his head bent, as a clerk stands who has come to ask for the loan of a file. He was talking, low, and Pike was listening with his chin down.

It was the first question that Cael had ever seen Seln put to anybody in that meadow.

He knew what it would be. A word had been dropped at that plank a quarter of an hour ago, in a voice that was not quite Pike's ordinary one, and Seln collected such things as other men collected debts. He would be asking what a shale-back was, and who had been under one, and what had become of them, and he would get it in fewer sentences than anybody else could have.

Cael could have walked back, and nobody would have thought it strange. He was the nearest thing that five people had to a captain, and there was a mountain in it, and he wished very much to know.

He had told that man at that same plank, ten days ago, that nobody was coming to take his report off him. It had been easy to say, and he had learned since that the saying was the smallest part of it. The promise had to be kept at exactly such moments as this one, when the report was sixty yards off and plainly worth having, and when nobody but himself would ever know that he had turned his back on it.

He turned his back on it, and went after the others.

The smith was at his door with a rasp. He was the same square silent man who had bought a cart from them in their first week, and he heard Brom through to the end without putting the rasp down. Then he walked round the corner of his shed, and they followed him.

It stood under a lean-to roof with its shafts on the ground.

"That's ours," said Lira.

"It was," said Brom.

Cael knew every board of it. They had loaded it in a yard on a bluff with a coach standing by, and it had gone under a gate and down a road and over a ridge, and its bed had carried three boxes and a locked case under one canvas for the whole width of a province. The smith had done what he said he would. The axle had been cut and the hubs set in, so that the wheels now stood a hand's breadth inside the line of the bed on either side. The cart looked narrower in the hips, and a little surprised.

Brom went all round it slowly, as round a horse he had sold and now met on a road. He set his right hand on the off wheel and rocked it, and stooped to look along the new axle-tree, and lifted the tail-board on its two chains and let it down again without a sound.

"He's done it well," he said. "The iron's good. He's kept my seat board."

"Six," said the smith.

It was the first word he had spoken.

"A day?" said Karis.

"Against its coming home." He jerked his head at the shafts. "You lay six on my bench and take her. When she's back under this roof, that's the hire, and we're square. If she's not back, it's six more, and I'll want to hear how."

"Twelve, for a cart with a cut axle," said Lira.

"I gave you more than twelve for her."

"You did," said Brom. "It's fair. It's cheap." He glanced at Cael, and Cael nodded, and Karis wrote it.

There was the matter of the boxes, and it was settled before anybody had the chance to argue it. The ferryman's boy had come up from the round shed with a message and stood twisting his cap. The hay was coming in from the river meadows that week, he said, against the cold, and his master wanted the whole loft over the drum, every foot of it, and was sorry.

"It was dry room while we were near," said Karis. "That was the bargain, and he's kept it. We shan't be near. It's a day and a half to the foot of this, and then a mountain, and six days home from the other side." She had gone a little pale. "I can't leave them three weeks in a meadow under a net."

"Thornwater has a loft," said Lira.

"Thornwater's loft is full of the east carts' grain, and it's half a day past the place where we turn off."

"Then they come," said Brom. "It's what she's for."

So three numbered boxes went onto the cart they had come east on, left and middle and right, with the corner that was worn white turned outward where Karis could see it; and at the bottom of the third, under the masonry chips, the reserve lay in its canvas with five thumbs in the wax. Seln came down the meadow as the canvas was being roped. He looked at the cart and seemed to recognise it, and put his hand for an instant on the place under the seat where a locked case had ridden from a bluff to a river. The case was on its strap across his chest. He said nothing whatever about a plank, and Cael kept his eyes off his boots.

---

They paid the six at first light on the seventy-first day, on the smith's own bench, and Karis said the new figure aloud so that everybody should have it.

"In hand, forty-six."

The cart went up to the saddle between two people. It had no beast, for there was not a mule in Lowmarch to be hired for a mountain by a crew that might not bring it back. Seln took the near shaft and Cael the off, and they leaned, and the narrow wheels went round. On the flat of the meadow it was nothing. On the hill Cael counted nine strides and felt the calf begin to close, and at the tenth Lira's shoulder came in beside his without a word, and took the shaft, and he fell back to the tail and walked with his hand on a box until the leg forgave him.

They changed so all the way up, by nines, and nobody remarked on it.

Brom walked behind with his arms hanging.

He bore it as far as the dead tree. Then he stopped the cart with a word, and took the long rope from under the canvas, the ferryman's, which had drawn a wagon out of a wash and a boy's worth of bad air out of a well. He cut nothing. He doubled a bight of it, and knotted it, and passed the loop over his head and one shoulder so that it lay across his chest like a bandolier, and he made the two ends fast to the cart's frame on the near side, low, behind the wheel.

"What's that?" said Lira.

"A strap."

"You're not to pull."

"I'm not pulling. Look." He walked forward until the rope came taut, and then he simply went on walking, with both hands clasped behind his back like a schoolmaster, and the cart came after him. "That's my chest. She said nothing about my chest. There's not a finger on it."

"The hold's woman said not to lift."

"I've lifted nothing. I'm leaning." His whole plain face had lightened, for the first time since a river. "I leaned on a mule for five days at Thornwater and nobody stopped me."

Karis walked round him once and examined the knot, and the lie of the rope over his breastbone, and both wrists, which were behind him and idle.

"I can find nothing in it to forbid," she said. "I shall enter that I looked."

So he drew, and the two in the shafts had little to do but steer. They went over the saddle with the sun behind them and down into the grey scrub by a road they had walked five times, past the wash, which was dry and red, and they made their camp at the thorn stump on its eastern lip.

Seln lit the fire with his flint. It was the fourth they had had on a road, and Cael noticed that not one of them watched the first smoke go up; the thing had become ordinary in twelve days, and he thought that a carter with cold feet would have been glad to see it.

Karis ruled her table before the light went, on a long leaf that she had pasted up out of four. She drew two lines down the middle of it for the edges of a shelf that none of them had yet seen, and beside them six narrow columns, with a head over each in her small exact hand: the number of the stretch, its width, its pitch, the footing, water, and a last one that she left wide.

"What goes in that?" said Lira.

"Whether it takes a cart. Yes or no, and why." She blew on the ink. "It's the only column he's paying for. The other five are there so that he can believe it; and he writes them." Her pencil pointed across the fire, at Cael. "He writes small, and he has never in his life put down a number that he didn't count. I've had three pairs of hands since the river, and I know which one I want for a table."

He wrote by its light.

*Seventy-first day. The east road, above the wash. Taken at the plank yesterday: a hold's slip, Oxhollow's, for the high traverse between that hold and Thornwater. Walked with a cart and mapped. Seventy marks, paid at Oxhollow on the map. We walk it from this end and come down on his.*

*The cart is our own, hired back from the smith for six against its return. In hand, forty-six. The boxes are on it, since the ferryman wants his loft.*

*I took this one because it pays, and because nobody had asked to keep it, and because it is ground. I've put the third down last. It weighed the most.*

*The nine: held. No Path has been lifted by any of us since the ford. Deployment since the Line: one.*

"You've left out the strap," said Brom, who had read none of it and knew.

"It's a rope."

"It's a strap when it's on me." He turned a piece of bacon on a stick. "Put that I thought of it."

He put it.

---

They left the Thornwater road in the middle of the next morning, at the lower of its two crossings, and none of them had ever looked to the left there before.

A second water came into the river at that place from the north. It was no wider than a cart, and brown, and it ran out of a fold in the hills between banks of alder, and beside it there went a track. Wheels had made it, a long time ago. The ruts had grown over with a short wiry turf, and here and there a sapling as thick as a wrist stood up in the middle of the way. Seln, who was thirty paces ahead, bent each one down with his boot as he passed, and the axle went over it.

"Who came up here?" said Lira.

"Somebody who wanted stone," said Karis. "Or timber. I can't tell from a rut." She was walking at the off wheel with her eyes on the ground. "It was a loaded cart that made these, coming down. They're deeper on the low side."

The hills closed in as they climbed. The alders gave way to the red-barked trees with the paired needles, and then those thinned as well, and there was grey rock on either hand with heather growing in its cracks. The stream had gone down out of sight on their right. He could hear it, a steady rushing from somewhere below the level of the track; and then, after they had gone another half mile, he began to hear something else.

It was the cart. The off wheel had a dry place in its hub that ticked once at every turn, and he had been hearing it since the saddle without attending. Now he heard it twice. It ticked beside him, and then, about as long after as it took to draw a breath, the same small sound came again from across the valley and a little above, thin and exact.

Lira had stopped.

"Say something," she said.

"What?"

*What*, said the hill opposite, when he had almost ceased to expect it.

"It's late," said Lira, delighted. "Listen to how late it is. I've had an echo before, in the long hall at Fenmark, and it came straight back at you like a slap. This one goes away and thinks about it."

"There's a wall over there," said Seln, from the bend. He had not raised his voice, and the far side gave back only the hiss of it. "A high one. I should say three hundred yards across, and I should not care to guess how far down."

They came round the bend and saw what he was standing in front of.

The valley ended. It did not close gently, as valleys did in the country below; the two hillsides simply drew together until they were walls, and between the walls there was a slot full of blue shadow, with the stream coming out of the bottom of it white. The far wall went up sheer and dark for as high as he could tip back his head. The near one, on their own side of the water, was broken.

It was broken in one place only. For two hundred yards along its foot the mountain had been shedding its skin for longer than there had been carters. A slope of loose stone came down there out of the sky, grey and flat and steep as the roof of a barn, spreading as it came, like corn poured out of a sack; and it ended at their feet in a litter of thin slabs, none thicker than a hand, that rang under the cart's wheel like broken crockery.

He followed it up with his eyes. It narrowed as it rose, and at the top, a very long way above the red trees, it stopped against a level line. There was a shelf up there. He could see the edge of it against the light, running away westward along the gorge's lip as straight as a ruled margin, until the rock took it.

"That's the bench," said Karis softly.

"And that's the road to it," said Brom.

He had slipped the rope from his shoulder, and he stood with his head back and his mouth a little open, looking at two hundred yards and more of stone that would slide if a goat sneezed on it.

"Can it be done?" said Lira.

"I don't know yet. I've not put my foot on it."

Seln had not looked up at all. He was standing a little way out on the foot of the slope, where the slabs lay thickest, and he had bent and lifted one of them in both hands.

It was grey, like the rest, and the size of a shield, and somewhat curved. He turned it to the light. The others had broken edges, sharp and pale. This one's edge was rounded and thick, and along its upper face there ran a low ridge like the keel of a boat, and on the ridge a green crust of lichen was growing.

He rapped it with his knuckle, and it did not ring.

"Karis," he said. "I think that you will want to see this. It is not a stone."

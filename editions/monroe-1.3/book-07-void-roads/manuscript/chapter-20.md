# Chapter 20 — Who's Worst

They got the plate off her with a hedging bill and a man's back.

Brom would not have Cael touch it, and for once there was no arguing with him, since Cael could not have lifted a kettle. So Seln drove the ash haft of the bill under the slab's edge and bore down on it, and Brom knelt in the shale and got his right shoulder beneath the corner that rose. He kept the left arm folded against his chest. The stone came up by the breadth of a hand, grating, and the whole slope behind it stirred.

"Now," said Brom, through his teeth. "Whoever's got her."

Cael had her wrists. It was all that was left for him to do, and he could do it sitting, with his back against the pale rock. He drew, and she pushed with her elbows, and she came toward him over the plates a foot at a time with her face shut like a fist.

The left leg came out whole, and the right came out without its boot.

He saw the boot for a breath, down in the dark under the slab, with the lace still tied. Then Brom let the corner go, because he had to, and the plate settled, and the shale ran in round it and over it with a long sigh. By the time the dust had gone there was nothing in that place but hill.

"That was a good boot," said Lira. Her voice was very small. "I'd only just got it to stop rubbing."

They carried her round the end of the wall onto the bare floor. Nobody remarked on the leg until she was lying flat, and then there was no need to. Below the knee the stocking was whole, and under it the shin took a small wrong turn, a hand's breadth down, where no shin has a joint.

"It's not come through," said Brom. He had looked at a good many hurts on a good many yards, and he said it as the one thing in sight that was better than it might have been. "I'll not pull it straight, for I don't know how, and I'd do it badly. But it wants keeping as it is, till there's somebody who does."

He chose the splints himself, and they were the mountain's own; he found two plates in the bank above the wall, each as long as her shin and no thicker than a slate, and he laid one down either side of the leg. They had no canvas to tear, for the canvas had gone with the cart. Cael's coat had lost its left sleeve at a river. He got the knife into the seam of the right one, and Seln cut that as well, and Brom bound the two plates on with it in three places, one-handed, with Seln's fingers for the knots.

"There," he said. "You've a roof on."

"Like them," said Lira, and shut her eyes.

Cael took his inventory then, because it was the start of something, and because he could not think what else to do with the fear.

There were three ribs. He knew the number without touching them; he had heard it. They were low on the left side, and they let him breathe in to a certain depth and then they stopped him, quite firmly, as a hand stops a door. The calf had opened along the line of the old bite. Seln had taken off his own neckcloth and bound it, and the cloth was already dark.

Brom's forearm had parted at the wrist for the length of a finger, where it had parted once before above a wash.

Karis sat where she had sat down, and she had spoken twice since then and no more. When Seln went to her and put his coat round her shoulders she let him, and her eyes followed his hands as though they were a long way off.

There were three boxes. He counted them a second time, since they had cost what they had cost. In the third, under the masonry chips, there was a bag that had not been opened since the twelfth day, and it would buy nothing whatever on this shelf.

Then he counted what they had to eat, and that took less time. Brom had slung the bread bag on his own back that morning, from habit, and it held a loaf and a half and the hard end of the bacon. Seln had his flint, a twist of salt, and a water-skin a third full. Everything else had been on the cart.

---

Karis could not light the lamp.

It was her own, the small brass reading lantern that lived in the left-hand box with her pens, and when the dusk came down she asked for it. They were the first words she had said since morning. Seln set it in front of her with the flint and steel beside it, and she took one in each hand, as she had taken them a thousand times.

Her hands would not do it. He watched her bring the steel down on the flint, and there was no strength in the stroke at all; it slid off, and no spark came. She tried again, with no better result, and the third time the flint dropped out of her fingers onto the floor, and she sat looking at it.

"I've set nothing, and I want that understood," she said. "I didn't try a point; I only looked for the place I'd have set one from." She turned her right hand over. "It's like feeling in a coat for a pocket, and finding that somebody has taken the pocket out."

"It'll come back," said Brom.

"You don't know that, and I don't know it either. I've never spent it all before; nobody at Ternhall would have let me." Her voice had no fright in it, and that was worse than fright. "Nine points and eight channels, held for eight seconds across sixty yards. I should be able to give you the cost of that to a decimal. I can't find the figure. I can't find where I keep figures."

Brom picked up the flint and lit the lamp for her, with two strokes, and set it where the light fell on her knees. She thanked him. Then she lay down on her side with her back to the wall, in Seln's coat, and was asleep before he had drawn his hand away.

It was a cold night, and they had no blankets; the frost came down out of a clear sky, and they lay close under the rock in a row, the hurt ones in the middle, and kept one another warm as cattle do.

Cael did not sleep much. He could not lie on his left side, and on his right the ribs hung, and so he sat against the stone and watched the bay by starlight.

The herd was still there, though he could not have said how many of it. They stood here and there on the level, among the spilled shale, grey on grey, and through the first hours of the dark he heard them moving, a few slow steps and a clatter of horn and then quiet. By the middle of the night the sounds had spread themselves out along the foot of the face into something very like a line, and toward morning one of them began to scrape.

They had lost their hill and some of their number, and by the next light they would be mowing what was left. He could not hate them for it. They had run from a noise, as anything runs.

Above the wall, in the dark, a plate let go by itself and slid, and stopped; and a little later another.

"It's still coming," said Lira.

He had thought her asleep. She was lying on her back with her right leg laid straight in its two slates and her face turned up to the stars.

"A bit at a time. I've been counting them, and that's eleven since the lamp went out."

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes." She thought about it. "It's a different hurt from the ankle. That one was a thing I'd done, and this is only a thing that's there."

He knew what she was telling him. He had given her the order of it at the wall's end, the ledge and then herself, and she had been turning it over ever since to see whether it would bear.

"Karis turned thirty animals with a page she'd not tested," said Lira presently. "And Brom carried two boxes he's not allowed to lift. And you went back for the third and stood under one of those things with your hands shut. I saw the marks where you came down." A plate slid, somewhere above. "I ran away from it, and I was the only one who did what I was supposed to."

"You ran a hundred yards on a late foot without bursting. With a herd coming."

"I know what I did. I'm saying it's a queer morning when the one who made the noise is the one who kept the rule."

"That's twelve," said Cael.

She breathed out, and it was nearly a laugh.

---

It rained on the seventy-ninth day, a thin cold rain out of the west, and nobody wrote.

He had the book, for Hesk's satchel had been on his shoulder when the mountain came, as it was always on his shoulder, and it had gone under him when he fell and taken no harm. He opened it once, in the grey of the morning, and found the page with the last entry. It ended on the word *Sleep*, and above that he had written that the job had no fight at the end of it.

He read it through, and he was not ashamed of it, for it had been true when the pencil made it; but he could find no sentence to set beneath it that was a thing counted, and he would not put down one that was only a thing felt. He shut the book, and that day got no line either.

Karis made no move toward her notebook either. She slept, and woke to drink, and slept again; and each time she woke she looked at her two hands before she looked at anything else.

Brom had the bread.

He had taken charge of it without a word, and he made a ceremony of the dividing that was plainly meant to give five people something to watch. He cut the loaf and a half into fifteen with Lira's long knife, on the lid of the middle box, and laid the pieces out in three rows.

"That's today, and tomorrow, and the day after. Five a row." He pointed with the knife. "The bacon's for whoever's walking. I've done it in my head six ways and that's the one that comes out even."

"And the day after that?" said Lira.

"The day after that somebody's walked."

It was Seln who would walk, and they all knew it. He was the only one of the five with two legs, two hands and his wits about him, and he had spent the first night without a coat, saying nothing of it.

He went for water first. The seep was at the bay's eastern corner, a hundred and thirty yards from the rock, and every yard of the way lay under the stripped face, where the plates were still coming down singly, with no warning but a small dry tick. Cael watched him cross it without once hurrying. He stopped whenever a slide began, and judged its line, and stood still until it had gone by, sometimes within a few feet of him; and he came back in the rain with the skin full and grey grit to his knees.

"I should not send anybody else," was all he said. "It wants a kind of patience that is unpleasant to learn."

In the afternoon he went the other way.

He was gone three hours. When he came round the end of the wall the light was failing, and he sat down on his heels in front of Cael, and Cael understood that he was being given a report, unasked, since it bore.

"The ledge is whole for eighty yards from the bay," said Seln. "Beyond that it has gone, for thirty-one paces; I counted them, since I had to place a foot for each. What is left there is not a shelf. It is a lip of rock, where the shelf was rooted in the wall; four feet wide at the best of it, and tilted outward, and covered with what fell on it."

"Does it go?"

"It went for me, and I crossed it twice. Some of the stones on it are bedded and some are waiting to leave, and I could not tell the one from the other by looking; so I tried each with my weight before I trusted it, and I scratched a mark on every one that bore." He took out the narrow dull knife and showed its point, which was bright. "There are forty-four. A careful person may go over on those, facing the wall."

"A person with a leg in slates?" said Lira.

"No."

"Or three ribs?"

Seln considered Cael, and did not soften it.

"One at a time, perhaps, with a hand on his belt. Not this week." He put the knife away. "Beyond the lip the ledge is sound again to the shoulder, and from the shoulder a track goes down, an old one, steep. I went a little way. There is a road in the valley and I saw nobody on it. I judge it a day and a half to the road for a man alone, and I cannot say how far along the road to a roof."

So it was settled as Brom's bread had settled it. Seln would go down at first light with the bacon, and bring back whatever he found that had hands. The four would stay under the rock. Nobody said aloud how many rows there were on the lid of the box.

Lira's leg was hot that night. Cael felt it through the stocking when he reached across in the dark to find the water for her; the skin between the two slates burned like a stone that has lain in the sun, and she was shivering with her teeth shut.

"Don't count them tonight," he said.

"I'm at six," said Lira. "It keeps my mind off."

---

He saw the mule before he saw the woman.

It was the middle of the eightieth day, and the rain had gone through, and he had got himself propped at the western end of the rock, where he could watch the ledge. He could do that much. He had been watching it since Seln went out along it in the first grey light, a narrow dark figure under the overhang, and passed from sight where the shelf was broken.

Now something was coming back.

It was Seln, and he was walking in front, at an easy pace, with his hands clasped behind him; and behind Seln there were two long brown ears, and under the ears a small neat mule with a pack on it as high as its own back. And beside the mule, on the outer side of it, between the animal and three hundred feet of air, there walked a woman.

Cael read her as he read anybody, from the feet.

She walked at a carter's pace. It was the pace that he had fallen in behind on the fifth day, which is neither quick nor slow and can be kept from dark to dark; she did not lengthen it when the bay opened in front of her, or when she saw four people lying under a rock. She had one hand laid on the mule's shoulder. She kept herself on the side where the drop was and the beast on the side where the wall was, and when the ledge widened she did not change over.

She was not tall, but she was square in the shoulder and broad in the hand, and she wore a man's coat of some thick brown stuff that had been made for a bigger person and taken in. Her hair was cropped as short as a boy's. He thought that she was perhaps ten years older than himself, and he could not have said why he was sure it was not more.

On top of the pack, lashed along it in an oiled cover, there was something with the shape of a crossbow.

She brought the mule across the shale to the end of the wall and stopped it with a word, a stride and a half from where Lira lay. Her eyes went over the five of them a single time, and it did not take her as long as it takes to count five. He saw her eyes go to the slates on a leg, and to a dark cloth at a wrist, and to the way that he himself was sitting, with one arm shut against his side; and they rested longest on Karis, who had nothing wrong with her that could be seen.

"Who's worst?"

It was a low voice, and a little hoarse, like a voice that was not much used on the road.

She had not asked who they were, or what had happened to them, or how five people came to be lying under a rock at the top of a mountain with three boxes. Cael noticed that. He had been met at gates and counters and tables for as long as he could remember, and at every one of them the first question had been what he was; this woman had walked a day and a half up a hill to put a different one, and it was the only one she appeared to want answered.

"Her," said Brom, and pointed at Lira.

"I'm fine," said Lira.

"You're not. I can see your leg from here." The woman was already kneeling. She had Cael's coat sleeve unknotted in the time it took to say it, and she lifted off the upper slate and looked at what was under it without touching. "Who put these on?"

"I did," said Brom.

"They've kept it. That's all they were asked to do." She glanced up at Lira's face, as one looks at the head of a horse before going round behind it. "Name's Oryn."

"Are you a healer?" said Lira. Her teeth were knocking together. "A woman at a gate said there wasn't one. She said the nearest walked a route."

"Tide. Healer."

"She said the rest of you keep to the country where somebody ranks you."

"Most do."

"Do they rank you?"

The woman had laid two fingers on the shin, a hand's breadth below the knee, as lightly as on the string of an instrument.

"Iron. Four, if anybody's keeping a book up here."

"Nobody is," said Lira.

"I shall be," said a faint voice from under Seln's coat, "as soon as I can hold the pencil."

---

She took off the second slate, and then she put both hands round the leg and shut her eyes.

Her lips moved. Cael watched them, having nothing else to do, and found that she was counting; she went to ten, slowly, and stopped, and opened her eyes.

"It's broken through, clean, a hand below the knee," she said. "The one bone, and the other's whole. The ends have slipped by each other the width of my thumb. I have to bring them back to where they belong before I mend it, or I mend it short, and you walk on that for the rest of your life." She looked at Brom. "You. Behind her. Take her under the arms and don't let her come with me."

Brom got down on the floor and took Lira against his chest.

"That's the part you'll mind."

She said it to Lira, plainly, as a ferryman says that the next step is wet. Then she set one hand above the place and one at the ankle, over the dirty linen, and leaned back with her whole weight, steadily, as one draws a bucket.

Lira made one sound, and it was not loud. Thirty yards off, out on the level, a beaked head came up and went down again.

"There," said Oryn. "That's it over. You'll not get worse than that from me."

She did not let go. She shifted her hands until both of them lay round the shin where the wrong turn had been, with the fingers overlapping, and she settled on her heels, and her face went quiet.

There was nothing to see. He had half expected a light, or a colour, as there was grey in Iron Skin and yellow in a point; and there was a square brown woman kneeling in the dust with her hands on a girl's leg, breathing slowly. A minute went by, and another, and the only thing that changed was that the sweat came out on her forehead, though the air was cold.

"What are you doing?" said Karis. She had got up on one elbow. "I want it described, for I can't write, but I can remember till I can."

"Finding where it's stopped."

"Where what has?"

"What she mends with. Everybody's got it. It goes round you like water round a mill, and when you break a bone it does the knitting, if you leave it be for six weeks." Oryn spoke in pieces, between breaths, with her eyes shut. "At a break it stands, since it can't get by, and it piles up, and that's the heat you felt in this last night. I find where it's standing. Then I open the way with mine, and hers goes through, and I hold it open while it does the six weeks."

"All of the six weeks?"

"All of them. That's what I pay. It doesn't come from nowhere."

"Cold," said Lira suddenly.

Her eyes had opened wide, and she was staring at the pale rock over her head.

"No. It's going along."

"Then lie still and let it," said Oryn.

It took the greater part of an hour, and he counted it by the shadow of the wall, which crept the width of two hands across the floor. After the first of it the hollow was silent. Brom held Lira as he would have held a full pail, and Seln stood at the mule's head with the rein in his fingers; and Cael watched a stranger's face, and saw it grow a little older while he looked.

When she took her hands away, the shin was straight.

Lira lifted her head to see it. She moved the toes of her right foot, in the torn stocking, all five.

Oryn did not get up. She turned herself about on the floor, slowly, and let herself down with her back against the rock, a little at a time, as a carter sets down a load whose weight he has known for years.

"That's one," she said.

"One what?" said Brom.

"One mending. A Tide has a reserve, like a purse, and it's spent at the rate of what it mends. Mine holds three in a day, if I've slept under a roof and somebody's fed me, and on a road it holds two." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "After that the reserve's gone, and I'm a woman with a mule."

"Could you do his from where you sit?" said Lira. She was looking at Brom's wrist. "So you needn't move."

"Hands on. That's the only way it works."

She was unlacing her own right boot as she spoke. She drew it off, and the stocking after it, and turned the foot to look. The heel had been rubbed raw in a patch the size of a coin, and over the patch there was a strip of some stiff linen stuck down smooth.

"And I can't do it to me."

She pressed the strip smooth with her thumb and put the stocking on again.

---

She ate before she touched anybody else.

It was her own bread, out of the mule's off pannier, a dark close loaf from some hold's oven, and she broke it in six and gave five pieces away before she bit into hers. There was a cheese as well, and four onions. Brom looked at the three rows on the lid of the box, and then at the pannier, and swept the rows back into his bag without a word.

Then she had his arm across her knee.

"How old's this?"

"Twenty-seven days. I counted the stitches in and I counted them out."

"Then it should have closed in a month and hasn't, because you've pulled on it."

"I carried," said Brom. "Mostly."

"You pulled this week. I can feel where." She had her hands round the wrist already, and it took her a quarter of the time that the leg had taken. When she let go there was a seam on it, pink and dry, from the heel of the hand to the middle of the forearm, and it lay closed along its whole length. "That's two, and that's my day. You'll want telling what it's good for, and I'll tell you tomorrow, when I can think."

She went to Karis last of the three, on her knees, and read her as she had read the leg, with two fingers on the inside of a thin wrist and two on the side of the neck. Her lips moved to ten.

"Ember. And nothing. You're empty."

"I know the word. I didn't know that it could be felt."

"It's like a well you drop a stone in, and hear it land on the bottom dry." She sat back. "What did you do?"

Nobody answered at once. Then Seln lifted his chin at the slope above the wall.

The shale still lay banked there, in the shelter of the rock, where neither stream had carried it off; and across it, from a point high up down to either hand, ran two straight black lines. The plates along them had been burnt to the colour of iron.

Oryn looked at that for some time.

"How long did you hold it?"

"Eight seconds," said Cael. "I counted."

"Hm."

It was all she said of it, and she turned back to Karis.

"That isn't mine to mend, for nothing's broken. It fills again or it doesn't, and it fills quicker asleep and fed. Don't ask it for so much as a spark for a week." She got to her feet with a hand on the wall, and her eyes went to Seln. "Those were your scratches, on the lip."

"They were."

"They were right. I put the mule's feet on four of them."

Seln inclined his head. He looked, Cael thought, like a man who has had a report read by somebody competent, and it had been a long time since that had happened to him.

"Can I stand on it?" said Lira. She had been flexing the foot all this while, a little at a time, with the wonder of a person who has been given back a thing she had already begun to grieve for.

"Tomorrow, with somebody's arm. It's knit, and it's sound, but it's new, and new bone's like new bread; it wants a day before you cut it." Oryn looked down at the leg as a wheelwright looks at a spoke he has just driven. "It'll walk in a week as well as the other. You'll not run on it for a month, and I can see by your face that you were about to ask."

"I wasn't," said Lira, who had been.

---

She came to Cael when the others were eating.

"You sat up to watch me come in," she said. "So you weren't worst. Coat."

He got it open with one hand. She knelt on his left, and her fingers went in under the shirt and lay along his side, low, where the three small sounds had been; they were warm and very steady, and her lips began to move.

He counted with her. *One. Two.* It was the first time since he crossed the Line that anybody had laid a hand on him to learn what he was. *Three. Four.*

At five the reading stopped.

She took both hands away and held them palm upward in front of her, and studied them. It was the look of a carpenter whose rule has just given him a length that cannot be. Then she wiped them on her coat, and breathed, and put them back on his side, with the fingers spread wider; and this time she did not count at all.

He watched her face, and he knew what it was doing, for he had seen a brass plate in a district hall wait eleven seconds over a boy of fourteen before it lit. He had seen assessors look at a panel with that same small stillness, and a hooded seat, and a clerk with a form that had no box for him. It was what an instrument looked like when it reached for the thing it was made to read, and closed on air.

She was not an instrument, and that was the difference. She took her hands away a second time and sat back on her heels, and she looked straight at him.

"Your ribs are broken," she said. "I can feel that they're broken. I can't feel *you*."

Behind her nobody was eating any longer. Lira had come up on both elbows, and Brom sat with the bread halfway to his mouth; Seln's mild eyes were on the back of the healer's cropped head, and under his coat Karis had gone perfectly still.

"Whatever you are, I can't read it."

He waited for the next thing, since he knew the order in which it came. First the instrument failed, and then somebody asked him what he was, and wrote the answer down, and the answer became a word on a file.

The question did not come.

"I've never said that to anyone. Tape them. They'll heal on their own."

She was frowning at his side, as at a sum that had come out oddly, and she spoke the rest as though she were reading it off the bone.

"They always have, haven't they?"

"Yes," he said.

"That's not an answer," Oryn said. "That's a *finding*."

He knew that word. It was Karis's, and she kept it for a thing that had been looked at carefully and written down with nothing added. He had been a good many words in his time. He had been a flag on a board and a level on an advisory, and one word in capitals that a registry had settled on when it could find no tier; and every one of them had been a verdict, given by people who were angry with him for not fitting. This woman's hands had come back empty twice, and she had only said so, and put a name on the emptiness that made no claim about him at all.

She got up and fetched clean cloth from her pannier, and a flask; and she unwound Seln's neckcloth from his calf and washed what was under it, and bound it again in her own linen, tight and neat. She put no hand round it to count.

"I can't mend what's in you, since I can't find where it's standing," she said. "So you get what a carter gets. Cloth, and tape in the morning." She knotted the end. "A man at the foot of this hill told me the mountain had come down two days back, and that a crew off the Lowmarch plank had gone up it with a cart and not come off the other side. He said you were late."

"We were," said Brom.

It was dusk before Cael opened the book, and it was easier than it had been in the rain. He had five things that were counted.

*Eightieth day. Lira's leg is straight. The boxes are ours. The cart is in the gorge. A healer came up the road because a carter said we were late.*

He looked at that, and added what belonged with it.

*The herd's dead: heard, not sworn, which is Karis's wording. She cannot hold a pencil again, and will not be able to set a point for a week.*

*The healer put her hands on my ribs and could not read me. She told me so to my face, and asked nothing.*

*Deployment since the Line: one.*

On the far side of the hollow the healer was asleep already. She slept sitting up, against the mule's pack, with one boot off and her hands turned palm upward on her knees.

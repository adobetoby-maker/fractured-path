# Chapter 27 — The Rise

Karis made her long cord along Thornwater's fence in the first grey light, with Brom to do the knots.

Two of the three boxes were to stay in the loft, and so two of the three box-ropes were free. Brom married them end to end with a splice that he worked by feel, and hung the whole length from a fence-post with a stone on its end to take the stretch out, while she stood by with her short cord, the one that was knotted at every foot. When the rope had done creaking she laid the short cord along it again and again, and at each length she tied in a scrap of red wool from the hold's mending basket. Cael watched her count. She told nobody the figure, and wrote it in her notebook with her shoulder turned, as a person writes down the weight of his own purse.

"It's a cord now," she said, coiling it. "It was two ropes, and they are not to be thought of as ropes again until I have done with them. Nobody is to tie a box with this."

"What do I tie the box with?" said Brom.

"You carry it. You have told me for a month that carrying is a different thing."

The middle box stood open on the barn floor, and she had been at the others since before he woke. She had the lid off the third. He came up the ladder in time to see her lifting out the masonry chips, each in its twist of paper with a number inked on the twist, and laying them in a row on a sack; and when the last was out there was a flat canvas bag at the bottom of the box, sewn shut and sealed, with five thumb-marks in the wax.

She looked at that for a breath. Then she put the lid on over it, and drove the pegs home with the heel of her hand.

"Those stay," she said. "The chips come. I have a piece of every sort of dressed stone that I could reach between Denvash and the Line, and a date for each, and if there is so much as a doorstep at this place, I mean to know which of them it matches."

So the middle box went east on Brom's back, with the chips in it, and the lamp, and the calfskin and the printed sheet, and a quire of clean paper. He knelt and had it set on him, and passed a strap of plaited sacking over his chest, and stood up. He had refused to let anyone take a turn. Cael had seen that look on him at the edge of a moss.

Oryn was waiting at the east gate with the mule.

She had the two panniers on, and the hold's woman had roped a roll of six grey blankets across the pack, behind the long shape in its oiled cover, with instructions that they were to come back. The healer stood on the mule's far side from the hill. She had slept, by the look of her, and she gave the five of them one glance from her stride and a half, each in turn, and seemed to find nothing in it that wanted her hands.

"You'll walk at her pace," she said. "It's the only one I've got."

The bowman had come along the fence to that gate. It was not his; he kept the western one, and Cael had never seen him ten yards from his platform. He stood with his bow at the length of his arm and looked at the road going up the valley, and then at Lira, and he lifted two fingers from the stave.

Lira had a boot in each hand. One was the carter's left, which she had found standing outside the barn door at first light. She hung it on the mule's pack by its laces, with great care, against the other one going.

Karis stopped under the lintel. She took the printed sheet out of the box on Brom's back without asking him to kneel, and unrolled the eastern edge of it across her forearm, and found the third of seven dashed roads with her finger.

"It ends here," she said. "At about this fence, or an hour beyond; the plate isn't fine enough to say. I have carried this sheet for ninety-four days in case it should be of use, and from this gate on it is a clean piece of paper."

She rolled it, and put it back in the box.

They came to the fork an hour out. The valley divided there round a spur of hill, and one road went away up the left-hand water toward a smoke that he could just see. The right-hand branch climbed to a low place in the skyline and went over. It was a road like any other, two pale ruts with a ridge of grass between. There was nothing at its mouth to say what it was.

"That's the Fallow," said Oryn, and clicked her tongue to the mule.

---

He read the second letter at noon, on the far side of the hills, since it was a noon with nothing in it.

They had halted on a level of short turf where a spring came out. Brom had been helped off with the box and lay on his back beside it with his arms spread. The others had gone to the water. Lira had not; she had sat down next to Cael, and stretched out both legs, and was looking at Hesk's book where it lay on his knee.

"You've still got one," she said. "I can see the thread."

"I was going to read it now."

"Read it to me. You read me his."

He drew out the square of stiff paper. The waxed thread was tied in a flat knot that he remembered from the corners of a ledger, and when he slipped it the leaf opened along two folds. It was ruled in faint red, in columns, for figures. She had written straight across the columns, in a hand as plain as print.

He read it aloud, and it did not take long.

*Filed with your record. It reads: left in good standing, undefeated on paper. I've kept this ledger forty years and never had a line like it. The session still stands. The road still runs through Ardenmere. — V.*

Lira began to laugh before he had reached the end.

"*Undefeated on paper.* I've had you on your back with a lath more times than I've had breakfast."

"She doesn't keep a column for laths."

"No. I don't suppose she does." She took the leaf from him and read it through again herself, with her lips moving, and Cael watched her come to the end of it and go back two sentences. "She's got a book like the old man's, hasn't she? She writes down what happened. And she's put you in it as a thing that she's never had to write before."

"She had me in it before anybody would give me a number. I asked Pike once whether he'd heard of her."

"Had he?"

"He'd never heard of the town."

He had thought about that on the forty-ninth night, and it came back to him now on a turf bank two days past a fence. There was a woman in Ardenmere who kept a straight ledger of a fighting yard, and a one-armed man in a meadow who kept a straight book of a plank; and neither had the least idea that the other was alive. He was written in both. He did not think that any registry on the continent could have said as much of one of its own.

Lira had her finger on the last line but one.

"What session?"

He did not answer at once.

"I know what she's speaking of. I don't know what she means by saying it now, on a ledger leaf, to somebody who's past the Line." He took the paper back, and folded it along its two creases. "It's hers. I'd tell you if it were mine. I only keep it."

"You're not going to guess."

"I've been guessed at."

She let that be. It was one of the things that she did, and that almost nobody else had ever done for him. She leaned back on her hands and looked up the road, where it ran on eastward over the turf, as straight as a ruled margin.

"She puts the thing that decides it last."

He looked at the leaf. *The road still runs through Ardenmere.*

"So does Ephram."

"He put Rooke last."

"He put what Rooke wouldn't say last. It comes to the same." He tied the thread again, in the knot that it had come in. "They've each of them written to tell me that a door's open, and neither would be seen holding it."

"That's two," said Lira. "And an old man who wants to hear that you've a roof. You've more people behind you than you had when you'd a bed to sleep in."

He put the leaf inside the book, beside the other. There was nothing left now with a seal on it. He did not mind that either, since both of them had turned out to be the kind of letter that keeps.

---

The Fallow road ran east for the rest of that day, and it was the emptiest country that he had ever walked.

There was grass. That was nearly the whole of it. It began at the height of his ankle, on the turf by the spring, and by the middle of the afternoon it stood to his knee, and before dusk to his hip: pale stuff, the colour of dry straw, with a seed-head that hissed against his coat. No stock had cropped it. There was not a fence in sight, or a smoke, or a tree, only long slow swells of ground going away to a sky that seemed to begin at his boots. The wind came over it from the north with nothing in its way, and laid the whole of it over in one direction, like the nap of a cloth under a hand.

"Why does nobody graze this?" said Brom. He had the box up again, and he spoke without turning his head. "There's feed here for a thousand head."

"The holds either side won't put a beast on it," said Oryn. "I've asked. They don't give you a reason. They say it's fallow, as you'd say a field was resting, and then they talk about something else."

"For how long has it been resting?"

"Longer than anybody I've had my hands on."

They walked it quiet. That was the hold, as it was on any road, and here it needed no telling. Seln went fifty paces ahead and never once looked back. The ruts were plain enough; the grain carts had used them within the month, and here and there he found the mark of a small neat hoof, and beside it, on the low side, a boot.

He watched the healer walk, since it had become a habit. She kept the carter's pace. She was below the mule on every slope, though the slopes were so gentle here that he could not always have said which side was the low one, and she changed over without seeming to think. He set himself to her beat, and breathed to the tape, and for long stretches of that afternoon the tape had nothing to say to him.

They had a fire at dark, in a fold of ground, lit with Seln's flint.

"One more night of that," said Seln, feeding it. "I should not light one tomorrow."

"Because of the dogs," said Lira.

"Because I do not know what is there, and a flame is seen a long way over grass." He sat back. "It is the same answer."

Oryn had taken off her boot to look at her heel. She pressed the strip flat with her thumb, and then, with the boot still in her hand, she looked across the fire at Brom.

"Give me that wrist. The one I did."

He gave it, and she laid two fingers on the inside of it and two on his neck, and her lips moved to ten.

"That's there," she said. "It's sound, and it's yours, and I'd know it in the dark by now. I'll want it again the day after tomorrow, at this ring, so don't do anything to it."

"What for?" said Brom.

"To see whether I lose it." She let him go. "I can't tell whether my hands have stopped unless I've got them on something I know. I'm not putting them on a stranger's ribs at a place like that and guessing."

Karis had her notebook open, and her pencil was not moving. Cael knew what that stillness cost her. A primary source was sitting across a fire from her, pulling on a boot, and had been round the place thirty times.

"What do the carters say?" she asked. "I had it from you at the long house door, in a sentence. I should like it as they give it, if you'll tell me. I shan't ask twice."

"They say a healer's no good there. That's the words. *You'll not want her in the ring; she's no good there.* I had it first from a carter at the first gate I ever mended at, and I took it that he meant I'd be frightened." Oryn laced the boot. "Then I had it from six more, in the same words, and not one of them a man to be frightened of a field. So it's a thing that was seen once, by somebody, and handed down."

"Seen how? A healer went in, and tried to mend, and could not?"

"That's what it'd have to be. Nobody can tell me who, or when, or what was broken." She shrugged, heavily. "A hold keeps what it needs of a thing. It needed *don't*. It didn't need the rest, so the rest is gone."

Karis wrote that down, and underlined nothing. He saw her look at what she had written, and then at the dark beyond the firelight to the east, with an expression that he knew. She had been given a rule, and under the rule there had once been a finding, and somebody had thrown the finding away.

"The day after tomorrow," she said, half to herself, "there will be six of us who saw it. I mean to write down who."

---

They came to the rise late on the ninety-fifth day, with the sun low at their backs and their shadows going on ahead of them up the road.

It was a long swell of ground, no steeper than a ramp, and the ruts climbed it in a straight line. Seln had stopped at the top. Cael saw him against the sky, a narrow dark figure with his hands behind him, and he did not move as they came up. When they stood beside him they saw why.

The road turned. He had walked it for two days as straight as a plumb-line, and here, at the crest, both ruts swung out to the left together, toward the north, in a wide easy curve, like a stream going round a rock; and a long way off, beyond the curve's far end, he could see them come back and go on eastward along the line that they had left.

Straight in front, where the old line would have run, there was only grass. It lay over in the wind, unbroken, down a gentle slope and up another.

On that second slope stood the ring.

It was three hundred yards off, as near as he could judge. There were grey stones standing on end, close together, in a rough circle; he counted them, and had eleven. No two were of a height. One was as tall as two men, and the one beside it came no higher than a knee, and all of them ended in a break. Between their feet lay something pale and level that he could not make out.

"That's it," said Oryn. She had halted the mule. "I've never been nearer than these ruts."

Cael looked at the ground between the turn of the road and the stones, and it told him nothing. There was no ditch, and no bank, and no change in the grass. A carter of twenty-two years had drawn that curve on a calfskin and put nothing inside it, and Cael could not have said, standing where the man must have stood, how the man had known where to bend his wheels.

"I should like to learn where it begins," said Seln, "before I lie down beside it. There is perhaps an hour of light."

"Nothing declared," said Karis quickly. "By anyone. That's the hold, and it is my condition as well; I want to know what is felt, and I can't know that if somebody is doing something at the time." She was looking along the old line. "We go abreast, an arm's length apart, at a slow walk, so that we all come to it together. If you feel a thing, put your hand up, and say what it is like. Don't say anything before."

"And if we feel nothing?" said Brom.

"Then we shall have walked to a ring of stones, and a one-armed man has been wrong for thirty years."

They left the mule at the turn, with her rein under a rock. Brom knelt out from under the box and set it beside her. Then the six of them spread across the old line, in a row, and Cael took the right-hand end. Lira was next to him, and then Brom, and Karis, and the healer; Seln had the far end, on the north.

They walked east.

He counted strides, since somebody would want the number. And he did what a one-armed man had told them to do, though it had not been said to him. He turned his attention inward, to the place behind his breastbone where a small bright thing had gathered on the morning of his Kindling. He had gone looking for it a few times since, in the first months, on bad nights. He looked now. It was as it had always been: a dark place, like a niche in a wall where a lamp once stood lit.

*Seventy-eight. Seventy-nine.*

At the eightieth stride Lira's hand went up.

She had stopped dead, on both feet, as she had taught herself to stop on a gravel bar. Her head was a little on one side.

"It's like the air got thick."

Brom had halted in the same instant. He was frowning at the grass in front of his boots, and his big hands had come a little away from his sides.

"Like a door closing behind me."

Cael looked along the row. The healer had not spoken at all. Both her hands had risen, half way, with the palms turned up and the fingers spread, as a person holds them out to learn whether it has begun to rain; and she was staring at them.

"My Arbiter's gone."

That was Karis. Her voice was quite level, and her face had no colour in it whatever.

"Back up. Ten paces. Tell me when it comes back."

Seln had not raised his voice. He stood at the north end of the row with his hands at his sides, for once, and Cael had never before heard him give an order as though he expected to be obeyed.

They stepped back.

"Back," said Lira; and Brom said it with her, and Karis, and the healer, and Seln himself, a breath behind the rest. It was five voices on the one word. Not one of them had taken a second step.

"Same stride. All of us."

Karis was looking down the row, from face to face. Then she went forward again by herself, a foot at a time, with her hand held out in front of her as though she expected to touch something; and at a certain place she stopped, and drew back her toe by the width of a hand, and put it out again. She bent, and felt about in the roots of the grass, and came up with a grey stone the size of a loaf.

She set it down on the place.

Cael had gone forward the eighty strides with them, and back the one. He stood now on the near side of a stone, with five people who had each lost a thing and had it given back, and nothing in him had altered at either step. The dark place was dark. He had looked at it going over and looked at it coming back, and he could not have told the two apart.

The nine fragments lay in him where they had lain since a ford. He was sure of that as well. When Lira's hand went up, Wind had leaned in his own feet to go with hers, as it had leaned every day for two years, and he had put it down without thinking; and that had been on the far side of where the stone now stood. Anchor was asking about the stone already. *There?*

He did not know what it meant.

And nobody had turned to him. He saw why, and it was the simplest thing in the world. All four of them knew what he was. They had known for years that a brass plate had waited eleven seconds over him and gone dark, and that nothing had spoken to him out of that dark since. A line that put out a lamp had nothing in him to put out. It had not occurred to one of them to ask whether he had felt it, any more than they would have asked a man without a purse whether he had been robbed. The fifth knew nothing of brass plates, and she was looking at her own two hands.

So there were two reasons, and he looked at each of them squarely, standing in the long light. He did not know what he would be telling them. And he had not been asked.

Neither one was a good enough reason by itself. He knew that. He kept silent on the pair of them together, and his own shadow lay out in front of him across a grey stone and on into the grass.

---

He wrote it at the stone, kneeling, while there was still light enough to see the page.

*Ninety-fifth day. The Fallow road, at the rise. Eighty strides east of where the ruts turn.*

*Quiet ground. Karis's Arbiter stopped at a line she can mark with a rock. So did Lira's, Brom's, Seln's, Oryn's — same line, same step. Mine didn't. Mine's been stopped since I was fourteen. I don't know what that means. Nobody asked. Tomorrow we go in.*

*The nine: held. Deployment since the Line: one.*

He read it over. It was short, and it was set down as he would have set down a count, and under it he could feel the size of what he had not put.

They made their camp at the turn of the ruts, eighty yards back from the stone, and there was no fire. Seln had chosen the place. It lay a little below the crest on the western side, so that a person lying down could not see the ring and could not be seen from it. They ate cold bread from Thornwater's oven, and the hold's blankets went round, and the mule stood with her tail to the wind.

"Two and two," said Seln, "and nobody alone. I shall take the first with whoever will have it."

"I will," said Brom.

The mule had settled the matter of the line for herself an hour before. She had been left with her rein under a rock, and she had drawn it out and grazed her way down the old road; and when Oryn went to fetch her she was standing with her forefeet on one side of the grey stone and her hind feet on the other, pulling at the grass, as easy as a beast in its own paddock.

"She doesn't know it's there," said Lira.

"She's nothing in her for it to take." Oryn had the rein, and she stood looking at her animal's four small hooves with an expression that Cael could not read. "I've walked her round this place thirty times, by the ruts, because I was told to. I never once thought to ask what she'd have done if I'd let her choose."

Shadow lifted then, as it lifted at every dusk. Cael felt it come up soft behind the other eight, as it had come up beside a turf fire on the fifth day and at a ford on the fifty-third, offering to take him out of the light; and he told it no. It had come at the turn of the ruts as it came anywhere. He wondered whether it would have come a stride beyond the stone, and thought that he knew, and did not go to see.

He walked up to the crest once more before he lay down, since he wished to see the place with the day going off it.

The sun was under the western hills. The grass had gone grey, and the eleven stones were black against the last of the sky, and all the long slope between had lost its colour and lay like ash. Only one thing in that whole country was holding the light. It was the pale level thing among the feet of the stones. He still could not have said what it was, at three hundred yards. It lay there like a pool, and the last light stood on it as light stands on water after the banks have gone dark; and it went on shining, faintly, for a long while after it should have stopped.

He was not the only one who had come up to look.

The healer was at the stone. She stood on the near side of it, with her boots together, like a person at the edge of a pond. She had both arms stretched out in front of her at the height of her waist, so that her two hands were over the line and the rest of her was not, and the palms were turned down.

She kept them so. He did not go nearer, and he did not speak. He watched a square dark figure stand at a stone with its hands held out over nothing, as still as the uprights beyond, until the pale thing on the far slope had faded and there was no light left anywhere for her to see them by.

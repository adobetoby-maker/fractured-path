# Chapter 35 — Flat-Footed

They moved the dead hound in the afternoon, and Brom was not allowed to help.

It lay a yard west of the stone, where it had come down, with the frost of one night on it. Seln had passed a turn of the long cord round its hind legs. The healer brought the mule up, and the mule looked at what she was to draw, and laid her ears along her neck, and was led round twice before she would stand.

"It's a dog," said Brom. He had both hands tucked under his arms, like a man kept in from a harvest. "It weighs what a calf weighs. I could have it under one arm."

"You could," said Oryn. "You're not going to."

"I'd not be drawing anything. I'd be carrying."

"You'd be shutting four new fingers on a hind leg. Stand there."

He stood there. Seln cut the bolt out first, since there would be no getting at it afterward. He did it with the narrow knife and his back turned to the rest of them, and it took him very little time; when he straightened he had a short thick shaft in his hand with the iron unbent, and he wiped it on the grass and held it out.

Karis had the sleeve. She had taken charge of it at noon, when Brom was told what he might not do, and she slid the bolt in beside the rest and showed them the mouth of the leather.

Cael counted, since she could not say it. There were three that had never left the sleeve, and one that had sat on a string all night and not been wanted, and one out of a hound.

"Five," he said. "The sixth is on the floor, and it can stay there."

Nobody proposed to go and fetch it.

The mule drew the thing away to the south, outside the line, a hundred yards and more downwind, and left a flattened track through the grass like the lane on the far side. Cael watched it go. He had held one of those by the head for the length of thirty counts, and thrown another, and been struck by a third in the dark; and in daylight it was a long grey animal with frost on its hide, being pulled over a field by its feet.

Brom did the bread.

He could not cut it. He sat on a pannier with his hands on his knees and told Seln where to put the knife, and Seln cut, without expression, as he would have taken dictation. They had three loaves of Thornwater's, and a cheese of the healer's, and five onions. It was two days to the fence.

"Tonight. Tomorrow at noon, and tomorrow night. Then the morning after, and we're in by dark." Brom pointed. "That's four. Six to a row."

"Five onions," said Lira, from her blanket.

"I'm not having mine. I never liked them raw."

He had Seln lay the pieces out on the lid of the middle box, four rows, and Cael saw the healer's eyes go along the first of them. She had said at a stake that six was wrong. There were six heaps in that row, of a size, and the sixth had not been set a little apart, as a guest's is.

She said nothing about it. She ate hers.

It was a strange afternoon. They spoke of a good many things. Lira asked how far a mule could smell a dead dog, and the healer told her. Brom wished to know whether a bow was meant to pull to the left or whether that was him. Karis wrote that she would open the box under a roof and not before, since there was wind, and held it up; and they agreed that there was wind.

Once Brom got as far as "When you went up that hill—" and stopped, and looked at his new fingers, and asked whether the leg would walk tomorrow.

"It'll walk."

"Good. That's good."

Nobody said *yours*. It was the one word that Cael listened for through the whole of that day, and he did not hear it from any of them. Lira had said it once, under the stars, and put it on a shelf; and the other three seemed to have agreed among themselves, without a hand raised, that it was hers to take down.

He wrote before the light went.

*Ninety-seventh day. The stone. Two mended at noon, and nothing came. My calf read a fourth time, and left in its cloth. Five bolts. Bread in sixths, for four meals.*

*The paper was a question, and we answered it by coming out.*

*Nobody has asked me anything. I keep waiting to be glad.*

*The nine: held. Deployment since the Line: thirteen.*

---

That night, the ninety-seventh, Lira lay on her back with one arm tied across her chest and found that she could not get comfortable round it.

It did not hurt. That was the stupid part. She had been bitten to the bone at dusk and by midnight she had a forearm with eight small pink marks on it, as neat as stitching, and when she moved the fingers they moved. It was a perfectly good arm. It was not hers yet. It belonged to a square woman who was asleep six feet away with her mouth open, and it would go on belonging to her until there was snow.

*Not a rope, not a knife, not a fall, not a friend.*

She had said the whole of it over to herself that morning, to have it exact, as she would have got a card by heart. It was the last part that she kept coming back to. She had spent her life catching things. A lath, a wrist, a rail at the top of a climb; a small pale person who never looked where she put her feet. Her left hand went out for things before she had been told that they were falling.

She was going to have to watch it as she had watched her feet on a river bar, a stride at a time, with the difference that there was no stride to hang the *no* on.

She turned her head. The boy was sitting up, a little way off, with his dead arm in his lap and his face to the east. He was not on watch, and he had not lain down.

She had run a rim.

She let herself have that, for the length of a few breaths, since nobody else was going to give it to her. Twenty yards on a thing as round as a rail, in a boot like a bucket, on a leg that she had been forbidden to run on, with two dogs under her heels and one beside her knee; and she had been pulling away from all three. There had been nothing in it but legs. She had felt thirteen. She had felt the lanes at Fenmark come up under her as if they had only been waiting.

Then the boot had come down wrong, and here she was.

She left the ankle alone. She had asked a healer once, from the back of a mule, and had been told what it was worth and to whom it was owed; and last night that same healer had watched her not ask and had answered regardless. That made twice told. She would not make it twice asked.

It was the other thing that kept her awake.

She had been looking at Karis. There was a dog on Karis, and Seln's knife was coming up too slowly, and the healer had gone rigid under her arm; and on her left hand, where there was nobody, the grass had leaned.

She knew what that was. She had read it in his book before she ever saw it, in a boy's cramped writing, with *sealed* beside it and a mild man's name left out. She had seen it since, a handful of times, and never once in front of anybody who did not already know. It had gone by her elbow like a draught, and then he was on the dog.

That was one. She could have carried one.

Then he had set his feet. She had watched him set them, as she had taught him to in a lane in Ardenmere when they were both too young to know what he was; and the air had cracked.

It was hers. That was the whole of it, and she made herself look straight at it. He had had it off her before either of them knew that he could, and for two years it had been the one that he was shyest of, since it was hers and she was standing there. She had stood at that stone at noon, yesterday, and gone up on her toes, and asked for all of it with a panel's worth of wanting; and she had been given a step.

Seven hours later it had gone off in his feet like a whip, twenty feet north, inside.

She tried it for jealousy, as a person tries a tooth with her tongue. It was not that, or it was not only that. It was nearer to what she had felt on a gravel bar when she found out that she was fast: a thing that she had been certain of, about how the world was laid out, was not so, and nobody had asked her leave.

*What am I becoming, if nobody's measuring.* She had said that to four people at a fire. In there she had been legs, and the big fool had been a man with a pole, and the cleverest person she knew had been a small woman with a roll of paper in her coat. And he had been everything that he ever was, and a light besides.

He had known.

She had not got that from anything he said. She had it from his face, a foot from her own, over a dog's head. There had been a thing in it that she had not seen before in all the years that she had read him across a floor. He had been *choosing*. A person does not choose against a thing that he does not know he can do.

So he had known on that floor, with her arm in its mouth, and he had held its head and let Brom shoot.

She went through it. It was what she did with a plan that she hated. There had been nine on the white and two at Brom's legs, and Karis and the healer standing at the lip with nothing. If he had gone off like that there, every one of them would have come to the place where he stood, and that was the place where she was lying.

She would have made the same ruling. She would have been sick to do it. She thought, from his face, that he had been.

That left *since when*. She had the right to it. She could have had it out of him last night with one more word; he had been sitting there waiting for it with his breath held, and he would have told her everything, badly, in the wrong order, with his arm dead and hers tied, because she had asked.

She did not want it got out of him. She wanted it given.

*Later.* She had put it on her own side of the fire, and it was hers to keep there. It sat in her as the burst used to sit in her calves, wanting out at every stride. She said no to it. She had some practice.

---

Cael had the last watch, on the ninety-eighth morning, and so he was the only one who saw it.

The healer got up in the grey. She did not go to the mule. She walked the ten yards to the stone by herself, past the place where a dead thing had lain, and stood on the near side of it with her boots together, as she had stood on the first evening with her hands held out over nothing.

Then she stepped across.

He went still. She was one stride inside, and there were seven animals somewhere that he had not seen since a wall went down. He looked past her, at the grass, and kept his eyes moving over it; and he counted.

The first time she only stood, with her palms up, and stepped back.

The second time she had two fingers of her right hand on her own left wrist, and her lips moving. He saw them stop as her boot came down. She stood so for a breath, and came out, and they began again before her heel had left the ground.

She went over the third time by inches, as Karis had, sliding one foot; and the fourth time backward, with her eyes on the camp. The fifth time she shut her eyes and walked at the stone from six strides off, counting aloud, quite softly, and he heard the count break. She opened her eyes then and looked down, to see where her feet were. They were where the stone said that they ought to be. She did it again from farther back, and that was six.

For the seventh she fetched the mule.

She led the animal up until its forefeet were against the stone, and laid her whole hand on the warm neck under the mane, and stood with her head bent, reading whatever it is that a healer reads in a beast. Then, keeping her hand where it was, she stepped over, so that she stood inside with her arm stretched back across the line.

She was a long time at that one. The mule turned its head and nosed her elbow.

The eighth was the longest, and she did nothing in it at all. She let go of the mule and walked three strides into quiet ground and stood there, square, with her arms hanging and her face toward the pale thing on the hill; and he counted a hundred before she moved. He thought that he understood it. She had told four holds' worth of carters, for thirty rounds, that she had no business in that place. She was finding out what she was when she stood in it: a woman in a brown coat, with a mule.

She came out. She rubbed her hands together slowly, as she did when they had been in cold water, and she looked across the grass and saw him sitting with a book on his knee.

Neither of them spoke. She held his eye. Then she went to her panniers.

He had the book open. He had the day's number written at the head of a clean leaf, and nothing under it. He had entered every crossing of that line since the ninety-fifth evening: five people abreast, and Karis's nine in the dark, and each of the trials in its row.

He looked at the pencil. It was a finding. She had made it eight times over, by a method, and it was about the line; Karis would have given a week's paper for it.

It was also the only thing that he had ever watched that woman do for herself. She had not done it for a table. She had got up before the light to do it where nobody would be looking, and she had found that somebody was, and had said nothing, and gone to see to a mule.

He shut the book on a leaf with one line.

---

They walked west on the ninety-eighth, and Lira went the whole way on the flat of her foot, since she had been told to.

"Whole sole," the healer had said, at the stone, with the tape freshly on. "Heel and toe together, every step. You don't roll off it and you don't push. If it goes a fourth time, I'm not carrying you to that fence."

"I could ride."

"You could. And if she puts a foot in a rut, you come off, and you come off onto that." Oryn had looked at the sling. "A fall off a mule's a fall. You walk."

So she walked like a duck, across a country with nothing in it but grass, at the pace of a small brown animal that never hurried and never stopped. She had hated that pace for four days on a mountain. She found now that she could not have gone faster if she had been let, and it was restful, in its way, to have the matter settled by somebody else's legs.

It was the arm that she could not get used to.

She had not known how much of walking was done with the hands. Her balance was all on one side. When the wind pushed her she put out the right and nothing answered it from the left; when she stepped over a rut she felt the tied arm start, under the grey wool, and stop against the knot. It did that forty times in the first hour. It was like walking beside a dog that keeps going for the same gate.

The big fool was behind her with the box, and one hand held a little away from its strap.

"You walk like that carter," he said. "The one with the feet."

"Don't."

"I only said."

"You've got his other boot up there on the pack. If you say one word about my putting it on to match, I'll have it off that mule and you'll wear it."

He made the sound into his sleeve that he had made on a river bank, and she felt better.

It happened at about the end of the first mile.

Karis was in front of her. Karis had not said a word since the lane, and could not, and she had her notebook in one hand and her eyes on a thing that she was working out; she walked as she always walked when she was thinking, without the least idea where the ground was. There was a place where a grain cart's wheel had cut the rut a hand deeper than the rest. She put her left foot into it and went over sideways like a dropped rule.

She went toward Lira's left.

It was not a decision. Lira had caught that small person off a ladder, and out of a chair, and once by the collar on the edge of a wash; her left hand knew the way. It was out of the sling to the wrist before any part of her had been consulted. She felt the wool go slack on her neck and the fingers open.

*Not a friend.*

She stopped it. She did not know afterward how. It was like stopping dead off a full run at the lip of a gravel bar: there was all that going in her, and then there were two feet. She shut the hand and jammed the elbow back against her ribs, and she turned her right shoulder in, hard, the wrong way round, and took Karis on that.

It was too slow, and it was too clumsy. A right arm is not where a left arm would have been. Karis struck it and slid, and went down on one knee in the rut with a jolt that brought a noise out of her throat, a croak, the first sound that she had made since the lane; and the notebook flew.

"Sorry," said Lira. "Sorry. I had it. I'd have had you."

Karis looked up at her. She looked at the sling, and at the hand that had gone back into it, and at Lira's face. Then she got up by herself, brushing her knee, and found her pencil, and wrote something quite short and held it out.

*You were told. I shall fall more carefully.*

The healer had not broken step. She was ten yards ahead, on the low side of the mule, and she had her head turned on her shoulder.

"I didn't," said Lira. It came out louder than she had meant.

"I saw." Oryn faced front again. "That's one mile. You've the rest of it to do till there's snow."

It was not praise. Lira knew what praise sounded like; she had been given it on painted floors by people with slates. It was a count, said by a person who kept no book, and she found that she would sooner have had it than a sheet of figures.

She walked on, flat. The arm lay where it had been put. It had started again before she had gone a hundred yards, at a gust, and she felt it reach the knot and stop.

The boy was on the mule's other side, at the off shoulder. He was breathing to her beat, as he had learned to on a road while she was not looking, and every so often he stood still for the space of a breath with his weight on the right leg. She had counted his strides between. It was eleven when they left the stone. By the spring, where they halted for the night, it was nearer forty.

He had looked across the mule at her when Karis fell. He had seen the hand come out, and seen it go back. He had not said anything, and he had not looked away.

*Later.* It was still on her side. She could wait. She had waited four days on a mountain to admit that a mule had a kind eye.

---

They had a fire that night, at the spring, and Seln made the bread.

It was the first flame that any of them had lit on the Fallow road since the way out, and Cael noticed that nobody discussed it. They were a day west of a grey stone; the ground under them was only ground, and a flint was not a declaration. Seln struck it. He had gathered dead stems of the pale grass by the armful, since there was nothing else in that country to burn, and they went up with a hiss and wanted feeding every minute.

Brom sat on a pannier and gave orders.

"More water than that. It wants to be like a wet clay. Now fold it; no, toward you. You're not folding a letter."

"I have folded a great many letters," said Seln. He had flour to the wrists, and his face was as mild as milk. "I had not understood that it was a disadvantage."

"Flat stone. A hot one. Give it a count of sixty a side and don't touch it."

Seln gave it a count of sixty a side. Cael heard him do it, under his breath, exactly; and when the cake came off the stone it was pale gold on both faces, and risen, and there was not a black place on it anywhere.

They all looked at it. In ninety-eight days of road nobody had seen such a thing come off one of their fires.

"That's not right," said Brom. He sounded deeply wronged. "It's meant to catch. It always catches."

"You told me not to touch it."

"I tell myself that every night."

Lira laughed, and put her right hand to the sling as though it had jarred her, and went on laughing. Karis had her notebook out. She wrote for some time, with her tongue between her teeth, and turned the leaf to the fire.

*Finding. A method correctly dictated for ninety-eight days and never once followed by its author. Tested: one cake. I am entering it.*

"You can enter what you like," said Brom. "I'm eating it."

She opened her mouth then, and Cael saw her throat work. It was the first time that she had tried since a lane. What came out was a small rough noise with the shape of a word in it, like a hinge that wants oil.

"No," said Oryn, without looking up from her heel. "Tomorrow, if it wants to. Not because you've a joke ready."

Karis shut her mouth, and wrote *Tomorrow*, and underlined it.

He sat among them with the cake warm in his hand. He had walked a day at a mule's shoulder with nine things shut, and they had gone back under his refusal like dogs that had been run; the heaviest of them lay easier tonight, and the cold had left his fingers at about noon. He had a sore leg and an arm that was coming back. He was, he supposed, mending.

He looked round the fire at five faces. Every one of them had watched a thing go north out of a lane that they had been certain could not happen, and had walked beside him for a day without a word about it. They had not gone quiet, or careful. The big man had let another person make his bread sooner than leave the others without, and the small one had made a joke of it on paper, and Lira had told a carter's boot what she thought of it. It was the same crew that had sat at a fire above a wash, with a healer added, who was looking at her own heel.

He had not known that it could be. He had supposed, at a far lip with his pencil lifted, that whatever was true by dark would cost him this; and it had cost a good deal, on other people's bodies, and it had not cost this.

It came to him that he was being trusted. That was all the silence was. Lira had said *later*, and three people had taken their time from her; and the fifth asked nobody anything that was not a hurt. They were waiting, without making a show of it, for him to have a sentence.

He did not have one. He ate his bread, and it was very good, and he said so; and Brom told him that there was no call to be unkind.

---

By the second noon the leg had stopped counting.

Cael noticed it as a person notices that a noise has ceased. He had been walking at the mule's shoulder since first light, across the last of the pale grass, and he reached for the number of his strides and found that he had none; he had not stood still since the spring. He tried the calf on purpose, a long step and then a quick one. It was sore, and it was a leg.

It had opened on the ninety-sixth evening, in a ditch, with nothing under his soles. This was the middle of the ninety-ninth day.

He looked across the mule's withers at the healer. She was on the low side, as she always was, with her hand on the cheek-strap and her eyes on the road, going at the one pace that she had. She had said the ninety-ninth for his ribs, at a gate, and the hundredth for the other thing. The hundredth was tomorrow.

He had known what the other thing was since a yard at Oxhollow. He had heard her describe it, in a healer's words, and had watched four faces change; and after that he had put it where he put the things that he would look at when there was leisure. There had been a letter to ration, and a line to pace, and a ring. For three days there had been an arm in a sling, and a hill that nobody spoke of. He had been glad of every one of them, and he saw now what he had been glad of them for.

It was a mile to the fork. He walked it at a mule's shoulder with a whole leg, and for the first time in fifteen days he let himself look.

She would lay both hands on him in the morning, and all that she was, for as long as it took. She would do it in earnest, since she did nothing in any other way. He had felt that ninth thing come to him at the end of a bout with a friend who had asked only to be shown straight; he knew, as well as he knew anything about himself, what her morning was likely to be made of.

He wanted it. That was the first thing that he found when he looked, and he was shocked at the size of it. He had wanted very little for himself since he crossed the Line.

The second thing took him the rest of the mile. A woman was walking on the low side of a mule, six feet from him, who had added a stop to the only thing she owned in order to read a boy whom she could not read; and she had not the least idea that the boy might come away from her table with a piece of what she read with.

They came down to the fork an hour after noon, and up the valley with the smoke of a hold standing over the fence in front of them. The east gate was open. He had gone out by it five days before with five other people and a calfskin, to pick up something that somebody had left lying.

It was the bowman who saw them first.

He had no business at that gate. He kept the western one, and in three stays Cael had seen him leave his platform once, to watch six people go out by the east; and he was standing beside the east gate now, with his bow at the length of his arm, like a man who has been coming to the same place at the same hour for five days. He looked at nobody but Lira. He watched her come up the last fifty yards on the flat of her foot, with a strip of grey wool across her and a hand tucked under her collarbone, and he did not lift two fingers from the stave. He took off his cap.

Lira saw it. She put up the great boot an inch, and set it down whole.

There was broth. He could smell it from under the lintel. The steading's woman came out of the long house with a ladle in her hand, wiping the other on her apron, and stopped on the step, and went along them with her lips.

"Six. Well."

It was all that she said, and she said it to the ladle. He thought it the longest speech of approval that he had ever had from her; she had counted them out of that gate, and she had not been certain of the sum.

"There are six blankets," said Seln. He had them rolled on his shoulder. "One is a hand narrower than it went out. I regret it."

"I can see what it went for." She looked at the sling again. "Is that one of mine she's wearing?"

"It's the best of them," said Lira.

"It would be." The woman jerked her head at the long house. "It's on the board. The back room's swept."

---

The healer had him in the yard before he had finished the broth.

"You. Shirt. All of it."

He got it over his head with one arm and the other, which had come back to him on the road as far as the wrist. He stood by the well's coping in the last of the sun with five bands of linen round him that he had hated on the eighty-sixth day, and measured on the eighty-seventh, and since a certain evening had not once had the leisure to think about.

"Arm on your head. Breath out."

She took the first band at his breastbone, where its end was, and drew it off round his side to the spine in one pull, as she had laid it on. It came with a sound like a page tearing. The other four followed. He had expected to feel something give, and he felt only cold air on a place that had not had any for eighteen days.

She did not put her hands round him to count. She had tried that. She set her thumb on the lowest of the three, where the first small sound had been, and pressed, as she had pressed on Lira's shin under a rock; and she watched his face while she did it.

"Breathe. All the way."

He drew it in. He went past the place where a strip of pitch had told him *there* some four thousand times a day, and nothing told him anything. He had forgotten that there was so much room in him.

"Twenty-one days. A rib is six weeks. These are done."

"You said they'd be done on the ninety-ninth."

"I said the rule was twenty-one days. I'd have laid money on sooner, and I don't lay money." She was rolling the dead tape onto its peeled stick. "Leg."

He put his boot up on the coping. She undid a knot that she had once called a good one, and dropped a strip of grey blanket on the ground, and turned the calf to the light. There was a seam down it, pale and dry, a hand long, lying over two older marks; she ran her thumb the length of it and did not shut her eyes.

"That opened three nights back. It's closed. Four days, counting the one it went on." She let his leg go. "I've a man in this hold who cut his shin on a scythe at the start of the harvest and it's weeping yet."

He waited for her to ask.

She stepped back, a stride and a half, and looked him over from there, the whole of him, as she had looked five people over from the corner of a rock.

"Still and whole."

She said it as she would have said a weight. He felt it go into him like a key. She had told him that at a ford: when he was not broken she would run the long one, and she wished to feel it not read him properly.

"Tomorrow. First light. The back room, with the door shut, and I'll not have eaten, so nor will you. It's both hands. It's twenty minutes, near enough; I'll not know till I'm in." She tucked the stick into her coat. "It costs me a mending, and there's a bench under that window I owe four days to. They'll keep till after noon. I've looked at every one of them."

"And if it's nothing again? That'd be five."

"Then it's nothing, and I say so."

"I meant—" He did not know what he had meant. He had lain on a table at Oxhollow and felt an instrument close on air, and he had wanted, with a wanting that he had not written down, for this one to close on something. "Will you get anything?"

"You don't get a yes. You get told."

She was not being short with him. It was only how a healer's table was run: nobody was promised what the hands would find; they were told afterward, whatever it was, to their faces.

*You get told.* He had four people waiting in a loft, and he knew already what he was going to lay in front of them. Those three words would be in the middle of it.

"You lie still," said Oryn. "You breathe slow. You don't talk to me, and you don't hold anything, and you don't help."

"What does helping look like?"

It was an honest question, and he heard, as he asked it, what else it was.

"I'll know."

She went to her bench.

There were five people on it, under the window of the back room. They had seen a mule come up the valley and had come out and sat down, as eight had sat down at Oxhollow, and one of them was a man with his shin wrapped. She did not eat first. She went along them at her stride and a half, with two fingers for each wrist and her lips moving to ten, and at the end of the bench she turned round.

"Not one of you is worse than when I left, and one's better and won't care to hear it. I'll do what wants doing after noon tomorrow. I've a thing in the morning that needs all of me."

They got up, the five of them, and went back to whatever they had been doing; nobody on that bench had ever argued with her about a day. Cael stood by the well with his shirt in his hand and the cold on his ribs, and watched five people give up a morning to him that not one of them knew they were giving.

It was the same yard. He had not looked at it when he came in. There was the sweep of the well, and the cart-yard, and a boy forking straw into the barn under the loft where two boxes waited; the geese went by in a line, and somebody in the long house was scouring a pot. He had gone out of this place five days ago and stood on a floor that matched no stone in the world, and held a hound's head, and carried a light down a hill. Nothing here had altered by so much as a goose, and he found that he had been counting on that without knowing it.

Lira was at the foot of the loft ladder, looking up it, with one hand on a rung.

"I've climbed that with a foot on a stave," she said, when he came over. "I'm working out how you do it with an arm that's not allowed to hold." She kept her eyes on the rungs. "Don't help. I'd like to know."

She went up it sideways, in the end, with her right hand and both knees, and her left elbow tight against her ribs; and at the top she sat down on the loft's edge with her legs hanging, breathing hard, and looked at the sling as a person looks at a dog that has stayed when it was told.

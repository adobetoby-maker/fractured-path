# Chapter 32 — The Loudest Thing

There were two hundred and seven yards of lane and eight hounds, and he had both figures by heart before Brom had the string back.

It took Brom three strides' worth of standing still. He set his boot in the stirrup and bent, with the box riding up his shoulders, and drew. When the string dropped home he made a small sound through his nose. It had opened his fingers. Cael could not see how deeply, in that light; he saw only that Brom held one hand away from himself for a breath, as a man holds it after picking up something hot. Then he laid a bolt in the groove out of the sleeve on the stock. That left four in the sleeve, and one in a pale thing on the floor behind them.

They went down in the order that Cael had given. Seln was in front with the narrow knife, and Karis behind him with her hand on her coat. Then came the healer and Lira, as close as two people in one sack: Oryn on the right with her shoulder in Lira's armpit, and Lira's bound arm held up against her own collarbone, and the great boot coming down whole at every step like a duck's foot. Brom walked after them with the bow across his chest. Cael came last, four strides behind Brom's box, with Lira's knife in his hand and the read under his soles. He had the only eyes in the file that were not wanted for carrying somebody, and a rear guard needs room to see what it is guarding.

He looked back at the third stride. The eight were coming down off the lip, one at a time, without haste, and going into the standing grass on either side of the lane. After that there was only grass.

It was dusk in earnest now. The lane was a grey stroke going down between two dark walls as high as his hip. The west had a green light in it still, and behind him the floor lay on the crown of the swell like a moon that had come down to rest.

"A hundred and sixty," he said.

He said it low, for the file. His strides were yards, and he had paced this lane at noon behind a cord.

The grass was moving.

He had been waiting for it, and it was still a bad thing to see. On the south side, a few yards out from the lane, the seed-heads bent and stood and bent again in a slow line that went along beside the file. When he turned his head they were doing the same on the north. No shape showed in it anywhere; there was only the grass leaning, a little at a time, at exactly the pace of a girl putting down the whole of her foot.

Lira stumbled, and the healer held her, and they stood for a breath. On both sides the grass stood too.

"A hundred and fifty."

He did what he had done from the east tier of a yard, and at the head of a gravel bar with four people hurt: he set out what he had counted, and looked for the seam in it.

Nine had come home across that floor in no order, and one of them had sat down to scratch. Two had walked onto Brom's pole from the same place, a minute apart, with the second watching the first. Eight had stood by while a bow was loosed among them. They could have cut this lane ahead of the file at any stride of it, and they had not troubled to; they kept their station on the slowest thing that was walking, and waited for it to stop.

At a ford, seven of their kind had learned inside a quarter of an hour to leave a silent body alone. These had learned nothing whatever since sunset, with one of their own lying dead on their own floor.

They were careless. He turned the word over, and it fitted every count that he had. They had no cause to be anything else. Whatever walked over that line left its Path at a stone, and came on as a deer comes, or as a man with a stick; and in all the years that a pack had lain under that floor, no prey in this place had ever shouted.

"A hundred and forty."

*Give it one.* He had said that aloud on a gravel bar, and gone down thirty yards to do it. Whichever of them was nearest to a declaration moved first, and the rest fell in behind whatever moved; that was in his own handwriting, by Karis's dictation. He was the only thing between that floor and that stone that had one to give.

He saw then what the room was that he had wanted on the floor, with his hands on a skull. It was a lane, and five people on it walking away from him. He had been carrying the answer since noon; he had stood at a boulder with a foot of Wind going out of him, and thought only of what he had found, and not at all of what it was for.

It would not be enough to draw them off. They would come back to the lane when he fell quiet, or follow him to the stone and over it. On the far side of that stone there were four hurt people and the dark.

He lengthened his stride and came up past Brom's elbow, and along the edge of the lane to the small figure behind Seln.

"Karis. At the stone you'll have it back. How much of it?"

She wasted no breath on why. She thought, walking, with her hand on the roll of paper.

"I have set one point since the mountain, on a lamp-wick. It ought to be very nearly the whole." Her voice was small and dry. "Nine, as before, and the eight joins. I could hold them for a count of six and have something over. I was told not to spend it to the last copper where she was not, and she will be there."

"Along the line. The stone in the middle, and as far each way as nine will reach. Set them and leave them dark. When the last of us is over, join them."

"That is a wall. Across the only door we know of, against animals that hunt a declaration."

"They hunt one. I've not seen one go through sixty yards of fire to reach it. I saw thirty head of horn turn off yours on a mountain."

"That was derived."

"Then it'll be derived twice."

She was silent for four strides.

"Who is last?"

"I am. I've the rear."

She turned her head and looked at him. It was nearly dark, and he could see only the pale shape of her face. He thought that she had heard something that was not about the order of a file.

"Entered," said Karis.

He fell back to the rear. As he passed Brom he said, "At the stone, turn round and keep that thing pointing in," and Brom grunted. Lira had her head down and her teeth shut. The healer's eyes went over him from a foot away, tired and level, as they went over everybody, and found nothing that wanted her hands.

"A hundred and twenty," he said.

---

He counted them down by tens, and the grass kept pace on either hand.

At a hundred Lira's bad foot dragged, and she made no sound; he knew it only from how the healer's shoulder dropped to take her. At ninety Brom said, without turning, that there was something in the lane behind them, and Cael looked. One of the eight had come out onto the trodden grass thirty yards back, and was sitting there with its tongue out. It got up when they had gone on ten strides, and came after, and sat again.

"Eighty."

He could see the stone. It was a paler thing at the lane's end, the size of a loaf. The mule stood beyond it with her long ears forward, hobbled, and in no fear at all; nothing in that grass wanted anything that she had.

"Seventy."

"Sixty," he said, and the grass on the south side opened.

It was the dark-backed one. It came out low and from the flank, at the smallest thing in the file, with none of the idleness that it had shown on a lip. He had time to think that Karis walked alone, and that he had been a fool not to see what that would look like from the grass.

She went over on her back with the animal on top of her, and he heard the breath go out of her. It struck for her throat, and she got both her hands there first, shut, one fist upon the other under her own chin, as a man of Thornwater had once got a hand between; and the jaws closed across the two fists together and drove them back into the place that they were guarding.

She screamed.

It was cut off almost as it began. It was still the loudest sound that anybody had made on that ground since the six of them crossed a stone; and not one head in the grass on either side so much as turned to it. They did not hunt by that.

He saw all of it at once, as he saw a floor.

Seln was five strides beyond her, turning, with the knife coming up; and between Seln and Karis one of the three that Cael had never told apart had stepped out into the lane and stood there, broadside, with its head level. Lira and the healer were five strides on the near side; Oryn had stopped dead, with her whole weight under Lira, and both of them were looking at the thing on the ground. Brom was a stride behind the two women, with the bow at his shoulder already.

"No line," said Brom. It came out of him like a groan. "They're in it. She's under it. I've no line."

Cael was four strides behind the box on Brom's back, and ten from Karis.

He priced it. He had perhaps as long as it takes a jaw to finish closing.

He could run. It was ten yards; but the animal would hear him come, as it had heard everything all evening and not cared, and it would have all the time it wanted.

He could let Wind have him. It would crack like a whip over the lane and set him down beside her with his feet locked for a count, and it would bring eight animals to the ground where Karis was lying, with him standing in the middle of them unable to move.

There was a third thing, and it was already lifting. It had lifted at sunset behind the other eight, as it lifted at every dusk, with the one offer that it ever made. It did not put anything into the air. It was made to take something out.

It was sealed. He knew the seal's reason as he knew his own name, and he went through it in the half of a breath, since it was the one sum that he had never done carelessly. A defector's craft in his hands would tell whoever saw it where he had learned it. So: who could see?

Lira could, and Brom, and Karis from under the thing's jaw; and Seln, who was looking straight back up the lane, and whose own stair it had come off. Those four knew what he was and had kept it for years. There was no bowman on a bank. There was nobody else nearer than two days' walk, and a pack of dogs carries no tales to a ferry.

That left one. The healer knew nothing of him, and she spoke to every carter on seven roads. She had her back to him. A big man and a hundredweight of box stood between her and the place where he was, and her eyes were on Karis. She would see where he ended. She could not see where he began.

*Yes*, he told it.

He had not said so to that one since he crossed the Line.

The hiss of the seed-heads against his coat stopped. That was how it came: the world went on, and he went out of it. His boots came down on trodden grass and gave him nothing back; he could not hear his own breath. He went wide of the lane on the south side, through stems that stood to his hip and bent for him and did not rustle, past the box on Brom's back and Brom's left shoulder. Brom's eye came round and slid over the place where he was, as an eye slides over one fence-post among forty, and then came back to it, since Brom knew. He passed Lira on her left hand, a yard out. Her head turned. She was looking at grass that leaned with nobody in it, and he saw her mouth open.

The healer was on Lira's other side, a head lower, with the whole of a girl's weight on her shoulder, and she did not turn at all.

It was ten strides. On the tenth he let it go, with his hands already on the animal's back.

The sound of his own feet came back a beat late, like an echo off a wall. His hands had gone as cold as if he had held them in a spring. And the dark-backed one, which had heard nothing come and seen nothing come, shifted its grip to finish.

He had its weight. He had been carrying a pace and a half of the world since the far lip, and he knew where every pound of that long body sat and which way it leaned. He took it by the loose skin behind the skull and by the root of the tail. He did not pull it off her, since a thing that is pulled bites down. He lifted the hind end as high as his chest and turned his whole back into it, as he would have thrown a sack onto a cart.

Five bands of linen said *there*. He went through them.

A hound with its hind feet off the ground has nothing to bite from. It let go of two fists to find the earth again, and by then it was in the air; and it went over his hip and into the standing grass on the south side, a full yard out, and he heard it land.

Karis lay with her fists under her chin and her mouth open. No sound was coming out of it. Her eyes were on him, and they were quite clear. They went to his hands, and to the place ten strides back where he had been standing, and returned.

"Seln," he said. "Carry her. The stone. Don't stop for me."

"What are you—" said Brom.

"I'm going to make a noise. Walk."

"With what?" said Lira.

---

He did not answer her. He set his feet in the trodden grass as he had watched her set hers ten thousand times, with the lane across him and his face to the north.

He had said no at the first stride of every road for ninety-six days, and again at the second, on a bar of gravel and a hill of plates and a mile of grass with a grey thing pacing it. He had become so good at the refusal that he had taken it for what he was. He had kept the nine as a person keeps a purse shut; and he knew now what the purse had been kept for, and the relief of it was so sharp that it was nearly grief.

He stopped holding.

The air cracked.

It broke over the lane as it had broken over a river, and the grass went down flat behind him in a fan. He was twenty feet north of five people, in stems to his hip, and his boots had come down left foot first.

The landing locked. *One*, and everything that he owned went dumb; there was a boy standing in a field with a knife, who could not have shifted a foot to save his life. He had priced that count before he spent it. The thrown one was on the south side, finding its legs, and the rest were strung along sixty yards of lane. The nearest of them was a good deal more than one count from his ankles.

All along both sides of the lane, the grass had stopped moving at Lira's pace.

It stood. Then it bent, everywhere at once, toward him.

The count was over, and he had his nine.

He drew breath for Pressure. It was a deeper breath than a healer's tape allowed, and his left side told him so from the breastbone to the spine. It gathered under his ribs for the better part of that breath, as it had always gathered, slowly, where anything alive might feel it coming. There was no opponent to send it through. He sent it straight down.

The ground thumped like a drum. The grass lay over in a ring ten yards across, and the force of it came back up through his heels and his knees, as it came back off anything that would not move.

Ember was next. He gave it the one place where he could carry it, which was the tip of Lira's knife; and for the first time since the Line he did not stop at the setting. It lit. A seed of yellow stood on the steel, and then a flame as long as his thumb, burning on nothing, at the end of his arm.

He could see them.

They had come up out of the grass: there were low grey shapes on both sides of the lane and in it, and every head was turned to him, and in every head there were two small green fires where the flame looked back. He counted the pairs. He could not have helped it.

Eight.

Something bright and quick stirred in his right arm and asked what it had asked of every sill and rim and arris since the day it came to him. *Edge?*

*Yes*, he told that one too. He had never said so before. Along the knife's cutting side a line of white stood out a hair clear of the steel, as a tall exact person had once made one stand on a lath by saying that it did; and it rang. There was nothing for it to cut. It sang all the same, thin and high, like a wet finger going round the rim of a glass.

He had a light in his fist and a noise at the end of it, and he was the only thing on that whole hillside that had either.

He ran north.

Behind him he heard Lira's voice, once, and it was not his name; it was "Go!" and it was not said to him.

They came after him, and they did not come as they had come all evening. There was nothing idle in them now. They came through the grass as seven had come across a river to a seed of fire on a rock, flat out, with the leaders drawing together and the rest falling in behind; and he had the sound of them on both hands, a dry rushing, like a wind that had found its direction.

There was one thing more that he had refused at every doorway and coping since a river, and he let that off its hold with the rest. Anchor fixed nothing, for he had no point on that hill that he could afford to nail to the ground. It only looked, west, as a dog let off a chain looks first at the gate. It would be within ten feet of a thing before it could tell him what was there.

It was forty yards to the rut. He counted them, with the flame streaming back over his wrist and the edge singing. At the fortieth the ground went out from under his left foot, and he was in it to the knee.

---

West was the stone. That was why he turned east.

The rut ran up the swell toward the floor, a little south of east, and every stride that he took along it was a stride away from a lane with a lame girl on it. He put his back to five people and went up the hill the wrong way, with eight animals pouring into the channel behind him.

He had kept the heaviest thing for a place that would hold it.

A Storm lane wanted the air, and to have the air he must give up the ground; at that weight the read would carry one or the other, never both. He let the turf go. It was like shutting an eye. The pace and a half of the world that he had walked in since the far lip went out from under his soles. He was a boy running up a ditch in the dark, with a flame in front of his face and nothing to tell him where his feet would land.

He laid the lane down the rut behind him.

It was a corridor of air the length of a cart and team, high at his end and low at the other, and everything in it went downhill at once. The pale grass stood along both lips of the rut with its seed heavy on it. The lane stripped it. Seed and chaff and dry stem went down that channel in a flood, as thick as meal out of a sack, straight into the faces of whatever was coming up.

He heard them meet it. He let the lane go, and paid.

*One. Two.* For ten counts after a lane, everything that he had answered him a beat late. He had written it so in Hesk's book, on a road, from a bout. It was a different thing to run in. His foot came down, and then he felt it come down. The flame leaned, and then he saw it lean.

At the tenth count he laid the second.

It was in the second that his left boot came down on a stone. It was a round thing the size of a loaf, bedded in the rut's floor; he would have had it a stride and a half off with the read, and he had not got the read. The foot turned on it, and the calf took the whole of his weight slantwise, along a seam that a hound had made at a ford and a plate of shale had opened on a mountain.

It opened again. He felt it go, like wet cloth parting, and after that every stride on that side had a hot place in it.

*Eight. Nine. Ten.*

He laid the third. It was as many as he had ever laid; he did not know what a fourth would do to him, and it was not the night to learn. The rut behind him was a river of chaff. Twelve counts, for the third. His own breath reached him late.

He had counted his strides from the place where he dropped into the rut, and he was at a hundred and thirty when he heard it.

"*Back!*"

It came up the hill from the west, across two hundred yards of dark, and it was a voice that he had never in two years heard raised: mild, and exact, and pitched to carry. It was the word that five of them had said together at a stone, on the evening that they found the line.

A breath after it there was another, higher, and cracked in the middle.

"*Back!*"

Two. He made five people out of two voices, running. Seln would not have called with Karis still on the wrong side of that stone, and Karis could not call. Lira had a healer under her arm. Brom he had to take on trust; Brom would be the last of them over, and would not spend breath on a word.

They were out.

He stopped running east.

It was as near as he came that night to being caught. He turned in the rut, with the flame swinging, and they were there: grey, and close, coming up out of the chaff with their eyes shut to slits and their muzzles white with seed. The channel was full of them. He could not go back down it, and he could not stand.

He gave Wind his feet a second time.

It answered a beat late. He felt that as a man feels a stair that is not where he thought. Then the air broke, and he was out of the rut and over its southern lip, twenty feet down the open slope, with the knife still lit in his fist.

The landing locked.

*One.*

He had priced this count too, and the price was a bad one. The dark-backed one had not gone into the chaff. It had run the rut's rim. Whether a boy's two hands had taught it something in the lane he could not have said. It was the only one of the eight that had been on the right side of the channel when he left it, and it was three strides from him, and already in the air.

He could not move his feet. He could not have moved a finger.

It hit him at the end of the count, and Compression took it.

It took it late. For the first beat there was nothing between him and a hound's whole weight but the left forearm that he had thrown across his body, and he felt the bone of that arm ring from the wrist to the elbow like a struck rail. Then the thing that a fighter had once put through him, and left with him, came up through his frame and did what it was for. The rest of the blow went through his shoulder and his spine and his locked knees, and down into the hill.

He stood. The hound came off him as it would have come off a wall, sideways, and rolled.

His feet were his own again. He ran west.

---

It was two hundred yards to the stone, downhill. He ran it on a calf that was open, and a side that had been told *there* too many times to go on saying it.

He had no read. He had a flame at the end of his arm, which showed him ten feet of grass and blinded him to everything beyond. He could not see the stone. He knew where it was as he knew where his own hand was, and he ran down the dark toward the place where a lane ended.

They were behind him. He could hear them come out of the rut, one after another, with a scrabbling of feet on its lip, and then the rushing in the grass. One sound was nearer than the rest, and gaining, and that was a thing with long legs, built for this.

A grey shape went by on his right, as high as his hip, and it was a boulder. Twenty strides.

"Cael!"

That was Brom, from in front and below. Cael saw nothing of him. But Brom could see a light coming down a hill with a boy under it, and something low and fast a few yards behind the light.

At ten feet from the stone the last of the nine told him what it had been looking for.

It was Anchor. It had gone the whole length of that slope with its face to the west and found nothing that it could be sure of. Now it found nine things. They stood in a row directly across his road, a little more than a stride apart, to the right hand and the left, low on the ground, just beyond the stone. Not one of them gave any light. They were places that somebody had fixed and told to stay, as a bar of iron had been told to stay on that same line, three cords to the north. He knew the hand that had set them as he would have known its writing.

She had laid them along the line's inside edge. That was what her feet had been finding since first light: the last hand's breadth of ground where a point would hold, so close to the place where an Arbiter stopped that an animal coming out would meet it with its next stride.

He went over the stone between the fourth and the fifth.

There was grass under him that was only grass, and a mule's shape, and a big man down on one knee a yard in front of him with a bow-stock at his cheek. Brom's eye was not on him. It was on something past his hip.

The string slapped.

Cael turned as he fell. The rangy one was in the air over the stone, at the top of its leap, with its forelegs stretched and the flame's light on its teeth, and it was six feet from the nose of the bow. He did not see the bolt. He saw the animal fold in the middle of its spring, and come down on the western side of the stone like a thing dropped off a cart, and lie.

"Seven," said Lira's voice, from somewhere on the ground.

"Karis," said Cael. "Now."

She was on her knees a stride from the stone, with Seln's hands under her arms to keep her upright. She had no voice to answer him with. Her two hands were out in front of her, shaking, and he saw the right one make the smallest movement that it had ever made.

The line lit.

It went up out of the grass along sixty yards, thirty to the north of the stone and thirty to the south, a wall of yellow-white fire as high as a man's knee and as straight as her wrist could draw a curve that size. He felt her discharge go past him like the door of a furnace. It was the first thing that anybody but himself had declared on that hillside since noon, and after what he had carried down the slope it seemed to him a clean and ordinary thing, like bread.

The seven came to it.

He saw them only as shapes against the fire, and only for as long as it burned. They had been strung out down two hundred yards of hill behind a light, running to a declaration, and the declaration had gone over a stone and gone out; for he had let the flame die and the edge fall silent when the string slapped, and had shut his hand on all nine. What stood in front of them now was a fence of fire with their own road under it.

They split on it. The foremost flung left and the next flung right, and the rest went after one or the other, as thirty head of horn had gone on a mountain. They ran along the inside of that wall to its two ends. Then they were over the line, somewhere in the dark beyond the light, to the north and to the south; and the last that he knew of any of them was a rushing in the grass that went away along the line's outside in both directions, and thinned, and stopped.

Karis held it for a count of six. He counted it for her. At six the wall went down all at once, and she sat back against Seln's knees with her hands open in her lap.

It was full dark. The stars were out. He lay where he had fallen and went over it, since somebody had to.

Two were dead: one on a white floor, with a bolt in its eye, and one a yard from his boots. Seven had gone, north and south, and nobody on that hill could have said more of them than that. Six people were on the western side of a grey stone. One of them had an arm bitten through and an ankle gone for the third time, and one could not speak. One was looking at the fingers of both his hands by starlight. He himself had a calf laid open, and a forearm in which the feeling was going out like a tide, and nine things under his hand again that had not yet stopped shaking.

There were no instruments, and there would be no hundred marks.

He could say what had been done, as Pike would have said it. A thing loud enough to be hunted had been made in the one place where nothing could be loud. It had taken a pack off five people who could not make a sound that the pack would hear, and a line of fire had kept it from following them out. That was the whole of the win.

The pack had learned nothing from it that it could use. It had never needed to learn anything, and he did not suppose that it would begin now.

And five people had watched him go up a hill with fire in his fist. Four of them had seen one thing more than the fifth had.

Nobody spoke to him. Somebody was striking a flint.

# Chapter 47 — Millrace

They were out of Lowmarch two hours before the light on the hundred and thirteenth day, with the stars still hard over the river and every blade in the meadow standing white.

It was Seln's hour, and Cael had given it to him without argument. A loaded cart took the whole of a short day to reach the mill; a crew that walked as they walked could be on the ridge by sunrise and at the fork before noon, and Seln wished to have the afternoon in hand. So Brom loaded the mule by feel. Lira ate standing. They went past Pike's bare plank and the smith's cold chimney, and took the south road in file, with the frost ringing under five pairs of boots and four small hooves.

They passed the well at the fork in the dark. It had a new rope on its windlass, and somebody had mended the curb where six stones had been set; and Cael, who had hauled foul air out of that shaft for three days on a bitten leg, put his hand on the stonework as he went by.

The road climbed from there. By the time there was grey enough to see by they were on the back of a long ridge, bare and broad, with pale grass on it that the wind had combed all one way. The country fell off on both hands. To the east it was hills beyond hills, hatched with the red-barked trees that Karis would not name. To the west the ground went down in long folds toward a valley that he could not yet see into, and a line of mist lay along the bottom of it like wool caught on a fence.

"That's water," said Lira. "Under the white. It's the same that she walked down."

"The mill water," said Karis. She had the calfskin half unrolled against her chest, and she was reading it as she walked. "He draws it with three strokes where it is forded and with one where it is not. It comes round out of the hills to the north and east. A person leaving that hold by its northern wicket would meet it on the second morning, and it would bring her here."

The sun came up on their left hand, small and white. It showed them their own breath, and the mule's, and a road that ran on south along the ridge as far as they could see; and a little before the middle of the forenoon it showed them a place where the ruts divided.

One pair kept the ridge. The other turned down the western face, slantwise, through a notch that wheels had worn, and went out of sight.

"The fork," said Seln. He had walked the first hours of that road by himself, in their second week at Lowmarch, and had never said what he found. "The left hand is ours, later. The right is the mill."

They heard the wheel before they saw a roof.

It came up to them out of the notch while they were still among the rocks: a slow heavy knocking, with water falling under it, at about the pace of a tired man splitting wood. Brom stopped the mule to listen. Then the track came round a shoulder, and the valley opened.

It was a narrow green place, a good deal greener than anything on the ridge. The water came down it fast and brown over a weir of stakes and stone, and above the weir a channel had been cut away along the bank to a tall grey building with a wheel on its side as high as a barn door. The wheel went round steadily in a white fall of water. There was a fence of split posts about the mill and a dozen roofs, and below the fence a ford, with a flat stone on each bank for the wheels to aim at. Smoke rose straight up from four chimneys.

A woman was sitting on the nearer of the flat stones with a net of some kind across her knees. She raised her head at the sound of hooves, and counted them down the track with her chin, one at a time, as Pike counted a meadow. Then she turned and called toward the mill, without getting up, in a voice that had been pitched to carry over a wheel.

"Five off the ridge."

The answer came from a lean-to that had been built against the mill's southern wall, where the sun fell; and it came at once, low and hoarse, from somebody who had not troubled to come to the door.

"I know."

---

There was a bench along the mill's wall, and they sat down on the end of it.

She had told him at a well that if she was at a table when he found her, he would sit and wait his turn like anybody; and he had not understood, until he had the planks under him, what a plain relief it would be to do a thing exactly as it had been set. Nobody on that bench looked at him twice. There were fifteen people on it, or had been at first light; nine were left, and two more stood about the door with their caps in their hands.

The lean-to had no front. It was three walls of board and a roof of turf, with the mill's warm stone for its back; she had her plank on two kegs in the middle of it, and her things laid out along a shelf as they had lain on a sill at Thornwater. He could see the whole of her work from where he sat, and he watched it as he had once watched a sixth-year's bout from a rail.

She had a man on the plank when they came, with his right hand in her two.

It was a bad hand. He was one of the miller's men, and a rope had taken it nine days before, against the drum of the hoist that lifted the sacks; it had been bound up in a meal-bag since, and it lay in her palms like a thing that had been trodden on. She had her eyes shut. Her lips were moving, and Cael found that he knew where she was by the count: at three she had the banks, and by seven she had gone along the bed to where it was stopped. He had lain under those hands for twenty minutes. He had a fragment in him of what she was doing, the tenth, and it sat quiet under his breastbone and did not stir toward her, since it stopped at the length of his own arm.

She had been at it for most of an hour before they came, by the grey of the man's face, and she held it a quarter of an hour longer. When she laid it down on the man's chest, it had five fingers that he could move, and he was weeping without any noise.

"That's one," said Oryn. "You'll not close it on a rope before the thaw. Send in the child."

It was a girl of three or four, carried in by her father, with a wet rattling breath that Cael could hear from the bench. The healer did not put her on the plank. She sat down on a keg and took the child on her own knee, with one broad hand spread on the small back and the other on the breast, and bent her cropped head until her ear was against the shoulder-blades. She stayed so for a long time.

He saw her face over the child's hair. There was another chest, at another gate, that she had last listened to on the eighty-ninth evening. She had counted the days to it on a hill, aloud, with nobody by. None of that showed. She had one child under her hands, and she gave it the whole of herself, as she gave everything.

"It's low down, and on the one side," she said to the father at last. "It's not what you were afraid of. Hold her so. She'll cough, and you're to let her."

The mending of that took a good deal less time than the hand, and at the end of it the child coughed until she was red, and then slept.

"That's two."

The third came in on a hurdle.

She was an old woman, very small and very upright, with two grandsons to carry her and a stick laid along her side that she plainly meant to walk home on. Her left knee was the size of a loaf under her skirt. The healer looked at it from her stride and a half, and then at its owner, and something came into her tired face that Cael had seen there only once before, at a board with a goat on it.

"You were to wait for me on your own doorstep. I'd put a day by for it."

"I waited sixteen of them," said the old woman. "Then I heard you were at the mill. I'd sooner be carried down a valley than sit another night with it."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing. I was getting a ham down."

"Off a ladder." Oryn had her hands round the joint already. "It's got a ladder in it. I can feel every rung. You went through the fourth, and you came down on this with the rest of you after."

"The third."

"It was the fourth. Lie back."

That one took her until the shadow of the wheel lay across the door, and when it was done she sat on her keg with her head down between her shoulders for as long as it takes to count fifty.

"That's three," she said to the floor. "That's the day."

She had told them on a mountain that her reserve was a purse, and that three such mendings emptied it.

She did eleven of the rest with one hand each, and that took her less time than the knee had. There was a carter with a wrenched back and a woman whose tooth she looked at and would not touch. She laid two fingers on a wrist, or a palm on a forehead, for a count of ten, and told each of them the truth in a sentence. She put tape on a thumb. She told a big sullen man that there was nothing wrong with him that a week away from his brother would not cure, and the whole bench laughed, the man included.

Then there was only a boy with a swollen wrist between the five and the door. He had been sitting on his father's coat, and he came up when Oryn crooked a finger at him.

---

"You," said Oryn.

She was speaking to him. She had washed to the elbow in a bucket, and she stood drying her hands on her breeches, a stride and a half inside the lean-to, with the old woman's hurdle going away up the valley behind her.

He got up off the bench and went to the edge of the turf roof's shadow, and stopped there.

"How many?"

"One. The same one. I've not gone back to it."

"Then you've nothing for me yet, and you know it, and you sat an hour on my bench all the same." Her eyes went over him as they had gone over him in a yard. "So it's something else. Say it."

He had turned it over on the ridge since before the sun was up, and it had seemed a plain enough thing there. It was harder in front of her, with her sleeves pushed back and three mendings gone out of her since first light.

"I've read one thing with what I had from you, and it was myself. I found no bank in it anywhere, as you found none. I don't know what a bank is like under the hand. I've only your word that there is such a thing." He kept his own hands at his sides. "When you take the boy's wrist, may I set my hand on it too? I want to learn what reaches you through yours."

He heard Lira's breath stop, on the bench behind him.

The healer did not answer at once. He saw her look at his right hand, where it hung, and then at her own wrist; and for the space of one breath there was the hunger in her face that he had seen at a ford and in a yard, when she had wanted to know a thing more than she wanted her supper. She put it by as he had seen her put it by before.

"No."

She said it without heat. She hung the cloth on its nail, and turned back to him.

"You asked. That's the rule and you kept it. The answer's no. Next."

He stood for as long as it took him to be sure that he had nothing to add, and he had nothing. For the first time in his life, he had asked before putting his hand on another person; he had been told that he might not; and the whole of what was left to him was to go and sit down. He went and sat down. It was not hard to do, and he was a little surprised at that, since he had dreaded it.

Lira was getting to her feet beside him, with her right hand already at the knot of her sling.

He took Hesk's book out of the satchel. He had written nothing in it yet that day, and he wetted the pencil and put this down before anything else, with the date over it, so that it should stand first.

*Hundred and thirteenth day. Millrace, the healer's bench, a little after noon. Asked her leave to touch the boy's wrist while she held it, so that I could learn what came to her hand. She said no. I stepped back. Entered before anything else today, since it is the first refusal under the rule and I want it to head the leaf.*

Karis had been reading it upside down, from his other side.

"She looked at your hand before she said it," Karis murmured. Her voice was pitched for his ear alone. "And then at her own wrist. I have seen that look on a person in front of a shut press. She was curious about you. Enter that somewhere."

"It's not mine to enter."

"It is an observation. You may put my initial against it."

He put it down, with her initial; and he had hardly lifted the pencil when a hoarse voice from the lean-to said that the arm had taken no harm on two days of frozen road, that all eight were as they had been, and that the knot had been retied by somebody who tied like a carter.

"That was me," said Brom, from the bench. "At the ford. She'd got it wet."

"I can see who it was. Hand."

He gave her his hand across the sill, and she turned it over and gave it back. She put two fingers on Karis's throat. She gave Seln her short nod. In the time that it takes to draw a bucket she had gone round the five of them and found nothing that wanted her, and she sat down on her keg with her forearms on her knees.

"That's my bench, then. Which of you is going to tell me why you're a day south of your board, with a mule of mine and three boxes, on a road that goes nowhere but a ford?"

---

Cael told her, since it was his to tell.

He gave it in order and left nothing out: a line that was said twice at a hearth, and a nail, and a sheet of cream paper that had lain sealed in a broker's box beside the first. He gave her the sum on it. He told her what Seln had found in its margin, and what the five of them had made of it at a fire, and that one of the five had said walk away and been entered. He said that the place was half a day up the ridge from the fork over her head, and that he meant to be at its edge by dusk and across it in the morning.

"You said not without you. So I've not come to ask you for anything. I've come so that you should have it from me today, with the bench done, and not from a carter in a fortnight."

She heard all of it with her eyes on her own hands. When he had finished she spread the left one on her knee, and began to fold the fingers down.

"I was to be at this gate on the ninety-sixth. I came in on the hundred and twelfth. That's sixteen." The thumb went down. "There's a day's bench here still, that I'd have done tomorrow, and the road beyond it: two gates, and then the ford with the oxen on the hundred and twenty-first, where they looked for me on the hundred and fourth. That's seventeen." A finger. "Now put your ridge into it. It's a half-day up to wherever this is, and I'd not see the place before dark. A day while you're in it. A half-day down. That's two, if every one of you walks out on his own legs, and you've not once done that since I knew you." Another finger. "So the ford's nineteen. And beyond the ford there's a child that I heard on the eighty-ninth and can't hear now before the hundred and twenty-eighth."

She looked at the two fingers that were left standing.

"You're asking me to break the route," she said.

"I'm not asking. I'm telling you where we'll be."

Her head came up. She studied him for some time, and he could see her going back over the words, as he had seen her go back over a count that came out odd.

"That's worse. That's a healer's sentence."

"I had it from one."

"I know where you had it. I said it to a loft, with my hand on a ladder." She rubbed her palms together slowly. "It's what I say at every gate when I leave it. I don't ask them whether I may go. I tell them where I'll be, and then it's theirs to carry, and they can't say afterward that they weren't told. I never had it said to me before. I don't care for it."

"I'd not have known another way to put it that wasn't asking."

"No. There isn't one." She got up off the keg. "You've a girl there with a fortnight's arm, and you're taking her onto a floor that somebody chose for her. I said that somebody should stand outside with her sleeves up. I meant it when I said it. I didn't think that I'd be shown the place inside three days."

She walked to the lean-to's edge and stood looking up the valley, where a hurdle was still in sight, very small, between two boys.

"I'll say one thing, so that it's been said before any other. I'm not going to be anybody's sixth."

"Nobody's said it."

"That one-armed man said it under his eave, and wrote it. A clerk said it to a boat." She did not turn round. "I've a round. It's seven gates and twenty days and it's the only thing that I own. A woman who's one of six belongs to the six. I've seen what that does to a healer; she ends up mending the same four people for the rest of her days and calling it a calling."

She stood there for about as long as it takes to count twenty. Cael did not help her, and nor did any of the others; it was her sum, on her fingers, and they had each of them learned at a stone that she did not want it done for her.

Then she put two of those fingers into her mouth and whistled.

A man came round the corner of the mill with a sack on his shoulder. He was a carter by his whip, long and stooped, and he had a cart at the ford half loaded with meal for the gates up the water.

"You go north tomorrow," said Oryn. "You'll say this at every fence you stop at, in these words, and you'll have it said on to the ford with the oxen. The healer's late. She was seventeen at the ford, and she's nineteen now, or more. She's a half-day south of the mill, at the edge of bad ground, since there's a crew gone onto it that she's had her hands in." She waited until his lips had done moving. "And at the gate beyond the ford, where the little one is: if the breathing changes, they're to send down the water to this mill, and the mill sends up the ridge. I'll come from wherever I am."

"Late, nineteen, bad ground south of the mill," said the carter. "And the little one sends here."

"That's it."

He shifted his sack and went back to his cart. She watched him go, and Cael saw what he had seen on her face in a yard, when she unloaded a mule: it was the look of a person who has done the only thing that could be done from where she stood, and who knows to a grain how much less that is than enough.

---

"Now you'll hear what it is," she said, "since you'll want it for your book, and I want it plain."

She had come back to the middle of the lean-to. She did not sit.

"I'm not coming with you. I've put you on the round. That's a different thing, and I'll not have it mistaken." Her eyes went along the five of them, and they were as level and as tired as he had ever seen them. "You're on the route now. Which means when I say lie still, you lie still, and when I say I can't help you, you believe me."

"That's the whole of it?" said Brom.

"That's what every gate gets. They feed me, and they do as they're told on my plank, and when I tell them that a thing's past my hands they don't stand there looking at me as if I'd kept something back." She glanced at Cael. "You've stood there so. On a hillside, with her arm in my lap. I'll not have it a second time."

"You won't," said Cael.

"And I go when I go. The day that the round can't spare me, I turn north, and nobody says a word."

"I'll not ask you to stay. I'll write that down."

"Write it where you wrote the other."

It was Lira who spoke next, and she did it from the bench, with her foot set down whole and her chin up.

"We lay six bowls. We started at the stone, and we never got out of it; Brom puts six out every night and takes one back." She held the healer's eye. "Nobody's asking you to be a thing. I only thought you ought to know that there's a bowl."

Oryn regarded her for the space of a breath.

"I'll eat out of it," she said. "I'll not sit in a place."

"That's all I said."

"I know what you said. You said it a deal better than he would have." The corner of her mouth went down, which with her was most of the way to a smile. "Get that mule off my fence-post. She's eaten the bark."

They were half an hour getting away, since a healer does not leave a gate as a crew leaves a camp. She went into the mill and came out with a loaf and a cheese that somebody had pressed on her. She showed the miller's wife how the hand was to be bound at night. And then she went round to the back of the cart-shed with thirty feet of three-strand rope over her shoulder, and what she brought back on the end of it made Brom put his hand over his mouth.

It was a jenny mule, mouse-grey and enormous in the belly, with ears like a pair of oars. She came round the corner of the shed sideways, with her head stuck out and her eyes rolling at the wheel, and it took the healer three tries to get her past it.

"They've had her standing since the barley was in," said Oryn, a little breathless. "She's eaten her head off and learned nothing. The miller says he'll thank me to take her as far as the snow." She threw her one pannier across the grey back and buckled it. "She leads as a gate swings. I've told them so on thirty rounds."

The brown mule had turned her head.

She stood under her three boxes at the fence-post, quite still, with both ears pricked at the square woman in the dead man's coat. The healer did not go to her. She tightened a strap on the grey that did not want tightening.

"She knows you," said Brom.

"She's a mule. She knows where the hay used to come from." Oryn took up the rope. "You'll keep on the low side of her, as I told you. I'll go behind with this one, where she can't see the load and want it."

They forded below the mill, and the woman on the flat stone counted six of them and eight hooves across without lifting her net; and they climbed out of the green valley into the wind again.

At the fork there was a flat rock, and Cael sat down on it with Hesk's book while the two mules were let to blow. The grey had come up the notch backward for a good part of the way. The mill's wheel was still to be heard below them, very small. He wrote under what he had written on a bench.

*Hundred and thirteenth day, the fork above Millrace. Oryn came. On her terms. She'll turn north the day she has to and I won't ask her not to.*

He read it over. Then he looked at six people on a bare ridge, and at the left-hand ruts, which ran on to the south with the white sun over them.

# Chapter 25 — Tenth

Pike wrote the wall out again that evening, from the top, on a clean length of sailcloth, and Lira counted the lines as they went up.

The room was warmer than Cael remembered it, and emptier. The cold term had sent half the crews home to their own fences, and the benches down both sides had room on them; but there was a fire in the great hearth that a man could have stood in, and it threw its light across the plaster where the old sheet had hung since the sixtieth day. A boy held the new cloth taut with both palms. The one hand moved over it, steadily, and went to the horn pot on the bench and came back.

*Teague.* It stood by itself on the first line, as it always had.

"Two," said Lira. "Three."

She was not sitting. She stood in the middle of the floor with her arms folded and her weight on her own boot, and her lips moved at each name. The five of them had the boards nearest the door, where they had sat on the forty-ninth night, and on the boards between Brom's elbows lay a sheet of cream paper with a lamp beside it.

"Eight. Nine."

The pen went to the pot. Pike wrote five names on one line, in the order in which they had been spoken at a plank, and the boy shifted his hands down the cloth.

"Tenth," said Lira.

She said it quite quietly. Two lines that had stood over theirs since the day they came now stood under them, and the shingle pair were at the foot still. Cael looked for what the rung was made of, since he had learned in this room that it was always made of something. It was a mountain, less a cart. The wall had no place to say so. It said only that a hold which asked Pike who ought to walk a bad hill would now be given five names before it was given two others.

"We lost a cart and went up two," said Brom slowly. He had the jug, and he had not yet poured. "I'd not have reckoned it so."

"You'd have reckoned the cart," said Karis. "He reckoned the map as well. It's the same sum; you stopped halfway."

"Which two have we gone over?" Lira had come back to the boards at last. "I can't tell from here. Half of them are trades."

"Then go and read it," said Pike, from the hearth, without turning his head. "I don't call out who's gone down. I write who's where."

She went and read it, with her nose a hand's breadth from the wet ink, and came back looking thoughtful. One of the two was a crew of four who had brought a string of mules through the cold road's pass and lost none of them. She had seen them in the meadow, she said, and they were none of them young, and one had a wooden foot.

"So that's what tenth is," she said. "It's just in front of a man with a wooden foot who never lost a mule."

"I'd not sneer at him," said Brom. "I've lost a cart."

"I wasn't sneering. I was working out how good you'd have to be to get past him by two."

Pike had sat down at last, on the hearth bench, under his own handwriting, with the ledger open beside him and a cup that somebody had put into his hand. Cael took his own cup and went over, as he had gone over once before to ask about a ledger-keeper in Ardenmere.

He had a question, and he had not yet found the words for it. It concerned the name at the head of the new sheet. A man had come in the night before, Pike had said at the plank, with news of a cart in a gorge; and for the whole of that man's time at the oak a sheet of cream paper had been lying under a river stone, where anybody who read a plank from end to end must come to it.

Pike drank. He looked at the fire, and then across the room at the lamp on the boards by the door, and then down at the leaf beside him.

"Teague's nine up," Pike said, to the book, asked by nobody. Then, still to the book, at the same size, as if it were the next line and not a different thing: "He read that slip yesterday. Read it twice, same as the boy did. Put it back under the stone and took the fence. I'd have done the same, and I told him, and he said he knew."

Cael stood with the cup in his hand, and the question that he had not asked went out of him unspoken.

He had read that man's feet in the silt of a ford, and his halts at five halting-grounds after it. He had watched him count three people on a sill before he looked at a room, and had been told by him on a river bank, in a level voice, what a mountain was worth. Now he had the man's answer to a hundred marks as well, at second hand, from a book-keeper who gave out nothing that he had not seen with his own eyes.

It was a reason against. He entered it as one, with the name of the man who had given it; and he noticed that Pike had said what was done, and had not said why. He did not think that Pike had been told.

---

It had been the evening before, the eighty-ninth, with the five still a camp short of the river, when Teague counted his three off the ferry.

He did it on the eastern landing, with the scow's rail still under his hand. The spear was ashore already, and grounded. The boy had the two mules by one rein and was looking up the bank at the lower inn's chimney, where there would be something in a pot. The bow came off last, and stepped down onto the planks with the right foot, and stood on it.

Three, and whole. That hip had had six days of road since a healer told him the price of it, and the bow had not once asked to shorten a stage.

He sent the boy to the smith's with the mules and went up to the plank by himself, as he always did, before he had so much as washed. Pike was taking in his book for the night. Teague read the oak from the left-hand post, a slip at a time, and at the second slip he stopped.

*HOLD.* It was in the square capitals, and it was not his. There were five names under it, and five again under the same word on the shingle beside.

He stood in front of them for as long as it took to be sure what he felt. He had asked for that word once, on a ford, and had it struck with one line while he was on a ledge with a string of salt; and he had never grudged the striking, since a hold cannot wait on a captain's convenience. Now two holds had come down to a ferry and asked a one-armed man who they ought to have. That was the board doing its work. If he minded it, he would have to mind arithmetic.

He went on along the oak. There was nothing else on it that weighed, and he was turning away when he saw the stone.

Pike's stone lay on the book's corner, where it had lain for as long as Teague had been coming to that plank; and under its far edge there was the corner of a sheet of paper. A line of dust lay across the corner, fine and pale, drawn along the stone's rim. He had been four weeks up the north water, and that sheet had plainly lain there for most of them. A waybill does not gather dust under a stone.

"What's that?"

Pike's eyes went to the stone and came back, twice.

"Paper," he said. "It's been said. You weren't here."

"Then I'll read it."

He was given it, as he had been given a struck slip on the fifty-seventh day. It was good paper, heavier than anything the holds wrote on. He read it from the first word to the seal, and then he began again at the top, more slowly.

He priced it as he would have priced a stage, a thing at a time.

The price came first, and it was a hundred, which was too much. A surveyor's kit was a thing that one man carried on a strap; he had seen such a kit once, years ago and a long way west. It was two days out to that rise and two home, on a road he knew, and nobody paid a hundred marks for four days and a case. But *too much* was only a reason if a man could say what it was too much for, and he could not.

The hirer was the broker, by the wax. Teague had carried that man's bales. He paid to the day, and argued every copper beforehand and none after; and he had never in his life sent a crew farther than his own landing. That was odd, and it was not wrong; a broker posts for whoever puts money in his hand.

The ground he knew. He had been round the Fallow Ring eleven times, with carts, by the ruts, at noon, and had looked across the grass each time at a row of grey stumps and kept walking. Every carter's child knew what was said of it. He had never put it to the proof, and he did not need a paper to tell him why not: he had three behind him, and in that place the spear would be a man with a spear, and the bow a man with a bad hip, and the boy a boy. He would not take them in for a hundred, or for twice a hundred.

That was a reason not to take it. It was a good one, and it was his, and it would have done for any captain on the sheet.

But it was not what had stopped him at the second reading. Something in the paper itself was wrong, as a load is wrong on a mule a mile before the girth shows it. He went round it twice more, looking for the place. The witness was not named, and there was nobody at the far end to say that the things had ever lain there. The words were a clerk's. None of that was a reason either. He had taken worse paper than this from better men, and brought it home.

He could not find it. He knew only that he would not have liked to see one of his own carrying that sheet up the east road, and that he could not have told them why.

He laid it back under the stone himself. He set the corner on its own line of dust, as nearly as he could, and took his hand away.

"That's what I'd have done with it," said Pike.

"I know."

It was all either of them said about it. There was a fence on the oak, four slips along: the upper inn's paddock, in sight of the ferry, three days of posts and rails at five marks, and work that a person with a bad hip could do sitting. Teague drew its tack and held it out, and Pike wrote his name to it before he shut the book and tucked it under what was left of his arm.

Teague stood a breath longer in front of two slips with five names on each.

They would be at the ferry tomorrow. He had kept a day ahead of them from Oxhollow without trying to, and he knew their pace, with a taped boy and a girl on one boot. The boy would read this plank as he had read a doorway, from one end to the other, leaving nothing out. He would find the stone.

Teague had said four things to him on a river bank, and every one of them had been counted before it was spoken. This was not a thing that could be counted. A captain did not walk up to another crew and hand it a feeling, as though a feeling were a figure; if he had, he would have been asked what it rested on, and rightly, and he would have had no answer but the hair on his own neck.

So he said nothing, and it sat poorly with him. He went up the bank to find the boy, and whatever was in the pot.

---

They held the council once, at their own boards, with the lamp turned up.

Cael had set the cream sheet in the middle of the table, where all five could reach it, and he gave the order as he would have given it for any contract: each of them to speak, and nobody to answer until the last had done. Brom poured. He had allowed one jug, on the strength of a line said at a plank, and had made it plain that the paper under the lamp had not yet earned a second.

"Karis," said Cael.

She had her notebook shut under both hands, and she did not open it.

"I shall be brief, since I am not disinterested, and I want that known before I begin." Her voice was small and exact. "I have three boxes on a cart outside. Every leaf in them is about the country we left, except the handful that I have written myself since a river. In ninety days I have met one thing on this side that somebody else thought worth a sheet of paper, and it is lying there."

She nodded at the lamp.

"He says that there is a place on a road where an Arbiter stops. He says it stops at a line, and that a person knows the line by her own. If that is so, then it can be found with nothing but feet, by anybody, and found again in the same place the next day by somebody else. I have been taught that an Arbiter cannot be tested at all; that it is the one thing every instrument we own takes for granted. I should like to stand where one fails." She drew a breath. "I would go for nothing. I say so in order that nobody should count my hand as a vote for the hundred."

Cael had known her for two years, and in all that time he had heard her ask for a room, and for silence, and once for an hour more with a restricted shelf. He had never before heard her say what she wanted in front of four people and then decline to argue for it. She sat with both hands on the shut notebook, a little flushed, and looked at nobody.

"Brom."

Brom had turned the paper toward himself with one finger, and he had been doing a sum. Cael knew the look of it. He had worn that look over a wash and a fan and a cut axle.

"A survey kit's a cart's worth. Brass. A level, a chain, the rule. Nobody puts a cart's worth down and walks off from it unless they walked off fast." He looked at the jug. "That's not the reason. That's what it is."

He pushed the paper back to the middle of the table and picked up his cup.

"Lira."

She had sat down at last, sideways on the bench, with the great boot stuck out into the room.

"I've walked the east road four times and I know every stone of it as far as that fence. Past the fence it's two days more, on ruts that the grain carts use, to a hill. Then it's whatever a surveyor leaves behind him, picked up, and brought back the same way." She looked round at them. "I'm saying it's a walk and a carry. I'm not saying it isn't the other thing."

"The dogs live in them," said Brom.

"I heard him. I've been bitten by one; I've a foot to prove it." She shrugged, with some effort. "That's mine. A walk and a carry, and my eyes open."

They were looking at him now. He was the nearest thing that they had to a captain, and it was his to say last but one.

He had a good many reasons. He could have said what he had said to Lira under the eave, that he wished to see it, and it would have been true. He could have said that a hold had asked for them by name, and that a crew which was asked for ought to find out what it was made of. There was a rope with no figure against it. There was a cart in a gorge, and a line in a book.

All of those were his, and he saw that each of them was a way of dressing the plain thing so that it should look better on him. The plain thing was that five people had a hundred and eight marks and the cold coming, and that this paid as much again for four days of road.

"It pays."

Lira's eyes rested on him a breath longer than the words needed, and went back to the lamp. She did not say what else she knew.

"Seln."

---

Seln had not touched the sheet since it came in from the plank.

He drew it toward him now by one corner, and held it up to the lamp without reading a word of it, so that the flame stood behind the sheet, and he looked through the paper as a person looks through a window.

"I have listened to four cases that turn upon what this says," he said. "I should like to add what it is."

He laid it down, and touched the left-hand edge.

"There is a margin. It has been pricked, here and here, at even distances, with a wheel; that is done so that every sheet from a desk is ruled alike. No hold has such a wheel. The broker's clerk writes on whatever comes to hand." His finger moved to the head of the sheet. "*Recovery.* One word, set by itself, with a stop after it. That is how a class of business is named at the top of a paper that will afterward be put away with others of its class. A carter would have begun, *I have lost.*"

"And the sum," said Karis softly. She had seen it at the plank.

"And the sum. It is written in words, and then again, in a bracket. A person is taught to do that who has been warned that a figure may be altered and a word may not. I was so taught, at a ruled desk, when I was not a great deal older than he is." The mild eyes came up. "The place is given as a description. A day, and a rise, and a road that bends. Every carter in that meadow has a name for it, and our friend at the plank used the name at once. Whoever wrote this either did not know it or was not permitted to put it down."

He folded his hands.

"Somebody wrote this who learned to write inside."

"Inside the Line," said Brom.

"Inside the Line, at a desk, from a book of forms. I do not say whose desk. I say that it was not composed on that landing. It was copied there, or else it came over the water as it stands, with the money."

For some time the only sound was the fire. Cael looked at a cream sheet that had lain a month under a river stone, and saw it as he had not seen it at the plank: a thing with a ruled margin, lying on an inn's boards in a country that kept no paper. He had walked east for ninety days, and here was a piece of the west on his own table, with a seal on it that told him nothing.

"It goes in."

Seln said it mildly, to nobody in particular.

"Is that you telling us to leave it?" said Lira.

"That's not a reason not to go."

He gave them no more than that, and he sat back out of the lamplight, like a man who has handed in a report and does not mean to argue it.

Karis had opened the notebook after all. She was counting on the edge of a leaf, in small strokes.

"If we mark it in the morning, that is the ninety-first," she said. "The Oxhollow cart rests a day and goes east on the ninety-second, with our boxes, and we in front of it. That brings us to the fence on the afternoon of the ninety-third."

She stopped, and Cael saw the stroke of her pencil stop with her.

Nobody at the table needed the sum done twice. A square woman had stood in a gateway on the eighty-fourth day, with a mule's cheek-strap in her hand, and had said nine days. The road to the Fallow Ring went through Thornwater, and by no arrangement of theirs it went through on the day that she had named.

Brom was looking at his left wrist. Lira had her eyes on the great boot, and on what was taped inside it; she had asked for that ankle once, and been told what it was worth and to whom it was owed. Seln studied his cup. And Cael had stood by a well with a sentence ready, six days ago, and had found that he had no right to it.

Not one of the five said that a healer might be brought. He watched four people not say it, each for a reason of his own, and he was prouder of them than he could have told them.

Karis wrote. She turned the leaf so that the lamp fell on it, and they could all read what she had put.

*Thornwater, ninety-third. The healer.*

"Hands," said Cael.

There were five. He drew the cream sheet toward him, and folded it once along its old fold, and put it inside Hesk's book for the night.

He wrote by the same lamp, when the others had gone down to the meadow.

*Ninetieth day. Lowmarch. The seventh line is said: seventy in the purse, sixty-four on the wall. Tenth. The smith is paid, and we have a hundred and eight. A rope is owed to the ferryman, with no figure. Two holds have asked for us by name, and will wait a fortnight.*

*Twelve weeks. Seven contracts. Pike says there's ground out here where the Arbiter goes quiet, and nobody goes in twice. Karis has been waiting her whole life for a sentence like that. Seln says the contract was written by someone from inside. I say it pays. Two days east, through Thornwater, on the day the healer said. For twelve weeks nothing out here has read me. I'd like to see what a place that reads nothing does with that.*

*The nine: held. Deployment since the Line: one.*

---

He gave the paper back to Pike in the morning, across the oak, before the mist was off the river.

Pike took it without a word. He read it twice, though he could have said it in his sleep, and then he turned his shoulder to the meadow and gave it out: the recovery, and the Fallow road, and the rise, and the hundred. It was the first time in a month that those words had been heard on that grass. He said the whole of it a second time. Then he said *taken*, and five names, and dipped his pen.

Cael counted who heard it, since a thing said at that plank was only as good as its hearers.

Teague was there. He stood a little way out from the eave, on the trodden grass, with his thumbs in his belt, and his three behind him at their distances: the spear, grounded, and the bow on the good leg, and the boy with a heel of bread. That made four. The Oxhollow carter had come up from his mules and stood chewing, which was five.

The hide-woman had her arms folded. Under the ash the old carter had lifted his chin at the first word and was holding it there. Two men in tarred aprons had stopped with a bundle of shingles between them; and the boy who took the standing-money stood on one leg beside a tent-rope, with his bag forgotten in his hand. That was ten.

The eleventh was at Pike's elbow. He was a thin young man with ink on his cuffs and a ledger under his arm, and he had come up from the landing at a trot when he was sent for.

"You're the witness for the hirer," said Pike.

"I am told so." The clerk looked at the five as if they had been bales of a kind that he had not handled before. "My master had it by the last sack, sealed, with the sum counted in a bag and a line of direction. He was to post it and to lodge the price. Whatever is brought down, I am to see it at the landing and put our mark to the slip, and it goes west by the next boat." He swallowed. "I don't know what the things are. I was not told who wants them."

"Nobody's asked you," said Pike, and wrote five short strokes at the foot of the cream paper, side by side.

"What if they're not there?" said Lira. "The things. Somebody else might have picked them up. It's been lying under your stone a month."

"Then you come down and tell me so, and I say that." Pike was blowing on the strokes to dry them. "I write what was done. There's a hundred in my box against his mark on this paper, and if his mark never goes on it, the box stays shut. I've nothing in the book that pays a crew for walking."

"So we could go four days and come home with a line and no money."

"You could. You've read my lines. Every one of them says what came back."

It was done in the time that it takes to lace a boot. Cael waited for something to come from the trodden grass, and nothing came. The man with the leather on his wrist had watched the pen make its strokes. He did not nod, by so much as the width of a finger, as he had nodded on a river bank; he stood with his eyes on the paper until it was folded, and then they moved to Cael, and rested there, and told him nothing that he could have written down.

There was one thing more, and Cael had turned it over in the night.

"The two holds," he said to Pike. "With our names. We'll be nine days on this, if it goes as it's written. If we're not down in their fortnight, don't keep a bull waiting on us."

"I'd not have. I told them a fortnight, and after that the top of the sheet." Pike blotted the strokes. "It's good of you to say it where he can hear."

He had not said it for that. But he saw, from the corner of his eye, that the boy with the bread had stopped chewing, and was looking at his captain.

# Chapter 23 — Round by the Ferry

The mule went over the top of the brown hill, and after that there was nothing on the north road for him to watch.

Cael stayed where he was, on the gravel at the water's edge, with the book shut on his knee and his left arm held in against five bands of linen. Six days ago the side of a mountain had come down on five people and a cart, with a herd of frightened beasts on top of it, and all five had walked off; he had not yet got used to counting them and finding five.

It was the start of something, and so he took the inventory, as he took it at the start of anything. Three ribs were broken on the left side, low; they had been taped on the eighty-first day, and the tape was to come off on the ninety-ninth and not before. The calf was bound in a healer's cloth and was closing by itself, as his hurts did. Lira had a shin that was four days old, and an ankle that no healer on this road was going to mend, and one boot. Brom's left wrist wore a linen cuff, and he was to hang nothing from it until the middle of the eighty-seventh day. Karis had been told to ask nothing of herself for three days more, and she was asleep against a box.

There were three boxes, and they stood on the bare ground by a gate-house wall. That was the item he liked least. A hundred and sixteen marks lay in Karis's purse, and six of them were a smith's. The plank where those six would be paid was six days down a road, and each box weighed as much as a grown sheep. He had one man who was allowed to carry, and four who were not fit to.

He was still turning it over when a stick came down the bank behind him, tapping.

It was the keeper. He let himself down onto a flat stone by stages, with the notched hazel across his knees, and he looked at the river for some time before he spoke, like a man who has all his life counted what crossed it.

"I've a cart goes out at first light," he said. "For grain."

"To Thornwater?"

"Where else is there grain? I'd meant him to go over the top, when the dry spell came, and be home in six days." The brown eyes came round. "You've told me what's over the top. So he goes round by the one-armed man's ferry, the same as his father went, and that's eight days out and eight home, and the frost won't wait on me while I think about it."

Cael said nothing. He had learned on a hill to let a carter come to the end of his own load.

"He's a lad and two mules. It's sixteen days of road, and I'd as soon he had somebody walking in front of him for the first six." The keeper tipped his stick at the gate-house wall. "Your boxes'd ride. There's room on the bed going out; it's coming home that she's full."

"What's the hire?"

"There's none. I've no paper for it and I'm not sending to a plank for some." He scratched his jaw with the stick's end. "You walked my hill for seventy and brought me a no. I'd have paid the seventy three times over in carts, by next thaw, if you'd brought me a yes to please me. I can find a bed for three boxes."

So there it was. Cael went through it once, from habit, for the place where it cost something, and he could not find one. A column on a pasted leaf had ended in the wrong word, and the five of them had written it anyway, and for that a man was offering him wheels for six days. He had been paid for a great many things in his life, and most of them had been somebody's convenience. He did not think that he had ever before been done a kindness on the strength of a no.

"I'll ask them," he said.

"Ask them. He goes at first light whether or not."

He asked them in the yard, by the well, with the light gone grey. Brom looked at three boxes and then at his own left wrist, and Cael watched him weigh a road of six days against a rule of three, and put his hand up. Lira's was up already. Seln lifted two fingers from his folded arms. They had to wake Karis for hers, and she raised it before her eyes were properly open and asked afterward what she had voted for.

"Wheels," said Lira.

"Whose?"

"His. They go round."

"Then I am in favour of it," said Karis, and went back to sleep.

They lay that night in the keeper's cart-shed, under a heap of empty grain sacks that smelled of last year's barley, since their own blankets were at the bottom of a gorge. It was warmer than a bay of shale had been. He lay on his right side, where the ribs hung, and listened to four people breathing, and counted nine days to a fence in a valley where a healer meant to put her hands on him again.

---

They went through the ford at first light on the eighty-fifth day, and nobody spoke until the far bank.

The carter was a long young man with a narrow sleepy face and the largest feet that Cael had ever seen on anybody. He was the keeper's sister's son. He had two grey mules, and a cart a foot wider in the bed than the one that lay in the gorge. In the six days that followed he said perhaps forty words, and most of them were to the mules. The red oxen drew his wheels over for nothing, since the wheels were the hold's own. He walked through behind them with his breeches rolled to the thigh and his boots hung round his neck by their laces.

The five waded after. It was a hundred yards of gravel under a foot of quick water, cold enough to make the bones of his feet ache. The tape told him where the edge was whenever a stone turned under him. He kept his breath shallow and his eyes on the cart's tail. Seln waded a little upstream of him. He had taken up the strap of the locked case by two holes before he stepped in, so that it rode high between his shoulder-blades, well clear of the river; it had been on him on every road since their own cart was sold, and he had declined, mildly, to let it ride on another man's bed with the boxes. Three boxes sat on it under a sheet of patched canvas, left and middle and right, with the corner that was worn white turned outward. On top of them, roped down by her own instructions, sat Karis, who had been lifted up there like a sack and had made no objection.

On the south bank he stopped to put his boots on, and saw the prints.

They were a day old. The dew had softened their edges, but the gravel gave way there to a strip of grey silt, and the silt had kept everything. Four people had come up out of the river in single file, each a crew's length behind the one in front, and two mules after them on one rein. He crouched, as far as five bands of linen would let him, and read it.

The first prints were a man's, of the middle size, set down evenly. Beside the right foot, here and there, was a small round dent, where the haft of a hand-axe had swung at his thigh and touched ground on the slope. The second walker had grounded a spear at every step. The third set of marks was shorter on the left side than on the right by the width of two fingers, every stride, and no deeper for it; whoever made them had been favouring that hip for so long that the body no longer argued. The fourth were the smallest feet of the four and went in the deepest.

"That's the boy," said Lira, over his shoulder. She was standing on one boot and one bare foot, with her arms wrapped round herself. "He's carrying more than the others. Look how his heels go in."

"More than his share."

"Somebody ought to stop him."

"I should think somebody does, about once a day." He stood up carefully. "They went up this bank at dusk, with the light going and a river to get over, and there isn't a scuffed print among the four of them. Nobody turned to look back. Nobody stepped out of the line."

He had not expected to enjoy it. But there was a pleasure in reading a thing well made, and he had it now. The difference was that he did not want to beat this one. He had no notion what he wanted from it; the man had told him to be good enough to be glad of, and Cael was reading the silt to learn what *good enough* walked like.

The carter had halted his mules on the bank and was waiting, with his sleepy face turned toward them. He had seen Lira's foot. He looked at it for some time, and then he went round to the cart's tail and took down a pair of boots.

They were his spare ones. Each was as long as Cael's forearm.

"Two marks," said the carter.

It was the first thing that anybody had heard him say.

"For the one?" said Lira. "I've only the one foot wants it."

He considered that, and shook his head slowly. "They're a pair."

"I've got a left. Look at it; it's a perfectly good left. What am I to do with yours?"

"Keep it by."

"Against what?"

The carter thought about it with real care, and the mules stood, and the river went past.

"The other one going," he said.

Brom made a sound into his sleeve. Lira looked at the boots, and at the road going away south between its alders, six days of it, and at her own bare foot with the linen crossed over it in a figure of eight.

"Two marks," she said. "Karis."

"I heard." The purse came down over the cart's side in a small pale hand. "I shall want it understood that this is the board's money and it has bought a boot and a half's worth of leather that nobody can wear. In hand, a hundred and fourteen."

Lira packed the right one. It took the whole of a spare shirt, torn in strips and wound round her taped foot until the foot was the size of the boot. When she stood up in it she was two fingers taller on that side than on the other. She walked a few steps, and stopped, and walked a few more.

"It's like wearing a bucket," she said. "I can't feel the ground through it at all."

"Can you feel where your foot is?" said Cael.

She tried it again, and her face changed a little.

"Yes. That's her tape. I'd know where it was in a barrel." She tied the left boot to the cart's tail by its laces, where it hung all the way to Lowmarch, turning slowly, like a sign over a shop. "Don't anybody tell me what it looks like."

---

By noon of the second day he hated the tape, as he had been told that he would.

He could have borne the pain of it; he had carried pain before, on worse terms, and gone on. What he could not bear was that the thing was always right. He would reach for the water-skin, or turn his head to see what Lira had laughed at, or simply draw the breath that a hill wanted; and five bands of linen would close across his side a hair before the bone did, and tell him *there*. Every time, when he stopped to check it, the tape had known and he had not. A person can forgive an instrument for being wrong. He was learning that it was a good deal harder to forgive one for being right four thousand times a day.

There was no arguing with it, and he could not bear a thing that he could neither argue with nor measure, so he measured it.

A breath went in to a count of three before the tape spoke. On a level road he could take that breath at every fourth stride and never touch the edge. On a rise it wanted every third, and on the steepest of the valley's little hills every second, and there the breaths came so shallow and so quick that his head swam. He had always walked in fits. He had gone quick and then slow since he was a child on the lanes of Denvash, running to look at a thing and standing still to count it. He had watched a healer come down a mountain for a day and a half at one unbroken beat, and he had not been able to copy her for a hundred yards.

He tried it now beside the near mule, since there was nothing else to do and six days in which to do it.

The mule did not hurry and did not stop. Its near fore came down, and he put his left foot down with it; four of those, and a breath, to the count of three; four more. Whenever he ran ahead of the beat the tape caught him, and whenever he fell behind it the cart's wheel came up at his heel. Between those two there was a place the width of a plank where neither of them touched him, and by the afternoon of the eighty-sixth day he could stay on the plank for a mile together.

It was a small thing to be proud of. He entered it all the same. In two years of other people's fights and eighty-six days of road, nobody had ever taught him anything that he had so little wished to learn, and it had been taught him by a strip of pitch and a mule.

Lira walked at the off wheel, on the other side of the cart, and she counted nothing at all.

He knew, since he could hear her. She had been told a month, and it was plain that she had taken the month as she had taken the mule, as a thing agreed to and not to be discussed. She set the great boot down on its whole sole at every step and looked at the country. There was a good deal of it to see. The road went south down a broad valley of alder and rough pasture, with the grey mountain shrinking behind their left shoulders, and twice a day it forded some small brown water where the mules drank below the crossing. She named nothing and timed nothing. Once, in the middle of the second afternoon, she said across the cart that she had never before walked anywhere slowly enough to see what was growing on a wall, and that it was mostly moss.

Karis slept. She woke to eat, and to ask which day it was and write the number down, and then she folded herself up again between two boxes with Seln's coat over her and was gone. Cael watched her do it and was easier each time. On the mountain she had looked at her own hands before she looked at anything else, and by the third morning on this road she had stopped.

Seln found the sleeping places, and gave no report of how.

It was Brom that Cael watched on the eighty-seventh day, from the first light.

He walked behind the cart with his hands hanging, as he had walked behind another cart up a saddle, and every little while he turned his left wrist over and looked at the cuff. He ate his bread with the right hand. When the carter's off mule cast a shoe-nail in the middle of the morning and stood on three legs, it was Seln who held the hoof, and Brom stood by with both arms folded so tightly that the knuckles showed.

At the noon halt he cut a hazel rod from the roadside, a yard long and as straight as he could find, and pushed it upright into the turf of the halting-ground. Then he sat down on a chock-stone in front of it.

"What's that for?" said Lira.

"The middle of the day."

"You can see the middle of the day. It's up there."

"I can see near enough. She didn't say near enough." He had his eyes on the rod's shadow, which lay along the grass to the north, as long as his hand and growing shorter. "She said a week from the middle of the eightieth. I was there. I saw where the rock's shadow lay when she took her hands off it."

So they ate, and the shadow drew in. Nobody spoke of it, but Cael noticed that nobody strayed far either. The carter chewed, and looked from the rod to the big man and back, with the mild puzzlement of a person watching the customs of a foreign hold.

It took the better part of a quarter of an hour. The shadow came in to the width of three fingers and stood there, and stood, and then, by a hair that Cael would not have sworn to, it was on the other side of the rod.

"That's the week," said Brom.

He untied the cuff with his right hand and his teeth. Under it the seam was pale and dry, from the heel of the hand to the middle of the forearm. He bent the wrist down and brought it back. Then he folded the linen in four and put it inside his shirt, on the left, where he kept a dead slip with six words on it.

"Well?" said Lira. "Aren't you going to lift something? You've been looking at that cart for three days like a dog at a door."

"There's nothing wants lifting."

"There's three boxes."

"They're on a bed, where they ought to be. I'd only be taking one down to put it up again." He stood, and pulled the rod out of the ground, and laid it across his knee as though to break it; then he seemed to think better of that and slid it under the cart's canvas. "She gave me a week, and I've kept it to the hour. I'll not spend the first minute of the next one showing off."

Cael had half risen to say something, and he sat down again. He had been on the point of telling Brom that the week was out, and that he was free of it. He saw now that it would have been like telling a man the price of something he had made himself. Nobody had kept that week but Brom. He could finish it in whatever way he pleased.

---

The moss came on the fourth day, and the carter stopped his mules at the edge of it without being asked.

The valley had flattened and gone wet. For two hundred yards and more in front of them there was no pasture and no alder, only black water standing in sheets among yellow reed, with tussocks between as big as kneeling sheep; and across the whole of it somebody had laid a road. It was made of logs. They lay side by side, crosswise, each as thick as a man's leg and twice as long as the cart was wide, bedded on brushwood and sunk to half their depth. They ran away over the moss in a grey ribbed strip, like the backbone of something very long.

"How old is that?" said Karis. She had sat up.

The carter lifted one shoulder. "My father's."

"And when was it last mended?"

He lifted the other.

Brom had gone forward onto the first of the logs. He stood on it, and on the next, and then he came to one that was darker than its neighbours and put his heel down hard, and the heel went in. He drew it out with a sucking noise. The wood under the bark was the colour of wet bread.

"One in ten," he said. "Maybe one in six. They've gone soft from the middle, where you can't see it, and they'll bear a man. I'd not put a loaded axle on them." He looked back at the carter. "What did you weigh, coming this way last time?"

"Never came. He did."

"Then we'll do it as your father would have, if he'd any sense. She goes over light. The load walks."

It was the fan again, Cael thought, and he was glad to see that it was the fan made small; there was no slope in it and nothing overhead. But somebody had to learn which logs were sound before a wheel was trusted to any of them, and there was only one way that the five of them had ever done that.

Lira had the same thought. He saw it come over her face, and he saw her set it against a healer's month.

"Walking," she said. "I'm allowed to walk. She said it'd walk in a week as well as the other, and that was eight days ago."

"She said no running for a month," said Brom.

"I know what she said; I was under her hands when she said it. This is a flat road, at a walk, in a boot the size of a trough. Somebody mark them behind me."

She went out along the logs. She did not run them, and Cael, who had watched her run sixty-two hundreds of a mountain, found it stranger to see her walk these. She set the packed boot down in the middle of each log and leaned on it, and waited, with her arms a little out from her sides; and at every one that gave she said "Soft," without turning her head, and stepped over. Seln followed three logs behind with a lump of chalk that the carter kept for his tallies, and drew a cross.

It took her the better part of an hour to do two hundred yards, and there were a hundred and forty crosses.

Then they emptied the cart. The mules were led over one at a time, picking their way between the chalk marks with their ears laid back. After them the cart itself went across with her bed bare, the carter at her head, and Seln and Cael each at a wheel to turn it by the spokes whenever the rim came up to a cross. He could do that much. It was done at the height of his hip and it asked nothing of his ribs that the tape would not allow.

The boxes stood on the last of the dry ground, in their order.

Brom looked at them. He had stood so in front of a cart in a wash once, Cael remembered, and in front of two hundred and sixty yards of scree, working out a weight. He was not working anything out now. He walked up to the middle box, with the corner that was worn white, and bent his knees, and took hold of it by its two rope beckets, one in each hand.

He did not swing it to his back. He stood up with it in front of him, held against his chest in both arms, as a man carries a thing through a doorway; and for a breath he only stood there.

His left hand had been in a sling and a cuff, by turns, since a river on the fifty-third day. That made thirty-five days. Cael watched the two wrists, as he had watched them under a cart on the eighth narrowing, and no grey started at either. There was a hundredweight of copied paper on them and they were the colour of wrists.

"It's not heavy," said Brom, to nobody. He sounded a little surprised. "I'd thought it would be heavier."

He carried it the whole way over. He came back for the left-hand one, and then for the right; and when Seln stepped forward for the third, since three crossings of that moss was a long way under a box, Brom shook his head and took that too. The carter watched him go by each time with a hundredweight in his arms and a look of deep content on his plain face, and plainly could not make out what he was seeing. Carrying boxes was only what a big man was for.

"He's pleased about something," the carter said at last, to Cael. It was the longest speech of the six days.

"He's been waiting eight mornings to pick that up."

"It's a box."

"Yes," said Cael. "It is."

---

They made their last camp on the evening of the eighty-ninth day, on a halting-ground at the top of a long hill, with a river shining below them in the dusk.

It was Lowmarch's river. He knew its colour, and the way it bent. They had come back to it from the west, by a bank that he had never stood on; and somewhere down there in the dark was a round thatched shed with a drum in it, and a horse walking a circle that had once drawn their cart across a province. Seln had a fire going with his flint. The carter sat at it as a matter of course, and held his enormous feet to the warmth.

Karis was awake. She had been awake since noon, sitting up between the boxes with her notebook on her knee, and she had the colour back in her face that six days of sleep could give it.

"Tomorrow is the ninetieth," she said. "The plank, and a smith. I have a slip in my coat with a horned head on it, and I want to hear what he does with a job that ended in a no." She turned a leaf. "And Thornwater on the ninety-third. That's four days, and two of them are road."

"We haven't said we're going to Thornwater," said Lira.

"She said nine days, and he said nine days back to her. I was there; I had my eyes shut, but I was there."

"He didn't say we'd come. He said the number."

"With him it's the same thing."

Lira looked across the fire at Cael, and he kept his eyes on the book.

"You've been reading that crew the whole way down," she said. "At every halt. I've watched you go round the stones."

"They don't light a fire."

"What, never?"

"Five halting-grounds, and not a new ash on one of them. The chock-stones are where they found them. They eat cold, and they sleep in a row with the mules at the windward end, and in the morning there's nothing to show for it but where the grass lay down." He turned a page back to look at what he had put. "We've had a fire every night since the ford, on the strength of a finding. He's had none for longer than I've known what a hold was, on the strength of never having needed one."

"That's not better," said Lira. "That's only older."

"I didn't say it was better. I said that I could read it."

Brom had the carter's pot between his knees, since their own kettle was at the bottom of a gorge, and he was scouring it with a handful of grass, slowly, with his lips moving. Cael knew that look as well. He was getting two speeches ready, one for a ferryman and one for a smith, and he was putting the worse of them first.

Cael had the day's lines to put down, and they were short ones.

*Eighty-ninth day. The west bank, a morning short of the ferry. The boxes rode from Oxhollow on the keeper's grain cart, for nothing, since the map said no. In hand, a hundred and fourteen; a boot. Six owed to the smith and a rope to the ferryman.*

*Mine: I breathe to the tape and walk to a mule. Brom: the week kept, to the hour, and three boxes carried over a moss. Lira walks. Karis is awake.*

*A crew went down this road a day ahead of us and never left a mark out of its line. I read them at every halt. They'll have been at the plank tonight.*

*The nine: held. Deployment since the Line: one.*

He blotted it. Down in the valley a single light had come out, low, by the water, and he thought that it was most likely the lower inn.

# Chapter 18 — The Eighth

The first narrowing came at the thirty-third hundred, and Karis gave it a number of its own.

"One," she said, into his ear. "Of his eight. I shall keep them in a separate column, since they're what a carter will ask about first."

The hill had put down a foot there. A buttress of the grey slab came out of the face and ran right across the bench, and where it reached the edge it had left a shelf eleven feet broad where there had been twenty-five. It was room and to spare for a cart that the smith had cut to four feet between the wheels and five from hub to hub. Even so she looked different on it, with the wall at Brom's left elbow and nothing at all beyond the off wheel.

It was the seventy-sixth day, and they were making up for the one that Karis had spent on a rock. The second narrowing was nine feet, and the third nine and a half. The fourth was seven, on a pitch of two full hands to the yard, and there Lira walked backward in front of the off wheel for the whole length of it, laying a flat stone under the rim at every turn so that it should have something to bite.

"Cart: yes, with a man at the outer wheel and dry weather," breathed Karis, at the end of her figures. "I shouldn't care to say it of a wet day, and I've said so in the last column."

That column had been growing more careful since morning. On the first afternoon every line of it had said a plain *yes*, and now each *yes* dragged a condition after it, like a cart with a pole lashed under its corner.

Brom leaned on his rope through all of it, and Cael thought that he had never seen him so content. He had stood idle in a yard for five days and in a meadow for eleven. Here he had found a way of putting his whole weight to use that a woman with a needle could not have forbidden, and he had found it by himself. Iron Skin had not once risen to meet a load on that shelf. Cael had his own things to keep down there, for Compression rose whenever the rope snatched, and Wind leaned at every yard that Lira ran, and he paid the rent on both fragments and spent neither.

Overhead the rasping went on. The line had been above the second overhang at first light, and by noon it lay over the fourth narrowing, and they had not got ahead of it by a yard.

"They're following us," said Lira at the next halt, with her mouth almost on his collar.

"They're going where the moss hasn't been eaten," said Karis, who had heard, "and so, as it happens, are we. I shan't write *following*. I've nothing under it."

They slept beyond the fifth narrowing, which was eight feet, under a brow of rock that Seln had marked for them with a pebble. Cael wrote four lines by a small fire, of which the last was the one he wrote every night.

*Seventy-sixth day. Forty-seven lines. Every one says yes, and most of them on a condition. The herd is over us still.*

*Deployment since the Line: one.*

The sixth was six feet and a hand, in the middle of the next morning, and the seventh was the one that Cael remembered afterward.

It came at the fifty-fourth hundred. The buttress there was not slab; it was a rib of darker stone, and it came out so far that the bench seemed to stop against it. Lira had gone round the corner at a walk and stood a long time out of sight. When she came back she held her two hands apart in front of her, a little wider than her shoulders, and then she shrugged.

Karis measured it twice. She lay down on her stomach to do it, with her chin over the edge and one end of the cord pinned under her elbow.

"Five feet nine," she said, when she had wriggled back. "At the narrowest, for four yards. The cart is five. That is a hand to spare beyond either hub, if she goes through exactly in the middle." She brushed grit from her chin. "And the outer edge is not a good edge. It's sound for the first foot. After that I think it's a crust."

He went to look, since the line would be in his hand.

The shelf under his boots was whole. But at the very rim, where the dark rib had weathered, the stone had come away underneath in a long hollow, and what was left on top was a lip no thicker than a plank, with daylight below it. A wheel that strayed nine inches to the right would be standing on that.

Something in him had found the lip before his eyes did. Anchor had asked at every sill and coping since a river, and at every one of seven narrowings, and it had never asked as it asked now. *There?* It showed him the place, under the crust, where a fixed point would sit: one point, set against the sound rock and told to stay, and that plank of stone would have carried a loaded wagon. It would have cost him nothing that he could not have paid by supper.

He looked up the slope at thirty grey heaps, and thought of a page in his own book that had her three clauses on it.

*No.*

It was the plainest refusal he had made on the mountain. The reason was above him, eating moss. But he noticed that he would have refused it without them. A point of his under that lip would have made the seventh narrowing safe for one cart on one morning, and then he would have had to write *yes* in a column that a ford-keeper was paying to believe; and the next wheel over it would have found only the crust.

"I want to measure it again," he said. "Myself."

Karis looked at him. Then she held out the cord.

"That's what the second hand is for."

He lay where she had lain, with the edge of the mountain under his breastbone and a long blue emptiness in front of his face, and he drew the cord tight from the foot of the rib to the last sound inch of the rim. He found the knot. It was five feet nine.

He had expected nothing else. But he understood, getting up, what she had asked of him at the slab two days before: a hand that might doubt a number on that table before a wheel was put on it. It was an odd use to be wanted for, by a person who had once kept an index of everything he could do, and he liked it better than any of the rest.

They took her through an inch at a time. Seln knelt at the off wheel with his cheek nearly on the rock, watching the rim against a scratch that Karis had made, and lifted one finger for every hand's breadth of travel. Cael had the shafts alone, so that there should be one pair of feet to steer by. It took a quarter of an hour to go four yards, and the only sounds were the dry hub and five people breathing through their mouths.

"Cart: yes," whispered Karis, when the tail was clear. "This cart, with a man at each wheel who has nothing else in his hands. And under that: *a wagon of five feet six between the hubs, no.* I want him to have the number where the yes stops."

---

Seln had said that he did not like the eighth, and when they came to it Cael saw why he had refused to say more.

It was wide. That was the first thing, and it was nearly a disappointment after the seventh; the shelf ran out from the wall a good nine feet, sound slab from the face to the rim, and the pitch was no worse than a hand.

It was simply not all there. A crack went across it from the wall to the edge, as straight as a sawn board, and the two sides of the crack stood two feet apart.

He looked down into it. The sides went down smooth and close, without a ledge, and where they passed out of the light there was no floor that he could see. Karis let a pebble go, and it clicked from one wall to the other for a count of three and was still clicking when he lost it.

"It's clean," said Brom. He had got down on one knee and laid his right hand on the western edge, and then on the eastern. "Look at that. It's not crumbled. The frost's got into a joint and opened it like a book." He sat back. "That's the same height both sides, to a finger. If there was a plank across it you'd never know."

"We've a tail-board," said Lira. "And his seat."

"Elm, and three feet long, with a wheel on the middle of it? She'd go through them like bread." He shook his head. "There's nothing on this shelf to fill it with, and nothing to bridge it. A wheel that size'll drop a hand into a two-foot gap and come up against the far lip, and the far lip's a knife."

"Then it stops here," said Karis. Her voice had gone thin. "At the fifty-ninth hundred. With the end in sight."

"I never said that." Brom was looking at the cart as he had looked at a rung of ash in a barn. "It wants carrying."

"No," said Karis.

"It's two feet. It's one stride."

"You are not to lift. A woman with a needle told you so, and I wrote it down."

"She told me not to pull, and that the wrist would say if I had. I carried a box up a fan on my back, and you looked at the wrist yourself, and it was dry." He got up. "A cart's a box with wheels on. It sits on me. There's nothing hanging from a hand."

Karis opened her mouth and shut it. She turned to Cael, as people turned to him when there was a ruling to be given.

He had it ready, and it was not the one that she expected. On any floor he had ever stood on he would have decided this himself, and in a breath, since the plan was always his. But the thing in front of him was a weight and a gap, and whether a back could take the one over the other; and there was a person on that shelf who had known such sums since he was twelve years old, and it was not Cael.

"It's a load," he said. "Loads are his. If he says it goes, it goes, and he tells us where to stand."

Brom gave him a slow look, as if a thing had been set in his arms that he had not thought to be handed. Then he nodded, and began to give orders, very low.

The boxes went first, passed across the crack from two pairs of hands to two more. He had the cart emptied to her boards. He folded his coat into a pad and laid it on the back of his neck, and he had Seln and Cael step over the gap and take a shaft each from the far side.

"When I say, you lift the shafts to your shoulders, and you walk. Don't pull her. I'm bringing her. You're only keeping her nose up." He looked round. "She'll want to tip back when I've got her, and that tail-board'll swing."

He did not say what was to be done about that. He crouched, and went in under the bed between the wheels, backward, until the axle-tree lay across his shoulders on the folded coat. He set his hands on his knees.

"Now."

Cael lifted. He felt the shaft come light in his hands, and then lighter, and he looked back along it and saw the wheels leave the rock.

Brom had stood up under her.

He had not straightened altogether; he was bent like a man beneath a low beam, and the cords of his neck stood out, and every part of the cart that was not on two shoulders at the front was on his. It was the whole of her. Cael watched the two wrists, on the two knees, and waited to see Iron Skin start at them, as he had seen the grey start at a ferry and a wash whenever a weight came on suddenly. Thirty animals were scraping on the hill above, and there was a page in Karis's coat that said what a start might be worth.

The wrists stayed the colour of wrists.

Brom took two short steps to the edge. Then he took one long one, with his left boot going out over two feet of nothing and coming down on the western lip, and the cart went over the crack on a man's back. The tail-board swung out on its chains as she tipped.

It did not strike. Lira had a hand under it on the near side, and Karis had the back of her wrist against it on the off, and they walked it across between them and laid it against the frame as one shuts a door on a sleeping house. Nobody had told either of them to be there. He had been looking at Brom, and had not seen them move.

The wheels came down on rock. Brom backed out from under her on his knees and stayed there, breathing.

"Carrying," he said, when he could.

"I've stopped arguing," said Karis. "I'm only counting now." She had his left arm turned to the light already. The cloth was dry at the wrist, and she let it go. "Eight. Nine feet. Broken across, clean, two feet wide, no bottom found. Cart: taken over on one man's back, with four to steady her." She stopped. "And then I shall write that a carter by himself does not cross it, and that two planks of oak might serve, eight feet long, and that I haven't tried them."

"Put that it didn't bang," said Lira.

"That isn't a measurement."

"It's the best thing I've done all week. Put it."

---

Beyond the eighth the mountain let go of the bench at last, and it opened like a hand.

He had lived for five days on a shelf that he could have thrown a stone across. Now the wall on his left fell back in a long curve and came round again two hundred yards on, and between the wall and the drop there lay a bay forty yards deep, floored from side to side with fine loose shale that had come down the face and spread. The slope above it was the same roof-pitch of plates, but lower; the upper shelf ran across it not three hundred yards up. He did not need to count what was on that shelf, and he counted it all the same, and it came to thirty.

Seln had a fire laid, and he was sitting beside it with his hands on his knees.

He had chosen the eastern corner of the bay, hard against the eighth narrowing, where a slab leaned out from the wall and water ran down the rock behind it into a basin the size of a hat. The cart stood on sound stone there, and that was as well, for she would have floated on every yard of the bay's floor.

"You'll have seen the rock," said Seln.

Cael had. Nobody could have missed it. Two-thirds of the way along the bay a blade of pale stone stood up out of the shale, quite alone, halfway between the foot of the face and the edge. It lay square across the fall of the ground, like a wall across a field, and it was as long as a barn and no thicker than a garden wall. At either end it was no higher than a man's waist, and in the middle it was twice his height, and the evening sun lay along its upper side and left the lower black.

"I walked round it this afternoon. The shale is heaped against the upper side of it to the height of my chest, and it has gone round both ends, as water goes round a pier. On the lower side there is bare floor, a piece as large as this camp, with nothing lying on it at all." Seln turned his hands over to warm the backs. "That has not happened once. A slide has come down this face and gone round that rock a good many times, and never yet filled the place behind it. I should have slept there, if it had water. I do not care to carry a skin seventy yards across loose stone in the dark."

"A fin," said Karis. She was shading her eyes. "It's a harder bed, standing on its edge. I've seen it drawn."

Cael put it in the catalogue, with its length and its height and the bare floor on its lee side. He did not know what he would want it for, and by now that was no reason to leave a thing out.

Lira had not sat down. She had walked on along the wall, past the fin, to the place where the bay shut; and after he had drunk he went after her, in two stints, across shale that sighed and crept under his boots.

She was standing where the bench began again, and it was not a bench any longer.

"That's it," she said. "That's his ledge."

It ran out from her feet along the wall of the gorge, dead level, as straight as a plank laid on trestles. He judged it six feet wide, and in places eight. For most of its length a roof of rock leaned over it. It went on for three hundred yards, and there it came to a shoulder of the mountain, and beyond that shoulder was nothing but evening air, with a brown valley under it, and in the valley a pale thread that was a road.

"Three hundreds," he said. "That's all that's left of his table."

"And then down the other side to a ford, and a man counts out seventy." She had her arms folded. Her right foot was set a little forward, on its whole sole, and she was looking along the ledge as he had seen her look along a painted floor at Norhold before the bell. "It's the best ground on the whole mountain. It's rock from end to end. There isn't a plate on it."

"It's six feet wide."

"So was the seventh, near enough, and that was a crust. This is a shelf." She glanced back, once, at the face above the bay, and lowered her voice though they were alone. "I'll run it at first light."

He said nothing against it. It was her work; she had chosen it on the first day, and he had written sixty-two lines on the strength of her feet.

They went back to the fire. Brom had the bacon out, and Karis was holding the long leaf to the flames to read down her last column, and up on the slope the scraping had begun to slow as the cold came on.

He wrote with his back against the third box.

*Seventy-seventh day. The bay at the west end of the bench. Sixty-two lines, and Karis's table says yes to every one of them, with a reason. Eight narrowings. The seventh has a hand to spare. The eighth is broken clean across, two feet, and Brom took the cart over it on his back; his wrist is dry. The tail-board did not bang. Lira wants that entered.*

*In hand, forty-six. Seventy more at the ford.*

*The nine: held, the whole length of it. Anchor asked at the seventh as it has never asked. Deployment since the Line: one.*

*The traverse holds. The map's good. Karis says the herd moves off declarations, which means the loudest thing on this mountain is us, and we've been quiet for five days. One more day. First honest job I've ever had that didn't have a fight at the end of it. Sleep.*

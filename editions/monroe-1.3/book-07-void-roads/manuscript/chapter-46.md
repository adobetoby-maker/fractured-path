# Chapter 46 — Doors Open Both Ways

Seln asked for the other paper before he would speak about this one.

Cael took it out of Hesk's book, where it had lain since the ninetieth night with five strokes at its foot. Seln held the two sheets up to the fire, one in each hand, so that the flame stood behind both; and for some time he looked through them, as he had looked through the first on an inn's boards, and read no word of either.

They were alone under the ash. The nearest of the other fires was forty yards off, with two carters asleep at it, and the mule stood between with the boxes under her blanket. Brom had built their own fire up. Lira sat on the middle box with her foot set down whole and her right hand over the knot of her sling.

"The margin is pricked by the same wheel," said Seln. "There is a tooth missing from it. Every eleventh hole stands a little wide of its neighbours, on both sheets, in the same place. And the hand is one hand. He makes the foot of that letter with a second stroke, which is a fault, and he has made it twice." He lowered the papers. "They were written at one desk, by one clerk, I should say at one sitting. He ruled two sheets and filled them one after the other, and they were folded into the same wrapper before the ink of the second was a day old."

"Before we'd so much as seen the first," said Brom.

"Before anybody on this side of the water had seen it. Whoever sent them knew, when he sealed that wrapper, what he would wish to ask next if the first were answered."

He laid the older sheet on his knee and kept the newer in the light.

"The first was a question, and I said so at a stake. It sent a crew to fetch what was not there, in order to learn what became of the crew. A hundred marks is a fair price for a question. It is high, since nobody had ever taken such a paper; but a carter could believe in it." His finger moved to the bracket. "Nobody who has kept a book believes in this. It is two days out and two home, for a case that one person can carry on a strap. There is not a hold on that sheet that would pay thirty for it."

"So he's overpaid," said Lira.

"No; he has measured. He knows what the first was priced at and who took it. He knows that five people took it who have a hundred or so in hand and the cold term coming, and he has asked himself what sum such a crew cannot leave hanging on a nail." Seln's voice did not change. "That's not a wage. That's a lure, cut to fit."

The fire settled, and Cael kept his eyes on a sheet of cream paper and went back, since he could not help it, to an evening at an inn's boards twenty-two days before. Four of them had given reasons for the first paper. He had given his own last but one, and it had been two words, and he had been rather proud of how plain they were.

"There is a second thing," said Seln. "Read me the place again. Only the place."

Cael gave it to him: south of Lowmarch, and the first day, and where the road drops off the ridge and the ground opens.

"The first described a rise and a bend, which any carter could have told a clerk across a table. This one is written by somebody who has stood on that ridge, or who had beside him the notes of a person that did. *The ground opens.* He knows what is at the foot of the hill, and he has chosen it." He laid the second sheet upon the first. "I have sat at the desks where such things are drawn up, though never for a matter of this weight. Before an office moves to take a thing out of somebody's keeping, it makes a list. It writes down what is to be taken, and who holds it, and what each of the holders is able to do if he should object. Then it looks for a place where he cannot."

"That's what they call it," said Karis slowly. "At the hearing. An inventory."

"This isn't a contract. It's a pre-restriction inventory. They've mapped what we do inside, and they've priced a floor where we can't do it."

Nobody at that fire needed the list read out. There was a Wind on it, and an Iron Skin, and an Ember, and a Shadow, each with a tier and a rank, in files that had been kept for years on the far side of the Line. And there was a boat gone west ten days ago to say that every one of those four had walked onto a certain kind of ground and been able to do nothing there.

"They don't know about Cael."

It was Karis, low and quick. She had both hands on her shut notebook, and her eyes were on Seln.

"They don't need to."

His hand had gone to the strap across his chest, as it went a dozen times a day, and he did not seem to know that it had.

"They want the case. The boy's the price of the case."

He said it to the fire, in the mild voice in which he had once read out a floor schedule, and then he sat back out of the light. Cael understood him well enough: a broker's clerk had stood at a plank for a hundred days and watched which crew took which paper, and it had wanted only one fact about a boy to write the second sheet; the boy had supplied that fact himself, aloud, at an inn's boards, when he showed that he had never yet walked past a paper that paid.

---

"Each of you says it," said Cael. "Nobody answers till the last has done. Lira."

He gave her the first place because she had not taken her eyes off him since the nail, and she did not get up from the box to use it.

"Walk away."

She let that stand by itself for a breath, so that nobody should mistake it for the start of something longer.

"He's just told you what it is. It's got a ruled edge and a word for itself. I don't need it read to me a third time." Her right hand had closed on the knot. "There's a walk on that oak at fifty that nobody's scratched, a string of salt to the middle holds before the pass shuts. There's a fence at forty, the day the hold comes off it. That's ninety marks of work that was posted by people who want a fence. We'd be down from both before the snow."

"And the circles?" said Karis.

"I'm coming to them. You said four wheres. One's walked. One's this, with a paper nailed across it." She nodded at the sheets on Seln's knee. "The third's a long way south and east, with no road inside a day of it, and nobody's put a price on it at all. So we go and pace that one, on our own time, with a healer at the edge. I'm not saying leave them alone. I'm saying I'll not walk into the one that somebody's paying us to walk into."

She looked down at her sling, and up again.

"And I've an arm that's two weeks old. I'd as soon you heard me say that as think that I'd left it out."

"Brom."

Brom had been turning a stick in the fire, and he laid it down, and rubbed the four dark lines on his fingers with his thumb, and was a good while beginning.

"Three hundred's a number a man doesn't reach for."

"That's what I said," said Lira.

"It isn't. You said what the paper is. I'm saying what the number is." He frowned at the coals. "My father bought a horse at a fair, the year I was nine. A grey, sixteen hands, with a neck on him like a bridge. Every man on that field could see that he was worth three hundred of anybody's money, and the dealer wanted a hundred for him, and wouldn't say why."

Cael kept still, since it was the third time in twelve days that Brom had spoken of that house, and he had never before told a whole thing out of it.

"My father knew horses. I'll give him that; it was the one thing that he did know. He went all over that animal with his hands for an hour, and there was nothing wrong with a leg or an eye or his wind. So he stood there. I can see him standing. He knew that a sound horse doesn't go for a third of his price, and he knew that he'd found nothing, and the two wouldn't lie down together." Brom's big shoulders moved. "And then he reached, since it was three hundred for one, and he could not bear to see another man lead it off."

"What was wrong with it?" said Lira.

"It went over backward. Not every day. It would carry you a fortnight like a lamb, and then it would stand straight up on a hard road and come down on its own saddle. It broke his collarbone, and a groom's leg, and the groom never walked right after." He picked the stick up again. "The dealer hadn't cheated him. That's what I've kept of it. The dealer had priced that horse to a penny. It was my father who read the figure as luck."

He looked across the fire at a cream sheet.

"I don't know what's wrong with this one. Seln's told me, and I believe him, and I still couldn't put my hand on the place. But I know what my father's face looked like. I've been wearing it since that bench." He shut his mouth, and opened it once more. "That's mine. I've not said go, and I've not said stay."

"Karis."

She had her notebook shut under both hands, as she had held it at an inn's boards.

"I would go alone."

Lira's head came round.

"I am saying it first, so that it is not taken for a vote on anybody else's arm. I have a cup of barley and a bowl in the middle box. In the cup are the kinds that I should swear to. In the bowl are the kinds that only a second floor can give me or take away, and I have had my thumb over the figure for nine days." Her voice was small and quite steady. "A man with apple trees told me that I should not tell a keeper from an eater in the one basket. I have one basket. I shall have one basket until I have stood on another of those floors with paper in my hand, and here is another, a day and a half from this fire."

"With a lure nailed across it," said Lira.

"Yes. I have heard what it is, and I do not dispute a word of it. I say only that I should go if there were nobody to go with me, and that I should very likely not come back, and that I should go regardless." She drew a breath. "That is not a reason for any of you. I want it known for what it is. It is the thing that I want, and I have learned to say those aloud."

---

"Seln," said Cael.

"Take it."

"They'll send people. A lure's no use if nobody's waiting at the end of it. People have faces. Six years I've wanted a face."

He had not raised his voice. Cael had heard that voice give the width of a road and the hour of a watch, and he had heard it tell a first-year where the copying ink was kept; he had never before heard it want anything. It was the same voice still, and that was what made the hair stand on his arms.

Lira was staring across the fire.

"You'd walk us onto that floor to get a look at somebody."

"I should walk myself onto it. I have told you what I think the paper is. I did not say that it was wise to answer it." Seln folded his hands on the two sheets. "I was asked what I would do, and I have said. I should be sorry to have it counted for more than one hand."

Karis had opened her mouth and shut it again, and Cael was glad of it. Whatever lay behind those few words was Seln's, and he had laid on the table as much of it as he chose.

It was Cael's turn, and he had had the whole of the walk down from the inn to find a reason; he had found several, and he set them all by, since he had learned at an inn's boards what his reasons were worth when he dressed them.

"I'll say the thing that none of you can say for me. Then I'll say why it matters."

He looked round the four of them, one face at a time.

"Inside the circle, I'm the only one who works. They chose a floor that makes the rest of you helpless. They don't know it doesn't do anything to me."

It was the first time that he had said it to all of them in so many words. He had told it on a gate-walk as a thing that frightened him, and in a loft as a thing that he had hidden, and he had never before set it down in front of them as a fact to be used: four Paths stopped at such a line, and ten fragments did not. It lay there among them in the firelight like a tool on a bench.

"Seln's read the paper right, and Brom's read the number right. It's a trap, and it's been cut to our measure by somebody with our files open. Everything in it has been reckoned." He kept his voice low. "But it stands on one belief, which is that a certain kind of floor does the same to all of us. And that's the one thing about this crew that nobody in the west can find out. They sent a paper to learn whether we'd come out of the first, and we did, and what they've learned from it is wrong."

"They can't know that it's wrong," said Karis.

"No. That's the whole of it. A man who's made a mistake can find it, if he looks. They can look at this for a year. Every file they own says that it's sound."

Brom was frowning.

"So we walk into it since it's got a hole in it."

"We walk into it since it's the only one that ever will have. If we leave that sheet on the nail, the people who wrote it don't go home. They write a third, and they cut it closer, and they pick the ground again; and the next ground mightn't be a floor at all. It might be a ford, or a street." He turned to Lira, since it was hers to hear. "You're right about the walk and the fence. You're right about the arm. If this were only for money, I'd hang it back myself and we'd go and carry salt. But I think that this is the one time they'll come for us to a place where I'm more than they've counted, and I don't think we'll be offered it twice."

"And what does it cost?" said Lira.

"Your arm on a floor again, a fortnight old. Four of you with nothing, inside a line, if it goes badly. And a healer who said not without her." He did not soften any of it. "So it's Millrace first, tomorrow, and she hears where we'll be. I'll not ask her to come. But she isn't going to learn afterward that we went."

---

Brom had got up while he was speaking, and gone to the mule, and stood with one hand on her withers and his back to the fire.

"Rooke said a thing to me once," he said, without turning round.

Lira's eyebrows went up, and Cael felt his own go. In two years on a bluff, and a hundred and twelve days of roads, he had never heard Brom repeat what another person had said to him. Brom carried other people's words inside his shirt, on dead slips, and did not say them over.

"It was my first season up there. I'd got a second-year into the corner of the frames, and I had him shut in, with my back to the gap. I thought that I'd done something clever. And Rooke came down off the rail and stood behind me, in the gap that I was so pleased with, and he said it in my ear."

He turned round then.

"Doors open both ways," he said. He stopped there, and let his breath go, and his jaw worked once. "I didn't know what he meant for a year. I thought he was telling me that the other man could get out. He was telling me that anything I'd made to shut a man in was a thing somebody could come through behind me." He came back to the fire and sat down heavily. "They've built a door to shut us in a room. It's a good door. I've looked at it all evening, and I can't see what it's hung on. But it opens both ways. He's just told you which way they've forgotten."

He looked at the cream sheet, and then at Cael.

"I don't like it. I'm in."

"Hands," said Cael.

Karis's went up, and Seln's, and Brom's, and his own. Lira kept her right hand on the knot of her sling, and she looked at the four, without any anger, as she might have looked at a ledge that she had already made up her mind to run.

"I want it entered that I said walk away. I was right. We're going anyway."

"It's entered," said Cael. "In those words."

"I'll read it over your arm."

"I'd expect you to."

Seln had the two papers still. He held the newer out across the fire, and when Cael took it he did not at once let go.

"One rule, before it is marked, and I shall say it again at the place. You have told us what you are on that floor. You are that only for as long as nobody finds it out." His mild eyes were on Cael's. "If there are hands, in the dark, at arm's length, do as you think fit. If there are eyes, then you are a boy with a knife, and you do nothing whatever that a pair of eyes could carry to a boat."

"And if doing nothing costs somebody an arm?"

"Then you will have to choose. You have chosen it once, on a white floor, and I watched you. I do not pretend that it grows lighter." He released the sheet. "But the hole in their paper is that they do not know. Use it in front of them once, and there is no hole."

Cael wrote by the fire while Brom banked it, with Lira reading over his arm as she had promised.

*Hundred and twelfth day. Lowmarch, the meadow. Pike said the eighth line in our hearing, twice. Eleventh; Teague ten up. He says his book has no column for what the line means. A hundred and two in hand.*

*A second paper, off the nail. The same clerk and the same wheel. Three hundred. Lira said walk away, and she said it first, and she was right; four hands went up after it, and mine was one.*

*Priced too well. Seln says it's for the case. I say the floor's for me and they don't know it. South, tomorrow. Lira's arm is two weeks old. Brom says doors open both ways. He's quoting Rooke. He's never quoted anyone.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirteen.*

"*She was right*," said Lira. "You've put it in."

"It's what happened."

"I know that it is. I only wanted to see whether you'd write it when it was against you."

---

He carried the paper back up the bank alone.

The room was thinner still: the three grey men had gone, and their dice with them, and a girl was turning the benches up along one wall. Pike sat where he had sat, under the cloth, with the fire fallen to red and the book shut on his knee.

Cael laid the cream sheet on the bench beside him.

"Taken."

Pike looked at it once, and then at Cael, and then at the sheet again; and when he had done he opened the horn pot and dipped his pen without a word. He wrote five short strokes at the foot, side by side, as he had written them on another paper twenty-two days before.

Then he wrote one thing more. He set it above the strokes, in the square capitals that he kept for it, and Cael read it upside down as it was made.

*HOLD.*

"I never asked for that," said Cael.

"No." Pike blew on the ink. "Anybody can ask. I've said yes to one man in thirty years, and he's sitting behind you. This one I'm putting on myself."

He got up, which he seldom did once he was down, and turned his shoulder to what was left of the room. He gave it out as he gave out a line: the recovery, and the Stair road, and the ridge, and the three hundred. He said that it was taken, and by whom. And then he said the other word, and he said it slowly.

"Hold. On my own mark. If they're late down, nobody comes to me for that slip. It stays theirs till I've seen them or seen where they stopped." He sat down again. "That's said. I'll say it to the meadow in the morning."

Cael stood with nothing in his hands, and he knew what that word was for: a captain asked for it so that a job should be kept for him. Pike had turned it round, as a healer had once said that her table was turned: he had put it on a paper so that if five people did not come back, nobody else should be sent up that ridge after three hundred marks to learn why.

"Thank you," he said.

"It's a line in a book. Don't thank a man for his book." Pike's one hand lay on the cover. "I said what I'd do with that paper. You've done the other. I'd as soon have you down to tell me which of us was right."

Cael turned to go, and the man with the leather on his wrist was between him and the door.

He had not heard him cross the room, though the man's three were on their feet behind him, at their distances, and it was the first time that Cael had stood within a stride of him since a river bank. He was no taller than Cael. He had grey over the ears and a hand-axe in his belt with its edge forward; and he kept his eyes on Cael's face and off his hands.

"You're taking the Stair contract."

"Yes," Cael said.

"It's a bad contract."

"Yes."

The man nodded, once, by the width of a finger. He put no question as to why anybody would take a paper that he knew to be bad. Cael watched him weigh one and set it down again, as a thing that he had no means to price; and he saw the grey eyes go, for a breath, to the door, beyond which there was a meadow, and a fire, and a girl with one arm.

"My crew's at Millrace in two days," Teague said. "If you're late back, we'll come look."

He had said four things to Cael at a ford, and every one of them had been a price. This was not a price. Cael went over it for the catch, since he had been taught in a hard school to look for one, and there was none in it: there was a place, and a day, and four people who would walk up a ridge.

He had said a certain word to registrars and to magisters, and to a hooded seat, whenever he was told a thing that he could neither fight nor thank them for. He had never once said it to anybody who stood above him on a wall. It was the only word that he had.

"Noted."

Teague stood aside from the door.

---

On that same night, the hundred and twelfth, Seln had the second watch, and he spent it reading two papers that were no longer in front of him.

He needed no light for it, and he sat against the ash with his knees drawn up and the case on his chest, under his coat, where it had hung on every road past the Line; and the two sheets lay side by side in his head as plainly as they had lain on his knee. He could have counted the holes in either margin, and indeed he had done so, in the room, while a one-armed man was speaking.

The frost was coming down, and he could hear it, very faintly, in the grass. Four people slept behind him, and a mule stood over three boxes; and at eleven other fires in that meadow there was nobody awake.

He began with what he knew, which was the clerk.

He had never met the man. He knew that he was no longer young, since his capitals were of a pattern that had not been taught for twenty years; that he ruled his own sheets and had been given a damaged wheel to do it with; and that he had been told what to write and nothing about why.

Seln could hear the dictation in it. *South of Lowmarch; the first day.* A clerk left to himself would have put *one day south*. Somebody had stood at that man's shoulder with a surveyor's notes in his hand and read them out a stop at a time, and the clerk had set them down as they came.

So there had been two people in that room, at the least: there was a hand that wrote, and a voice that knew the ground.

He had spent fifteen years as the first of those, and he knew what it was to write a true line at another person's bidding without being told what the line was for. He had written sixty-three subjects into bundles in that way, each of them a person with a face that he could have drawn; and he had sent every bundle up a stair to people whom he had never once seen.

That was the part of it that he had not been able to make four friends understand, and tonight he had not tried.

He let himself go back to the corridor, which he allowed seldom. It was distempered green to the height of a man's shoulder, in a district seat three postings and twelve years behind him, and he had stopped dead in the middle of its floor with an armful of somebody's travel endorsements. A clerk had put his head out of a side door to ask whether he was unwell. He had not been unwell. He had understood, in the space between one step and the next, what a certain run of ledgers was that he had copied the year before as dull upkeep; and whose question it had been all along.

After that there had been a press, in a records room on a top floor, and five weeks, and a case.

Twelve years. He had kept it by never once describing it. He had carried it from posting to posting in the bottom of a satchel, under his shirts, and written *nothing to report* every week over the top of it; and in all that time he had never learned the name of one person who had made what was inside.

He knew their paper: how they folded it, and where they filed it, a little above where it should have sat, as only somebody could who was able to reach. He had their handwriting by heart, and it had not one face in it.

He had been taught four grades of pressure, in their order. There was the record, and then the instrument, and then the knock, which was paper that nobody would own. Last came the hand, which was a road with no paper at all. He had believed for fifteen years that the hand was the worst of them.

Then four people had come home to a hill from a chalk cut on the Ostrand road, and had told him what the hand looked like when it came: people with staves, who did their work without a word, and broke off in four heartbeats, and left nothing behind them but footmarks. He had told them that what stood in that road was a glove.

A glove had something inside it. He had thought so then, and had kept it to himself, since it was not a finding.

Now there was a fifth grade, which nobody had taught him, and he had held it in his two hands tonight. It was a lure, and a lure was a different kind of thing from the other four. The record could be kept by nobody in particular, and the instrument read by any clerk; the knock came through a door and the hand out of a ditch. But a lure that is taken must be met. Somebody had to be there.

*People have faces.*

He had said it at a fire, to four people, in his own voice, and he was a little astonished at himself still. He went over it now as he would have gone over a return before it was sealed, to see whether it was true or only well put.

---

It was not revenge, though he looked for that first, since it was the commonest thing. He turned out every pocket of it, and found that he did not want anybody on that rim hurt, and had no wish to see a hood pulled back by force. He had spent too long being the quiet man at the edge of a room to suppose that the people who would wait at the foot of that ridge were anything but quiet men at the edge of somebody else's room, sent by a stair that they had never climbed.

What he wanted was smaller than that, and he thought that it was worse.

He wanted to know that it was done by persons.

For twelve years the thing in the case had been like weather to him. It had fallen on forty children, and on a good many more before them, as rain falls on a field; and a man does not go looking for the face of rain. He files what it did; and Seln had filed it, and had been very good at that, and it had let him sleep.

But a boy had come up a hill with a word on his file where a tier should have been, and had looked at every instrument that was turned on him as though somebody had built it. That had been the end of the weather. If a thing was built, then there was a builder, with a walk, and a way of holding a pen, and a tiredness at the end of the day. Seln could no longer do without seeing one: he wished to stand on the same ground as a single one of them, in daylight, and learn what a person looked like who did this for his living.

It was a clerk's want, since a file wanted a name at its head; he did not think that it was a noble one, and he entered it as it stood.

Then he audited his vote, since he owed that.

He had been asked what he would do, and he had answered for himself, and he had said that it should count for one hand. All of that was correct. It was also true that four people had heard him want something for the first time in two years, and that three of them were of an age to be moved by it. He had seen the researcher shut her mouth on a question. He had seen the big one look at him as he looked at a load that had shifted.

Had he put his thumb on the scale? He thought not. The boy had ruled on his own ground, and the boy's reason was better than his: it was about what the paper did not know, and it would have stood if Seln had never spoken.

He was not certain. He wrote that down too, in the place where he kept such things, which was nowhere.

There was a way to be certain, and he had been looking at it since the fire was banked.

The paper followed the case. He had said so himself. If the case were to go south tonight, by itself, on one man's chest, then by first light there would be nothing at this fire that anybody in the west had priced. Five people would wake to a walk at fifty and a fence at forty.

He knew the south road; he had walked the first hours of it alone in their second week here, as he had walked every road out of this town. He could be on the ridge before the stars turned, and he would have his face, very likely; and an arm two weeks old would never come within a mile of that floor.

His hand was on the strap, and he let it stay there, and looked at the thing squarely.

He had lost an argument on a shelf above a valley with no name, on the second night past the Line. He had proposed that the watches should be his to arrange as he judged best, since he had fifteen years of judging them; and a girl with a ruled table had told him that the rota was not set aside for anybody's judgment, his own included. He had been angry for an hour and grateful ever since. A man who has decided things alone for fifteen years does not stop because he has resolved to; he stops when four people will not let him.

And there was the other reason, which he had given a boy in a loft. He had told him that he would sooner see a healer warned of what might be done to her than be used as the reason for keeping her ignorant. If that was good for a healer, it was good for a crew. He could not carry the danger off in the night and leave them to find a cold place under a tree. That was the service's way, and it had been his own, every week, for fifteen years: to know what was best for a subject, and arrange it, and say nothing.

He had resigned from that. He had merely never filed it.

Four hands had gone up at that fire, and one had stayed down, and had asked to be entered, and was going regardless. He was not entitled to take a decision out of five people's hands by removing its object, however kindly he meant it.

He took his own hand off the strap.

The case lay where it had lain, against his breastbone, with its lock toward him. He knew what was in it to the leaf. He had not opened it since a reading room above Ostrand, and he did not open it now; there was nothing in it that would tell him who was waiting at the foot of a ridge, and there was one leaf in it that was not a copy.

The stars had turned as far as they turned in one watch, and he got up, without a sound, and went round the four sleepers, and the boxes, and the mule, as he had gone round every camp of theirs since the shelf. Then he bent and touched the girl's right shoulder with two fingers. He chose that shoulder with care.

She woke at once, as she did everything.

"Anything?" said Lira.

"Nothing," said Seln.

It was the report that he gave at the end of every watch. She took the blanket that he held out, and put her back against the ash, and he lay down where she had been lying, which was still warm.

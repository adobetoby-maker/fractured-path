## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —
  present throughout, not just in set-piece fight chapters.
- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult
  crossover register, short-to-medium sentence construction, low jargon-density outside the
  progression-vocabulary spikes.
- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 52 — Bearing

Cael went up to the brow when the sun was a hand above the ridge, and the man who stood there did not turn round until he was within five paces.

It was the hundred and fifteenth morning. He had slept as a stone sleeps, and woken to find that the ground came to his feet when it should, and that his right arm would bend. His left palm was wound in what remained of a roll of tape. He had counted four upright shapes on the rim before he had his boots on, one at each of the places where a hood had stood the day before; and for the length of a breath he had been on his feet with nothing in his hands.

Then he had seen that every one of the four had a face.

"You've put your crew round it," he said.

"I have." Teague was looking down at the floor, and at the square in the middle of it, with his thumbs in his belt, over the hand-axe. "There's a thing down there that you were sent for, and I'd guess that you mean to go and get it."

"Yes."

"Then you'll be an hour or two with your backs to the sky. I'd not care to be, after yesterday." His grey eyes came round. "We'll hold it till you're up."

Nobody had asked it of him. Cael had lain against a bar of iron the night before, shaking, and had watched a lantern go away round a quarter of a mile of ruts to look for whatever six people had left; he had thought then that it was more than a captain owed to a crew ten lines under him, and he had not known how to say so. He did not know now. He had one word for a thing that he could neither refuse nor repay, and he had already given it to this man once.

"Noted," he said.

Teague nodded, by the width of a finger. Then he stooped, and took up the horn lantern that stood by his boot, and held it out.

"That's a deep hole. You'll want two lights."

Karis had the long cord out by the time he came down.

It was the same that she had made at Thornwater, two box-ropes spliced end to end and marked off with twists of red wool; and she had it laid along the turf from the grey stone northward, with its near end under her boot. She looked as though she had not slept, and she looked very happy.

"I said that I should pace it in the morning, and the morning was otherwise engaged." She was winding the slack round her fist. "Brom may not haul; he is to stand and be seen. So I want Seln for the turfs, and I want Lira on the line."

"I've one arm," said Lira.

"You have one arm, and a hand at the end of it, and a sigil, and I need exactly those three. You take the far end, and walk out along the edge with your boot half on it, until the cord is taut. Where you stop, he cuts."

"How do I walk along a thing that I can't see?"

"As you found it last night: your Arbiter will tell you which foot has gone over."

It took Lira the first three cords to learn the trick of it, and after that she did not put a foot wrong. Cael walked behind with the notebook, and wrote each station in a bad hand. She went with the cord's end shut in her right fist and the sling tied high against her collar-bone, a pace at a time, as a child walks along a kerb; and every few strides she tipped her head, as if she were listening, and moved her left boot a hand's breadth out or in.

"Gone," she would say, under her breath. "Back. Gone. It's like a wall that you can lean your foot on."

At each cord's end Seln knelt and cut a turf, and Karis came up along the rope, counting, and laid her short cord from the turf to the place where the long one would next lie; and at every station it was the same short measure, as it had been on the Fallow.

They went the whole way round, with the Quieting on their right hand and four people watching from the rim.

The spear stood on the south, a tall man, grounded, with the butt of his weapon on the frozen turf and both hands on the shaft; and he watched a girl with one arm find an edge with her foot, sixty times over, and said nothing whatever. On the west it was the boy, sitting on his heels in the ruts with a heel of bread. He stopped chewing when Lira came abreast of him, and he had not started again when they were a hundred yards on.

They passed a good many bars of iron. Every one was old, and scaled, and stamped with the same two figures, and each stood within a boot of a turf that Seln had just cut.

It was the middle of the forenoon when Lira's fist came back to the grey stone.

Karis went down the page with her pencil, twice. Then she turned back in the notebook to a leaf that was nineteen days old, and laid her finger on the foot of it, and looked from the one to the other.

"It is the same," she said. "Cord for cord, and the odd strides are the same number of strides." Her voice was not quite steady. "I said two hundred, from the ridge, by my eye, and I was twenty short of it, and a stride. It is the Fallow's round, and so it is the Fallow's radius."

"The same size," said Lira. "The both of them."

"To a stride. I should like somebody to tell me how a thing comes to be the same size twice, five days' walk apart, by accident." She shut the book on her finger. "Nobody will. I am going down to look at a wall."

---

They went down with two lamps, and the stair was a different place. Karis had filled her brass one to the neck from the can in the left-hand box.

The day before, Cael had gone down it at a run, in the dark, counting edges. He walked it now behind Teague's lantern. The steps were the grey of the floor, cut out of it and not laid; every edge was as sharp as on the day that it was made, since no foot had ever worn one. On the thirty-first a bolt lay, with three grey feathers, and Lira bent and took it up as she passed, and put it through her belt.

"That's twelve," she said. "He'll want it for his count."

At the twentieth the air changed, as it had changed in the dark, and both flames stood up straight and still. It was colder there than the frost had been, and there was no smell in it of earth, or of water, or of anything that grows.

They were all six of them on that stair. The healer came last.

She had said at the stone that she would not sit outside a second day and listen. Four people were holding the rim who knew what a crossbow was; and she had found out what it was worth to be on the right side of a line when the line was shut. She meant to be where a certain shoulder was; and if anything opened, it could be carried up forty steps and two hundred yards with her walking beside it.

Brom went in front of her, with his right arm bound across his chest, and did not once put his hand to the wall.

Cael catalogued the room properly, since nobody had been able to the day before.

It was ten strides from the door to the western wall, and ten across, and he paced both. The roof was the underside of the floor that they had crossed, a foot above Brom's head, without a beam or a crack; he held the lantern up to it and found his own light looking back at him, dully, as from still water. The floor was the same, and there was dust on it. It lay as thin as the bloom on a plum, and it had taken their boots of yesterday along the northern wall and beside the door, and nobody's anywhere else.

The walls were what he had seen by one small lamp, and a great deal more.

They were cut from the floor to the roof. The work ran in courses, one above another, five to a wall, each as tall as a forearm; and in every course the fine sunk lines stood side by side in their groups, straight and hooked and crossing, as close as the words on a page.

Karis stood in the middle of the eastern wall with her lamp held up, and for some time she did not move at all.

"There is more on this one wall than I rubbed in an afternoon," she said. "There are four."

"Can you copy it?" said Brom.

"No. I have six hours of oil and a roll of paper; I can sample it, and I shall have to choose." She had not taken her eyes from the stone. "It is the same hand. I may say that now, since I can see the bars."

The healer had stopped inside the door, at her stride and a half from the nearest wall. She had been round eleven stones on the Fallow thirty times and had never gone near enough to see that they were cut, and she had sat with her back to this for a whole day in the dark. She held up her two palms to it, slowly, as she held them to a patient whom she had not yet touched; and then she let them fall.

"What are they," said Oryn.

It was hardly a question. She had put it to the wall.

"Lines," Karis said.

She said it at once, without turning round.

"I do not know that they are letters. I said so on a floor nineteen days ago, and I have counted them every day since, and I do not know it yet. They are lines, cut in groups, by somebody who meant something, and that is as far as I can swear." Her lamp moved along the course. "It is a great deal farther than I could swear a month ago."

Seln would let nobody near the middle of the floor until he had been round it himself.

He went on his knees, with Teague's lantern set down on the stone and pushed ahead of him a foot at a time, and he looked at the dust more than at the thing that lay in it. When he had made the whole circuit he sat back on his heels.

"One person came to this. I have his track in from the door, a little to the left of ours, and the dust has lain on it a long while. He knelt here, and here, and I cannot find where he went out." He glanced up. "That may be the dust; I say only that I can read his coming and I cannot read his going. There has been nobody since, and nothing is tied to it, and nothing is under it."

"Then we can look," said Cael.

"We can look."

It was a case. He had taken it for one by lamplight, and it was plainer by two: a flat box as long as a man's arm, of board covered with tarred canvas, with a strap to go over a shoulder. The lid stood open and thrown right back. Nothing was left in it.

Everything that it had held was on the floor beside it, in a row.

"He was at work," said Seln quietly. "A man who is packing puts things in. These have been taken out, and laid down in the order of taking, by somebody who meant to pick them up in the same order when he had done."

There was a book, nearest the case, lying open on its face, and a mallet with an iron-bound head; and beside the mallet lay a square bar of iron as thick as two fingers. And from the case's other end a cord ran out along the floor, pale and thin, toward the eastern wall. It had a knot in it at every fifth span, and it lay as straight as a ruled line for half the width of the room; then it stopped, with the rest of its length in a coil.

"That's all?" said Brom. He sounded almost injured. "I said that a kit was a cart's worth. I said brass."

"It is a kit for one thing," said Karis. She had come away from her wall at last. "A cord to find how far, and a bar to mark where; a mallet for the bar, and a book to say that it was done. He did not come to map this country. He came to put iron on an edge."

She knelt by the bar. Cael held the lantern for her.

It was the same pattern as the two at the stone, and it was clean, since no rust had come to it in that dry cold. Under the beaten head there was the registry's stamp; and under the stamp, punched deep, were two figures, and beneath the figures a short upright stroke with a bar across its foot.

"Thirty-two," said Lira. "With the mark. It's the fellow of the newer one."

"It was never driven," said Seln.

"No." Karis had bent until her nose was a hand from the iron, and she spoke slowly. "Look at the head. It has been beaten flat, as they all are, at the forge; but there are two bright places on it, here and here, where something has struck it since. And look at the point."

Cael looked. The bar had been drawn down to a point at its lower end, four-sided, like a great nail, and the last hair of that point was bright, and turned over on itself.

He had seen a thing like that once before, on a trestle in a barn, on the tip of a pen-knife.

Karis put out one finger and laid it on the floor beside the bar, and moved the lantern nearer. She went over the grey stone an inch at a time, for a foot in every direction, and then for another foot.

"There is not a mark," she said.

She sat back.

"He tried to drive it. Twice, by the head. It wouldn't go. Nothing goes into this."

Cael thought of a man kneeling where she knelt, with that mallet in his fist, in a room under the ground. He had come down forty steps to set a bar of iron in the middle of the thing that he had been sent to measure. He had struck it once, and looked. He had struck it a second time, harder. And then he had laid the mallet down beside the bar, quite neatly, and had not struck a third.

"So where is he?" said Brom.

"I do not know," said Seln.

"He left his tools. A man doesn't leave his tools."

"Nobody leaves a registry kit. It's signed out. You don't get another." Seln's mild voice had an edge in it that Cael had heard only once before, by a grey stone, the night before. "I signed for a satchel and four keys in my first posting, and I answered for every one of them for fifteen years. A surveyor answers for this. If he had walked out of that door on his own feet, he would have carried it, or he would have come back for it. And the people who sent us here knew that it was lying. They wrote *abandoned* on a sheet of cream paper. They left it where it was for as long as they wanted a thing in this country that somebody could be sent to fetch."

"Then he's dead," said Lira.

"Or he could not come back. I shall not choose between those, since I have no leaf that tells me."

It was Seln who turned the book over, and he did it with two fingers, as he would have handled another man's letter.

It was a field ledger, bound in limp hide, no bigger than Hesk's, with a pencil lying in its gutter. The leaves were ruled in faint blue, with a narrow column at the left hand and a wide one beside it; and the wide one was written from top to bottom, down a page and a half, in a small upright hand that used the pencil hard.

"This is not the clerk," said Seln. "This is somebody who wrote standing up, on his knee. It is his own book."

"Read it," said Karis.

She had gone back to her wall, with Lira, and had a sheet of the thin paper spread against the lowest course. She kept her face to the stone; but Cael saw that her pencil was not moving.

"There is a line at the head, set by itself, where a place is named." Seln held the leaf to the lantern for some time. "There are four signs, and after them the number that is on that bar. The number I can read; the signs are from an index, and it is not one that I ever used, or was ever shown."

"What sort of index?" said Brom.

"I don't know what this is in, and I'm not going to say."

He went on to the next line before anybody could ask him again.

"Then there are distances, measured in cords, as she measures; his cord is that one, knotted at the fives. He begins at the edge. *North station to floor.* *East station to floor.* There are eight of those, by the points of the compass and the half-points, and against every one of them he has put the same figure."

"The same," said Karis, to the wall.

"The same, eight times. He seems to have found that as remarkable as you did, since he has underlined the eighth."

"And then?"

"Then a line by itself. *Reach, edge to centre.* She would call it the radius. It is in his own figures, and he has gone over it twice with the pencil, so that it has cut the paper." Seln read it out.

Cael saw Karis's shoulders move, though she said nothing. She turned her head, a very little, toward the leaf of her own that she had filled that morning, which lay on the lid of the middle box; and then she turned back to the stone.

"Below that he has a few lines on the floor and the stair, such as she has in her own table. I shall read them to her when she has a hand free." Seln turned the leaf. "And on the second page there is one line only."

He stopped.

It was so unlike him that all five of them looked up. He was holding the book quite still, a hand's breadth from the horn of the lantern, and his eyes were going along one line of pencil from its beginning to its end and back again.

"It is not finished," he said. "There is a line, and after it a stroke of the pencil that goes off toward the edge of the leaf, and then nothing. The rest of the book is clean."

"What's the line?" said Cael.

Seln read it as it stood.

*third site confirmed on bearing* —

A sheet of thin paper slid down the eastern wall and folded itself on the floor.

Karis had let go of it. She had been kneeling at the lowest course, with her left hand spread to hold the sheet and her right about to begin; and she had come up off her knees as if somebody had laid a cold hand between her shoulders. She stood with her back to the stone, and she had gone white, and then red, and both her hands were open at her sides.

"Say that again," she said. "The last word."

"*Bearing.*"

"A bearing is a line with a direction to it." It came out of her very fast, in the small exact voice. "It is a thing that a person takes with an instrument, between two places, in order to find out what else lies along it. He had two places. He had put iron on both, and he drew through them. And he is saying that there is a third thing on that line, and that he has confirmed it."

"He doesn't say where," said Lira.

"He does not need to say where. That is the whole of what a bearing is." Karis's eyes had gone to the middle box, which Seln had carried down forty steps on his back and set against the northern wall. She took one step toward the box, and Cael saw her stop herself.

"No. Not down here, by a lamp, on my knee. I shall do it once, in daylight, on a level thing, with every one of you looking." She drew a breath that shook. "Lira. I want a sheet."

Lira had picked up the fallen one already, and was holding it out.

They were six hours in that room, by the oil.

Karis worked along the walls on her knees, and then standing, and then on Brom's bent back, which he offered without being asked and would not hear refused. She did not rub everything; she went down each course with her lamp held close and her lips moving, and wherever a group of strokes stood that she had not yet got on paper, she put out her hand behind her.

"Sheet."

It was there. It was there every time. Lira could not hold a sheet against a wall and rub it; that wanted two hands. But she had the roll under her right arm and the pebbles in her belt, and before the first hour was out she had learned the walls better than their reader. She had counted the courses, and had seen that a group which stood in the second course of the east wall came again in the fourth course of the south; and she was standing at the south wall with a sheet torn off and ready while Karis was still on her knees at the east.

"Sheet," said Karis, and found it against her fingers. She frowned. "I had not asked."

"You were going to."

"How do you know where I am going?"

"I do not."

"You do," said Oryn, from the door.

Cael wrote. His right arm would not carry a pencil for more than a line together, and he did it with the left, in a hand that a child might have been ashamed of. Karis called from the wall. *That is the hook with two bars. It is on the east, second course. It is close.* He put a stroke against the slip. *That is the same hook. South, fourth. Close again.*

"It is close on both," said Karis, an hour later, in a voice that he had not heard from her before. "It was wide on the ninth stone. So wide was only the ninth stone. It comes out of the bowl, and it goes into the cup with its fellow."

The cup and the bowl stood on the lid of the box, where the lamp could reach them; she had carried them from Thornwater in a twist of cloth. One by one, through the afternoon, he watched the grains leave the bowl: some went into the cup, and some she laid on the stone by themselves, in a row, and those were the ones that the second floor had told her were no kind at all.

There were twenty-nine sheets when the first lamp began to gutter.

She came to the box then, and sat down on the floor in front of it with her skirt full of pencil dust, and tipped out the cup.

"Kinds," said Lira.

Here." The pencil moved along the sheet to the next one, and the next. "And here. And here. I've paced the spacing because I pace things. It comes every — " she looked at the notebook, where she had written it, and read it out as a number she had checked twice — "every ten cords and a piece. Two hundred and twenty yards and a stride, to the stride. It recurs, on the wall, at the perimeter's radius."

She had gone quite still with the sheet in her hand.

"The floor was carved by somebody who knew the circle's size," she said.

"I am not resting it on a number of theirs."

She had answered him at once.

"I paced that round this morning with two box-ropes and a girl's left foot. He paced it with a knotted line, some years ago, for people whom I should not trust to tell me the hour. We have the same figure, and I do not care in the least who sent him." She laid her hand on the short cord. "A measurement is a fact about a ruler, not about the hand that held it."

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

# Chapter 30 — The Far Lip

Six of them went over the stone a little after noon, and the mule stayed where she was.

Oryn had hobbled her on the western side, with a full net and the two panniers still roped on, and the long shape in its oiled cover lashed along the top of the pack as it had been since a mountain. The healer looked at that cover for a breath before she turned away. Then she walked across the line with her hands open at her sides, and Cael saw her fingers close once, hard, as the count went out of them.

They took very little. Brom had the middle box on his back, with the chips in it and the paper, since whatever a surveyor had left would want carrying home in something. Karis had the long cord and the short one. There was the hedging bill, and Lira's knife, and Seln's, and a skin of water. Everything else that they owned was either in a loft two days west or under the feet of a mule.

"East, along the old line of the road," said Karis. "I want the cord laid from this stone to the middle, a length at a time, as we laid it round the edge. If I have my sum right, the tenth will end among those stones. If I have it wrong, I should like to be told so by the ground and not by a table."

So they went in as they had gone round. Brom held one end at the stone, and Karis walked the other out until it came tight, and stopped; then he came up to her, and she went on. The other four walked behind, in file, in the track that the cord left. The grass stood to Cael's hip. It closed over each of them as they passed and did not quite rise again, and when he looked back from the third length there was a lane trodden through it, as straight as a ruled margin, with a grey stone and a small brown mule at the far end.

He had his nine fragments as he always had them, and he held them. Wind leaned, since Lira was in front of him. Anchor had fallen quiet at the stake and had not asked since. He had opened nothing after a boulder, and he did not open anything now; but he walked those two hundred yards as the only one of the six who knew that he could have, and it was a strange thing to carry at the back of a file.

At the eighth length the ground began to rise, gently, and the grass grew shorter. At the ninth it was at his knee, and he could see the whole of what stood on the swell in front of them.

It was a floor, and it was round.

It lay on the crown of the rise like the lid of a great jar, grey and pale and quite bare, and the short turf came up to its edge and stopped there as cleanly as water stops at the side of a bowl. Round its rim, a stride in from the edge, stood the eleven stones. He had counted them from three hundred yards and had the number right. They were grey, like the floor, and squared, each as thick through as a man's body. Not one of them was whole. The tallest stood twice Brom's height before it ended, and its neighbour came no higher than a knee, and between those two there was every height that a broken thing can have.

"Tenth," said Karis.

She had gone up onto the floor with the cord's end in her hand. It came tight while she was still walking, and she stopped where it stopped her. She was standing at the middle of the round, as near as his eye could tell, with the eleven stones in a ring about her at an equal distance.

She did not say anything about her sum. She laid the end of the cord down very carefully on the grey stone, and set her notebook on it for a weight, and then she paced from where she stood to the rim, in four directions, counting. The four counts came out within a stride of each other.

"Ten cords," she said at last. Her voice was not altogether steady. "I worked it at the stone from the round, with a pencil. And I have walked it now with a rope, from the edge, in a straight line, and there is not a full stride between the two. I have never in my life had a figure come home like that."

Nobody had stepped onto the floor after her. Five people stood on the turf at its western edge, in the track of the cord, and looked.

---

The edge was the first thing that he catalogued, since it was the first thing that his boots came to.

The floor had a lip. All the way round, as far as he could see to either hand, its rim turned up and over in a smooth roll, like the rim of a dish, so that the edge of it stood a hand above the turf outside and a finger above the floor within. He set his foot on it and stepped up. It was a small step. It was enough that a person running would have had to think about it.

Something stirred in him at that rolled edge and asked its one question, as it asked of every arris and sill, and for once Cael could not have answered it. Blade wanted to know whether the thing was an edge. He looked at a rim that curved over like the lip of a bowl, without a corner anywhere on it, and he did not know.

Then he was on the floor, and he stood still.

It was colder than the turf. He felt that through his soles. It was level, so far as his feet could judge, and it gave back no sound at all; Lira had come up beside him in one boot of her own and one the size of a bucket, and neither of them rang. The low sun lay across it from the south-west and threw the shadows of eleven stones in long bars to the far rim. Between the bars the grey shone a little, dully, as a slate shines that has been wetted.

"Pace it," said Karis. "Rim to rim, through where the cord ends. I want yours, since yours are yards."

He paced it from the western lip to the eastern, past her notebook, and had twenty-six strides. He paced it again across the other way, from south to north, and had twenty-six.

"It is level under the cord," she said, when he came back.

She had been busy. She and Brom had drawn the long cord tight across the whole width, lip to lip, a hand above the stone, and she had gone along beneath it on her knees with the short cord, measuring the gap. Now she had the brass straightedge out of the box, with its pebble on a thread, and was laying it toward each side of the floor in turn.

"The same gap from one side to the other, to the thickness of a knot. And no pitch. I have laid this brass on a mountain for seven days, and on every hundred of it the thread hung to one side by so many hands. Here it hangs on its mark whichever way I point it." She sat back on her heels. "A threshing floor is not as flat as this. A table is not."

"What is it?" said Brom. He had let the box down and was crouched beside it, with one big hand laid on the grey. "It's not slab. Slab's got a grain. It's not laid, either; I can't find a course."

"I am about to try to tell you."

She had the lid off the middle box. The masonry chips lay in their row, each in its twist of paper with a number inked on it, and she began to take them out, one at a time, and to hold each against the floor in the slanting light. Cael watched the first dozen. There was a honey-coloured stone that he knew from the walls of Ostrand, and a dark close one from a district hall, and a white chip with a sparkle in it that had come off a bridge. She laid every one of them down on the grey and looked from the chip to the floor and back, and put it away.

It took her a quarter of an hour, and she had more than forty.

"None," she said. She was very pale. "It is not the colour of any of them, or the grain. It has no grain. I have every kind of dressed stone between Denvash and the Line in that box, and a date for each, and this matches not one."

He had knelt down a little way off while she worked.

He put both palms on the floor. It was smooth, and cold, and very slightly rough under the skin, like the shell of an egg. He moved his hands outward over it as far as his ribs would let him, feeling for the thing that every floor he had ever knelt on possessed somewhere: a crack, or a line where one piece met the next.

His fingers found none. And he knew that fingers could be wrong, on a surface so close, and that the others were all looking at a box of chips.

He opened the read.

It was the same thing that he had done at the line, done now for work, and he did not count it a third. For a pace and a half round his knees, and a pace and a half straight down, he had the weight of what he was kneeling on. It was one thing. There was no bed under it, and no joint through it, and no place where it changed. An upright stood within his reach, the short one, and he took in the foot of that as well. It went down into the floor as a horn goes into a skull. He could find no line between them.

He closed it again. Nothing had come through the grass.

He had known since a hill of plates what he thought of a reading that nobody else could check, and it had not altered. So he took the pencil and wrote in Karis's table, under her *level*, the only part of what he knew that any one of them could have got with a hand and a pair of eyes.

*No seam.*

"Nor at the foot of the stones?" said Karis. She had read it upside down.

"Not that I can find."

"Then I shall write that two of us looked." She put it down. "It is a poor sort of catalogue. *Round. Level. No seam. Matches nothing.* I have written four things about it and three of them are that something is absent."

Lira had wandered to the nearest of the tall stones, and she was standing in front of it with her head tipped back.

"There's writing on it," she said.

They went to see. It was not on that one alone. Round every upright, at the height of a person's chest, there ran a band a hand wide; and in the band there were lines. They were sunk in the grey to the depth of a fingernail, fine and even, some straight and some hooked, some crossing others at a slant. They went right round the stone and met themselves. The same band ran along the inner face of the lip, low down, all the way about the floor. Cael looked for a letter in it, and found none that he knew, and he could not have sworn that they were letters at all.

"Whose hand's that?" said Lira. "You've got every sort of alphabet there is, in those boxes. You copied half of them out yourself."

Karis had her nose a hand's breadth from the band. She went the whole way round the stone before she answered, and when she came back to them she was not pale any longer. She had two spots of colour high on her cheeks.

"None I own. And I own more than the registry prints."

She was already reaching for the paper.

It took the rest of the afternoon. She would not copy by eye, since she said that an eye puts in what it expects to see; she laid a clean sheet against the band, and had Lira hold it flat with both hands, and rubbed the side of her pencil over it until the lines came up white out of the grey. She took a sheet to a stone. She numbered each in its corner, and wrote beside the number how high the stone stood, and which way it faced, and Brom carried them to the box, one at a time, on the flat of his two hands, as he would have carried a tray of eggs.

---

"There is nothing here," said Seln.

He had let them work. Cael had seen him go about the floor, in no hurry, with his hands behind him and his eyes down; he had been to the foot of each of the eleven stones, and all round the outside of the lip, and twice he had gone off into the grass and come back. Now he stood at the middle, by the cord's end.

"I have been looking for what we were sent to fetch. A case, I supposed, or a tripod, or a chain. A peg. The mark where a peg had been." He turned one hand over. "There is nothing on this floor but ourselves, and nothing at the foot of any stone. I have walked a round in the grass at twenty yards from the lip, and another at forty. I find our own track, coming in from the west. I find nothing that a person has set down, or dropped, or driven."

"It's been five years," said Brom. "By your stake."

"It has. Leather would be gone, and wood. Brass would not, nor iron. And I should expect to see where a thing had lain, on turf that has never been grazed." He looked at Cael. "I do not say that nothing was ever left. I say that I cannot find it, and that I have looked as well as I know how."

Cael got up off his knees.

He had expected a good many things of this place, and that had not been among them. He stood on a grey floor with the paper's second sentence going through his head: instruments, and abandoned, and a site. Somebody had sat at a ruled desk and written that, with the sum in words and again in a bracket, and had sent it over a river with a hundred marks in a bag. It had lain a month under a stone. A captain had read it twice and put it back.

There were no instruments. There was a stake on the line with a registry's stamp on it, and that was the only thing within a quarter of a mile that a surveyor had ever touched.

"He said he'd write what was done," said Lira slowly. She had her two hands spread on a sheet of paper against the ninth stone. "The old man. If the clerk's mark never goes on it, the box stays shut."

"Then it stays shut," said Brom. "We've walked four days for a line."

"For eleven sheets and a line," said Karis, without lifting her pencil. "I should not undersell the sheets."

Cael looked at the sun.

It had come round into the west while they worked, and it stood now above the hills that they had crossed two days ago. He put his arm out straight and laid his hand along the skyline, fingers together, and then his other hand on top of that. The lower edge of the sun sat on his upper knuckle.

Two hands. He had said it at the eastern runs that morning, with a print in front of him, and Seln had said that he would hold him to it.

"Two stones more," said Karis. She had seen what he was doing. "The tenth and the eleventh. A quarter of an hour, if Lira doesn't sneeze. I shall never stand here again; you heard what he said about going in twice."

He weighed it. He had set a limit, and he knew what a limit was worth that gave by a quarter of an hour the first time it was leaned on. He also knew that nine sheets out of eleven was a thing she would carry for the rest of her life. And the hillside was as empty as it had been at noon. No bird had got up. Nothing had stirred the grass.

"The two," he said. "No more. Seln, go round the far side once, by the east, and see that we've missed nothing under the lip. When you're back, we're in that lane and walking."

"You said two hands," said Seln mildly.

"I did. I'm spending a quarter of an hour that I told you I wouldn't, and I want it entered against me."

Seln gave the small bow that he gave to an order he disliked, and went.

Oryn had sat down on the western lip with her boot off. She had watched the whole afternoon from there, pressing the strip on her heel now and then with her thumb, and she had not once asked what any of it was for.

"It's a queer thing," she said, to nobody. "I've been on a great many floors with people on them. This is the first where I couldn't have told you which of you was tired."

"I feel thirteen," said Lira.

She had said it lightly, with her hands on the tenth sheet. Then she seemed to hear it herself.

"I do, though. That's what it's like. The year before my Kindling I used to run the lanes at Fenmark against the big girls, with nothing in me, just legs, and I'd forgotten there was ever such a person." She shifted her palm for the pencil. "I didn't think that I'd meet her again. I'm not sure I mind."

---

Seln did not come back round.

Cael saw him stop. He had gone along the outside of the lip, on the turf, past the south and on toward the east, where the tallest of the stones threw its bar of shadow off the floor's edge and down the slope beyond. He was a narrow dark figure at the far rim. And he had stopped as a man stops who has put his hand on a hot dish: all at once, with every part of him.

He did not call. He lifted his left hand a little from his side, with the fingers spread, and kept it there.

"Leave that," said Cael.

Karis had the eleventh sheet half rubbed. She looked up at his face, and took the paper off the stone as it was.

They crossed the floor together, the five of them, twenty-six strides, and their boots were silent on it. At the eastern lip Cael stepped down onto the turf beside Seln and saw what he was looking at.

The ground had gone from under the rim.

On every other side the turf came up to the floor's edge and stopped a hand below it. Here the slope fell away to the east more steeply, and at some time the earth beneath the lip had slumped and been carried off, so that the rolled edge of the grey stone stood out over nothing, like the eave of a roof. Under it there was a hollow. It ran back beneath the floor farther than he could see, low and dark, with a mouth as long as a cart and as high as his thigh.

The earth in front of that mouth was bare, and it was trodden as hard as a threshing floor.

He looked at it a piece at a time, as he looked at everything. There were prints. They lay one upon another, hundreds deep, so that it was only at the edges that he could tell a single foot: as broad as a palm, without a claw. The freshest had broken the morning's frost, and their rims were still sharp. To one side there was a heap of bones, deer for the most part, cracked and grey. There was scat, old and new. Tufts of coarse hair had caught on the underside of the stone lip, at the height of a low grey back. And just within the mouth, where the daylight still reached, there was a place in the bare earth the length of a hound, scooped and worn as smooth as the seat of an old chair.

There were a good many such places. He could see the first four. They went back into the dark in a row.

Nothing was in them. He checked that twice. The hollow was empty, as far as light went, and no sound came out of it, and the wind brought him only the smell of cold earth and something rank and dry.

Seln had gone down on one knee at the edge of the trodden ground. He had not touched it. He was reading, with his head a little on one side, as Cael had seen him read a wet margin beside a pool on a mountain.

"This morning," he said, very low. "Before we had laid the first cord. More than six. I should say eight, or nine; they have gone over each other's marks and I cannot do better." He pointed, without lifting his hand from his knee, at the newest prints. They led away from the mouth, down the slope, to the east. "Out. All of them, and none back since."

"Then where are they?" said Lira. It was hardly a breath.

"I do not know. I have seen no animal on this hillside since the sun rose, and I have been looking for nothing else."

Brom had come up on Cael's other side, with the box on his back. He was looking at the bones.

"Why here?" he said. "There's a thousand hollows in a country like this. There's a whole mountain two days west with caves in it. Why would a thing with teeth choose to lie under the one floor that the carters won't walk on?"

Seln did not turn his head.

"They live in here because nothing that walks in can fight."

It lay on the cold air. Cael heard it, and so did the rest, and he watched it reach each of them in turn.

A stillhound hunted what went out of a person. Every hold in that country kept its Paths in its pockets on a road because of it; and here was a place, a quarter of a mile across, where a Path could not be taken out of a pocket by anybody. A deer that wandered over that line was a deer. A carter who did was a man with a stick. And five people who had cleared a ford with a declaration and a knife were standing at the door of the den with a hedging bill and two knives, and a healer whose hands had stopped.

Lira was looking west.

They all were. He saw their heads turn, one after another, across twenty-six strides of grey floor and the eleven stones, to the lane that the cord had left in the grass. It ran away from them down the slope, straight, pale in the low light. The grey stone at its end was too small to see. He knew where it was. From the middle of the floor it lay two hundred and twenty yards to the west, and they were standing on the wrong side of the floor from it.

Then they looked at the sun.

It was on the ridge. He had measured two hands under it a quarter of an hour ago, and spent the quarter of an hour, and the lower rim of it was touching the hills now, red and flattened, and going. The shadows of the eleven stones had run out across the floor and joined. Between them, where the last light struck it level, the grey had begun to shine.

Then four of them looked at Oryn.

She stood a little apart, with her broad hands hanging open. She had told them at noon what they had in her, on this side of a stone, and she met their eyes one after another and did not pretend that it had altered.

And then, all five, they looked at him.

He had the ruling. He was the nearest thing that they had to a captain, and it was his to say which way and how fast. That was the whole of what was in those five faces. Not one of them was asking him anything else.

He stood with it. *Nothing that walks in can fight.* It was true of five of the six. He had known since a boulder that it was not true of the sixth, and he had told nobody, and he was the person they were all waiting on.

---

He wrote it there, at the far lip, standing, with the book against his forearm.

It took him less time than it took Brom to shift the box higher on his shoulders and draw the strap tight. He had always written at the start of a thing. He did not know how to begin one without it, and he thought that this might be the last quiet minute of the day.

*Ninety-sixth day. The Fallow Ring, at the eastern lip of the floor. Sunset.*

*The circle is perfect. The stakes are the registry's. The dogs are inside. Everyone's Path dies at the line and mine didn't, and I've told nobody, and I'm going to need to think about why I haven't. Not tonight. Tonight we're inside the one place on the continent where a stillhound can't lose.*

He looked at what he had put.

There was a line that came next. He had written it at the foot of every entry since a shelf above a valley with no name, and for the last forty-three days it had ended in the same word. His pencil went to the place.

He did not write it. It would not have been true, and he did not yet know what would be. He shut the book on an entry that stopped short, for the first time since he had crossed the Line, and put it away in Hesk's satchel.

Something lifted in him then, soft, behind the other eight. It came as it came at every dusk, in a meadow or on a road or at the turn of a carter's ruts. It was offering to take him out of the light. He stood ten cords and more inside the line, on the far side of a floor where a Shadow of fifteen years had found nothing to lift, and it came up in him exactly as it always had.

He told it no.

The sun was half gone. Along the foot of the eleven stones the floor had turned the colour of milk. Lira had drawn her knife, and Karis had the last sheet rolled inside her coat, and the healer was lacing her boot with quick hard pulls.

Seln had not yet got up off his knee.

# Chapter 26 — White Paper

The ferry brought its sack at noon, and Pike gave out the mail as he gave out everything.

There were nine pieces, and the meadow had come up from its five fires to hear them; it stood about the eave in a loose half ring, leaning a little each time the one hand went into the canvas. The smith had one. The hide-woman had none this month, and went away with her jaw set, and a fold of bark tied with red thread was held up for the ferryman, twice, until a boy ran with it.

"*Cael,*" said Pike. He was frowning at a sheet folded very small. "And under it, *past the Line.* I've had that direction once before." He looked over the heads, twice. "I've the one Cael."

Cael went up for it.

"Stand where you are," said Pike, and his hand was in the sack again. "There's another. Same name, same three words, and a different hand altogether."

So he walked back down the grass with two letters, and thirty people watched him do it.

He knew the first by its writing before he had it fairly in his fingers. It was a narrow upright hand, every letter the same height, and he had read it on a yard's rota slates for two years, and once at the foot of a bout sheet. The second was a square of stiff paper ruled faintly in red, folded in four and tied with a waxed thread. It was a leaf out of a ledger, and he knew of only one person who would have cut a page from her own book in order to write to him on it.

He had sent one letter west to her, on the fourth day, by a courier with a split lip, and it had carried no place to answer to; to the other he had sent none at all. Both of them had written into the air, as an old man in Denvash had, and the air had brought both to the one plank that there was.

"Well?" said Lira. She had come up on his left. "Aren't you going to open them?"

"Not here."

"The river, then. You went down to the river with the last one."

"I had the last one read in a quarter of an hour, three times over, and then there was nothing left of it." He turned the red-ruled square in his fingers. "I've had one letter in ninety days. Now I've two, and four days of road in front of me. I'm going to have one of them at the wash tomorrow, at the noon halt. I'll keep the other for a noon with nothing in it."

Lira stared at him.

"You're rationing your post."

"He's quite right," said Brom, with feeling. "I cut a loaf and a half into fifteen on a mountain, and it was the only thing that week that came out even. A letter's no different. You don't eat it all the day it comes."

"It's a letter. It doesn't go off."

"It does, though," said Cael. "The first reading goes off. You only get the one."

He put them both inside Hesk's book, between the leaves, where a cream sheet had lain the night before. It was a small thing to have decided; but he had gone a long time with nothing in front of him except what he was holding down, and he liked the weight of two unbroken seals a good deal more than he would have liked knowing what was under them.

They spent the rest of that day getting ready for a road. The Oxhollow carter had his off mule at the smith's for a shoe. Brom went over the three box-ropes a hand's breadth at a time, since they were all the rope that the five now owned. Seln was gone from the meadow between the mail and dusk, and came back with pale dust on his boots, and said nothing; and Cael, who had made a promise at that plank, looked at the dust and then at the fire.

---

They were at the wash by noon of the ninety-second day, with the cart behind them and the saddle behind the cart.

It lay as it had lain on four other crossings, dry and red, thirty yards from lip to lip, with a ramp cut down either side and the thorn stump standing on its eastern edge. The carter took his mules through at a walk and halted them on the far flat to blow, and the five sat down among the chock-stones where they had twice made a camp.

Cael took out the sheet that was folded small.

"Aloud," said Lira. "If it's his. I want to hear how he sounds on paper."

"It's mine."

"I know whose it is. I stood on a yard with him for two years, and I'd like to know he's well, and you'll only give me the half of it afterward in your own words." She pulled her knees up. "Read it as it's written."

He looked at the others. Karis had her chin on her fists, and Brom had stopped with the bread halfway out of the bag; and Seln, a little apart as always, had turned his head.

Cael broke the wax, and read it as it was written.

*Cael —*

*Weather first, because you'll want it in order, and I've watched you want things in order.*

*The Crown yard has a new cohort captain. The intake came up the bluff a fortnight ago, forty-one of them, and they've put a third-year over them who was a second-year when you left and whose name you would not know, so I'll not spend the ink. He is fair. He counts slower than you did. They stand where he puts them.*

*Withrow's tenure holds. That is the whole of the sentence. I asked whether there was a longer one and was told there was not, in a way that meant there had been, for a week.*

*A query came down to the registry from somewhere above it. Bracken would not say where and I did not ask twice. It wanted former enrollees' last known direction of travel. Bracken answered it. He gave them a map reference to the Line and nothing past it — which is true, and which Bracken enjoyed being true. He told me so at the plaque wall with his hands behind his back, and then told me he had not told me.*

*I still lose to you every time I run it. I have stopped running it in the yard.*

*Rooke says the yard's quieter. He didn't say it was worse. He also didn't say it wasn't.*

*— E.*

A small wind came down the wash and lifted the corner of the sheet, and it was some little time before any of them found a word to put after that narrow upright hand.

"Good."

It was Lira, and she had said it between her teeth, with great satisfaction.

"What is?" said Brom.

"Bracken. A map reference to the Line and nothing past it. Somebody up a stair wanted to know which way we'd gone, and he gave them a place that everybody already knew we'd been, and wrote it out properly, I expect, with a number." She was grinning. "I never liked him. I'd like to shake his hand."

"He'll have enjoyed it for a week," said Karis. "It is the only kind of joke that a registrar is allowed." But she was smiling too, in her small tight way; and then she sobered. "Withrow holds. I had wondered. They had a week of something, by that, and it went no further."

Brom had put the bread down, and for a little he only looked at it.

"He's quoted Rooke," he said at last. "That's Rooke to the life. He'd not tell you a thing was good, and he'd not tell you it was bad; he'd tell you what he'd seen, and you could carry it home and find out." He looked at his own hands. "*Quieter.* I'd like to know which of us he meant."

"All of us, I should think," said Lira. "You broke more benches than I did."

Cael had read the last lines but two again, to himself. *I still lose to you every time I run it.* He could see the bout as it had gone, on sixty feet of sprung oak, with a blade's edge declared against him in earnest and nothing held back on either side; and he could see a tall, exact person walking it through afterward in an empty yard, alone, looking for the place where it turned. Something in Cael stirred at the thought, quick and bright, and asked *Edge?* of the nearest arris, which was the lip of the wash. He told it no. It was Ephram's, that question. He carried it for him, and paid the rent on it, and had never yet spent it.

"Read me the fourth again," said Seln. "The query."

Cael read it. Seln heard it out with his eyes on the ground, as he listened to anything that he meant to keep, and at the end he nodded once, and asked for nothing more.

"Does it bear?" said Cael.

"I do not know yet. When I do, I shall say."

Cael folded the sheet along its creases. He had two things now, and he laid them side by side in his head, as Karis had once laid a noun beside six numbers. Somebody above a registry had asked which way certain people had gone; and a paper had come over a river with a hundred marks, written by a hand that had learned its trade at a ruled desk, sending whoever took it two days east of a fence.

He did not join them. It would have been very easy, and he had been joined to things himself on no better evidence, by people with seals. Two points could be ruled together by anybody. He would wait for a third.

---

Karis had the printed sheet out that evening, for the first time since a shelf above a valley with no name.

They had camped short of the lower crossing, on a halting-ground that they knew, and Seln had a fire going. She took the roll from the middle box herself, and carried it to a patch of bare beaten earth where the last of the light fell, and unrolled it there on the ground with a chock-stone on each of its corners; and beside it she laid the calfskin.

"Come and look," she said. "I want it seen by more eyes than mine. I have carried this for ninety-two days and not opened it once, since I knew what was on it."

They stood round it, and it was a handsome thing. The registry had printed it from a plate, in black, on a sheet as long as Brom's arm, and the whole of a continent was there: every district with its bound ruled round it, and every chartered road a firm double line, and the towns in three sizes of letter by their standing. Cael found Ardenmere, and a river he had crossed on a bridge, and a bluff above Ostrand with a small square on it. Somebody had written *ridge* beside a pass in the east, in pencil, and a little farther on, *Shelf; a pine; water at 36*, in the same small exact hand.

A hand's breadth past that pencilled word, the plate ran out of things to say.

"There's the Line," said Karis. Her finger went down a ruled stroke that crossed the sheet from top to bottom. "And here is what they have printed east of it."

It was a river or two, laid in without their bends, and a scatter of hills, drawn all alike; and it was seven roads. They left the Line at seven places down the length of the sheet, and each was drawn as a row of short dashes, with no name beside it; and each ran out into the white paper for the length of a thumb, or a finger, and stopped. There was no town at the end of any of them. The dashes simply came to an end, as a person's voice does who has forgotten what he meant to say, and beyond them the sheet was clean to its margin.

"Seven," said Lira. "Which is ours?"

"I make it the third from the top, by where it crosses the first water. They have drawn the crossing and left out the ferry. They have left out Lowmarch." Karis put her little finger on the last dash of the third road. It ended in nothing, three fingers east of the river. "That is about the saddle. By this sheet we walked off the end of everything that is known the first time we climbed out of that meadow, and we have been standing on blank paper ever since."

"He called them that," said Brom. "The old man, at the plank. Void roads. I didn't know what he meant by it."

"It is a clerk's word," said Seln. He had come up behind them. "An entry is void when nothing has been written against it. The road is granted; it is on the sheet. It has no particulars. I should think that a carter was shown one of these once, by somebody who had come over with it in his coat, and asked what the dashes were, and went home with the answer."

Karis had turned to the hide.

It looked a poor thing beside the print. It was scraped calfskin, brown at the edges, drawn in oak-gall and burnt stick by a man who could not write his name; and it had on it every ford between the river and the eastern holds, and every hold as a square with a dot, and the days as ticks.

"Now his," she said. "I have been over this again since the plank, and I have found two things that I had read wrongly. I took them for his thrift."

She showed them. The first they knew: it was the double line that left its straight, two days beyond Thornwater, and went round a patch of bare skin and came back, and she had ringed the patch in pencil.

"That is the only place where one of his roads walks round a blank. I said so on the fifty-eighth day, and it is still so. But he draws a hill with short hatching, and water with three wavy strokes. Look here, south of the ring with the bar, a day down and a little aside. He has hatched hills all across this corner, close as thatch, and in the middle of the hatching there is a round place the size of my thumbnail where he lifted the stick."

There was. Cael would have taken it for a thin place in the hide.

"And here, a long way south-east, among his water. Strokes all round, and a round of bare skin in the midst, with no road within a day of it." She had ringed both. "Three blank circles. One I can source, since a one-armed man has named it. The other two are a man lifting his hand from a skin. I have written *guess* beside each, and I shall not call them anything else."

"Could you rule a line through them?" said Lira. "You've got that brass thing."

"On this? No. It is a map of days. He has drawn a day's haul the same length uphill and down, and bent his roads to fit the skin. I could lay an edge across it and learn something about the calf." She settled back on her heels, and her eyes went from the one to the other, the black print and the brown hide, lying side by side on a carters' halting-ground.

"Their map has no roads. His has no quiet ground. Between them you get the country."

She rolled the printed sheet up again before the dew could reach it. Cael noticed that she rolled the calfskin second, and more slowly.

---

They came up to Thornwater's gate an hour after noon on the ninety-third day.

The ford had been only a ford. Its white stones ran out along the lip of the sill to the bar, a yard apart, without a gap, and there was nothing on the gravel but a wagtail; he had looked down the bar to its tail all the same, and at the bend below, where two had gone on the fifty-third evening and not been seen since. They took the stretch at noon, a mile of pale grass with the frost still lying in its roots and a white stone standing in it, and he walked it with his hands at his sides and nine things shut.

On the platform the bowman lifted two fingers from his stave. Lira put up the great boot, an inch, and set it down.

Then the bar was drawn, and they went in under the lintel, and there was a bench against the inner face of the fence that had not been there before.

It was only a plank on two kegs. A small brown mule stood beside it with her pack still on and her head down, and on the pack, lashed along its top, lay a long shape in an oiled cover. Astride one end of the plank sat a carter whom Cael had not seen before, a heavy man with a grey stubble, who held his right hand out in front of him, palm upward, as a person holds a hand out for coin.

A square woman in a brown coat sat on the other end, and she had the hand between both of hers.

Her eyes were shut and her lips were still, and the sweat had come out along her cropped hair in the cold; and Cael understood that she had come through that gate herself not long before them, and had sat down to this without so much as slipping a pannier. She had owed it for fifteen days.

They stood and waited, and it was not long. She drew a slow breath and opened her fingers, and the carter turned his palm to the light. There was a pink seam across it from the root of the thumb to the heel, dry and shut. He closed the hand, and opened it, and closed it again on nothing, as a man tries a tool that has come back from the smith.

"That's one," said Oryn.

She said it to the fence. Then her eyes came round, tired and level, and went over the five of them from a stride and a half, a single time, and stopped on Cael's left side.

"Fifteen days. Since the break."

He had not opened his coat. She had got it from how he stood, and from a sum that she had plainly done on the road.

"Breath stops at it."

It was what she would want first, and so he gave it first: the tape was where she had laid it, and he had learned the edge.

"Ninth day. I said nine. You've counted."

"So did you."

"I count days. It's the only thing I do count." She got up off the plank by pushing on her knees. "I eat now, and I'm on my back two hours, and then there's a yard of people. You can tell me where you're going while I've a bowl in my hand. Five don't walk up this valley in the cold to keep an appointment about three ribs."

He told her at the long house door, with the steam of a barley broth going up between them.

He did not make a sentence of it. He gave it as he would have given Pike a line: a paper off the plank, under a broker's seal; something to be fetched from a place on the Fallow road, two days east, at a rise; out by the east gate at first light. He said what the place was called, and what Pike called it, and he asked her for nothing. He had no more right to that sentence than he had had at a well, and he found that it was easier to leave unsaid the second time.

Oryn ate, and heard him out, and scraped the bowl.

"The ring," she said. "I know it. I've been round it thirty times, with the mule, by the ruts." She set the bowl on the sill. "You broke on the seventy-eighth. It's twenty-one days by the rule, and I don't lift a tape on a guess, so that's the ninety-ninth. The long one wants a morning with nothing else in it. That's the hundredth. I'd meant to sit at this gate's table from tonight until then; the grain's moving, and that bench fills as fast as I empty it."

She was doing it aloud, as she had done it over a girl's foot on a hillside.

"Two days out. Two back. Whatever you spend there. That has you at this gate again by the ninety-ninth, if nothing goes wrong, and I've walked behind you people long enough to know what *if* is worth." She held up a thick finger. "That's one reason. I want a patient whole and still for the long reading, and I've seen what your crew brings home from a road. I'll not have him back here on the ninety-ninth with something new broken, and wait three weeks more."

"And the other?" said Karis. She had come up without a sound.

"The other's mine." Oryn looked at her own right hand. "Every carter on my round has told me the same thing about that place, for thirty rounds. That a healer's hands are no good in it. Not one of them's seen it. They had it from their fathers. I've let it go by me all that time, since I'd no business in there and a list to keep." Her mouth went hard. "I don't like a thing about my own hands that I've only been told."

She took up the cheek-strap, and let it fall again.

"Six gates wait on me the same days, whether I spend them at this table or behind you on that road. I know who's at every one. It's this bench that goes short, and I'll pay it when I come back through." Then she looked straight at Cael, as she had looked at him once across a hollow of shale. "You're a stop. I've added you."

He could find nothing to say that was the size of it.

"Don't make it a long stop."

She went in to sleep.

He stood at the long house door with an empty bowl on the sill beside him. She had a round of seven gates, and she was late at every one of them already, and nobody had asked this of her; and she had just spent five days of it on a road that every carter she had ever mended told her to keep off. He had wanted to say the sentence for nine days. He was glad now, with his whole heart, that he had left it for her, since from him it would have been a request, and from her it was a decision.

---

From the loft door, that evening, with his legs hanging over the yard, he watched the healer work.

It was the place where he had written on his first stay at Thornwater, and on his second. Two of the boxes stood behind him among the barley sacks, and the third, with the corner that was worn white, waited by the ladder's head for the morning. Down in the yard the Oxhollow carter was backing his mules to an empty bed. He would load grain at first light and go home by the ferry, sixteen days in all, and he had said goodbye to nobody; but Cael had seen him stand for some time looking at a boot that hung from his cart's tail, and then untie it, and carry it over, and stand it carefully outside the barn door.

The steading's woman had given the healer her back room without being asked; and when Karis inquired whether that was the custom, she had looked at her as at a person asking whether the well had water in it.

"It's hers when she's here," the woman said. "It's nobody's when she's not."

There was a bench under its window, and by dusk there were seven people on it, three of the hold and the rest come in with the east carts, and one of them a child. Oryn came out when the shadow of the fence had crossed the well, and washed to the elbow, and went along them as he had seen her go along eight at Oxhollow; and she chose two.

That was no longer new to him, and he did not count her lips; he watched the yard instead, where nobody stood about. He had seen a healing house's corridor once, from a bed, and it had been full of people waiting to be told their turn by tier. Here the five whom she had passed over got up from the bench without complaint and went back to what they had been doing, and the woman of the hold set a lamp on the back room's sill, unlit, and a bowl beside it.

Oryn glanced at the lamp, and then across the yard.

"You. Ember."

Karis was on the well's coping with her notebook. She looked up.

"I told you a week, on a mountain, and three days on top of it at a ford. That ran out on the eighty-seventh, and this is the ninety-third. Have you asked it for anything since?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I have been on roads, where one does not. And because—" Karis stopped. She did not often stop in the middle of a sentence. "Because I looked for the place, the first night, and it was gone. I had sooner not look again and find it gone a second time."

"That's honest." Oryn jerked her head at the sill. "This is a yard, and I want a light. Come here."

Karis got down, and crossed the yard with her hands held a little away from her sides, as she had carried them when they were bound, and stood before the lamp. Cael saw her draw a breath. Then her right hand made the smallest dip that he had ever seen it make, as a person sets a full stop.

A seed of yellow stood on the wick.

It was no bigger than a grain of barley; it caught, and the flame came up round it, ordinary and smoking, and the seed was gone into it, and Karis let out her breath in something that was not quite a laugh. He had last seen her do that across sixty yards of mountain, with her hands shaking like a rope under load.

"Somebody has sewn the pocket back into the coat," she said.

Oryn had two fingers on her wrist already, and two on her neck, and her lips moved to ten.

"Three parts full. It'll be the whole by the end of the week." She let go. "You spent it to the last copper once. Don't do that again where I'm not."

She turned to the first of her two, and the lamp shone on her hands, and after that there was nothing to see but a woman kneeling.

Cael sat on in the loft door until the cold drove him in. Tomorrow there would be an east gate, and a road with a bend in it; and for the first time since he had crossed the Line, six people would go out by a gate together where there had always been five.

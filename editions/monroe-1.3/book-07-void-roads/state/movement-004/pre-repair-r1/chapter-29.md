# Chapter 29 — A Stride Inside

They held the trials at the stone, at noon, and Karis set the order.

"Smallest first," she said. She had ruled a fresh leaf into five rows and given it to Cael, with the pencil. "A one-armed man has told us that nothing can be declared in there, and five of us felt our Arbiters go, and I believe both. I believed in a ledge as well. There are prints in the grass on the far side of this field, and I am not going to learn that he was mistaken by having Lira crack the sky open a quarter of a mile from whatever left them."

"I'd not crack it on purpose," said Lira.

"You did not crack it on purpose the last time. That is rather my point." Karis looked round at them. "So I go first, with one point, unlit. If it sets, I shall know it, and nobody else will, and we stop there and think. If mine does not, then Brom. Then Seln. You are last of the four, and you go only if the three of us have had nothing."

"And outside the line nobody tries anything," said Seln. "It is a road. I should like that kept."

"Nobody tries anything outside. We know what we are outside."

The sun stood as high as it would go at that time of year, pale and small, and their shadows lay short on the frosted grass. They had gathered on the western side of the grey stone, with the turn of the ruts at their backs and the ring two hundred yards and more in front. Oryn had the mule by the cheek-strap a few yards behind the rest, and her free hand tucked into her armpit for warmth; she had watched the whole of the morning from that stone without a word.

Cael looked at the leaf that he had been given. There were five rows on it, each headed with a letter, and his own was not among them. That was as it should be, since he had been handed the pencil for the very reason that there was nothing of his to enter. He had kept the count at a great many trials of other people, from the east tier of a yard, and had thought himself used to the seat.

Karis stepped over.

Cael saw her shoulders tighten as her foot came down, and ease again; she had done this nine times in the dark, she had told them at first light, and it plainly did not grow pleasanter. She stood a stride inside the line, with her back to the ring, and her eyes went slowly along the slope in front of her from left to right, as he had seen them go over a district floor before a bout.

"I can see them," she said. "Every place. I could put sixteen down on that bank and tell you where each would sit, to the hand. That is not altered in the least."

"That's only knowing how," said Brom.

"It is knowing how, and it is still here; I want it written that it is still here." Her right hand had come up. "Now I am going to set the first, eleven paces off, by that tuft with the frost on it."

The hand dipped, the small exact dip of a person dotting a page.

Nothing happened that he could see, and that proved nothing, for a set point gave no light; so he watched her face instead, and it had the look of somebody who has put a foot down for a stair in the dark and found the floor.

"No," said Karis. She tried it a second time, and a third, quickly. "No. It has not set. A lecturer would say that the declaration did not render. And that is not the whole of it. When I am empty and try to set one, I feel myself reach into the purse and close my fingers on nothing. Here I am not even reaching. I am three parts full, and I can't find the hand to reach with."

"Does it hurt?" said Oryn, from behind the stone.

"Not in the least."

"Does it tire you? Trying."

Karis thought about that with real care.

"It costs nothing. There's nothing to spend."

She stepped back over. He saw the colour come into her face as the foot came down, and her eyes go, for an instant, to something at the edge of her sight that nobody else could see.

"Back," she said. "First row. Write it as I gave it: every place seen; none set; no cost."

He wrote it, and looked at it a breath longer than a line needed. He had sat in a whitewashed room for the better part of a year while that small exact person laid points on a wall for him to study, and he carried one of them still, the fifth of his nine, with its rent paid; and he had not known until this noon that there was a place anywhere where her hand could make that dip and have nothing come of it.

---

Brom went over with his sleeves rolled.

He had done that on purpose, Cael thought, so that all of them should be able to see his forearms: the left with its pale dry seam from the heel of the hand to the middle, and the right, which had never had a mark on it. He stood a stride inside and held them out a little before him, with the palms down.

For forty-three days he had refused that Path, and Cael had watched him do it on a ferry, and in a wash, and under a cart on a crust of rock. He had seen the grey start at those wrists whenever a load came on suddenly, and each time he had seen Brom put it down again, like a man pressing a lid onto a pot; it had been the hardest kind of work, and it had been done every hour.

Now, for the first time since a river, Brom told it to come.

"Skin."

He said it aloud, in his ordinary voice, as he might have called a dog that was lying a yard off.

They waited, and the big brown wrists stayed the colour of wrists. He turned them over to look at the insides, and turned them back.

"Well," said Brom.

He did not seem distressed; he stood there with his arms out, frowning a little, like a person who has walked into his own kitchen and found a chair moved.

"What's it like?" said Lira. "You said a door, yesterday. Closing behind you."

"It's that. The door's shut, and I'm on the wrong side of it from where I keep the thing." He let his arms fall. "It's queer. I've not used it since the ford, and I'd have told you I'd got used to going without. But I always knew where it was. I could feel it in the next room, as you can feel a fire through a wall. There's no next room."

He looked about him, and his eye fell on the middle box, which sat on the grass just outside the line where he had put it down. He leaned across the stone and took it by its two beckets, and lifted it over, and stood holding it against his chest.

"Same," he said, after a moment.

"The same what?" said Karis.

"Weight. I wanted to know whether a hundredweight was a hundredweight in here, with nothing behind me." He set it down inside the line, quite gently. "It is. So that's what I've got. Write that."

"I am to write that a box weighs the same."

"It's the only thing I've tried that worked."

Cael wrote it. He wrote *Skin called aloud; none*, and under it, since the row was Brom's, *lifted a hundredweight; no difference*; and it seemed to him that of the two findings the second was the one that Brom would take home.

Seln had been standing at the end of the row with his hands behind him.

"I shall be brief," he said. "I have very little to show."

He stepped across, and for a breath he only stood, with his eyes half shut. Cael knew what he was reaching for, since he carried a piece of the same thing himself, sealed, and had felt it lift in him at every dusk for two years.

"There is nothing to lift," said Seln. "I looked for it as one feels for a banister. It is not that it resists. It is not there to be asked."

"Then you're only a man in a coat," said Lira. "Like the rest of us."

"I have been only a man in a coat for a great deal of my working life. Will you all look at the mule for a count of three, and then look back?"

It was an odd request, and they did it. The mule was regarding a tussock; Cael counted three, and turned his head, and the place where Seln had stood was empty.

There was the grass, and the short noon shadows, and a stride beyond the stone a patch of bent stems where two boots had been. Lira made a small sound. She had the best eyes of the five, and Cael saw them go the whole length of the slope and come back with nothing.

"Four yards to your right," said a mild voice. "And down."

He was crouched low against a tussock of the pale grass, side on to them, with his face turned down into the crook of his arm and his hands drawn up into his sleeves. He had put himself to the south of them, where the low sun came from behind him into their eyes. There was no Path in it, and no trick that a child could not have understood once it was shown; and it had still taken five people, all of them looking for him, the better part of a breath to find a man in a dark coat on an open hillside at noon.

He stood up and brushed the frost from his knee.

"I've a knife and fifteen years. It was never most of what I do."

"That isn't a trial of anything," said Karis. But she was looking at the bent grass, and at where he had got to, and her pencil hand had twitched. "I suppose that it is a trial of you. Third row. *Path sought; none. Practitioner not found for a count of two.*"

"A count of three," said Seln, coming back over the stone. "I should not like the record to flatter you."

---

Lira went last of the four, and she did not make a joke of it.

She sat down on the grass outside the line and took off the great boot, and unwound the whole of a spare shirt, and stood up on her own taped foot with the frost under it. She was to run on nothing for a fortnight yet, and nobody reminded her of it; this was one stride, and she had plainly weighed it against a healer's month before she touched the lace.

"I want all of it," she said. "Not a lean. I'm going to ask for the whole thing, the way I'd ask on a card with a panel watching, and I'm going to ask along the line, that way, north. So if the old man's wrong I come down in grass and not on top of one of you."

"And if he is wrong," said Karis, very quietly, "it will be heard at Thornwater."

"I know what it'll be. I heard it come back off a mountain." Lira's face was pale. "It went off by itself, that morning. I never told it to. I've got to walk into a place with dogs in it on this foot, and I'd like to know, before I do, whether it can."

There was no arguing with that. Cael had the ruling, and he gave it with a nod, and she stepped over the stone and turned to face north, with the ring on her right hand, and set her feet.

He had watched her do that a thousand times, from a rail at Norhold, and on a gravel bar in the mist, and at the mouth of a ledge on the seventy-eighth morning. He knew the order of it as he knew his own name: the weight going forward onto the balls of both feet, and the heels lifting, and then the gathering in her calves that came a breath before the air broke. For three months he had seen her reach that last place and refuse it.

She did not refuse it now. He saw her ask.

Every sinew in her answered. Her whole body went into it at once, and in his own feet Wind leaned to go with her, as it always did; he put it down.

She took one step.

It was a long one, and very fast, and it carried her a yard and a half along the line; she landed on her left foot and brought the right up beside it, and stood. The grass hissed, and that was all the sound there was. Over the whole wide hillside the air lay as it had lain before, cold and still, and no echo came back from anywhere, since nothing had been sent.

Lira stayed as she was, looking down at her own two feet on the frost, a yard and a half from where they had started.

"That was a step," she said.

"I asked for all of it. I did everything. You saw me; you'd have seen if I'd held any back." She lifted her head. Her eyes were very wide, and for an instant Cael thought that she was going to weep, and then he saw that it was not that at all. "It's in me. It's not in the air."

She turned it over, standing there.

"I'd always thought of it as mine. They told me at Fenmark that it was a thing I *did*, and I believed them, and I was proud of doing it well. And every piece of the doing's still here. I can feel it. I could do that step again exactly." She set her right foot forward on its whole sole, and drew it back. "There's just nothing out there to take hold of."

"Come out," said Oryn. "You've a bare foot on frost."

Lira stepped back over the grey stone, and stopped dead on the near side with her breath caught.

"Oh," she said.

"Back?" said Karis.

"Back. All at once." She had her hand flat against her own breastbone. "I felt it at the stone last night, but I wasn't looking then. I was looking this time."

"What is it like? I want it in your words. I have Brom's door, and my own purse."

Lira frowned, and hunted for it.

"It doesn't come on like a lamp. It's just there, and it's where it was, like a clock that stopped and started again without losing the time."

Karis had Cael write that down as it was spoken, the whole of it, in the fourth row. She read it over his arm when it was done.

"That is the best of the four," she said. "It is the only one with a measurement in it."

Lira wound the shirt on again, with fingers that were not quite steady, and pulled the great boot over it, and stamped.

"I've spent three months not doing it," she said, to the boot. "I thought that was hard. It's nothing to standing there and asking with everything I've got and being given a step." She looked up at Cael. "I know what's left now. When it isn't me doing the *not*."

"What?"

"Legs," said Lira. "And they're good ones. Somebody took trouble with them."

---

"Now me," said Oryn.

She had given the mule's rein to Seln, and she came up to the stone at her own pace, rubbing her hands together, and looked at Brom.

"The wrist. The one I did, that I read at the fire."

He held it out. She took it between both of hers, as she had taken a carter's palm on a plank at Thornwater, with two fingers of her right hand laid along the inside where the pulse was.

"This is how it goes," she said. She was speaking to Karis, since Karis was the one who would have it written. "It's the short reading; it costs me nothing. I'll have it running, and I'll count aloud so that you can hear where I am. We go over together, him and me. Then back. Then I'll want a third, and I'll tell you what when I've had the two."

"You have never done this," said Karis.

"Nobody has that I ever met. That's why I walked two days."

Her lips began to move, and this time there was sound in them. "One. Two. Three." She nodded to Brom, and the two of them stepped over the stone together, the big man and the square woman, hand in wrist. "Four—"

It stopped. It did not trail away; it stopped as a word stops when a door is shut on the speaker. She stood a stride inside the line with Brom's forearm between her palms, and her mouth was a little open, and there was no count in it.

Cael had seen that look once before, on a mountain, over his own ribs.

"Out," said Oryn hoarsely.

They stepped back, and her lips moved at once. "One. Two." She went all the way to ten this time, with her eyes shut, and Cael saw her shoulders come down as the last of the count went by.

"That's there. It's his, and it's sound, and I could tell you what he had for breakfast." She opened her eyes. "And a stride that way I'd my hands on a piece of warm meat."

"Is it Brom?" said Karis. "Forgive me; I must ask it. His Arbiter stopped at that stone, and yours did. If the thing that you read is his current, and his current is shaped by his Path, then perhaps there was nothing in him to read."

"That's the third." Oryn had not let go of the wrist. "You. Stand where you are, this side. Give me the arm across."

Brom stood on the western side of the stone, where he was an Iron Skin with his Path in the next room. He stretched out his left arm. Oryn kept her two hands on it, and she stepped over the stone by herself, so that she stood inside and he outside, with the mended wrist held between them across the line.

Her lips did not move at all.

She stayed so for a count of twenty, and Cael kept it for her, since she could not. She shifted her fingers on the wrist, and pressed, and shifted them again; then she let go of Brom with her left hand, and bent, still holding him with the right, and laid the left on her own shin.

She straightened.

"Nothing. Not nothing in him. Nothing in my hands."

The wind went over the grass. Nobody at the stone had anything to put beside that.

"He's standing out there with everything he owns. I had it not a minute ago. And I can't so much as feel that he's alive, except that he's warm." She looked down at her two broad palms, as she had looked at them in a hollow of shale, and this time there was no puzzlement in her face. "They told me, and I never checked. Thirty rounds."

"Then the carters were right," said Karis.

"A healer's hands stop working there."

She stepped out. The count came back to her lips before her second boot was down, and she let it run to ten on Brom's wrist, with something like greed, and only then released him.

"So you'll all hear this, and you'll hear it once," said Oryn. "I'm coming in with you. I said I'd see him whole to the ninety-ninth, and I can't do that from a stone. But understand what you've got. If one of you puts a leg through something in there, I can pull it straight. I can splint it, and I can carry my end of you. I can't mend it. Not a scratch. Not till you're this side of that." She pointed at the stone with her chin. "Whatever happens in there happens to six people with no healer."

Cael looked at her as she said it. She had walked two days up an empty road to learn that the one thing she owned in the world, besides a route, would be taken out of her hands at a stone; and having learned it she was going in regardless, with her sleeves pushed back, to be of whatever use a pair of ordinary hands could be. He had not asked her to come. He did not think that he would have had the face to ask it, knowing what a stride had just cost her, and he saw that she would have come all the same.

"Fifth row," said Karis, in a small voice. "As she said it."

---

For a quarter of an hour after that, nobody at the stone had an eye to spare for anybody else.

Cael watched it happen. Five people had each lost a thing, a stride from where they stood, and had it handed back, and every one of them had turned inward to look at it. Oryn was hobbling the mule, with her lips moving in a count that she did not seem to know she was keeping. Brom knelt by the middle box. Karis sat with her eyes shut, and Lira walked up and down on the great boot, a little apart, and Seln stood looking east. There were five rows to be ruled fair before the leaf went into a box. He took it a cord along the line to the north, to Brom's first turf, where there was a flat place to kneel; and nobody marked his going.

He had seen that it would be so. He had been waiting for it since first light.

He stood beside a square of black turf and priced the thing, as he priced everything, and he did it honestly, since he was about to do it regardless.

The finding in his book said that the hounds answered what went out of a person; and a fragment answering went out of him, as he had felt it go at a ford, where one burst had drawn three. That was the cost, and he set it down first. Against it there was the hour, at which no hound on any road had ever been seen abroad, and there was the choice of what to ask. The Iron read was the quietest thing he owned. He had refused it on a fan and a lip and a bench, when it would have told him what he was being paid to learn, and each time he had refused because a boy's word could be checked by nobody.

This was a different matter from a map. Six people were about to walk into a place where every one of them believed that all six were alike, and he was the only one who could find out whether that was so.

He opened it.

It came as it had always come, at once and without effort, like an eye that has been kept shut. For a pace and a half on every side he had the ground: the weight of the turf, and the frost standing in it, and a flat stone a foot down that he would never see. He had his own two boots, and the lean of his body over them. It was mass, and the way that mass meant to go, and nothing else.

He was a pace short of the line. He walked.

He had been certain, in the part of himself that kept accounts, of what would happen: a thing taken out of a Path would stop where Paths stopped. He had supposed it since a plank, as the others supposed it, and for the same reason, which was that no other sum came out even; and he had braced for the read to go from him as a count had gone from a healer's lips.

It ran across the stride without a break.

The turf under his left boot was outside the line, and the turf under his right was within it, and he had the weight of both as he had the weight of his own hands. Nothing dimmed. He took another step, and a third, and the ground went on telling him what it was.

He stood still, three strides inside, with his heart going.

Then he did the second thing. He had not been sure, a minute ago, that he would.

He walked in, as a man goes who means to look at the ground that he will presently lead people over, without haste, twenty strides toward the ring. A grey boulder lay there in the grass, as high as his hip, and he walked straight at it. He kept his face to the east; if anyone at the stone had glanced his way, there was only a person with a notebook going to see what lay beyond a rock.

At the twentieth stride he let Wind have his feet.

It was a long way short of a burst. It was the smallest piece of what Lira's fighting had taught him, the lean that takes a body off a line a hair before the blow arrives; and he asked it for a foot. His weight went over without his deciding it. The boulder, which had been in front of him, was on his right hand, and he was past it, and the grass had hardly stirred.

Anybody watching from behind would have seen a person walk round a rock, and no more.

He stood on the far side with the tape drawn tight across his ribs and something going out of him into the cold air, small, as a breath goes out. He listened, and there was the wind, and the hiss of seed-heads, and far off toward the east a bird that got up and settled again. Nothing whatever came through the grass.

He shut them both. He put Wind down, and closed the read as one closes a book, and they went back to where the other seven lay.

He did not know why. He set that beside a healer's word from a hollow of shale, and added nothing to it, as she had added nothing. Five people had lost what they had at that stone, each in a different way, and one of them had asked with her whole body and been given a step. And twenty strides inside it, the things that he carried had answered him as they answered him anywhere.

He walked back along the line, and stepped out over the first turf, and came down to the stone with the leaf in his hand.

Karis took it from him, and read down the five rows, nodding at each. Then she laid it on her knee, and took the pencil, and ruled a sixth.

"And yours?"

She said it to the leaf. It was a clerk's question; she had a table with a row empty.

He had perhaps a breath. He saw Lira, stamping her foot down into a boot the size of a bucket, with her face still stripped from that one step, and he saw a healer flexing her fingers; and he saw that the healer was the plainest reason of all, since she knew nothing of what he was. He could not have said, at that stone, in front of her, that the things he carried had answered him in there, without telling her what he carried; and that was not his alone to tell.

It was a real reason. He distrusted it for coming so readily to hand.

"It was dark before," said Cael. "It's dark now."

Both halves were true. He had looked at that place going over and coming back, and it had been the same dark. Karis wrote. He watched her pencil put the two halves down, one after the other, and he knew what she would take them to mean, and what the other four would take them to mean when they read her leaf; and that the whole was not the truth, though there was not a false word in it.

It was the first thing of that kind that he had kept from any of them in a long while, and he had done it at a stone, at noon, with a pencil going.

"The sun's over," said Lira. She was looking up, with her hand shading her eyes. "If we're going, it's now."

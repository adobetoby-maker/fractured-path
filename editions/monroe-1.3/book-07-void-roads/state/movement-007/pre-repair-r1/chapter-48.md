# Chapter 48 — Two Stakes

"Well?" said Oryn.

She had called the halt herself, an hour south of the fork, where a grey rock as big as a cart stood up out of the ridge and broke the wind. The two mules were nose to tail in its lee. Brom had the bread out. She had sat down on a ledge of the rock with her coat pulled round her, and had looked at Cael across the trodden grass for as long as it takes to count ten before she spoke.

He knew what she meant, though he had been turned away at a bench three hours before, in six words, and had gone and sat down, and it had not once crossed his mind since that the matter might be opened again by her.

It was hers to open; he saw that, and he saw also that if he was to have it, he must put the whole question over again from the beginning, as though the first had never been answered, since she would not have him take a thing that he had not asked for.

"May I read you?"

"Yes. That's the answer, and I'm the one giving it, and I want you to notice that it's the same day as the lean-to and a different question, and that the rule held both times. Put your hand on it. Surface first. Count of ten. Don't go in past the count until I say."

Lira had stopped chewing, and Karis, who had her notebook half out of her coat, pushed it back in.

"I'd three mendings just gone out of me at that bench, and eleven people looking on," said Oryn. "You'd have put your fingers on a tired woman who was being stared at, and gone off thinking that was a Tide. I told you what a first reading is. It's the reader's morning. I'd not have yours spoiled by mine."

"And now?"

"Now I've eaten, and walked an hour, and there's nobody here who hasn't seen me asleep." She pushed back her left cuff. "And there's a reason for it, which there has to be. You said it yourself, and you said it well enough. You've read one thing in your life, and it's got no banks, and you don't know what one is. You can't tell what's missing from a thing until you've had your hands on one that's whole." Her eyes were level. "That's what it's for. It's not to learn whether your fingers work. I'd not sit here for that."

"I know that you wouldn't."

"Then come here."

He went to her. She held out her wrist, inside upward, as he had seen a hundred people hold theirs out to her; it was broad and brown, with a white scar across the heel of the hand that he had never been near enough to notice. He put his right hand on her wrist.

It was strange beyond telling to stand so. She had stopped a stride and a half from every person that he had ever seen her meet, until her hands were wanted; and he had been read by her six times and had never once touched her.

"Breathe out," said Oryn. "You're holding it. Start when you've none left."

He let it go, and under his breastbone the tenth fragment, the lightest thing that he carried, turned toward his fingers, as a person turns her head at her own name.

*One.*

It went out through his right hand and into her wrist as it had gone out of his palm in a loft, blindly, like a stone dropped down a well. In himself it had come home to his own two fingers after the whole length of him. Here it came to his left, under her jaw, almost before he had counted.

*Two. Three.*

There was something there.

He nearly took his hands away. In himself, at ten, he had had only his own gait, quick and slow, and nothing whatever to say what it went along in. This went along *in* something. He felt the sides of it first, close on either hand, and high, and as plain as the banks of a mill-race where the stone has been laid by somebody who knew his work; he could have walked along the top of them.

*Four. Five. Six.*

And he knew it. That was the thing that he had not been ready for. Nobody had ever told him how a healer learned a Path from a count of ten, and he had supposed that it was a sort of reckoning, with rules, which took years. It was no more a reckoning than knowing a friend's step on a stair. Those banks had a shape, and the shape was hers, and it was a kind; and the name of the Path was in his mouth before he had asked for it.

*Seven. Eight. Nine.*

"Tide," he said, at ten, and took both hands away.

---

"Well, I'd hope so," said Oryn. "I've been one for eleven years."

She was rubbing her wrist with her other thumb, where his fingers had been, and she was watching his face with an attention that he had seen her give only to a thing on her plank.

"It was *there*," said Cael. He heard his own voice, and it was not steady. "At three. I'd counted to ten on myself and had my own feet and nothing else. I thought that was what ten gave you."

"It's what ten gives you on a thing with no sides to it. I had it five times off you, and I'd never had it off anybody." She nodded slowly. "So now you've felt both. That's worth an hour of my telling."

"What did it cost you?"

"Nothing that I'd notice. A count of ten never does." She considered him. "You've gone the colour you went on my table. Sit down."

He sat down on the turf at her feet, since his knees had some views of their own on the matter, and his right hand felt as though he had been holding it in a stream.

She was silent for a little while, and behind him the grey mule was trying to eat Brom's sleeve, and being told in a low reasonable voice that it was not hay.

"You'll have a minute of the long one," she said. "No more. I'll count it, and I'll say when, and you'll come out on my word and not on your own."

"You said that it wasn't run on a road."

"I said that I don't run it on a road, since it costs me a mending and I've none to throw about. It's you that's running this, and I'm only sitting under it." She was turning her collar down. "And a minute's no reading. It's a look through a door. But the short one shows you that there's a bed, and it never yet showed anybody what was running in it. I'd not have you go back to yourself knowing the half."

She turned round on the ledge, so that her back was to him.

"Your left hand on the back of my neck, under the collar. The right stays on the wrist." She held it out behind her. "It goes along from the one and comes home to the other. You know how it goes. You've had it done."

He got up on his knees. He laid his left palm on the back of her neck, under the cropped hair, where her own left hand had once lain under his for twenty minutes, and took her wrist again; and he felt her draw one long breath and let the whole of it go, as she had taught him to.

"Still and whole and willing," said Oryn, to the rock in front of her. "That's the three. Go on."

He let it go out, and went with it.

It was the banks again at first, close and high, and he went along between them at his own uneven pace. Then he was past the place where a count of ten would have stopped him, and for the first time he was *in* a person who was not himself.

There was one channel.

He had not known that he expected more until he found the one. In himself there were ten things going ten ways, each running into the next like water into water; he had lain in a loft and failed to find where any of them ended. Here there was a single bed and a single thing in it, which was deep, and did not wander.

And it ran.

That was the difference, and it took him some of his minute to find the word for it. His own went along, quick or dragging, as he was glad or tired, and it had never seemed to him to be going anywhere in particular. Hers was going somewhere: it ran as a river runs in its last mile, when it can smell the sea. Wherever his attention rested on it, he found it already leaning that way, toward his left hand, and then toward his right; it came to meet whatever touched it. It did not pool in any place, and there was no slack water in her at all. The whole of that woman, from the neck to the heels, was on its way *to* something, and had been for so long that the banks had worn to fit.

"Forty," said Oryn's voice, a long way off. "You're helping. Don't."

He eased his neck. He had seen a man's shin close under those hands in an hour, and had watched her walk to a mule and unload it, for five days that she could not spare; and he had taken her for a person who gave things up. She gave nothing up. She was made as a road is made, to get to where the hurt was, and everything in her that was not road had been spent on it years ago.

"Sixty. Out."

He took his hands away, and the going-along came home through his right hand and lay down, and he sat back on his heels.

She turned round on the ledge and put her collar up; she asked him nothing, and he understood that she did not mean to. She had sat in front of a great many people who had just been told what was in them, and she knew what it was to have the telling pulled out before it was ready.

It was Cael who spoke first after all, and it was not what he had meant to say.

"You're built to arrive."

It was only the truest thing that he had brought back.

"Yes," she said. "That's what I am."

She said it without any pride, and without the least surprise, as she might have agreed that her coat was brown. Then she got up off the rock and went to take the grey mule's rope out of Brom's hand.

Cael stayed where he was a little longer, with his right hand lying on his knee like something that he had borrowed, and he was as hungry as he had been after a day under barley.

"He asked twice and got one," said Lira, to nobody in particular.

"That's the rate," said Brom, and gave him the rest of the loaf.

---

They came to the end of the ridge with the sun a hand above the western hills, and the ground opened under them.

Cael stopped where the road tipped over, and counted what there was, as he counted anything that he would have to walk into.

The ridge did not run down into a valley. It broke off, as a table breaks off at its edge, and its western face went down in one long slope of pale grass to a low place that lay below them like the print of an enormous thumb. It was a bowl, though so shallow that a cart might have been driven anywhere in it. He put it at five hundred yards from side to side, which was far wider than the swell that the Fallow Ring had stood on, and it had not half that swell's height.

The carters' ruts did what he had seen them do once before. Halfway down the slope the ground eased into a kind of brow, and at the brow the live ruts swung away to the north and went round the whole of that low place, keeping their distance from it, and came back to their line on the far side. He could see them all the way, two pale threads on the grass, and inside them an older road went straight on down, grown over, with a hundred years of turf in it.

At the bottom lay the floor.

It was the same grey, and he knew that grey now as he knew the colour of his own hands. It lay in the lowest part of the bowl, round, and a good deal bigger than the other had been, and the low sun slid over it without finding anything to catch on. There was no ring of stones, and not an upright thing on it of any kind; it was as bare as a threshing floor.

"There's a hole in it," said Lira.

She had the best eyes among them, and he followed her finger, and found it: a small square of dark, exactly in the middle, cut so true that it looked like a thing drawn with a rule.

"And steps. On this side of it, going down away from us. I can see three, with the light on them." She shaded her eyes with her one hand. "Then it's black."

"A stair," said Brom.

"It's what he calls the road," said Karis. Her voice was very small. "It is on no map that I own, and every carter in that meadow has known for a hundred years what is at the end of it."

Nobody moved, and the wind had dropped; in all that low ground there was not a bird, or a beast, or any smoke; there was a bare grey floor with a square cut in it, and three steps that somebody had made for feet.

Seln went down first, as he went down everything: he walked the old road to the brow and stood there; then he went along the live ruts to the north until he was small, and came back, and went a little way south. When he returned to them his face told nothing.

"There are runs in the grass, on the north side. They are old. Nothing has used them since the frosts began." He glanced at the healer. "I should have looked for a den, and I find none from outside. I do not know why, and I shall not guess. On the ruts there is no mark newer than a salt cart's, a week old."

"So nobody's here," said Brom.

"Nobody has been on the ruts. I have said nothing about anywhere else."

They brought the mules down to the brow. Karis gave Brom her box-rope to hold, and went on by herself down the old road toward the floor, with her arms a little out from her sides, counting under her breath. Cael walked a stride behind her and to one side, and he had no need to count, since he was watching the back of her neck.

At forty paces she stopped as if she had come to a wall. Her sigil had gone out at that stride, as it had gone out on the Fallow.

She stood for one breath. Then she stepped backward, a single stride, and let her shoulders down; and she bent, and picked a stone out of the old rut, grey and as big as a loaf, and set it on the turf at her feet.

"Here," she said. "The edge of the Quieting. It is the same as the other; there is no warning in it at all."

One by one they came down and tried it, since no one of them would take another's word for such a thing: Lira, and Brom, and Seln, each a stride past the stone and a stride back. The healer went over last, with two fingers on her own wrist and her lips moving, and came out again and nodded. Cael walked across beside Brom, and felt what he had felt on the Fallow, which was grass under his boots.

Karis was looking down the old road to the floor, and then at the square in the middle of it, with her head on one side.

"Two hundred. I'll pace it in the morning."

---

It was Lira who found the iron.

She had gone a few steps along the line to the north, with her eyes on the turf, as she went along any new ground; and she stopped with the toe of the great boot against something that stood a hand's length clear of the grass.

"There's your bar," she said. "And it's got a number on."

It was the same pattern as the one that Cael had knelt by on the ninety-sixth day, a cord north of a grey stone: a square bar as thick as two fingers, with its head beaten down by a mallet. This one was scaled all over with rust, in leaves, so that it looked more like a thing grown than a thing made. A thumb's breadth under the head there was the registry's stamp, and under the stamp, punched deep, were two figures.

"Thirty-two," said Lira. She did not crow; she said it as she would have read out the height of a wall. "I said that there'd be one."

"You said *like as not*," said Karis. "I shall enter that you said it, and the day."

"Enter that I was right."

"I shall enter the figure. It will do the same work."

Seln had gone down on one knee, and he did not touch the bar; he looked at the scale on it, and at the way the turf had closed about its foot, and then he turned his head, slowly, and looked across the old road.

"There is another."

It stood on the south side of the grown-over ruts, eight feet from the first, with Karis's stone between the two. It was the same bar, but the rust on it was thin and bright, a skin and not a bark, and the grass about its foot had not yet healed.

Seln went to it on his knees.

"That first has stood five cold terms. I said so of its fellow on the Fallow, and I see no cause to alter it." He bent closer. "This has had one. Two, at the most. And it is the same number. Thirty-two."

"Two bars and one number," said Lira. "So it's the place that's thirty-two. It's not the bar."

"That is an inference," said Karis, "and it is a fair one, and I am still not writing down what the number counts."

"There is something beneath it," said Seln.

He had not raised his voice, but Cael came and knelt beside him. Under the two punched figures, on the newer iron only, there was a mark that the old bar did not have. It was small and deep, and made with two blows of a different punch: a short upright stroke, and under it a bar laid across.

It was not a letter that he knew, and it was not a figure; and when he looked at Seln, Seln was looking at it as he looked at any paper, through and not at.

"Newer. This one's been re-surveyed."

"What's the mark?" said Brom.

"I do not know. I carried sheets, and I never saw it on a sheet." Seln got up, and brushed the frost from his knee. "I can tell you what a second bar is. Somebody came back to a place that was already measured, with the first iron standing in plain sight, and measured it again, and wished it known that he had. I cannot tell you why he came. I shall not put a meaning on two strokes of a punch."

Karis drew the mark in her notebook, at its own size, with the number over it, and wrote nothing beside it.

Cael stayed on his knees a breath longer than the others.

There were two bars of iron, eight feet apart, with a road going between them. Anchor had been asking at every sill and gate and rail for the better part of a year, and it asked now, in its thin held note: here was a doorway, and here were two posts to it, and would he not like something set across? He told it no, as he had told it at a stake on the Fallow. He left unexamined whatever might have been set there already, since he had no cause to examine it; and that refusal he was to go over more than once in the day that followed.

They made their camp on the outside of the stone, between the two irons, with the brow forty paces above them and the mules hobbled a little up the slope. Brom laid out six bowls, and he did it without looking at anybody, and the healer ate out of the last of them standing up.

She came by Cael afterward, at her stride and a half, with the grey mule's rope over her shoulder.

"You've your hand on your wrist."

He had. He took it off.

"Not tonight. I can see what you're thinking; you've had a bank under your fingers and you'd like to go and look for your own while you remember it." She shook her head. "You'd not be reading yourself. You'd be reading tomorrow, and calling it you. I said a night that's only a night. This isn't one."

"No."

"Then sleep, if that quiet one lets you."

---

The quiet one did not let any of them sleep until he had said his order, and he said it at the fire, when it was full dark, with the six of them close about it.

"I have walked the ruts," said Seln. "All the way round, at dusk. It is sixteen hundred paces and a little more. I found no person and no sign of one, and I shall walk it again before light. That is a fact about the ruts." He folded his hands. "If I had laid this, I should not be on the ruts tonight either. I should be a quarter of a mile back from them, on my face, in grass of this colour, and I should come up to them when you were over that stone and not before. So I shall tell you what I can see, which is nothing. And I shall tell you what I should assume, which is that they are here."

"All of that, and we go," said Brom.

"We ruled it at a fire. I am saying how."

He gave it to them a piece at a time, as he had once given five people the order of a watch.

"We go at first light. We go in file, ten yards apart, down the old road: myself, and then Lira, and Karis, and Cael, with Brom last. The bow is spanned before we cross, with a bolt in the groove, and Brom carries it. Each of us has a day's water and a day's bread on his back. I have known a morning's errand to take a day." His eyes went to the boxes. "Nothing else goes over. The three boxes stay at this stone with both beasts."

"My paper is in the middle one," said Karis.

"It will be there at noon. If the floor is as empty as it looks, you will come up for it yourself, and I shall carry it down." He turned to the healer. "You said that somebody should stand outside the line."

"I'll stand at that stone with my sleeves up. I said it, and I meant it." Oryn was sitting on her heels. "I've learned what my hands are worth on the far side of one. If a thing goes wrong in there, you bring it to me. It's two hundred yards. I've seen an arm carried as far."

"Then this stone is the objective, as the other was. Whatever is seen, whatever is heard, the word is *Back*, and every one of us comes to it by the straightest way." Seln looked at each of them in turn. "It is a good plan for a place with nobody in it. I do not know of a good plan for the other."

He looked at Cael last.

"I said a thing at the fire in the meadow, and I told you that I should say it again here. If there are hands, in the dark, at arm's length, you do as you think fit. If there are eyes, you are a boy with a knife. Nothing that a pair of eyes could carry to a boat."

"I heard it."

"I am saying it to five people now, so that when you stand still with all of us looking at you, four of them will know why."

Karis had got up while he was speaking. She went to the middle box and undid its cord, and lifted the lid a hand's breadth, and felt down the inside of it without a light. What she drew out was a single leaf. Cael knew it by its ruling, even by the fire: six rows, and a numeral struck through at the head of the sixth, and three lines of small exact writing against it.

She folded it once and put it inside her coat, against her shirt, and tied the box again.

"There is nothing else in there that says a word about him," she said, to nobody. "That one goes where I go."

He wrote the night's log with his back against the old iron.

*Hundred and thirteenth day, night. At the edge of the second one. A stone, and two bars, both numbered thirty-two; one five cold terms old, one a cold term or two, with a mark under its number that Seln won't read. A floor with no stones on it, and a hole, and a stair. Nobody on the ruts.*

*Tide: once, on the healer, on the ridge road. I asked twice today and she granted the second. The short count and then a minute of the long, at one sitting; I am counting that as one, since it was one asking and one yes. At three there were banks. I knew the Path by ten. In the minute, one channel and one thing in it, going somewhere. It cost her nothing that she would notice. It cost me the use of that hand for an hour, and most of a loaf.*

*She says not tonight for the other, and she is right.*

*Deployment since the Line: fourteen.*

He slept a little toward morning. When Brom woke him the stars were going out, and the frost lay so thick on the old road that it showed like a chalk line all the way down to the floor. Seln was coming back along the ruts from the north, and he shook his head at them from thirty yards away.

They ate cold. Brom put his boot into the stirrup of a healer's bow, and set the hook on the string, and straightened his back; the string came up the stock and dropped behind its catch, and he laid one of the five bolts in the groove. Karis had her lamp and her notebook and a flask, and Lira drew the knot of her sling tight with her teeth.

Then Seln stepped over the stone.

# Chapter 49 — Forty Steps

A hawk over that place at first light on the hundred and fourteenth day would have seen three rings, one inside another, and a line drawn across them.

The outermost was the carters' road, two pale ruts going the whole way round the low ground. Forty paces inside it lay the edge that could not be seen, where a grey stone sat on the turf between two iron bars. And in the middle, two hundred and twenty yards from the stone, was the floor, as round as a coin, with a square hole at its centre no bigger than a cart. The line across all three was the old road. It came down off the ridge from the east, over the brow where the ruts turned aside, between the two bars, and straight on down the frosted grass to the floor's edge.

Six people stood at the stone. The sun was coming up behind them over the ridge, and their shadows went before them down the old road.

Nothing had changed in that low ground since they looked down on it at dusk. Seln had come in along the ruts not a quarter of an hour before, shaking his head; the frost lay unbroken on the old road as far as the floor, and the only marks that it would carry by noon were the ones that their own boots were about to make.

Seln was already over. He walked as he walked every approach, without haste, with his hands a little away from his sides and the strap of a locked case across his chest. When he had gone ten yards he lifted two fingers, and Lira stepped over the stone after him, on the whole sole of the great boot.

"Gone," said Karis, ten yards behind Lira.

She said it quite quietly, at the stride. She had crossed such an edge more often than any of them, and she had told Cael at the fire that she did not expect ever to grow used to it; but she did not break her pace.

He went fourth. He felt the turf under his boots and the cold on his face, and nothing else whatever; the place behind his breastbone was as dark on the far side of that stone as on the near. The ten things that he carried lay as they had lain. He had made up his mind in the night that he would ask none of them for anything this morning unless the roof fell in, and he walked down the old road ten yards behind a small straight back with his hands empty.

Brom came last. Cael heard him grunt at the stride, as he had grunted on the Fallow, like a man who has felt a door shut behind him. The healer's bow was on his left shoulder, spanned, with a bolt lying in its groove.

The healer herself had not moved; he glanced round once and saw her, square and brown against the sunrise, with her sleeves pushed up to the elbow and two mules and three boxes on the slope behind her.

He was facing the floor again when he heard the sound.

It was not loud: a short dull knock, like a mallet on a wet post, and after it a hiss of breath between somebody's teeth.

He turned.

Brom was five yards inside the stone, where he had taken one step that he had not meant to take, and he was standing very upright, with his head a little on one side, as he stood when Karis told him something that he could not at once believe. A hand's length of ash wood stood out behind his right shoulder, with three grey feathers on it.

"Ridge," he said.

Cael looked up the old road, into the sun, and had to put his hand over his eyes. On the brow, where the ruts turned north, there was a shape standing that had not been there when Seln walked the ruts in the dark. It stood forty paces above the stone. There was a second shape beside it.

Forty paces to the stone, and five yards beyond: that made forty-five. He knew that distance, since he had walked a mile once in front of a cart with a grey thing keeping it behind him in the grass, patiently, waiting upon a signal.

"Back," said Seln's voice, from far down the road.

It was the word, and all four of them came round to it as they had agreed. But the healer had not waited for a word. She was over the stone already, running, with her coat flying out behind her; she had seen a man hit five yards from where her hands worked, and she had come to fetch him out.

---

Cael ran too, and he was thinking as he ran.

Something was wrong with that shot, and though he had only fifteen yards to cover, it took him the whole of them to say what. A bow on that brow could have let all five of them walk down to the floor, and had them two hundred yards inside, with nothing over them and nowhere to go. It had chosen to shoot the last man instead, when he was five strides from the way out, with a healer standing in plain sight at the door.

A shot of that kind did not want them to go on. It wanted them to turn round, or it wanted somebody to come in.

He was twelve feet from the stone when he made up his mind. He had refused Anchor, the thinnest of his ten, at these two irons the evening before, and again in the frost an hour ago. He let it look now, on purpose, with his eyes open; and since he had stopped dead to do it, he stood on a frozen road for the length of two counts while a friend with a bolt in him was being turned about by the arm.

At twelve feet there was a greyness in the air between the bars, like breath. He took one stride more, and it was there.

Three lines were going across the old road.

They came from the old iron, on the north side, and they went to the newer one, eight feet away, at the height of a knee and a chest and a hand above a tall man's head. He saw them arrive, and they did not creep; they swung across as a gate swings to, all three together, and caught on the newer bar, and ran on past it to the south until they were beyond his seeing. On the north side they ran away from the old iron along the turf in the same three courses, and went out of his sight in that direction too.

He had seen such things once before, in a chalk cut on the Ostrand road, and he knew the craft as he knew his own handwriting. A point was set against a surface and told to stay, and a line was drawn from it to the next.

But these were not hers.

The woman in the cut had laid nineteen points round him while he fought her, each as fine as a fishing line, from fifteen feet, with the stillness of a person threading a needle. Whoever had laid these was forty paces off. And they were thick: every one was as thick as his wrist, and there were three where she would have used thirty. It was the same trade in a different hand, as a ship's cable and a stitch are the same trade: this hand threw.

"Stop," he said. "It's shut."

The healer did not so much as turn her head. She had Brom's left arm across her shoulders and she was bringing him up the road toward the stone at a stumbling run, with her eyes on the grey wool of his coat where a dark stain was beginning. She had three strides to go, and Cael saw her thinking of nothing in the world but those three strides, at the end of which she would be a Tide again.

"Oryn. *Stop.*"

She walked into it.

Her right shoulder met the middle course exactly as a shoulder meets a door that somebody has shut in the dark. She stopped as if she had been struck. Brom's weight came on past her, and his chest found it, and he made a sound that Cael did not wish to hear again; and the two of them stood swaying against nothing at all, a yard short of a grey stone, with the sun in their faces.

She put up her free hand. She laid the palm of it on the air, at the height of her eyes, and pressed.

Then she looked at the hand.

"Four," she said. Her lips had been moving. "I can't get past four."

"There's a fence across it. From that bar to that one, and on round. You can't see it. I can." He had her by the elbow. "It wasn't there when you came over. It went up behind you."

She understood him. He saw her understand all of it at once, as she understood a break under her hands: the bolt, and where it had been put, and for whom. Her face did not change. She shifted Brom's arm higher on her shoulder.

"That was for me, then," said Oryn. "That bolt."

Seln was beside them, having come back up forty yards of road without a sound, and Lira and Karis were behind him.

"They are standing up," he said.

---

Cael turned on his heel, slowly, all the way round, and counted what the morning had on its rim.

There were two on the brow to the east, where the old road came over. One had a crossbow, with its nose on the ground and his boot in the stirrup; he was drawing the string with a hook, steadily, like a man doing a thing that he had done ten thousand times. The other stood two yards south of him and had nothing in his hands at all, and his arms were coming down to his sides.

There were two on the southern rim, a quarter of the way round. They had risen out of the white grass behind the ruts while he was looking at the brow. One of them was kneeling over a bow, and the other only stood, a little apart, with his arms folded.

There was one on the west, a long way off beyond the floor, so small that Cael could tell only that it was upright and that it held something across its body.

And there was one on the north, kneeling.

That made six, and four of them had bows. Every one was the grey of the road in a dry month. They wore hoods, and under each hood there was a cloth drawn up across the face, of the kind that carters wore against dust. Not one of them had called out, or so much as lifted a hand to another. They stood at four stations round five hundred yards of grass, outside an edge that none of them had needed a stone to find, and they looked down.

"Six," said Seln. "Two at the gate, two south, one west, one north. Four bows. I walked past every one of those places twice."

"Can we rush the two?" said Brom. He was grey round the mouth, and his voice was quite steady. "It's forty yards."

"Through that?"

Brom looked at the air in front of his chest.

Nine fragments had come up in Cael at once.

He had not called them. They had risen as they had risen in a lane on the Fallow, each offering what it had. Wind was in his feet, and though it could not take him through three courses of that fence, it could take him a long way along the inside of it, faster than a hooded man could run round the ruts. Storm, the heaviest of them, lay ready; there was a rut at his feet, running straight up the hill to the brow, with seed on the grass at either lip. Ember had fire for his hand, and there was a great deal that a boy could do from where he stood, in a place where four of his friends could do nothing.

And six pairs of eyes were on him, from four sides.

*Nothing that a pair of eyes could carry to a boat.*

He held them. He stood on a frozen road, with a bolt in Brom's shoulder and a healer staring at her own hand, and refused nine things one after another in the only country on the continent where every one of them would have answered. He had done as much once before, on a white floor, with a girl's arm in a hound's mouth. It was no lighter. Seln had told him that it would not be.

Lira was looking at him.

She had seen it on the Fallow from a foot away, and she saw it now from three yards. He watched her know what he was doing, and why, and what it was going to cost somebody before the day was out. Then her eyes left his face, and went up to the brow, where a man had got his string behind its catch and was reaching to his belt for a bolt. They went south, and west, and north, right round, in the time of one breath.

"He's nearly spanned," she said. "And there's not a yard of this grass that one of them can't see."

Her gaze had come down the old road to the floor, and stopped.

"There's one place."

She did not wait to be answered. She stepped in close under Brom's good arm, and with her right hand she lifted the healer's bow off his left shoulder by its stock, spanned as it was, with the bolt held in the groove under her thumb.

"I can't span it," she said to him. "I can carry it."

Then she ran.

She ran *down*. She went away from the stone, and from the two mules, and from the only ground on which a Path would answer, straight down the middle of the old road toward a bare grey floor with a hole in it. She ran on the whole of her foot, slapping, like a child in its father's boots; a healer had told her four days ago that a certain bone was her own again, and she was using it.

"After her," said Seln.

---

They went as a unit, since they had been one for a long time.

Karis was away first, with her coat held up in both fists. Brom ran. A bolt in the shoulder is not a bolt in the leg, and he went down that road like a bullock, with the feathers of the bolt nodding at his shoulder and the healer at his left hand, matching him stride for stride with her fingers hooked in his belt. Seln ran a yard to his right. Cael came last, since somebody must, and since if any of them fell he wished to be the one who saw it.

It was a hundred and ninety yards of grass. He had paced none of it, and he measured it now with his lungs.

Wind lay in his feet the whole way, asking. He ran on his own legs, as he had run before ever he knew that there was anything else to run on; and a boy who could have been at that hole in four breaths went down the hill at the pace of a hurt man, at the tail of his own crew, with his back to a spanned bow.

At sixty the bolt came.

He heard that one. It went over his right shoulder with a sound like cloth tearing, and he saw it strike, far ahead, on the grey of the floor between Karis and Seln. It did not stick. It skipped, as a flat stone skips on a pond, and went skating away across the plate with its feathers spinning.

"Two," said Seln, running. He had not turned his head. "Both from the gate."

Lira was on the floor. She had not slowed, and her boots made no sound on it at all.

Two more came out of the sky.

They were not aimed, or not as the first had been. Cael saw them only when they were falling, two dark strokes dropping steeply from the north and from the south. They had been lofted from better than two hundred and fifty yards by people who knew to a hair how far their bows would throw. One struck the plate ten feet from Brom and leapt up again, end over end, and the other came down beyond the hole and slid.

"Three. Four."

He was on the floor himself then. It was as cold through his boots as the other had been, and as silent, and thirty yards of it had never seemed so far. The hole was in front of him. It was two strides square, with an edge as sharp as a box's; and on its near side a step went down, and another, and a third, each of them white with frost and sunlight.

Lira's head was already below the floor. Karis went in after her. Brom took the first step sideways, with the healer under his arm, and the dark had them.

Cael looked back from the lip, since he was the last, and since it was his to know.

The six had not moved from their stations. On the brow a man was setting his boot in a stirrup for the third time. Nobody was running down the slope, and nobody was shouting. They had watched six people go into a hole in the ground, and it seemed that this was a thing that they had expected to see.

He went down.

Blade, in his right arm, which had come to him from a friend's sword, asked its one word at the first step, where the stone turned a sharp corner under his heel. *Edge?* He told it no, and it asked at the second. He counted the steps by its asking, since he could count them no other way. Below the third the light stopped as if it had been cut off with a knife, and he was going down into black by the feel of a wall on his left hand, at a run, behind the sound of other people's boots.

*Eighteen. Nineteen.*

Somebody cried out below him.

It was Lira's voice, and it was cut short. There was a scrape, and a thud of something soft against stone, and then her breath, loud and ragged in the dark.

"Keep on," she said, between her teeth. "I've got it. I've got the bow. Keep *on*."

*Twenty.* The air changed at that step. It had been the frost's air above; here it was still, and dry, and a good deal colder, as it is in a cellar that has been shut for a long time. He went down through it with the edges asking under his feet.

At the fortieth there was no forty-first. His boot came down on the level, and he walked into Seln's back.

---

"Stand where you are," said Seln's voice, close to his ear. "All of you. I am striking a light."

There was a scrape of steel, and a spark, and another. Karis's breath went in and out somewhere to the left. Cael knew what she was doing as well as if he had seen her. She had put out her hand in the dark to set a point on her own wick, as she had done every night of her life since she was fourteen; and there was no hand to reach with.

The tinder caught. Seln held it to the lamp, and the small brass thing took the flame and steadied. Nobody had spoken since the fortieth step, and the only sound in that place was six people getting their breath.

It showed them a room.

The room took their breathing and gave none of it back. It was colder than the frost above, with the cold of a thing that has not been warm in a very long time, and it smelled as the bottom of a dry well smells, of stone and of nothing else.

It was square, and Cael put it at ten strides each way. The floor was the same grey, level and seamless. So was the roof. He looked up, and it took him a breath to understand what he was looking at: the roof of that room was the underside of the floor that they had run across. There was no beam in it and no arch; it lay over them like a lid, a foot above Brom's head.

The walls were cut.

Karis made a sound in her throat. From the floor to the roof, on all four sides, the grey was covered with the fine sunk lines that had been round the feet of eleven stones on the Fallow: straight, and hooked, and crossing. They ran in bands, one above another, each as tall as a man's forearm. There was more of it on one wall of that room than on the whole of the other place.

And in the middle of the floor, where the lamp only just reached, something lay.

It was a dark oblong, the length of a man's arm, and it had a look of leather or tarred cloth about it, as a case has. From one end of it a pale line ran out along the floor toward the wall that they had come in by, and stopped.

"A cord," said Brom thickly.

"Nobody goes near that," said Seln.

He had spoken more sharply than Cael had ever heard him.

"It is what the paper says we were sent for. Six people brought us to this room this morning by a road that they had prepared, and that is lying in the middle of it. It has lain there a good while, by the dust. It will lie there until I have read the floor round it, with more oil than we have." He took the lamp out of Karis's hand and gave it to the healer. "That is for him. Use it."

He went back to the doorway.

It was a plain square opening in the wall, the width of the stair. Seln stood in it with his back to the room and looked up forty steps at a small square of blue.

"They can shoot the bowl," he said.

He was speaking to himself, as a man does who is taking an inventory. He put his hand on the stone of the door's edge, and then on the wall of the stair, and measured something with his eye.

"They can't shoot the stair."

"Why not?" said Lira. She was sitting on the lowest step with the bow across her knees and her left side turned away from the light.

"They are two hundred and sixty yards from that hole, on every side, and very little above it. A bolt that comes in at the top must come in nearly level. It strikes the wall or the upper steps." Seln turned round. "From the rim, nobody can see into this. Nobody can see so much as the fourth step. And anybody who comes to the edge of the hole to look down is a black shape on a bright square, to a person sitting where you are sitting, with that."

Lira looked at the bow.

"I've one in the groove," she said. "There's four more in the sleeve on his belt, and I can't span it."

"I can span it. I trust that I shall not need to." He took the leather sleeve from Brom's belt as he spoke, and laid it on the step beside her. "Go up six. Sit so that you can see the square and it cannot see you. If anything stands in it, do as the man on the gate-walk taught you."

"Shooting's stopping," said Lira.

She got up, with her jaw shut, and climbed six steps on her whole foot, and sat down again with the stock laid along her right knee. That was the thirty-fourth, counting from the top. Cael saw her set her boot on the step below, heel and toe together, and let her breath go out.

---

The healer had Brom on the floor by then, with his back against the wall beside the door.

She had cut his coat away from the shoulder with Seln's knife. Karis held the lamp where she was told. In its light the thing was plain enough, and Cael made himself look at it, since it was his crew's: the bolt had gone in behind, a hand below the top of the shoulder, and its iron head stood out in front, under the collar-bone, by the length of a thumb. There was not much blood. What there was came slowly.

Oryn put her two hands round the shoulder, above the wood and below it, and shut her eyes.

Her lips moved. They stopped. She opened her eyes and took her hands away, and for the length of one breath she looked at them as she had looked at them on the road, in front of nothing.

"No," she said, to the hands. "Well. I knew that."

Then she became somebody else, as Cael had seen her do once before, at the rim of a white floor, with a strip of her own shirt in her teeth. She took the lamp from Karis and held it close, and looked at the wound from in front and from behind, for a long time, without touching it. She laid two fingers on the inside of Brom's left wrist and counted, with her lips, to a good deal more than ten, and put her ear to his back.

"Breathe in. All the way. Does it catch?"

"No."

"Cough."

He coughed, and went greyer.

"Shut your hand. That one. Now open it." She watched the four dark lines go across and come back. "Is it cold? Can you feel me pinch?"

"I can feel you pinch."

She sat back on her heels.

"I can see what's wrong. I can't feel it and I can't touch it. Tape it. Leave the bolt."

"You'll not draw it?" said Seln, from the door.

"It's high. It's missed the lung and it's missed the big vessel, or he'd not be talking. Leave it in, because the bolt's the plug. When my hands work again I'll take it out and close what it opened. Not before."

"Tape," said Lira's voice, from up the stair. A small hard roll came down the steps, one at a time, and lay on the floor in the light. "It's what I've left from the ankle. It's hers anyway."

Oryn had it in her hands before it stopped rolling. She laid the first strip round the shaft in front, close, and the second behind; she did it by lamp and by eye, as a carter's wife would have done it, and it took her a quarter of the time that it would have taken anybody else in that room. When she had done she pulled what was left of his coat across.

"You'll sit there. You'll not lean on it, and you'll not be brave." She wiped her fingers on her breeches. "I said in a loft that somebody ought to stand outside one of these with her sleeves up. I was outside. I'd my sleeves up. And I'm in here with a roll of tape, and that's a clean wound that I could shut in an hour on the far side of a rock." Her voice was low and quite even. "I came south for exactly this. Enter that."

"It's entered," said Cael.

"I know that it is. I wanted to hear myself say it."

She stood up with the lamp, and turned, and held it toward the stair.

"Now you. Come down here where I can see."

"There's nothing," said Lira.

"There's a sleeve on that sling that was grey this morning."

For a breath there was no answer. Then Lira came down the six steps, slowly, and into the light; and the wool across her breast was dark and wet from the elbow to the wrist.

"It was the twentieth step," she said. She was looking at the wall above the healer's head. "The ankle went. I'd the bow in my right hand, so I'd nothing to put out. And then it was out. I never told it to. It hit the wall, and it took me, the whole of me, and I stood up off it."

Not one of them repeated what she had been told about that arm, since they all knew it, and so did she.

The healer undid her own knot and drew the wool down, and two of the eight small marks had opened, the lowest of each row, and were bleeding steadily.

"The rest have held," she said, after a long look. "It's not gone through. It's opened where it was newest." She was already tearing a strip from the tail of Cael's shirt, which he had held out to her. "I can't shut that either. I can bind it, and I can tie it higher, and you can keep it against your chest until there's a sky over us."

She bound it. She tied the sling again, shorter, so that the hand lay almost at the collar-bone.

"Back up your six steps," said Oryn. "And if you put that arm out for anything again, I'll take the other one off you myself."

"Yes."

When Lira's boots had stopped on the stair, the healer looked into the lamp. It had held a finger and a half of oil when Seln lit it. There was a finger now.

She blew it out.

The dark came down on them like a cloth. Far up, at the head of forty steps, there was a small square of sky. Cael stood under a lid of grey stone and went over what the morning had left him. There were six of them below it, of whom two were bleeding. There were six on the rim. There were five bolts in a healer's bow and its sleeve. Of somebody else's there were three lying on the floor above, and a fourth in Brom. It was not yet the middle of the forenoon.

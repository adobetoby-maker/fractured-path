# Chapter 1 — The Valley With No Name

*Second day past the Line. One fire. Deployment since the Line: none.*

He had written that under the inventory an hour ago, and since then he had done nothing at all but keep it true.

The fire had burned down to a red eye, and Cael let it go, for Brom had said at the start of the watch that a man who feeds a fire all night ends by looking at the fire, and Brom was generally right about anything that had weight. So he sat with his back to the embers and his face to the dark, on a shelf of turf that nobody had ever troubled to name, and he counted.

It was an old habit, and he had it from a hill. For a year there had been two men at the foot of a road with a tin cup between them, and a third on a tier with a pencil, and a mild clerk at a copying table who had turned out to be the best of the four; and he had learned to find each of them in the first minute of any morning, as a man feels for his purse. He ran the count now over a country he could not see. He took the ridge behind them first, and the scree, and the black shoulder to the north where a night bird kept its two notes, and then the gulf in front, in sections, from the near ground outward.

Nobody was there.

He counted again, since the first answer had been such a strange one, and the second came out the same. The habit did not know what to do with nothing, and it went round a third time without being asked, like a dog that has been called off and has not understood.

"Zero," said Brom.

He sat at the shelf's lip, ten paces off, with his back to what was left of the light, and he had not turned his head.

"You can't hear a man count."

"I can hear you stop breathing at the end of each one." Brom spoke low, as he had been taught in an orchard. "That's three. It's zero. I've checked."

"How many times?"

"Twice. I'm not you."

Cael let his breath go, and found that Brom had been right about that as well.

He had no Path, and so no tier and no rank, and he said so to himself plainly, because the dark was a good place for plain things. An Arbiter had woken in him at fourteen, on a brass plate in a windowless room, and it had taken eleven seconds over him and gone dark, and it had been dark ever since. What answered him now were nine fragments, which was the log's word for them: pieces of other people's Paths, each one taken inside a fight that somebody else had begun in earnest, and each one written into Hesk's book with a name beside it and a price.

They were not quiet, either, though he had supposed, coming east, that they would lie still on a road where nobody was asking him for anything.

Shadow, the quiet one, had lifted at dusk, as it lifted at every dusk, soft behind all the rest, and he had held it where it was while Seln laid a fire ten feet away. Blade was the newest of the nine and the quickest, and it had asked about the edge of the scree, and for once had been told that it was right. Storm did nothing whatever. It was a Gold's worth of weather, the heaviest fragment he owned, and it sat in him as a full pail sits on a step, so that he was aware of it all day in the way that one is aware of a pail.

None of that was use, and he had used nothing since a ring of churned turf beside a split thorn. But he had begun to see that carrying nine things and not using them was a kind of work, and that it went on all night.

He took Hesk's clock out of its stocking and tilted the face to the embers, and it said a quarter past the hour. It lost, as it always had, and a researcher with her pencil out had ruled two days ago that it was right by definition, being the only clock east of a line. He supposed the watch was over.

Lira woke as she did everything. He had put two fingers on her shoulder, and she was sitting up with her hand shut on his wrist before he had taken them away.

"Anything?"

"Nothing."

"You say it as if it were bad news."

"I counted three times, and there's nobody." He heard how it sounded. "I don't know what to do with the end of the count."

"Do what Brom does, and count us." She was feeling about for her boots. "We're harder. We move."

Karis woke more slowly. She put out one pale hand before her eyes were open, and it found the canvas and went along it to the knot over the middle box, and rested there. Then she sat up and asked what the clock said, and wrote the figure down before she had so much as looked at the sky.

He looked for the fifth, and it took him a moment, which he supposed was to the man's credit.

Seln lay under the pine, on his side, with an empty satchel beneath his head. He had been out-argued at the fire by his own syllabus, and had lain down to prove a table right, and somewhere in the first hour he had actually gone to sleep. Cael had sat across a counter from that face for two years and had never once seen it with nothing on it.

"Leave him," said Lira, very low. "He's the fifth. It's in the table."

"I wasn't going to wake him."

"You were going to look at him until he woke."

That was fair. He lay down where she had been lying, in the warmth she had left on the turf, and he was asleep before she had finished with her second boot.

---

They finished the hill by hand.

There was as much of it under the shelf as Brom had said, and it was no kinder than what lay above. The boxes went down a leg at a time, and the cart followed on its locked wheels with a large man leaning back on a rope; and Cael went ahead with the wedge, his back to the slope, and said where the edge was. He said it forty times that morning, and Blade agreed with every one of them.

At the bottom the scree ran out into grass, quite suddenly, and they stood in the valley.

It had looked like a thread from the ridge. It was a river, forty feet of fast brown water over stones, the colour of weak tea, and so loud after the silence of the hill that Lira had to say everything twice. Trees stood along it, which he had seen from above as a darkness; and they were a darkness still, tall and close, with bark as red as a fox and a smell he could put no name to.

"Pine," said Lira.

"It isn't a pine." Karis had a twig of it and was turning it over. "The needles are flat, and they come in pairs. I don't know what it is, and I shan't write a name for a thing I can't source."

"Red pine."

"It isn't one."

"Red not-pine, then. You'll want to call it something when you're telling Brom to tie the horse to it."

Karis looked at the twig. Then she wrote, and Cael, who was near enough, read it over her arm. *Conifer, red-barked, needles flat and paired; unidentified. L. says "red not-pine." Entered as L.'s.*

"That's my name in a book," said Lira. "For a tree."

"It's your name against an error. I should be less pleased, in your place."

The track was there, on the near bank. It was only two ruts with a ridge of grass between them, as it had been on the upland. But it kept to the river and it ran downstream, and that was more than any of them had been promised. Lira opened her own book on her forearm and wrote as she walked, while Karis kept the printed sheet rolled under her arm and did not once unroll it, since there was nothing on that part of it but a list of what the symbols meant.

Brom walked at the tailboard and looked at the ground.

He had done so all the way from Ostrand, and Cael had taken it for tiredness, and been wrong. Brom was reading, slowly, as he read anything, and a little after noon he gave them what he had.

"Mules," he said. "Small feet, and narrow. A good many, going both ways, and none of them lately." He nodded at the gelding's quarters. "And one horse, which is him. There's not a shoe-print on this road that isn't his."

"Is that bad?" said Lira.

"I don't know what it is. I'm only telling you it's so."

They made eleven miles by Karis's count of the gelding's stops, which she kept in a column, and toward evening they came on a place where people had halted before them. It lay a little back from the water, on a flat, where the grass was worn to earth and there was old dung, small and dry. In the middle of the bare ground somebody had set a ring of stones. Cael was glad of that ring out of all proportion to what it was, for it was the first thing since a station's sill that a person had made on purpose.

Karis paced it from the bank and got forty-one.

"So they keep the rule out here," she said. "Somebody's rule. Forty, from water."

"Or it is only the nearest flat," said Seln, and went on walking the edges of it.

Brom had the gelding out of the shafts. He stood over the ring a while with the hobbles in his hand; then he crouched, and turned one of the stones over with two fingers, and turned it back.

"There's no black on them."

"It's rained," said Lira.

"It's rained on the dung as well, and that's still here." He stood up. "And there's no ash under the grass, for I've had my hand in. Nobody's lit a fire in that. Not once."

"Then what's a ring of stones for?"

Brom thought about it honestly. He had no answer, and he said so, and went to see to the horse.

So they laid their own fire in it. Seln's flint was at the bottom of his coat. Karis put out one finger and lit the kindling with a point no bigger than a pin's head, as she had lit a page for Cael under a cart two nights before. The red wood caught fast and burned with a high clear flame; and its smoke stood up out of the valley, straight, in the still air, to twice the height of the trees, where anything with eyes on any of seven ridges might have looked at it.

It was a good fire. They cooked on it and sat round it late, and nobody had told them anything yet.

---

On the fourth day the river they were following met another, and the road went into the water.

It was a wide place, a hundred feet across and more, where the two streams ran together over shingle, and from the bank it looked no deeper than a boot. A cairn stood on the near side, a neat one, waist high, a good forty yards upstream of where the ruts went in; and there was another on the far bank, as far downstream.

"Straight over," said Lira, who had the gelding's cheek-strap. "It's nothing. It's the upland one again."

"Then why has somebody built two cairns?" said Karis.

"To show where the ford is."

"They aren't opposite each other."

Lira looked, and she had her mouth open to answer when a mule came down through the red trees on the far side, with a rider on it, and walked into the river at the cairn.

The rider did not hail them. She put the mule's nose at the upstream cairn on their own bank and came across on the slant, a long quartering line, and the water was never over the animal's knee. Halfway she passed within ten yards of the place where Lira had meant to cross, and Cael watched the surface there as she went by: it was smooth, and darker than the rest, and it turned slowly in its own length, like something asleep.

"Oh," said Lira.

"How deep?" said Brom.

"I don't know. Over the bed of the cart." She let go of the strap and wiped her hand down her coat. "I'd have put three boxes in that."

The mule came up out of the shallows beside their cairn and stood dripping. The woman on it was fifty or so, brown and spare, with a lip that had been split a long time ago and had healed a little crooked. Her hat had the brim cut away on the right side. A hatchet hung in a loop at her saddle-bow, edge outward, where anybody could see it, and behind her lay two flat leather bags with long flaps.

She looked them over. She took in the horse's feet, and the iron on the wheels, and the shape of the canvas, and gave each about as long as a clerk gives a line he has seen before. Then she lifted her chin at the cairn beside her and at the one across the water, once each, and turned the mule, and rode it forty yards down the bank before she let it drink.

Cael understood the second thing a moment after the first. An animal drinking stirs up the bed, and a ford is a thing people read by its colour; so one watered below the line, and left the crossing clean for whoever came next. Nobody had told her that he needed it explained, and she had not explained it.

"She is going west," said Seln quietly. "I think she will be the only one who is."

Cael had the two letters inside his coat. He had carried them two days in the pocket of Hesk's book, where a Gold's letter had lain for most of a year, and he had not expected to be rid of them before a town. He walked down the bank to her.

"Will you carry two?"

She put out her hand. He gave them to her, and she did not turn them over to read the outsides; she laid them on her palm, one and then the other, and weighed them.

"How far past the turf fire?"

"A long way. One's for Denvash, and the other's Ardenmere."

"Far end, the both." She thought. "A copper each to the fire. What the old man asks to send them down his hill is his and not mine, and they'll want it at the other end."

"That's fair."

"It's the price." She opened the nearer bag and slid them in among the rest and buckled the flap.

He waited. A ford-keeper had asked him for his name not a fortnight ago, and had been a little sorry not to get it, and a man on a rock had wanted to know where she was to write to. So he stood on the shingle with two coppers in his hand, and waited to be asked what the letters were, and who he was, and where five people thought they were going with a shod horse and three roped boxes.

She took the coppers and looked up the valley, over his head.

"You've had fires," she said. "I saw the one last night from the top. And the one before."

"Yes."

"Hm."

"Is that wrong?" said Lira, from the cart.

The woman looked at her without unkindness, as one looks at a child who has asked a guest how much he earns.

"It's your wood," she said.

She gathered the mule's head and rode up through the red trees by the way they had come down, and she did not look round. Nobody out here seemed to, and he had begun to think that it was a kind of manners.

They took the cart over on the slanting line, cairn to cairn, and the water never reached the hub. On the far bank Lira walked the gelding forty yards downstream before she let him put his head down, and she did it without a word to anybody, with her chin up.

Cael sat on the shingle and wrote while the horse drank.

*Fourth day. The letters to Hesk and to Vell went west by a courier on a mule, a copper each, as far as the turf fire. She showed us the ford by riding it. She did not ask what the letters were. She did not ask what we were either. Entered, because I can't remember the last person who didn't.*

He looked at the last line a while. Then he added the other, which was getting to be a habit.

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

---

Below the ford it began to be a road.

Other ruts came into theirs out of side valleys, one after another, like streams joining; and by the fifth day's noon the track was a cart wide and beaten hard, with the grass gone from its middle. The valley had opened. The red trees stood back on the slopes, and there was pale grass in the bottom, shoulder high, that went over in the wind like a cat under a hand.

There were deer in it, of a sort. They were grey and long in the leg, with white rumps, and they stood in the shallows by threes and watched the cart go by with the steady disapproval of magistrates. Brom watched them back.

"They're not afraid of us."

"Should they be?" said Lira.

"Deer are afraid of people. Every deer I ever met was." He walked a few paces. "Either nobody hunts these, or whatever hunts them doesn't look like us."

"What doesn't look like us?"

"I'm not saying anything's so. I'm only saying they stand very easy, for an animal you can eat."

The gelding's shoes rang on the stones. It had not mattered on the upland, where there was turf, and Cael had not heard it in the ruts. But the road was hard now, and wherever rock came through it the iron struck, and the valley gave the sound back. He caught Seln listening to it, with his head a little on one side.

"He can be heard from that ridge," said Seln.

"He's a horse," said Lira. "He's got feet."

"So has a mule, and a mule goes unshod on this ground. I have been looking at the prints for two days, as the large one has, and I do not think that anybody on this road has ever wished to be heard from a ridge." He considered the gelding without ill will. "I do not say he is wrong. I say he is an announcement."

"He hasn't got anything to announce."

"He is announcing us."

Lira opened her mouth, and shut it, and walked on with her hand a little tighter on the strap.

They went on late, since the light held. Cael walked last, behind the tailboard, where the dust was; he had taken that place because his arm was sound again and he thought it his turn, and because from the back he could see all four of them and the road behind as well. He had not stopped looking at the road behind. He had only stopped expecting anything of it.

The sun went off the valley floor. It did so all at once out here, when the western ridge took it, and the grass turned from gold to grey between one breath and the next.

It lifted.

It was Shadow, the one fragment of the nine that he kept sealed. He knew it at once, and he was ready, as he had been ready at every dusk since a door log in a wool exchange. It came up soft behind the other eight and offered him what it always offered, which was to make less of him. He told it no. He had told it no so many times that the word had worn smooth, and he was tired, with eleven miles in his legs, and he said it as a man says good night.

His boots stopped making any sound.

He did not notice for a stride, and then the gravel was not answering him. He put his left foot down on loose stones and they took it in silence, like wool, and his right the same; and he looked down at his own feet walking a hard road with no more noise than a cat makes on a stair, and for one stride more he let it be, out of pure astonishment.

Then he shut it, as he would have shut a lid, with his whole hand. The stones spoke again under his heel, loudly and at once, and he had never been so glad of a small ugly sound.

"Your left boot went first," said Seln.

He was at the off wheel, ten paces ahead, and he had not turned round.

"I've got it."

"You have it now. For four strides you had not, and by the second it was both feet." Seln walked on a little. When he spoke again it was no louder, and the others, at the horse's head, could not have heard. "I should say that I know it. I learned it on a stair, as a boy, by wanting very much not to be heard by the person at the top. It took me a year to do on purpose and three more to stop doing by accident. It always came at dusk, when the eye is worst and the ear must do the work."

"I didn't ask it for anything."

"No. It is not a thing that waits to be asked, at that hour, by a tired man, and I was a tired man for some years." He turned his head a very little, not so far as Cael. "It was not spent. Nobody was on the road to see it, and I have the best reason of anyone living to know what it sounds like. I only remark that I heard a person stop being there, and I should prefer that you did not grow to like it."

"I didn't like it."

"Good. I did, and that was the difficulty."

Seln went forward to the wheel and laid a hand on the tailboard, as though it had needed steadying, which it had not.

Cael wrote it that night by the fire, in pencil, with the book on his knee.

*Fifth day. Deployment since the Line: none. At dusk, on an empty road, Shadow got as far as my boots, seal and all. Four strides, both feet, before I had it shut. I didn't ask for it and I didn't use it for anything, so it isn't a deployment. It's what the thing costs to carry. Call it rent. Entered as rent, and Seln heard it, which is right. He would.*

---

They came up with the carts in the middle of the sixth morning.

There were three, going the same way, at a mule's walk. They were narrow things, high in the wheel, hardly more than half the width of the road, and Cael saw why when the first of them went by a place where the bank had slipped: it passed with a foot to spare, and their own cart, coming after, had to be lifted round.

"Don't pass," said Lira. She had sat on a landing wall for a week learning carters, and she said it as a rule. "You don't go by a cart on a road. You fall in behind, and you wait until somebody speaks to you."

"How long?"

"She never said. I think that's rather the point."

So they fell in, forty yards back. A woman walked beside the last cart, which was stacked with hides under a net, and she looked round once, at the gelding's feet, with a look that Cael had begun to know. Then she faced front again, and that was all the greeting there was.

He watched them for the whole of a day, having nothing else to do with his eyes.

They did not talk. He noticed that first, and it was a good hour before he believed it. An old man led, with two mules, and behind him came a boy of fifteen or so with one, and then the woman; and when the old man wanted the boy he did not call back to him, but lifted a hand, and the boy looked. Once the woman clicked her tongue, twice, and the old man slowed. From the middle of the morning until the sun stood in the west Cael heard no word spoken above the creak of the wheels.

They wore steel, and they wore it outside. The old man had a hatchet through his belt, over his coat, with the edge forward, and the woman carried a long knife on her thigh and had a boar spear slung in rings along the cart's side, where one pull would free it; even the boy had a hedging bill, too big for him. None of it was hidden and none of it was held. It was simply there to be seen, like the courier's hatchet in its loop.

At noon they stopped and ate, and Lira went a few steps up the bank, by habit, for sticks.

"Don't," said Brom.

"We've flour. I was going to—"

"They're not." He jerked his head up the road. "Look at them."

The carters sat on the shafts of their own carts, each with a cloth open on the knee, eating something cold and flat, and none of them had so much as glanced at the wood lying under the trees. Lira came back down the bank with her hands empty.

They halted at dusk, where the road widened by the river. The old man pulled his cart off the crown and the boy pulled in behind him. Then each of them walked to a ring of stones in the trodden ground, and took up one in either hand, and set it behind a wheel.

"Chocks," said Brom, under his breath, like a man who has found his own boot. "It's where they keep the chocks."

It was cold by the water once the sun had gone, and nobody lit anything.

The boy came back along the carts while there was still some light. He had been looking at the gelding over his shoulder all day and wanted a nearer look. He grinned at Lira as boys had been grinning at her since Ardenmere, and stood there with his thumbs in his belt. Then he seemed to feel that five strangers and a horse deserved something of him. He took out his right hand and turned it palm up, with the fingers spread, as Karis spread hers before she set a point.

A hand came down across his wrist.

It was not a blow. The old man had come up behind him without a sound, and he put the boy's arm down as one puts down the lid of a box, and the boy's ears went red.

"Not on the road," said the old man.

He did not raise his voice, and in that quiet he had no need to. The boy went away along the carts with his head down.

The old man stayed where he was. He looked at the iron tyres, and the three shapes under the canvas, and the shod horse, and at each of the five of them in turn, and he was a good while about it. He had pale eyes, and the skin round them was folded very small.

"You're paper, aren't you," he said.

Cael had never heard the word used so, and he knew what it meant before he had finished hearing it. It meant a person from the country of stamps and seals, who had been written down.

"Yes," he said.

"Then you know how to keep quiet. Keep it."

He went back to his mules. He had said nothing further, and even so it came to more than the woman on the mule had given them.

Cael sat down on the cart's shaft. He did what he had done on the east tier of a yard every week for two years, with a notebook on his knee, and it steadied him as it always had: he set out the claim, and what stood under it, and then he ruled.

The claim was that out here nobody on a road declared a Path, at any tier, for any purpose; or lit a fire he could do without, or spoke where a hand would serve; and that everybody showed steel.

Under it he had five things. Three carts had gone a day without a word, and every one of their people wore an edge outside the coat, the boy included. There were rings of stones on the halting-grounds that had never been burned in, because they had never been meant for fire. A courier had counted their smokes from a ridge and told them it was their wood. And a boy had been about to show them something, and had had it taken out of his hand.

He did not know why. That was the weakness in the ruling, and he looked at it squarely, for not one of the five things said what the rule was a rule against. But he knew what a rule looked like when a whole country kept it and nobody troubled to say it aloud.

"Hold," he said.

They were all near enough to hear. Lira had her chin on her fist.

"Hold what?"

"All of it. Whatever each of you has. Nobody declares anything on a road, for any reason, until we know what it brings." He kept his voice where the old man had kept his. "Not a Wind burst. Not Iron Skin. Not an Ember point to light a pipe by."

"I haven't done one thing since the Line," said Lira.

"Karis has lit three fires with her finger."

"Four," said Karis. "There was the one under the cart. I've entered all of them, with the hour." She had gone a little paler. "I shall enter that I didn't know."

"And if a wheel goes down in a river?" said Brom.

"Then you lift it like a large man."

"I am a large man."

"Then it ought to go well."

Brom thought that over, and seemed to find it sound.

"And you?" said Lira, looking at him. "You've nine fragments, and not one of them's a Path. Does it count?"

"I don't know that either. So I hold all nine." It sounded like more, said aloud. "It's the one thing I've had real practice at."

He wrote the day's lines in what light there was. *Sixth day. Deployment since the Line: none. Ruling: hold.* Then he put the book away and ate cold flour-cake with the rest of them, in the dark, with the river going by.

Forty yards up the road, somebody else's mule shifted its feet. Nobody anywhere lit a thing.

# Chapter 2 — Fourteen Slips

They walked behind the three carts for two days more, and in all that time nobody spoke to them.

It was not unfriendly, for Cael had been ignored by experts, in a house on a bluff, and he knew the feel of it; this was something else, and he came to think of it as being let alone. On the eighth evening the woman with the hides walked back along the road at dusk and laid a flat cake on the shaft of their cart, still warm from being carried inside her coat, and walked away again before anybody could thank her. Lira broke it in five, and Brom said it was barley, and nobody found anything to add.

The country came down as they went. The red trees thinned and gave over to a broad pale tree with leaves that showed silver when the wind turned them, which Karis also declined to name, and the river they were following grew slow and wide and took in others. It was warm in the bottom. The dust stood behind the carts until long after they had passed, and there were flies about the gelding's eyes all day.

On the ninth day, a little before noon, the road went up over a last low rise, and the old man at the front lifted his hand without turning round. It was the first sign he had made to them since the halting-ground.

Cael came up to the crest beside the wheel and looked down at a town.

It lay on the far side of a river a hundred yards across, green-brown and unhurried, and he read it from the water upward, as he had been taught to read a floor. There was a landing, with a flat boat at it. Above the landing stood a long low building with a deep eave, and on the rise behind that a taller one with two chimneys; between them a smithy was sending up the first smoke he had seen in three days. Along the river below the buildings ran a meadow, half a mile of it, with a single great ash standing in the middle and carts and tents scattered round the tree like washing.

That was all there was, and he went on looking for the rest of it.

He looked for the wall first. Every town he had ever entered had kept one, even Denvash, which was nothing, and a wall meant a gate, and a gate meant a man in it with a book. He looked for the station next, the low stone house with the windowless building behind, and for a board of hours, and for any roof with a seal burned into its sign. His eye went over the place three times in search of them, like a tongue going back to where a tooth has been.

"There's no wall," he said.

"There is no anything," said Seln. He had come up on the other side of the cart, and he was not looking at the buildings at all. He was looking at what left them: a road that went north along the water, and another that climbed east over a saddle, and a third going south into low hills. "I count four ways out, with ours, and I think there is a fifth that somebody does not much use. I should like to know where each of them goes."

"Ask someone," said Lira.

"I do not think one does, here."

A cable ran over the river, bank to bank, sagging to the water in the middle. The old man had gone down to a post at the road's foot and was pulling on a cord, and after a while the flat boat on the far side began to move, though nobody was rowing it.

It took them the better part of an hour to work out how. The boat was a scow, square at both ends, with a rail, and it hung on the cable by two running blocks; a second rope went from its bow to a round shed on the far bank, open at the sides, and inside that shed something walked in a circle. When it walked, a drum turned and the rope came in. When it stopped, which it did every few turns, a man's voice came across the water, patient and tired, and after a while it went on again.

"That's a mule," said Brom. He had his hand over his eyes. "An old one. He's got a bad off hind, and he's being asked to do it anyway."

"You can't tell that from here."

"I can tell how he's putting it down."

The carts went first, as was plainly their right, two to a crossing. A boy took a copper for each wheel and a copper for each beast, and he looked at the gelding as the courier had looked at it, and charged for it as a mule.

Their own turn came last. The gelding had not cared for the Ost and did not care for this, and he went onto the planks sideways, with the whites of his eyes showing. Lira told him in a hoarse steady voice exactly what she thought of an animal that had forded three rivers on his own feet and now objected to being carried over a fourth. Halfway across the rope went slack. They hung there on the cable, turning a little, with the water sliding by underneath, until the tired voice on the bank had said its say and the drum began to creak again.

---

Nobody told them where to stand in the meadow, so they stood and watched where everyone else stood.

The carts lay along the river side with their shafts down and a stone behind each wheel. The tents were farther in, in no order he could find, of hide and tarred canvas and old sail, and they were not the same tents from one hour to the next. While Brom was getting the gelding out of the shafts a crew of four struck one within thirty yards of him, rolled it, and walked out by the north road with it on a pole between two of them; and before the flattened grass had lifted, a woman with a handcart had pitched on the same place. He did not see anybody ask anybody's leave. He had counted seventeen tents from the ferry, out of habit, and there were twenty when he counted again; and by evening only nine of the first seventeen stood where they had stood at noon.

The old man had gone straight to the ash. He drew his two mules into its shade as a man walks into his own kitchen, and nobody else stood within twenty feet of him, though the shade was the best in the meadow.

"That's his," said Lira. "The tree. Look how they've left it for him."

"Or he is only the first who thought of it, and the least pleasant to move," said Seln.

"It can be both."

It smelled of mules, and tar, and of something frying. A woman went between the tents with a board on her head and flat loaves stacked on the board; a man sat cross-legged in the grass with a harness across his knees, driving an awl through it with the heel of his hand. Three children and a yellow dog were trying to get a goat to go somewhere it had decided against. After nine days of ridges and a river, it seemed to Cael as loud as a city.

There were fires here, and he saw it almost at once, and felt Karis see it beside him, for her pencil came out of the spine of her notebook. There were cook-fires at half the tents, and a woman frying something over one, and smoke lying along the river in a low blue shelf. People were talking, too, in ordinary voices, and somebody laughed aloud. Down by the smithy a man was holding the end of an iron bar across the anvil with his bare forearm, and the arm was grey to the elbow, and nobody round him so much as turned to look.

"So it is the road," said Karis, very low, writing. "It isn't a rule about fire, or about speaking, or about a Path. It's a rule about doing any of them on a road. Here they do all three." She underlined something. "That's narrower than I had it. I don't know that it's any clearer."

"Does it change the ruling?" said Brom.

"No," said Cael. "It tells us where the ruling runs. We hold on a road. I'd hold here as well, until somebody's seen what we're like."

A boy came for the standing-money. He was the same sort of boy as the one at the ferry, brown and quick, with a tally stick in his belt, and he walked round the cart and counted its wheels aloud.

"A copper a night to stand. The grass is free for a mule." He looked at the gelding, and then up at Brom, with some sympathy. "He'll not do on the grass, will he. They don't. Hay's by the net."

"How much is the net?"

The boy told him, and told him as well how much a horse of that size would want of them in a day, which he seemed to know from sad experience of other people's horses. Brom's face did not move at all, but he stood on with his hand on the gelding's neck when there was nothing further to ask.

"And if we want work?" said Lira.

"Then you want the board." The boy jerked his chin up the meadow, toward the long eave. "Pike's plank. Under the low inn. He'll be at his dinner, but the plank don't eat."

Lira was already walking. Cael knew that walk, having followed it down a lane in Ardenmere toward a yard with a groove in it, and into every hall since; it was how she went toward anything that might have her name on it.

"A board," she said, over her shoulder. "They've got a *board*."

He went after her. Behind him Karis had lifted a corner of the canvas and was counting to three, and Brom was doing some arithmetic of his own, with his lips; and when Cael looked for the fifth of them, he could find no dark coat anywhere in the meadow.

---

It was a ship's plank.

Somebody had brought it up from some river long ago, twelve feet of oak as thick as his wrist, tarred black, and set it breast-high on two posts under the eave of the lower inn, where the weather could not get at it and the whole meadow could. Slips were tacked along it from one end to the other. They were on anything that would take a mark: bark, mostly, peeled thin and written on the pale side, and scraps of hide, and one cedar shingle, and a single piece of real paper that had been somebody's waybill and was written across the back. Each hung by one iron tack, and the oak round them was pitted with the holes of older tacks, hundreds of them, in drifts, so that he could see where the slips had clustered thickest in other years. It was a board that had been used hard for a long time by people who did not waste a nail.

Lira went along it fast, with her finger out, and her face fell as she went.

"There's no card," she said. "There's no bouts at all. Look at it. There isn't a single name on the whole thing."

"There are fourteen slips."

"I can count. What I mean is that nobody's *fighting* anybody."

Cael did not answer her, because he had started at the left-hand post and was reading.

He had done this once before. He had been new to a district, and not long past his Kindling, and he had stood in front of a board at a crossroads in Ardenmere for the better part of a morning before he let himself go near the yard it spoke of. A girl had told him afterward that almost nobody did that. Most people either never looked, or walked straight in and were carried out the same day, and he had done a third thing, which was to stand in the road and try to understand the board first. It had served him then, and he did it again now, slowly, a slip at a time.

*Six crossings, the landing's goods; a crew to stand; two marks.* There was a seal on that one, pressed in dark wax.

*Fence, the steading on the north water; posts cut and set; four marks and flour.*

*The well on the south road; four.*

*Shingles, the upper inn's lee side; one mark and board while it lasts.*

*Fleece down from the high fold, eleven bales; a mark the bale, delivered dry.*

*Strayed, one mule, grey, off the cold road; half its worth to who brings it.*

There were eight more. One wanted a night-watch kept on the landing, and one wanted stones picked out of a field, and one was a message to be walked to a hold four days off, for a mark and feed. Most were in the same square black hand, as if one person had written them out for others; but beside the words on each somebody had drawn a little picture, a wheel or a fence or a boat with sticks standing in it, for the use of people who could make nothing of the words.

"Eleven bales against one crew," said Lira, who had come back along the plank and was reading the fleece over his shoulder. "I'd back the bales. Is that all there is? Carrying wool down a hill?"

"And a strayed mule."

"I saw the mule. I'm not fighting a mule."

"Nobody's asked you to fight it. They want it brought home."

"That's worse," said Lira.

He read all fourteen from the left post to the right, and then he went back and read them the other way, because he had found what was wrong with them and wished to be sure.

Every name on the board in Ardenmere had carried letters after it, and a number: *Cu 3*, and the like. They had been ratings, borrowed from the registry's tiers and kept in somebody's private ledger, and a stranger could not have stood in that yard for a minute without being told where he stood among them. He had learned to read a card by them before he learned anything else. Here there was a job, and whoever wanted it done, and what they would pay.

Not one of the fourteen said who might take it. None of them asked for a tier, or a rank, or a Path of any kind, and none said *Iron or above*, or *a Bronze to lead*. The slip with the seal on it wanted a crew to stand on a boat, and it was all one to that slip whether the crew were five Golds or five carters' boys.

"It doesn't price us," he said. "The board, I mean. It prices the work, and it doesn't say one word about who does it." He put his finger under the shingle, without touching. "At the Cinder House you'd have read this in a breath. You'd have known who was above you and who you could reach. There's nothing here to read that way."

Lira looked along the plank again, more slowly. He watched her understand it, and it was not a happy understanding. She had gone up a ladder a rung at a time since she was a girl, at Fenmark and in a yard and on a hill, and had paid for every rung with her feet.

"Then where do you go up?" she said.

"I don't think you do."

"Somebody's best. Somebody's always best. How does anyone know who?"

"You ask who came back," said a voice behind them.

---

He was sixty, or a little over, and he had one arm.

The left sleeve of his coat was folded and pinned at the elbow, neatly, with a brass pin gone dull. Under the right arm he carried a tall book in boards, and he had a pen behind his ear. He was broad and not tall, with a grey stubble and a brown bald head, and his eyes were the colour of the tar on the plank.

He looked at Lira and then at Cael. Then he looked at each of them again, in the same order, just as carefully, as though the first look had been only a draft.

"That's all a hirer wants to hear from me," he said. "Did they come back, and did his goods come with them. I've been asked it every day for thirty years and I've not yet been asked a second question."

"Are you the keeper?" said Lira.

"I keep the book."

"What are you?"

It was a western question, and she asked it in the western way, chin up, as one fighter asks another across a yard. Cael saw that he understood exactly what she meant by it. Something moved at the corner of his mouth.

"Copper, Force, thirty years on the roads," he said. "Pike. Now you know less than you did."

He set the book on the plank. There was a hole bored in the oak at that place, the size of a hen's egg, and a horn ink-pot sat down in it to its lip, where no elbow could tip it; beside the hole lay a flat grey river stone as big as a hand. He opened the book and put the stone on the corner of the leaf. Cael understood then what it was for, since a man with one arm cannot hold a page down and write on it as well.

The others had come up while he spoke, Brom large and quiet, and Karis with her notebook shut on her finger. At the plank's far end, reading the last slip as if he had been there for some time, stood a mild man in a dark coat.

"Copper," said Brom slowly. "And you keep the book."

"Somebody's got to. The Golds weren't asking for it." Pike took the pen from his ear. "You'll be the five with the horse, that came in behind the old man. You'll want a heading."

"We want work," said Lira. "There's no bouts. What do crews do, here?"

"Walk, carry, climb, fight."

"In that order?"

"Depends on the day."

He dipped the pen, and he had a way of doing it without looking, down into the hole and up, that must have taken him a year to learn.

"Who's captain?"

Nobody answered him, for Lira looked at Cael, and Cael looked at Brom, and Brom looked at the ground, and Karis opened her notebook as though it might be written there. It had never once come up. They had been four enrollees and a clerk; before that they had been a provision, and a ladder fighter, and a docket, and a research budget, and a man who sent in returns.

"We haven't one," said Cael.

"Somebody decides."

"We put our hands up," said Brom. "All five. It's worked so far."

Pike looked at them for the third time, and the fourth.

"I've forty headings in this book," he said, "and every one's a name. One name. A man, or a woman, and whoever walks with them." He scratched his jaw with the feather end of the pen. "Well. It'll be five names, then. It takes more ink. Say them slow, and I'll write what I hear."

They said them, Lira as she would have given hers to a registrar, and Brom as he would have told the hour, and Karis spelled hers out letter by letter. Then there was a gap, no longer than a breath, and Cael knew whose it was without turning his head.

A man who had been posted from town to town for fifteen years had been written into a book in every one of them, always by some other hand, and under a grade.

"Seln," said Seln. He seemed a little surprised by the sound of it.

"Cael."

Pike wrote it and did not ask for the rest. There was a rest, long and correct, with a hyphen in it, and it stood on a withdrawal leaf in a registrar's hall and on a file that had not been tied; but nobody in this meadow had any use for it, and Cael had none either.

"How old are you?" said Pike, without lifting his head.

"Eighteen."

"I'd two arms at eighteen. Never did me the good you'd think."

He ruled the leaf with the edge of the stone, drawing the pen down beside it four times, and when he had five columns he wrote a word at the head of each. Cael read them upside down, across the plank, as he had read a deposit book and an enrollment book and most of what he knew.

*Job, Hirer, Witness, Outcome, Price.*

He looked for the sixth, though he knew that it was not there, having counted five; he looked for it all the same, the way you look for a stair in the dark, with a foot. Every book that had ever had his name in it had kept a place for what he was. There had been a column in a district office at the far end of the country with one word in it, and a narrow column with no heading in a station's register, and a line on a form where a registrar had hunted two years for a better word and written *unclassified*. His foot came down on nothing at all, and the jolt of it went through him.

Pike had seen him do it.

"You read upside down."

"I've had to."

"Then you'll have seen there's nothing under it."

"Not yet."

"No. Not yet." Pike lifted the stone, and blew on the ink, and shut the book. "A line goes in when a job's been witnessed. The book makes the wall, and the wall's inside, by the hearth. You're not on it." He tucked the book under his arm. "Take a slip or don't. I'm at my dinner."

---

They had a fire that night, in a meadow where fires were nobody's business, and they sat round it later than they should have.

Seln came back at dusk and did not say where he had been. He sat down in his own place, a little behind the rest, and held his hands out to the flames, and there was dust on his boots to the ankle that was not the meadow's dust.

"He never asked," said Lira.

She had been turning it over since the plank. She sat with her arms round her knees and her chin on them, and she was frowning at the fire as she frowned at a floor that had been chalked wrong.

"I had it ready. I've had it ready for a year. *Wind. Iron, Rank One.* I've a certificate for it in this coat, with a seal, and I thought he'd want to see that before he'd so much as write my name." She shook her head. "He wrote my name and went to his dinner."

"I'd mine ready as well," said Brom. "Copper. For good, and I can say why." He turned his big hands over. "And then the man with the book's a Copper himself, and he says it the way I'd say I was tall."

"Iron, Rank Three," said Karis. "Certified, and retained. I've not had it counted since a counter, and I find that I expected him to count it." She considered. "I think I was looking forward to his declining."

"Bronze," said Seln mildly. "Nobody has asked me in fifteen years. They had it on a roll."

They looked at Cael then, as he had known they would.

"I had the word ready," he said. "The one Bracken wrote. I've had it ready since I was fourteen, for every door and every desk, and I'd got so I could say it before they asked." He picked up a stick and put it down. "And there wasn't anywhere for it to go. He ruled five columns and I went looking for the sixth. I think I'd have minded less if he'd had one and left it empty."

Nobody laughed, and Brom nodded slowly, as a man nods at a weight he has also lifted, and Karis wrote something short and did not underline it.

It was a strange lightness, and he was not sure that he trusted it. Out beyond a line he had waited for the ground to tell him he had crossed something, and it had said nothing. Today a book had been opened and ruled for him, and it had not wanted to know what he was. Both times he had braced for a step that was not there.

"I don't see how it works," said Lira. "That's what I can't get past. If there's no rank, then how does anybody out here know I'm better than I was last month?"

"You go out," said Brom. "And you come back."

"That isn't a rank."

"No. I think that's the idea of it."

She made a sound in her throat, and got up, and walked off up the meadow toward the long eave, between the fires, with her hands in her coat. She was gone as long as it takes to walk to a door and stand in it, and when she came back she sat down in the same place and put her feet to the heat.

"There's a sheet on the wall by the hearth. I didn't go in. Twelve lines on it, one under another." She put her chin back on her knees. "I couldn't read them from the door, and there was a room full of people who'd have watched me try. I'll read it when I'm on it."

Karis had the field archive open, by the light. She had written five words across the head of a clean leaf, in her small exact hand, with a ruled line under them. Now she went back along the row and drew a second line beneath one of the five.

"You've underlined it," said Lira.

"*Outcome.* Yes."

"Why that one?"

"Because the other four are facts about a contract. Who wanted what done, and who saw it, and what was paid." Karis laid the pencil in the fold. "That one is a fact about us. It's the only place in his whole book where he writes down anything we did. I should like to know what words he uses."

Cael wrote the day by the same light, on his knee.

*Ninth day. Lowmarch. A river, a ferry, two inns, a smith, a meadow, and no wall. Fires and voices and Paths in the meadow; none of them on the road. A board with fourteen slips on it and not one that prices the crew. A heading in a one-armed man's book: five names, five columns, nothing under them. He asked how old I was. He asked nothing else.*

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

He shut the book and looked across the fire, to where the gelding had his nose in a net by the cart. Brom had gone over to stand with him, and was counting something on his fingers, slowly, and then he counted it a second time, and from the set of his shoulders he liked the answer no better.

# Chapter 3 — What the Board Pays

Brom laid it out for them in the morning, on the tailboard, with oats.

He had been awake a good while, as Cael knew by the gelding, who had been watered and picked out and now stood with a full net in front of him, and by the tailboard itself, which had been swept clean. On the clean oak Brom had set down four small heaps of grain in a row and a fifth by itself, and he waited until all of them were looking before he began.

"That's a day," he said, and touched the four. "Four nets, on this grass, for a horse his size, and the boy wasn't lying to me. I've watched him eat three since yesterday noon and look round for the next." He moved one finger to the fifth heap. "That's the copper to stand the cart. And none of it's us. We haven't had a mouthful out of that yet."

"What does it come to?" said Lira.

"There's a slip on that plank with a seal on it. Two marks, for a crew to stand on a boat all day." He put his palm flat over the four heaps and the fifth together. "He eats that in two days. So does the cart, standing. Every second morning one of those fourteen slips goes down a horse, and we've done the work for it, and we're no further on."

Nobody spoke at once, and the gelding pulled at his net, loudly, as though to make the point for him.

"He draws the cart," said Lira. "That's what he's for. You can't carry three boxes on your back to wherever the work is."

"Where is the work?"

It was Seln who had asked, mildly, from his place a little behind the rest; and when they turned he was looking at the eastern saddle.

"I walked a short way last evening, and again before it was light," he said. "I do not make a practice of saying where, but this bears upon the horse. The east road is a cart's width as far as the top of that rise; beyond it there are two ruts cut in scrub, and I could span the pair of them with my arms. The north road is the same once it has passed the last roof." He considered the cart with something like regret. "It was built for a metalled way with a ditch on either side, and it will go back along the road it came by, or it will stand in this meadow; I do not think there is a third thing it can do."

"The carts here are half the width," said Karis slowly. "I wrote that down on the sixth day and never asked myself what it meant."

"And there is the other matter." Seln turned his cup in his hands. "The courier looked at his feet before she looked at us, and so did the woman with the hides, and the boy at the ferry, and the boy with the tally. A shod horse on these roads is a notice, in large type, and what it says is the word the old man used. I should prefer not to walk into every hold east of here behind it."

Lira had her arms folded, and she was looking at the gelding and not at any of them, and Cael, who had watched her lose bouts, knew that she had already seen how this one would be scored.

"He did a hill a day," she said. "Every day. She said he would, and he did. He went through three rivers and down a scree on his own feet with a cart sliding behind him, and he never once lay down."

"He didn't," said Brom. "I'm not saying he's a bad horse. I'm saying he's a horse."

"Who'd buy him? Out here? He's old. They'll look in his mouth and send for a knacker."

"You told him you'd sell him to one," said Brom. "In the brown river, the second day out. The first one past the Line, you said. I was standing at the tailboard."

"I was *encouraging* him."

"What is his name?" said Karis. She had her notebook open. "I ask because I have been writing *the gelding* for a fortnight, and if there is to be a sale I should like the entry to be right."

There was a short silence.

"He hasn't got one," said Lira.

"You have talked to him every day since Ostrand."

"You don't name what you sell," said Brom gently.

"I didn't name him." Lira's chin came up. "I only called him things."

"I have a number of them written down," said Karis. "I don't think any will do for a bill of sale."

Brom made a sound that he turned into a cough, and Lira looked round at the four of them with her mouth gone very tight at one corner; and then, since nothing else could be done with it, she laughed. It was not a long laugh, and when it was over she wiped her nose on her wrist.

"Not a knacker," she said. "I'll put my hand up, but not for that. He goes to somebody who'll keep him on the flat, or he doesn't go."

Cael had been looking across the meadow while she spoke, at a round shed by the water with open sides.

"There's a man over there," he said, "with a mule that can't walk in a circle."

---

The ferryman was a long sour man who seemed to like animals and nobody else.

He was in the shed when they came, with the old mule's off hind foot between his knees, and he did not put it down to look at them. The shed was a ring of posts under a cone of thatch, and in the middle stood a drum of oak as thick as a barrel, with the wet rope coiled on it, and from the drum a beam ran out level, fifteen feet, with a padded collar at its end. Round the whole the earth was trodden into a perfect circle, as hard as a threshing floor.

"He's done," said the ferryman, to the hoof. "I've known it since the thaw. He stops, and I shout, and he goes on because he's honest, and it's a poor trade for the both of us." He let the foot down at last. "What's that?"

"A horse," said Lira.

"I can see what it is. What's it doing in my shed?"

"He's for sale."

The ferryman came out from behind the mule, wiping his hands on his thighs, and walked round the gelding once. He ran a hand down each leg in turn and lifted the feet, and then he took hold of the upper lip, not roughly, and looked in.

"Long in the tooth."

"He's experienced," said Lira.

"He's shod."

"There's a smith forty yards from your door."

"What's wrong with him? There's always a thing. Folk don't sell a sound horse at the far end of nowhere."

Lira drew breath, but Brom spoke first, as Cael had known he would, since it was not in him to let a man buy a load without being told what it weighed.

"He's stopped liking hills. He'll do one in a day if nobody argues with him, and he'll want the afternoon for it. He likes going down them less. That's the whole of what's wrong, and I've walked behind him for a fortnight looking for the rest."

The ferryman turned his head and looked at his own floor, which lay as level as water all the way round, and he went on looking at it for some time.

"There's no hill in it," he said.

They put him to the beam, and he stood there with the collar on and his ears back, plainly waiting for somebody to show him the slope. Then the ferryman clicked his tongue, and the gelding leaned, and the drum groaned and began to turn. He went a quarter of the way round and stopped, and looked at the ground in front of him, which was flat; then he went on again, and at the half he stopped once more, and found it flat there as well.

By the third time round his ears had come forward.

"Look at him," said Lira bitterly. "A fortnight I've hauled that animal over a continent, and talked myself hoarse at him, and all he ever wanted was to go round and round a post."

"He likes knowing where it ends," said Brom.

"It doesn't end. It's a circle."

"That's as near as he'll get. I'd not take it from him."

They settled the price on an upturned bucket, Brom and the ferryman, and it took a good while, because Brom had counted the old mule's stops. There had been eleven in a single crossing, the day before, and he knew how many carts had stood waiting on the far bank through each one, and he set that sum against the gelding's teeth and went on setting it there, patiently, until the ferryman stopped talking about teeth. Cael did not hear the figure, though he heard what went with it, for that had been his own notion: dry room for three boxes in the loft above the drum, where the ferryman's boy slept, for as long as the horse walked underneath.

"And the mule?" said Brom, when they had shaken on it.

"What about him?"

"What's done with him?"

The ferryman looked at him as though the question had been in some other tongue.

"He's had eleven years at that beam. He goes on the grass behind the landing and he's fed till he drops." He spat, without heat. "What do you take me for?"

"I didn't know," said Brom. "So I asked."

Lira said her goodbye in her own way. She stood at the edge of the trodden ring with her arms folded and told the gelding, as he came round, that he was a great idle brute who had been bought for more than he was worth and sold for more than that, and had now fallen into the one employment in all the world without a hill in it, having done nothing whatever to deserve it. He did not turn his head, for he was looking at the ground in front of him, with his ears forward, and it was flat.

The cart went to the smith the same afternoon. He was a square, silent man, and he wanted it for the iron on its wheels and the oak in its bed, and said he would cut the axle down to a mule's gauge and let it out by the day to anybody fool enough to want wheels on those roads. Brom emptied it first, and from under the seat board he drew out a hedging bill with a hooked blade and an ash handle worn dark.

"That goes with the cart," said the smith.

"It came with the cart. It isn't the cart." Brom turned the blade over and looked along its edge. "I've a use for it."

The smith shrugged, and then sold Lira a long knife, which was the last thing the old purse ever bought. She chose it by its balance, across two fingers, as she would have chosen a lath; and when he told her that it was a skinning blade she said that it was whatever she said it was, and wore it home on her hip, outside her coat.

They sealed the money that night, by the fire, with the meadow going quiet round them.

Karis counted it. She had the purse from Ostrand in her lap, and the ferryman's price and the smith's on a cloth beside her, and she counted the whole twice over in her small dry voice, under her breath, and wrote one figure at the foot of a leaf. Then she did a thing Cael had never seen her do with a number, which was to turn the notebook over on her knee, face down.

"I shan't read it out," she said. "I've thought about it. If we all have it by heart we shall all be doing sums with it by tomorrow, and I should rather we didn't know how long we could go on."

"Go on at what?" said Lira.

"At not earning anything."

"That's my rule, and I'd better say it," said Brom.

He sat on one of the boxes, which he had carried up a ladder into the loft that afternoon and then carried down again for this, since Karis would not have the bag sealed out of her sight, and his hands hung between his knees.

"We eat what the board pays. Only that. What's in that cloth doesn't buy a loaf, or a net, or a night under a roof, however wet it is." He looked round at them. "It's there for a leg that's broken, or a box that's burned, or a morning when one of us has to be got somewhere fast. It isn't for dinner."

"We've more silver in that bag than this whole meadow has seen," said Lira, "and you want us living on two marks."

"Yes."

"Tell me why. I'm not saying no. I want to hear it."

"I can give you one reason," said Seln, "and it is the least of them. A crew that spends silver in a place like this has told the place where it came from. We have just sold a horse so as not to say so."

"The estate's paid me a son's allowance since I was fourteen," said Brom. "Every month, whether I answered the letter or I didn't. I never earned a copper of it, and I never went hungry, and I can't tell you what I'm worth to anybody, because nobody's ever had to pay it. The registry says Copper and a district panel says Iron, and neither of them has ever paid me either." He turned his big hands over and looked at the palms. "There's a man here with one arm who'll write down what I did and what it fetched. I'd like to read that line without knowing there's a bag behind it."

Karis nodded once, as she nodded at a citation.

"Mine's shorter," said Cael. "Most of what I put in was Hesk's. He sent it a little at a time for two years, folded inside his letters, out of a shop that two polite men keep calling at. It was never mine to eat. I'd like to send some of it back one day and tell him that I didn't need it."

Lira looked at the fire for a while, and then she nodded, slowly.

"All right. I'd have said the same in a week," she said. "I only wanted it said before I'd agreed to it."

So Karis sewed the bag. She had a sailmaker's needle in her kit, for the spines of notebooks, and she closed the canvas with a flat seam and went back over it, and Brom warmed his wax at the fire and laid it along the stitches in a strip as wide as a thumb.

"Five," he said. "In the wax, while it's soft. Then it's opened by all of us, or we'll know which of us it was."

Lira pressed hers first, hard, and Karis followed her, and Brom, whose print took up a third of the strip, and Cael; and then the wax was cooling, and one of them had not moved.

"I have gone fifteen years without leaving one of those on anything," said Seln. He was looking at the strip with real interest. "It was the second thing I was taught. I find that the hand does not want to."

"It's in the table," said Lira.

"There is no table for this."

"There is now. I've just ruled it."

"Mm," said Seln, and he leaned forward and put his thumb into the wax beside the other four, and held it there until the edge had set.

Brom carried the box back up the ladder with the bag at the very bottom of it, under a layer of masonry chips. When he came down, Seln had taken the surveyor's case out from between the other two boxes and was putting its strap over his head.

"It rode under a canvas for ten days," he said, to nobody. "I had thought I might sleep the better for it, and I did not."

Brom went through the food bag by the last of the light and told them what was in it: oats, and a heel of the last keeper's cheese, and flour for six days if nobody was greedy. All of that had been bought before the wax went on, and they agreed that it was theirs.

"Six days," said Lira. "To be worth two marks to somebody."

"Five," said Brom. "I'm greedy."

---

The slip with the seal on it was the landing broker's.

He was a brisk, pleasant, well-kept man with ink on his cuffs, and he had a clerk at his elbow who carried a ledger and a stick of dark wax. He seemed to Cael to be doing several things at once and to have his mind on a different one. His goods stood on the western landing in a long stack: bales, and tarred casks, and a number of crates nailed shut, and on the end of every one a mark had been burned with an iron.

It was the mark that was in the wax, and Cael looked at it with care, as he looked at anything that came twice: a hook, with a bar under it, which told him nothing about the man who used it.

"Six crossings," said the broker. "It's more than the boat ought to carry in a day and I want it over by dark. All I ask is that you stand." He waved a hand at the scow. "On the rail, where both banks have a view of you. There are people on this river who count the heads on a boat before they decide what the boat is worth to them. A ferry with nobody on it loses a cask now and then. A ferry with five on the rail never has, in my experience." He smiled, quickly. "I find it cheaper than the cask."

"Stand," said Lira. "That's the work."

"That is the whole of it. I should be obliged if you would look as though you were paying attention."

So they stood, and Seln placed them without being asked: one at each forward corner, and one aft on either side, and the fifth amidships by the goods, where the casks were. He changed the fifth at every crossing, as he changed the sleeper on a watch, and Cael noticed that he never once put himself where the sun would be behind him.

It was a job that Cael had done before, at a higher price and for worse people.

He thought about it through the first two crossings, with his hand on a rail that had been worn pale by other hands. For fourteen sittings he had climbed four steps in Ostrand to stand on a number carved in a wool exchange's floor, and he had stood there to be looked at. A seat had looked, under a hood, and a procurator, and two hundred people on benches, and every one of them had been deciding what he was while he stood. It had cost a house its year, and a registrar thirteen sleepless nights, and four people their enrollments.

Here he was worth two marks among five, and a river was looking at him. There were alders on the far bank, and a heron, and upstream, a long way off, something that might have been a boat drawn up under a bank. Whoever was in it would see five heads on a rail and count them, and that was all they would ever want from him; nobody on that water cared what the heads had in them.

"You're smiling," said Karis. She was amidships, with her notebook, timing the crossing by Hesk's clock.

"I've been stared at for a living before. I didn't know it could be this restful."

"I shall enter that you said so. Nine minutes, and no stops." She looked across at the round shed, where a brown shape went steadily about a post. "He hasn't stopped once all morning. I've a column for it."

Lira had been looking at the shed as well, at every crossing, and had not said one word about it.

The log came down on the fifth.

It was not a log. It was a whole tree, a pale one, with its root-plate still on it like a great clenched fist, and it came round the upstream bend riding low and slow, and turning as it came. The ferryman's boy saw it first and shouted, for on a river, it seemed, a person might shout as he pleased.

"The cable," said Seln sharply. "If the roots take the cable it will have us round."

Three things happened in Cael at once, and he had been waiting for them. Pressure began to gather under his breastbone, as breath gathers before a shout, for a push at that range would have turned a tree. Storm leaned, and showed him the air between the scow and the trunk as a thing with a grain in it. And Wind remembered a painted floor in his feet, and offered to put him somewhere else.

*Hold*, he told all three fragments. It took him no longer than it takes to lower a hand.

Brom had the sweep, which was a pole of ash twenty feet long, shod with iron, and he had it out of its crutch and over the upstream rail before the boy had finished shouting. He set the iron against the trunk, just ahead of the roots, and the tree came on and leaned its whole wet weight into him.

Cael saw his forearms.

It was Iron Skin, which was the whole of Brom's Path, and he had seen it come a hundred times, in a harness room and in a river: the skin ceased to crease, and went the grey of a hearthstone, from the wrist upward. It began now, and it got as far as the cuff of his coat before Brom looked down at his own arm, as a man looks at a dog that has got up on a chair.

The grey went out of it.

He grunted, and his boots slid a hand's breadth on the planks, and he bent his knees and put his back into the pole until the cords stood out in his neck. He was a large young man pushing a tree with a stick, and he had never looked less like anything else. The root-plate swung, slowly, and went wide of the cable by the length of an arm, and the whole pale mass turned under the stern and went on down the river.

"Like a large man," said Brom, breathing hard, and looking at his forearms, which were red. "It's heavier that way."

"Is it worse?" said Cael.

"I don't know yet. I've never weighed one before."

The ferryman made his mark on the slip with a thumb dipped in tar: a ring with a dot in it, which Cael supposed was the shed.

Pike was at the plank when they brought it up. He took the slip in his one hand and read it, and then he read it again, with his lips moving a little, and after that he read it aloud. It was not for them. He turned his shoulder to the meadow and said it out over the nearest tents in a level carrying voice, and a woman frying dough looked up from her pan.

"Ferry, the landing's goods. Six crossings stood. Nothing lost. Two marks."

Then he opened the book at their leaf and put the stone on its corner and wrote, and when the ink was down he said the same words over again, exactly, to the same tents.

"He says everything twice," said Lira in Cael's ear. "He looks at you twice and he reads it twice. Is it his age?"

"I don't think so. I think it's for something."

"For what?"

"I don't know yet. He'd tell us if we asked, and I'd rather see it."

There was an iron box bolted to the nearer post, under the plank, with a lock on it the size of a fist. Pike opened it with a key from round his neck and took out two coins, and laid them on the oak in front of Brom, who happened to be standing nearest.

They were silver, small and thick, and a good deal worn, and Brom picked them up and held them in the middle of his palm, and went on looking at them after Pike had locked the box.

"What's the matter with them?" said Pike.

"Nothing." Brom closed his hand. "It's the first money I've ever had that nobody sent me."

Pike looked at him, and looked again.

"Well, it spends the same," he said. "That's the pity of it." But he went on watching Brom's back for a little while as they walked away down the meadow.

The woman with the dough had gone back to her pan. She had heard it, though, and so had the man with the harness across his knees, and the three children; and by dark, Cael supposed, the whole meadow would know that five strangers had stood on a boat all day and lost nothing. It was not much to be known for. It was also the first thing that anybody east of a line knew about them at all, and it happened to be true.

It bought flour, and salt, and a piece of bacon no bigger than a hand, and Brom told them that it would have kept the gelding for one day and the half of another. Karis wrote the line into the field archive as she had heard it said, and drew her second rule under two of its words.

"*Nothing lost,*" she said. "And *stood*. He gives an outcome no subject. He doesn't say who stood. He says only that it was done, and what came of it." She considered the page. "I think that's his whole grammar. I shall want a good many more before I'm sure."

Lira had gone up to the long eave again, and this time she was away rather longer, and she came back walking fast.

"We're on it," she said. "The sheet. There's a new line at the bottom, and it's wet." She sat down. "Thirteenth. Of thirteen."

"It's one line," said Brom. "We've been here five days."

"I know what it is. I'm telling you where it is." She pulled her knees up. "Every other line's a name, or a trade. *The hide-woman's two.* That sort of thing. Ours is the only one that's a list. And the top one's a single word."

"What word?"

"*Teague.*"

Nobody knew it, and Cael turned it over once, as he turned any new name, and found nothing under it.

"Aren't you coming to look?" said Lira. "You read everything. You read the back of a waybill this morning."

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

He thought about how to say it, for he had been on a great many boards. A crossroads in Ardenmere had carried him, in chalk, and a hall at Norhold, and four black ones in a sunk yard, and every one of them had told the people looking at it what to think of him before he opened his mouth.

"I'll read it when there's something on it that I did," he said. "One line for standing on a boat isn't worth the walk. I'd like the first thing that sheet tells me to be true."

He wrote the day's entry short. *Fourteenth day. First line: six crossings stood, nothing lost. Earned, two. In hand, less than one, and a side of bacon. The reserve is not counted here and never will be. Thirteenth of thirteen, Lira says. Deployment since the Line: none. Pressure, Storm and Wind all offered when a tree came down the river, and Brom's Path offered too, and all four were told no.*

"Two marks," said Lira, to the fire. "For standing still."

"It's harder than it looks."

"You've had practice."

"Two years of it."

"Then you had better show the rest of us how it's done," said Lira. "Because when that tree came round the bend I had one foot on the rail, and I don't remember putting it there."

# Chapter 4 — The Hold

He began with Lira, because she had asked first, and because she was a good deal the worst of them at it.

There was a bar of gravel below the meadow, sixty yards long, where the river had laid down its stones on the inside of a bend, and at first light nobody was on it but a heron. The mist lay on the water to the height of a man's knee. Cael scratched a line across the gravel with his heel at the bar's lower end and walked back up to where she stood, with her boots laced tight and her long knife left on the bank, shaking out her wrists as she had shaken them out before every bout of her life.

"Run it," he said. "As fast as you've got. Stop on the line."

"Is that all?"

"Stop on your feet. With nothing at the end of it."

She gave him the look she gave an easy draw, and went. She was very fast, which he had half forgotten on a road at a horse's pace. She reached the line and went straight over it, and it took her five more strides and both arms to come to a stand.

"I stopped," she said, coming back.

"You stopped at the water."

"There's nothing to stop *with*. I've always gone, at the end. That's what the end is for." She scowled at the gravel. "Again."

The second time she pulled up on the line itself, stumbling, with her weight all wrong and her face dark with temper, and said that it was like running hard at a door and then leaving it shut. The third time she ran as she must have run on a painted floor with a coach watching. She came down the bar like something thrown; and three strides short of the line the air cracked, and the gravel went up in a fan, and she was standing twelve feet beyond his mark with her hands out in front of her and the heron lifting off the far bank.

She did not turn round for a moment.

"I didn't do that," said Lira.

"I know."

"I mean that I didn't decide it. I was running, and then I'd gone." She looked at her own feet as if they belonged to somebody she had trusted. "It went off by itself."

"That's what I wanted you to see. I couldn't have told you." He walked down to her. "Out here a burst is a shout. I don't know who it is that listens, and I don't think Brom's deer were telling us, but every carter on that road believes somebody does. And yours has just shouted without asking you."

"So I say no at the line. Harder."

"You've been saying no at the line. That's why it doesn't work." It was strange to be the one explaining a thing to her, on gravel, with her breath still coming fast, and he went carefully. "By the line you've been saying yes for sixty yards, with every stride, and then you want one no to stop all of it. You say it at the first stride. Then you say it again at the second. It isn't one refusal. It's as many as there are steps."

"That's a hundred. Every time I run."

"Every time you do anything at all, until it's worn in. I had a book for it, on the hill. What to take late, and what to give. I refused something or other every minute of every Fifth-day for two years, and I thought it was the price of being let stay. I didn't know I was learning a trade."

She studied him, and whatever she saw made her stop scowling.

"Again," she said, and walked back up the bar.

Brom had found his own lesson without help. A pale trunk lay stranded at the water's edge, as thick as a man's body, and he got it up on his shoulder and began to carry it along the wet stones where the footing was worst. The rule was a simple one: when the load shifted, he was to let it fall.

It shifted on the fourth step. Brom's whole arm went grey to the shoulder between one heartbeat and the next, and the trunk stayed where it was.

"That's a shout," said Cael.

"I know what it is." Brom set the trunk down slowly and stood looking at his arm until the colour came back. "It's never been part of the way before. It's all, or it's none. And I don't drop things."

"Then drop that."

He took it up again. It rolled on his shoulder at the sixth step, and Cael saw him take a decision that plainly went against the grain of his whole life, and open his hands. The trunk hit the stones and bounced, and Brom stood over it, empty-handed, with the face of a man who has let fall somebody else's child.

"I never have," he said. "Not once. Not a plate."

"Was there any grey?"

"No." He looked at his arms, and seemed surprised. "No. There wasn't time for both."

Karis sat on the bank above them with a river stone in her lap and one finger resting on it. She had explained it to him in three sentences, as she explained anything. To set a point was not to light it; she chose the place and held it ready, like a thumb on a latch; and the task was to hold it so. She counted under her breath. At ninety a flame no bigger than a seed stood up on the wet stone, and she took her finger away and looked at it with dislike.

"Ninety," she said. "I've never kept one waiting half so long. I've never had cause. I set them without thinking, on a doorpost, or the corner of a page. I should think I've left a dozen standing behind me in every room I ever worked in." She wrote the figure down. "And I don't know whether a point that's set and not lit is a thing this country hears. Nobody has told us what the rule is a rule against."

"Then you don't set one. Not on a road."

"I haven't gone a day without one since I was fourteen."

"I know," said Cael. "Those are the worst to stop. The ones you never knew you were doing."

Seln was not on the bar. He was never anywhere at first light now, and came back when the meadow was stirring, with dust to his ankles and nothing to say about it; and Cael, who had spent two years being watched by that man, had decided to let him have his mornings.

Down the gravel Lira had turned and set her feet.

He had told three people what it cost them, and he thought he owed the book the same account of himself.

He wrote it on the eighteenth day, at noon, on the bank above the bar, while Brom carried his tree and Lira ran. It took him the better part of an hour. He had written the nine out often enough, on a hill, as a list of what each could do. He had never set them down as what each one charged him to be carried and left alone.

*Eighteenth day. The nine, as things held. Not what they do. What it costs to have each one and not use it, which I'm calling rent.*

*Wind, from Lira. It wants the first stride of anything. My feet lean before I've decided, a dozen times a day, and I put them back. If I ever spend a burst, the landing locks for one count, and that count has never got shorter. Rent: small, and constant.*

*Pressure, from the Bronze from Ardenmere. It gathers over most of a breath and anybody can see it gathering. The easiest to refuse, because it is the slowest to arrive. It tried at the tree on the river.*

*Iron, from Brom: the read. A pace and a half round me, the mass of a thing and which way it is going, never what it means to do. It is the cheapest and quietest thing I own, and the hardest to leave shut, because it doesn't feel like doing anything. It feels like looking. I hold it anyway. I don't know what looking sounds like out here.*

*Compression, from Reydan. Only at contact; the load goes through me into the ground and the bill comes later. Nothing to hold until something hits me.*

*Ember, from Karis. One point, one channel. It wants to set a point on anything I look at for long. Karis lasted ninety today and I'd not have done better.*

*Shadow. Source protected; sealed, as it was on the hill, and for the hill's reason, which was who might carry the seeing west. It costs from the moment I wake and it drifts at dusk. It got as far as my boots on the fifth day. There are two reasons on it now where there was one: the seal, and whatever it is a road hears.*

*Storm, from Daeva. Gold weight, and the heaviest thing I carry, and idle it does nothing at all but sit. If I open it, the read is the air or my own body and the ground, never both, and a lane takes the air. Three lanes so far. After each, everything I have answered a beat late: for ten counts, and ten, and twelve, and came clean at eleven, eleven and thirteen. That is a bill, not a flag. I have not tried a fourth. Rent while idle: I tire sooner than the miles account for.*

*Anchor. Unnamed; the hand with no face. Fixed points, and lines between them, and a cage. I have unbound one thing on purpose, once, and I once held a doorway for twelve seconds without meaning to. It sees what is fixed: sure at ten feet, faint at twelve, nothing at fourteen. I don't know its reach, or its rate, or its price. Rent: ever since the Ostrand road it has asked about every sill and gate and doorway I've gone through, and a town with no wall has a great many doors.*

*Blade, from Ephram. Partial still. It asks "Edge?" of anything with a corner on it, takes a name, and settles. It was right once, on a scree.*

*Nine. And one anomaly, not counted, that came from nobody. Still open. Still real. Patience.*

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

"Six. Compression doesn't charge, and Blade only asks."

"I've two marks."

"No. It gets quicker. On the hill, at the end, I didn't hear myself doing it; it was only a kind of leaning back, all day." He shut the book on the pencil. "And now?"

"Now four people have asked me before breakfast to show them how." He looked down the bar, at a pale trunk lying where a large man had dropped it on purpose. "It's the one thing I was ever made to practise. I hadn't thought it would turn out to be the thing that paid."

Lira thought about that with her boot in her hand.

"It doesn't pay yet," she said. "It pays two marks."

The fence was a day up the north water, and they kept the road's manners all the way there.

They walked without talking, and by the middle of the morning Cael found that he had stopped missing it. Lira's long knife and Brom's bill rode where a stranger would see them, and at noon they sat on a bank and ate cold cake, and not one of the five so much as glanced at the dead wood lying under the trees.

The steading stood in a loop of the river, behind a fence of split posts as high as a man's chest, and the old man who held it was at his gate before they had come within a bowshot. He had a white dog beside him, and a face like the bark of the red trees. He read Pike's mark on the slip, and then he looked at their hands, each pair in turn, and it was the only question he put to them.

"Forty," he said, and walked them along the fence to where a stretch of it had gone at the butts and leaned outward like a row of drunkards. "Cut them in the coppice. A stride apart, and deep. There's an axe." He turned back toward the house, and the dog stayed.

It stayed with Brom, who cut for six hours that day, in a stand of straight grey poles above the river, with the old man's axe and the hedging bill, and whenever he stopped to wipe his face the white dog walked over and sat down on his feet. He told it twice that it was in the way, and after that he worked round it.

Cael watched him for a part of the first hour, while he and Lira were getting the measure of a spade and an iron bar. The axe was an old one, and its edge had been ground a little out of true, so that every tenth stroke or so it glanced in the grain and kicked back up the helve into the hands that held it. That was a load shifting, with no warning at all, and each time it happened Cael looked at Brom's wrists, and each time they were as brown as the rest of him.

"You're counting," said Brom, not turning round.

"Eleven kicks. No grey."

"Twelve. I had one while you were looking at the spade." He set his feet and swung. "It's not that I'm stopping it. I've found the place just before it starts, that's all, and I stand there. It's a narrow place. I'd not like to have to find it in a hurry."

He kept the count of his posts on a length of cord, a knot to each, hung over a branch. In the middle of the afternoon a goat came up out of the river meadow and ate the cord to the last knot but three, and Brom, who had the number perfectly well in his head, laid down the axe and went along the stack and counted every post over again with his finger.

"I check," he said to the goat, which was unmoved.

Lira dug. She detested it, and said so at every hole, and was better at it than Cael by a long way, since she had a fighter's sense of where to put her weight. By evening they had eighteen posts in and the old fence drawn.

It was the new butts that Karis was looking at. She had read about fences, as she had read about most things, and she told Cael the principle while she chose a post from the stack. The end of a post that goes into the ground is charred first, to the depth of a finger, because rot will not take hold in charcoal; a fencer does it in a fire, and turns the post as he would turn a spit. She laid her post across two stones with its butt clear of the grass.

"This isn't a road," she said. She did not make it a question, quite.

"No."

"And there was a fire in his yard when we came. I saw the smoke of it." She put out her left hand, with the pale fingers spread, and reached toward the cut end of the wood.

"Not at my fence."

The old man had come along the line without a sound. He stood six feet behind her with his thumbs in his belt, and he was not angry, any more than a man is angry who tells a child that a pan is hot.

"There's a fire in the yard," he said. "Use the brands."

He went on down the fence, pushing at each new post with the flat of his palm as he passed it.

Karis had not moved. Her hand was still out, over the wood, and her mouth had come a little open; and Cael, who had sat beside her through fifty-one minutes in front of a hooded seat, watched her want something more than she had wanted to win that day. He saw her mouth close, and her hand come back into her lap.

She took out her notebook, and he read it over her arm, since she tilted the page for him.

*Twenty-second day. At the steading on the north water. Asked not to light at the fence line; directed to char with brands from the yard fire. A fire in a yard is permitted. A point at a fence is not. Therefore the rule is not a rule about fire.*

Under that she wrote one line more, slowly, and ruled beneath it.

*I did not ask him why. Entered: the not-asking.*

"He would have told you," said Cael.

"I think he would. I should have had a whole sentence from a primary source, and it's the one sentence I want in the world." Her voice was quite steady, and her knuckles were white on the pencil. "But the courier asked us nothing, and Pike asked you your age and stopped, and nobody in that meadow has yet asked me what's in my boxes. I believe that asking is a thing paper does. I should like to find out what one learns the other way."

"And what have you learned?"

"So far," said Karis, "one fact about a fence and one about myself. I'm not at all sure which I'd sooner have had."

She got up and went to fetch the brands.

They set the last post on the second afternoon, and the old man walked the whole of the line.

He was a long while about it, for he put his palm against every post of the forty and leaned, and the dog went with him and sat down each time he stopped. At the end he stood with his back to them and looked along the tops, where Brom had sighted each one against the last with a length of string. Then he went up to the house.

He came back with four coins in a sock and a boy behind him carrying a sack on his shoulder. The coins he counted into Brom's hand. The sack, which was flour, and a good twenty pounds of it, the boy let down at Brom's feet as though that were the natural place for anything heavy. The old man scratched his mark on the slip with a nail, three upright strokes and a bar across them, and gave it back.

He looked along his fence once more, and then at the five of them.

"Cheap," he said.

He shut his gate, and they heard the bar go across.

"He means us," said Lira, on the road. She had gone a hundred yards with her jaw set. "*Cheap.* I dug twenty-two holes in that man's ground."

"He means the work came to more than he paid for it," said Brom. He had the sack on one shoulder and the bill in his other hand, and he was walking more lightly than a man should with twenty pounds of flour. "He was pleased. He'd no other word for it."

"Then it's a complaint. Against himself."

"It may be. I'll take it, though." Brom walked a little way. "I've been told what I was worth by a district panel and a provincial counter and a letter with a red seal on it. That's the first time anybody ever said it and thought he'd had the better of the bargain."

They had started late, and dusk caught them four miles short of the meadow.

Cael felt it coming this time. The sun went behind the western ridge, all at once, as it did; the river turned from brass to lead; and he had dug holes for two days and his shoulders ached to the bone. It lifted behind the rest of what he carried, soft, and offered him the old thing. He had the refusal ready before it finished asking.

His left boot came down on the road in silence.

It was one stride only, for he had it shut before the right foot landed, and the gravel spoke up again as rudely as ever.

"Left boot," he said aloud. "One stride."

"I heard it." Seln was a little ahead, on the verge, and his voice was hardly more than breath. "I was waiting to learn whether you would tell me."

"I report everything. You know that. You read it for two years."

"I did. It was a great deal of true paper, and I burned my copy of every sheet." He seemed to turn something over. "One stride is better than four. I should expect none at all by the time the weather turns, if you are as obstinate as I was. It never quite stops asking."

"Does it ask you?"

"Every dusk of my life. I tell it that I am walking home."

Brom made bread that night, with the old man's flour, on a flat stone at the edge of their fire. He had seen it done, he said, and it could not be hard. The cakes came off the stone black on both faces and white in the middle, and Lira weighed one in her hand and asked whether the smith had been consulted.

"It's what the board paid," said Brom, with dignity.

They ate every one.

*Twenty-fourth day. Second line: forty posts, cut and set. Four marks and flour. He said "Cheap," and Brom has kept the word. Earned, six. At dusk the sealed one took my left foot for a stride, on an empty road. Rent. Deployment since the Line: none.*

---

Seln took the cold road on the thirtieth morning, and that was the last of the five.

He had walked the east road on the ninth evening, within an hour of stepping off a ferry, as far as a saddle and a little beyond it, and the north before light on the tenth. The south road had cost him two mornings, since it forked. The fourth was the road they had all come in by, and he had gone back along that one too, three miles, to see it as a person sees it who is arriving. This was the fifth. It left the north road at a dead tree and climbed, and the meadow called it cold; he learned why at the first turn, where a wind came down off bare ground that had no business in such mild weather.

He went up it in the grey, alone, at the pace he had once taught four young people in an orchard.

Nobody had sent him, and he was clear with himself about that, as he tried to be about most things. For fifteen years somebody had always sent him, and there had been a line on a sheet to say where, and a second line for the hour at which he was to be back. When he left the last of those houses he had expected that particular habit to be among the first to go.

It had not gone. He walked, and at each turn of the track he did the three things that he had always done. He found the place where a man would stand who wished to watch the road without being seen from it. He found the place where the road was narrowest. He looked for water, and paced it from where a camp might lie.

Then, since he could not help it, he wrote it up.

He did not use paper, for he had not put pencil to it for that purpose since a crossroads, where he had lit a fire with the last one. But the old form ran in him like a tune, and as he climbed it set itself out in the voice he had kept for the audit. *Cold road: leaves the north road at the dead tree, eleven hundred paces above the last roof. A mule's width from the outset. Water at the second turn, forty paces off the track, to the left. Three places of concealment below the crest, none showing use.*

None showing use. He stopped at the third of them, a notch between two boulders, and looked at the grass in it.

It was long, and it stood straight, and he had found the same at every one of them, on five roads, for twenty days. A man who stands in a place, morning after morning, to watch a road leaves the mark of his feet there, and of his waiting, and Seln knew that mark as a baker knows his own thumb in a loaf. He had left it himself in every town he was ever posted to. There was none of it here. The grass had never been stood in, and he did not think that there was, in all this country, one person whose work it was to watch another and write down what he did.

He had not known that he would find that restful. He stood in the notch himself for a moment, from habit, and then he stepped out of it again, feeling a little foolish.

*Nothing further to report*, the form said, and came to its foot, and waited to be folded.

That was the part he could not get used to. A return was a thing with a desk at the far end of it. He had made seven hundred of them, more or less, and at the end of each there had been somebody who asked for it, in a level voice, across a table; and he had given what could be checked and kept back all the rest, so evenly and for so long that it had been mistaken for a manner of writing. He had five roads in his head now, entire, down to the paces. They were better than anything he had ever sent in, for he had left nothing out of them.

And nobody had asked.

He did not offer it. That was not sullenness; he had examined himself for it, with some care. He told them whatever bore on the day in hand. He had told them the gauge of the east road when a horse depended on it, and he had put five people where they ought to stand on a boat. The rest he kept, as he had always kept the rest, and he went about with it and waited for the level voice. *What did you see?* It would come at a fire, he supposed. The boy would say it, since the boy wrote everything down.

The boy had not said it; on the first night in the meadow he had looked at the dust on a pair of boots, and then he had very deliberately looked somewhere else. Seln had been watched for most of his life and had watched for the remainder. He could not remember being left a thing to keep before, and he had not yet settled how such a courtesy ought to be entered.

The case knocked against his hip. He turned at the crest, where the wind was, and went down.

The sun was on the river when he came out above the meadow. Four people were on the gravel bar. One of them ran, and stopped dead on a line, on her two feet, and flung up both arms; and a large one carrying a tree let it roll from his shoulder and stepped out from under it as neatly as a dancer.

There was an alder on the bank that would have covered him. He stood clear of it, in the open, with his hands where they could be seen, until the large one looked up and found him and raised an arm; and then he went down to them with five roads in his head, and found that he was still waiting, and did not altogether mind.

A boy brought the slip in at first light on the thirty-third day, on a mule that had been made to trot.

Pike read it twice, and did not tack it up; he passed it to them over the oak. *Wagon in the wash, east road. Out before the water. Three marks.* There was a mark under the words, a square with a peg at each corner, like a hide stretched to dry.

"Before what water?" said Lira.

Pike pointed with his pen. Over the eastern ridge the sky had gone the colour of a plum, in a stack, and under it hung grey curtains that were rain falling on somebody else.

They were at the wash in three hours, walking fast and saying nothing, with a coil of the ferryman's rope across Brom's chest.

It was a dry stream cut deep through red earth, twelve feet down and thirty across at the bottom, with banks too steep to stand on. The road went into it by a ramp dug out of the eastern side and climbed out by another on the west. A trickle ran in the bed, no wider than a hand. Karis looked at that trickle before she looked at anything else, and went down and drove a peeled stick into the gravel at its edge, and cut a notch in the stick at the waterline with her knife.

The wagon lay at the foot of the far ramp. It was a long four-wheeled thing, and it had come down from the east loaded with baled hides. Its near front wheel had found a boulder. The wheel was a hub and a handful of splinters, and the wagon had slewed off the ramp's foot and settled on that corner, canted over, with its tail up the slope behind it.

The woman with the hides was on the western lip with her two mules. She had got a third of her load up the ramp by herself, a bale at a time, and she was grey to the hair with dust. Cael knew her, for she had once laid a barley cake on the shaft of their cart, and she had been standing ten feet away when an old man asked him what he was. She pointed at the bed of the wash with her whole hand, and that was her greeting.

"Hides first," said Brom, very low. "Then it's a third the weight."

They made a chain of it and had the bales out in half an hour, and while the last of them went up, Brom walked round the wagon as he walked round any load. When he came back he had the whole of it, and he gave it to them with his hands as much as his voice.

A pole went under the broken corner, lashed fore and aft, so that the wagon would ride on three wheels and a skid. The long rope went from the tongue up the western ramp to the mules, and to Brom. That was the haul. But the bed fell away downstream, and a thing dragged across a slope will always swing its tail to the low side and go over; so a second line must run from the tail-ring back to the eastern bank, and somebody must stand there and pay it out, a foot at a time, and never let it go slack.

"That's the light end," he said, and looked at Lira. "It wants somebody quick. There's a thorn stump on the lip. Two turns round it, and the stump holds the wagon. You only hold the stump."

Lira walked down the bed and up the far ramp with the line over her shoulder.

Cael had the wedge. It was a block of ash, split from a limb beside a crossroads fire, and he had carried it over a line and down a scree, and stopped a cart with it some hundreds of times. Seln had a pole to lever with, and they took a wheel each.

"One notch," said Karis, from the gravel. "The water's brown."

The mules leaned, and the wagon groaned and came a yard, grinding, and the skid bit and held, and Cael kicked the wedge in under the hind wheel. It came another yard, and on the eastern lip Lira let a foot of line go round the stump, and then another, with her whole body bent back against it, and the tail came round sweetly behind the nose.

Then the skid rode up on a stone.

The wagon lurched over toward its broken corner, and for the space of a breath it hung there, deciding. The check line came up out of the dust as taut as a bowstring. Lira went forward two strides on her heels, toward the edge, and Cael saw her knees bend in the way he had seen on the gravel bar, three strides short of a line.

She did not go. She threw a third turn round the stump and sat back on it with all her weight.

The wagon settled onto its skid again. Then, having been denied that way, it went the other: the rope up the ramp slackened and snatched, a mule lost its feet, and the whole dead weight of the thing came back down the haul rope into the one man who had it across his shoulders.

Brom's boots went from under him.

Cael saw the grey start at both wrists, and he saw it stop. Then Brom did the only thing that a very large man can do who has decided to be nothing more than that: he sat down. He sat down hard in the dirt of the ramp with the rope round his body and his heels dug in, and was dragged a yard on the seat of his breeches, and another foot, cutting two furrows; and there, like an anchor that has found bottom, he held.

Something in Cael had risen to meet all of it. A fixed point set under that hind rim and told to stay would have settled the matter in a breath, and the one that knew such things had seen the rim and said so. *There?*

*Wheel*, he told it. *Hers. Leave it.* He drove the ash under the iron with his heel, and Seln's pole came down beside it.

"I've sat down," said Brom, through his teeth, from the ramp. "It's all I had."

"Three notches," said Karis. "And there's a sound."

It came from up the wash, low and steady, like a cart crossing a wooden bridge a long way off.

The check line had fouled. Cael saw it as the mule found its feet again: a loop of it had dropped off the lip and caught behind a rock in the bed, twenty feet out from the eastern bank, and while it lay there Lira could neither pay out nor take in. She had seen it too, from above. It was twenty feet of open air to that rock, and she could have been standing on it in the time it takes to blink.

She ran down the ramp instead, and went through the water, which was over her ankles now and the colour of the banks, and freed the loop with both hands, and ran back up the way she had come. It took her a count of twenty where a burst would have taken one. From any ridge in that country, nobody would have heard a thing.

"Now," said Brom, and the mules leaned.

The wagon went up the western ramp on three wheels and a pole, a yard and a wedge at a time. Its tailboard had cleared the lip by perhaps the length of the wagon when the wash filled, and the water did not come as a wave. It came as a brown tongue a foot high, quite quiet, with sticks in it, and behind the tongue the bed simply rose. Karis came up the ramp at a run with her skirts in her fists, and when Cael looked back from the top her notched stick was gone, and the place where a wagon had lain was hip deep and moving.

The woman with the hides scratched her mark on the slip against her own tailboard. She had not spoken a word in three hours. She looked now at Brom, who was red earth from his heels to his belt, and at Lira's hands; and last she looked at Cael, for a good while, with her head a little on one side.

"You're not paper," she said.

It would have been easy to take. He was tired, and it was meant kindly, and it was the best thing anybody had said to him since a registrar wrote in a margin. But a clerk had written him down at fourteen, in a district office at the far end of the country, and he had been in somebody's book every day since. One morning in a ditch had not unwritten it, and he did not want the first good opinion he earned out here to rest on a mistake.

"I am," he said.

She thought about that, and she neither took her own words back nor argued with his; and then she clicked her tongue twice at her mules.

Lira came and stood by him while the hides went on again. Her palms were raw across the heel, and she was holding them a little away from her coat.

"I said no at the first stride," she said. "And the second. I lost count somewhere past thirty."

"And at the stump?"

"That wasn't no. That was a rope." She turned her hands over and looked at them. "A turn of rope round a stump does what a burst can't, as it turns out. Somebody might have told me that at Fenmark."

# Chapter 5 — The Stretch

By the forty-first day they had three lines in Pike's book and a place at the edge of the carters' fire.

Nobody had offered it to them. A fire burned most nights in the middle of the meadow, a little way from the ash, and the people with carts sat round it on their own shafts and upturned buckets. For a month the five had kept to their own fire, forty yards off, as they had kept forty yards behind three carts on a road. Then, on the evening after the wash, the woman with the hides had moved her bucket a foot to the left without looking at anybody, and it had left a gap in the ring. Brom had looked at the gap for a whole day before he would let them sit in it.

Nine marks earned, the book said. It did not say that Lira's palms had skinned over, or that Brom could now drop a tree at the first slip and catch it again at the second, or that Karis had held a point unlit to the count of three hundred and then put it out herself. Cael kept those in his own book, at the foot of each day, under a line that had not changed since a ring of churned turf.

The carter came in at dusk with one cart and one mule.

He was a short, thick man, burnt as brown as a nut, and his cart was loaded to the rails with sacks. He chocked his wheels, saw to the mule, and sat down in the ring with a yard of empty ground on either side of him. Cael had seen him once already that day, at noon, handing a slip across the plank; and he had watched the meadow read that slip all afternoon, crew after crew, and go back to its dinner.

*Thornwater road, the stretch. A crew to walk ahead. Nine marks.*

"Nine," Lira had said, at the plank. "For walking. That's as much as we've made since we got here."

"And nobody's taken it," Brom had said. "So there's a thing we don't know."

The carter ate what he had brought. He did it slowly, looking into the fire, and when he had done he wiped his knife on his knee and spoke to the flames.

"Two carts went quiet on it last month," he said.

Nobody answered him. On the far side of the ring the old man from under the ash shifted his feet.

"It's a noon road. Always was. You take the stretch with the sun straight over, and you've no trouble, and I've done it so for twenty-two years." He turned the knife over. "I lost a day at the ferry to an axle. I'll not be at the mouth of it by noon now, whatever I do. I might be there by the middle of the afternoon, if nothing else goes."

"That's a noon price," said the old man, to nobody in particular, "for a road that mightn't be walked at noon."

"It's what I've got."

"What's on it?" said Lira.

She had asked it clearly, across the fire, as she would have asked a coach what a drill was for. Cael felt Karis go still beside him, with her pencil half out of its spine. Several of the people in the ring looked at the ground.

The carter turned his head and considered Lira for some time. He did not seem offended. He seemed to be working out how much of an answer a person was owed who knew no better than to ask.

"Walk quiet," he said, "and you'll not need telling."

"That isn't—"

"It's what there is." He looked past her, at the rest of them, and his eyes stopped longest on Brom. "You're the ones had her wagon out of the wash. Before the water. And never lit, she says, nor shouted, nor one of you did a thing but pull."

"Yes," said Cael.

"I'd as soon have that."

He said no more, and the fire talked for a while by itself.

Cael did what he always did. The claim was a mile of road that carters crossed only at one hour of the day. Under it stood two carts that had not come home, and a price that the whole meadow thought too small, and a word, *quiet*, that everybody out here used for a thing done and also for a thing that happened to a cart. He had no notion what walked on that stretch. But a man who had kept the road for twenty-two years had just said what he wanted for it, and it was the one thing they had spent a month learning to do.

"Hands," he said, low.

Brom's went up first, and then Seln's. Lira's followed, with her jaw set, for she had not forgiven the answer. Karis kept hers down.

"The boxes," she said. "I left them three days in that loft, for a fence, and I didn't sleep. This is two days out and I can't tell how many back."

The carter had heard her. Out here, it seemed, everybody heard everything.

"What boxes?"

"Three. They're paper."

He looked at his sacks, and at her, and shrugged.

"They'll ride on the grain. I'll not charge a fare for paper."

Karis put her hand up.

---

Pike marked the slip at first light, with the mist still on the river.

He had made them a sign, since five names would not go on a scrap of bark: five short strokes side by side, like a tally with its bar left off. He drew it under the carter's own mark and read the whole aloud to an empty meadow, and then he handed it across the plank and looked at each of them twice.

"It's a noon road," he said.

"He's told us."

"Then you know what I know."

They went out by the east road, over the saddle. Beyond the top the country changed, as Seln had said it would: the road shrank to two ruts in a grey scrub that stood as high as a man's belt, and the mule cart, which had looked a toy in the meadow, fitted those ruts as a foot fits a boot. The three boxes rode on the sacks under their own canvas. Karis walked beside the off wheel, where she could have put her hand on them, and never did.

They passed the wash in the middle of the morning. It was dry again. The bank on the western side was still raw where a wagon had been dragged up it, and on the eastern lip stood a thorn stump with the bark worn off it in three bright rings.

Lira looked at the stump as she went by, and smiled with one side of her mouth.

Nobody spoke for the whole of that day, and Cael found that it was no longer anything he did on purpose. Brom carried the bill across his shoulder with the blade out. Lira's knife rode on her hip. At noon they sat in the ruts and ate cold cake, and the carter, who had watched them out of the side of his eye since the saddle, stopped watching.

The mule took to Brom at the first halt. It walked the length of its tether to reach him and laid the whole weight of its long head on his shoulder, and sighed.

"He doesn't do that," said the carter. It was the first thing he had said since the plank.

"They do, with me." Brom stood quite still under it. "I've never known why. I think it's that I'm the same height as a post."

Cael watched the carter at that halt, and at the next, and learned more from his hands than he had from his mouth. The man carried a lump of mutton fat in a rag, and he worked it into both axles every time the cart stood, though neither had begun to complain. His trace chains were bound in strips of old sacking from end to end, so that they could not ring. He had cut the iron heel-plates off his own boots, a long time ago by the look of the leather, and when the mule threw up its head at a bird he laid two fingers on its nose before it could think of making a remark. He had been asked what was on his road, and had declined to say. But everything he owned had been taught not to make a sound, and Cael supposed that was an answer too.

They camped cold, in a hollow, a good way back from a stream. Lira and Seln took the first watch and Brom and Karis the second, and it was Cael's night to be the fifth, which he spent lying on his back, hearing every sound the scrub made and failing to sleep through any of them.

The second day went well until two hours after noon.

They had forded the valley's water twice by then, at shallow places with no name, in good light, and the carter had begun to walk with his shoulders down. Then the off wheel gave a crack like a stick breaking. It ran on three turns more, wobbling, and came off its axle altogether, and the cart sat down on that corner with a jolt that sent Karis's hand to the canvas at last.

The pin had sheared. That was the least of it, for the wheel had dropped onto a stone as it went, and one of the curved timbers of its rim had split along the grain for half its length.

Brom looked at it. Then he looked at the carter, who was looking at the sun.

"How long?" said the carter.

"I don't know yet. Have you wire?"

He had wire. They got a pole under the axle, and Brom lifted, with the cords standing in his neck and no grey in him anywhere, while Seln and Cael built stones up under the bed. He whittled a new pin out of thorn with the hedging bill, and bound the split rim round and round, and twisted the wire tight with the back of the blade. Karis timed him, since nobody had told her not to.

"An hour and four minutes," she said, when the wheel was on. "By the only clock there is."

The carter said nothing at all. He took the mule's head, and they went on faster than before, and the wired wheel ticked at every turn.

---

They came to the mouth of it with the sun a hand's breadth above the ridge behind them.

Cael knew the place before anybody named it. The scrub ended, as though it had been cut with a knife, and the valley drew in between two long shoulders of hill, and from one shoulder to the other the whole floor was grass. It was the pale grass of the lower river, chest high, and with the light coming level along it every blade stood up gold on one side and grey on the other. The ruts went into it and were lost within thirty yards.

The carter had stopped the mule. He stood with his fist in its cheek-strap and looked up the valley, and he spoke for the second time that day.

"That's it. A mile, near enough. The fence stands across the head of it, and you can't see that from here."

"We could camp," said Brom. "Short of it. And go through at noon."

"Nobody camps at the mouth."

"Why not?"

"A man did," said the carter.

He seemed to feel that he had explained the matter, and after a moment Cael found that he agreed.

Seln had been looking at the grass, in sections, from the near ground outward. He turned round, and for once he did not wait for anybody to look at him first.

"I shall say where I want each of you, and I should be obliged if nobody argued until we are at the far end." His voice was hardly louder than the wind. "I go ahead, thirty paces, on the left verge. The boy and Lira walk ten paces in front of the mule, one to a rut, and you do not look at each other; you look outward. The carter keeps his beast's head. Karis is at its off shoulder, where the cart is between her and whatever is nearest. Brom is behind the tailboard."

"Behind," said Brom.

"A thing that follows a cart comes up on its tail. I should like it to find you there."

"And what do we do, if it does?" said Lira.

"What he has paid for. We walk." Seln looked at the carter's fist on the strap. "Everything you carry that has an edge, carry it where it shows. Nothing else. I mean that as exactly as I have ever meant anything."

They went in.

For the first quarter of a mile there was nothing but the noise, and Cael had not known until then how much noise a quiet valley makes. There were frogs. The bottom was wet, somewhere under the grass, and they were in it by the hundred, a creaking that rose and fell like something breathing. There were crickets, nearer, in a dry steady saw, and some small bird that ticked. Over all of it the wind went through a mile of standing grass with a sound like a hand drawn slowly down a coat.

The cart had its own noises, and he learned them, since they were the only ones he could do nothing about. The wired wheel ticked. The grain shifted in its sacks. And on the mule's harness, somewhere behind the saddle-pad, a loose buckle clicked against its own tongue at every step.

He walked in the left rut with his knife on his belt and looked outward, as he had been told. The sun touched the ridge behind him. His shadow went out in front, forty feet long, and Lira's beside it, and then quite suddenly there were no shadows.

A little way past that, on the left, something stood up out of the grass that was not grass. It was a cart-shaft. There was only the one, grey and split, pointing at the sky at the angle of a man's raised arm, and whatever it had once been fastened to lay down in the stems where nobody could see it. The carter did not turn his head as they went by. Karis did, and then looked very steadily at the mule's ears.

Lira put her hand up.

She did not stop walking. She only raised her right hand to the height of her shoulder, with the fingers shut, and held it there. She had spent her life listening to moving air and knew its sounds better than a carter knows his mule.

Cael listened where she was looking.

It took him a dozen strides to hear it, because it was a thing that was not there. Out on the right, forty or fifty yards into the grass, the frogs had stopped. They had not stopped everywhere. They had stopped in one place, a patch no wider than a room, and all round the edge of that place they creaked as before. It was a hole in the noise.

The hole was moving. It went along beside them through the bottom, up the valley, at exactly the pace of a mule.

"Don't declare anything," said Seln.

He had not turned his head. He was thirty paces in front, a dark coat walking the verge, and his voice came back along the ruts as small and plain as a pebble skimmed on water.

"Walk. Same pace."

He meant a Path. He meant Lira's burst, and Brom's Iron Skin, and any point that Karis might set; and Cael knew, with a coldness in his stomach, that he meant nine fragments as well, which were nobody's Path at all.

---

It showed itself at the half mile.

The ground rose a little there, on the right, in a low stony swell where the grass grew thin; and something came up out of the tall stuff onto that rise and went along the top of it, where the last of the light lay.

It was grey. That was the first thing, and for some strides it was the only one, for the grey was the grey of the grass in shadow and the eye kept sliding off. Then it moved against the sky, and he had the shape of it. It was low, and long in the back, and about as high at the shoulder as a calf. It carried its head level and stretched out, like a dog on a scent. It went at a walk, and not one stem of grass moved round its legs.

He measured the distance, since he had no other use for his eyes. He had stood on sixty feet of sprung oak often enough to lay that length down anywhere, and he laid it end to end across the bottom, from his own boot to the rise. Two lengths and a quarter. Forty-five yards.

"I could clear it," said Lira.

She barely breathed it. She was in the right-hand rut, a yard from him, looking straight in front of her, and every line of her was strung as it had been on the gravel three strides short of a mark.

"No," said Seln.

"I know. I'm saying it so I've said it."

She walked on. Her hands were open at her sides, and he saw her set her feet down one after the other with a care that had nothing to do with the ground.

Ten strides later a stone turned under her heel. It was nothing, a loose cobble in the rut, such as any of them had trodden on forty times that day; but her whole weight went sideways, and in the instant before she had her balance he felt the air beside him tighten, as it tightens in a yard before somebody goes. Her hand shot out and shut on his sleeve. She did not go. She hung there for one stride with her fingers twisted in his coat and her teeth showing, and then she had her foot under her, and let go of him, and smoothed the cloth down where she had gripped it as though that had been all she meant to do.

He looked back once, across his shoulder, because he could not help it.

Karis was at the mule's off shoulder with her face as white as her fingers. Her right hand had gone to the breast of her coat, where the notebook was. It stopped there. It came down. He saw it go up again within ten strides, and stop on the same button, and come down; she was a person watching the one thing in a month that she most wanted to write, at the one moment when taking out a pencil might be a thing this country heard. He counted, since counting was what he had. She decided against it five times in that mile.

Behind her the carter walked with his palm spread over the mule's nostrils, and the mule's ears lay back along its neck.

And behind the tailboard came Brom.

Cael had seen him fill a doorway and had seen him fill a district's frame. He had seen him make a harness room feel small by standing up in it. He had never seen this. Brom was walking with the bill held low across his thighs, and his arms were brown to the wrist, and somehow he had drawn in everything about himself that was more than bone and muscle and a coat. He was exactly as large as his body and no larger. It was still a good deal of body, and it was all there was, and it walked at the pace of a mule and looked at nothing.

Cael faced front again, and all nine of them were waiting for him.

Wind had his feet first. They had leaned for a month, a dozen times a day, and they leaned now toward the rise as if the ground sloped that way. Pressure began to gather under his breastbone, slowly, a breath at a time. The Iron read wanted to open and tell him the mass of everything within a pace and a half, which was nothing but a mule; it did not feel like doing anything, and he shut it. Ember offered a point in the dry grass at the foot of the swell, and behind it another and another, so that he could see how the whole slope would take fire along a line. Anchor had found the wired rim and asked about it. Blade wanted Brom's bill. Storm turned over, once, like a great fish under a boat, and showed him the air between himself and the grey thing as a lane already laid.

And it was dusk. Shadow came up behind all the rest, soft, the oldest of his refusals, with the only offer that he had ever truly wanted to take. It could make less of him. He could go up that valley and not be there at all.

*No*, he told it, and Wind, and Pressure. *No. Rim; his; leave it. No.* He said it to each of them at one stride and to each of them again at the next. They were small refusals, one after another, quicker than he could have put names to them, and none of them was any harder than lowering a hand; it was only that there were nine hands, and that they rose again as often as he put them down.

He had done this for two years so that a house on a bluff might keep its charter, and he had hated every Fifth-day of it. He was doing it now so that a mule, and a man with twenty-two years on a road, and four people he would not have traded for a tier, should come to a fence. It was the same act, and it felt entirely different.

---

He had it down to counting by the last quarter mile, and what he counted was the thing on the rise.

That was the method. He had used it on fighters for two years, from the east tier of a yard, and he had never once tried it on an animal; but he could find no reason why it should not hold. One wrote down what could be counted. One gave a name to nothing else.

So, first: it kept its station. He checked that three times. The ruts bent a little to the right, toward the swell, and the grey shape gave ground, drifting up the slope until there were forty-five yards between them again. The ruts bent left, and it came down. Whatever it wanted, it wanted it from exactly that far away, like a watcher who has been told his distance.

Second: it made no sound. He had known that from the grass. Now he saw the other half of it, which was that the frogs went dumb a little way in front of it and took up again a little way behind, so that it walked the whole time inside the hole that Lira had heard. He did not know how frogs had learned to do that. He thought it would take a long time.

The third thing he nearly missed. Its head was not turned toward the people. All the way up the valley it had been turned a little behind him, toward the cart, and when he looked for the reason he heard it: the buckle. It was the one thing in the whole party that kept time. He lifted his left hand and closed the fist, twice, where Brom would see.

There was a pause of four strides. Then the clicking stopped, for a large hand had been reached over the tailboard and shut on a strap.

The grey head swung. It went slowly to one side and then to the other, low, casting, as a dog casts that has lost a line. Brom let go. The buckle clicked, and the head came round and settled on it again.

*It hears. As a dog does.* That was worth having, though not for itself. It could hear a buckle at forty-five yards, and so it had certainly heard a wired wheel, and ten feet walking, and a carter breathing through his teeth. It had heard all of them for half a mile, and it had done nothing. Whatever it was waiting for was not a sound that they were making.

The fourth thing was the gravel.

A gully came down out of the right-hand hill, and at its foot the floods of other years had spread a fan of pale stones clean across the valley, a hundred feet wide, with a skin of water shining on it. The ruts ran straight over. The mule's feet rattled in the wet, and the cart came after with a noise like a sack of nails. Out on the right the grey shape came to the edge of the fan and stopped as if it had met a wall.

It would not put a foot on it. It turned, and for the first time it ran, a long flat lope up the slope and round the head of the gully, where the stones began; he lost it against the hill, and found it, sixty yards off, and seventy. Then it came down the far side at the same lope, and dropped to a walk, and it was forty-five yards from the road again before the tailboard was clear of the wet.

He did not know why. He set that down, in his head, with a ruled line under it, for Karis.

The fifth thing was not a thing it did, and it took him the longest to see.

It had done nothing. It had answered nothing. For a mile it had gone beside six people and a mule like a man kept waiting outside a door. Such a man has been told that a word will be said, and that he is to do nothing until he hears it. They had not said the word. He did not know what the word was. But he had put down nine hands at every stride for a mile, and Lira had held her feet, and Karis her pencil, and Brom had been only a large man with a hedging tool. Not one Path had been declared on that road, and not one fragment had answered; and so the thing had been given nothing, and had reacted to nothing.

There was a light in front of them.

The fence stood up out of the grass all at once, as the carter had said it would: split posts, as high as a man's head, from one shoulder of the valley to the other, with a gate in the middle and a lantern coming toward the gate from inside. He heard a bar drawn.

He looked to the right. The grey shape had stopped.

It had not sat, or turned. It stood in the thin grass with its head level, beside a white stone, and it came no farther. Cael fixed the stone. Then he counted his own strides from abreast of it to the gate, as Karis would have counted, with his lips shut.

A hundred and ten.

Nobody had struck a blow, or so much as drawn a knife. Behind him he heard Brom let out a breath that he seemed to have been keeping since the mouth of the valley. The gate swung inward on a leather hinge, and the lantern came up, and behind it he saw a woman's hand.

# Chapter 6 — Thornwater

The woman who held the lantern kept it low, at her knee, where it would light the ground and not her own eyes.

Cael noticed that before he noticed her. She was a broad grey-braided woman of fifty or sixty with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and she stood in the gap of her gate and counted them through it as a farmer counts sheep at a hurdle: the dark coat first, then the two of them abreast, and the mule, and the cart, and last a very large young man with a hedging bill. She moved her lips for each. When the tailboard was inside she put her shoulder to the gate and shut it, and dropped a bar as thick as his arm.

Then she let out her breath, and so did everybody else.

There was a platform beside the gate, on the inner face of the fence, six feet up, with a ladder to it. A man stood there. He had a bow in his left hand and an arrow on the string, and Cael had seen enough people threaten him to know that this one was not doing it. The bow hung at the length of his arm. The arrow pointed at the planks between his boots. He was looking out over the top of the posts, down the valley, at something a hundred and ten yards off beside a white stone, and he did not once look round at the people who had just come in under him.

Inside the fence there was noise. Cael had forgotten, in a mile, that there could be. A dog was barking at the mule, and a door stood open on a lit room, and somewhere a child was being told for the last time to come in.

The carter took his hand off the mule's nose. He wiped the palm down his breeches, slowly, and turned to the five of them, in the lantern's light, with an expression that Cael could not at first place. It turned out to be respect, on a face that was not much used to showing it.

"First crew I've hired that knew to shut up," he said.

He did not mean their mouths, or not only. Cael had seen him look at Brom's forearms as the tailboard came through the gate, and at Lira's feet.

"What was that?" said Lira.

She had her back against a post of the fence, and her hands had begun to shake now that they were allowed to. She had turned to the woman with the lantern as she would have turned to a referee.

"Stillhound," said the woman.

"A what?"

It was the carter who answered her. He was unbuckling a trace, and he spoke over his shoulder, in the tone of a man who has been asked what rain is.

"It's a stillhound. You shut up. It went home."

He seemed to think that covered it, and he led the mule away toward the barn.

Cael looked at Karis. She had the notebook out at last; it had come out of her coat the moment the bar dropped, as though it had been on a spring. She wrote one word at the head of a clean leaf, and looked at it, and drew a single line beneath it. Then she looked up at the woman with the lantern, and he saw the second question arrive in her face, whole, with its grammar. It was the question of what a thing like that could be waiting for.

She shut her mouth on it. He was close enough to see what that cost her, and it was a good deal more than a pencil had.

The woman had been looking at the cart. She lifted the edge of the small canvas and saw three roped boxes on top of her grain, with a paper tag on each. She let the canvas down. Then she raised the lantern a little, for the first time, and went over the five of them with it, their boots and their coats and the cut of Lira's hair, and stopped at Cael.

"You're paper."

"Yes."

"Hm."

It was the third time he had been asked, in three different ways, and the first time the answer seemed to be put away somewhere for later. She took the slip from the carter's hat, where he had left it on the shaft. She read Pike's hand, slowly, and the five strokes under it, and then she fished a stub of charred stick out of her apron and made her mark on the bark, pressing hard. It was a thorn twig, three spines to a side, with a wavy line under it for water.

"Delivered at my fence," she said. "And nothing lost that I can see. I'll stand to that." She gave the slip back to the cart. "There's a loft over the barn. The grain goes up in the morning, and you can have what room it leaves. Ladder's sound."

"We're obliged," said Brom.

"You're hired. That's different. He'll not go down again till the sun's straight over, and I don't feed people for sitting."

The bowman had not moved. But as Cael watched he eased the string, and took the arrow off it, and slid it back into a case at his hip. Whatever he had been looking at, by a white stone, was not there any longer.

Brom carried the boxes up the ladder by feel, one at a time, and Karis counted them from below in a voice that was not quite steady. The loft smelled of last year's barley. They lay down in it without lighting anything, and it was a long while before anybody slept.

"Five things," said Cael, into the dark. "I counted five. And a number."

"Tell me in order," said Karis. "I'll write them in the morning. I think that if I tried to hold a pencil now I should break it."

By daylight it was a hold like any other, he supposed, though it was the first he had been inside.

The fence closed off the whole head of the valley and ran on round, up one shoulder and down the other, a quarter of a mile about. Within it stood a long house, and the barn with the loft over it, and a yard for carts, and a well with a sweep; there were a dozen people or so, of every age, and a line of geese that hissed at all of them alike. The grain was the point of the place. It came up from the river country in sacks, on one man's cart, and it sat under that roof until other carts came down from holds farther east to fetch it, and the woman with the grey braid stood between the two and saw that nobody was cheated in either direction.

She ran it as Bracken had run a counter. Cael saw that within an hour, and it warmed him to her more than any welcome could have done. She had a tally of the sacks on a board by the barn door, in strokes, and when Brom took the first one up the ladder she drew her charcoal through a stroke without being asked.

There were forty-one sacks, and each weighed about as much as Lira.

Brom took them up one at a time, on his right shoulder, with his left hand for the ladder. It was the tree on the gravel bar over again, with a twelve-foot drop under it and a floor of flagstones, and Cael, who was drawing water at the well, watched every trip from the side of his eye. On the seventeenth, a rung went. It cracked through under the leading boot with a sound like the wheel had made, and the whole of Brom's weight and a sack of barley came down eighteen inches onto the rung beneath.

He did not drop this load, for Lira was standing under him with the next one. Cael had his own breath stopped, and Anchor had already found the ladder's rail and asked whether it should be held.

*Rail. Leave it.*

Brom's left arm took it. The knuckles went white and the shoulder stood out through his shirt, and the ladder bowed like a drawn bow and came back; and when he had found the rung with his foot he went on up, more slowly, and set the sack down in the loft before he looked at his own hands. They were brown, both of them. Iron Skin had not come.

"I didn't have time to choose," he said, at the foot of the ladder. He was a little pale. "It just wasn't there. I think that's the first one I didn't have to say no to."

"What did you have instead?"

"An arm." He flexed it. "It's enough, for a ladder. I'd not have known."

The woman with the braid had seen the whole of it from the barn door. She said nothing to Brom. She went and found a boy, and sent him for a hammer and a billet of ash, and the rung was mended before the eighteenth sack went up.

Lira ran the fence at first light, on the second morning.

She had asked nobody. She went round the inside of the posts, the whole quarter mile, flat out, with the geese scattering, and Cael saw her come down the last straight toward the gate with her head back and her arms going. Three strides short of the bowman's platform she did what she had spent a month on a gravel bar failing to do. She stopped. She did it between one stride and the next, dead, on both feet, with nothing at the end of it but herself, breathing.

The bowman had been watching her come. He was a lean, weathered man of no particular age, who seemed to live on that platform, and Cael had not yet heard him use his voice. He looked down at Lira standing under him with her hands on her knees. Then he lifted two fingers from the stave of his bow, an inch, and laid them back.

Lira saw it. She went red to the ears and ran the whole fence again.

"What does she stop like that for?" said the woman, at Cael's elbow. She had come out with a basin. "Like a cart with its wheels chocked."

"She's learning to. It's harder than going on."

"She was fast enough before she stopped." The woman threw her water at the geese. "Is that a thing they teach, where you're from?"

"No. They teach the other. Every school west of the Line gives you a rank for declaring quicker than the next person, and none of them has a word for this." He watched Lira go round. "We call it the hold. We're having to find it out for ourselves."

She gave him a long look, the same that she had given him over a lantern, and went in.

Karis spent that day and the next with one leaf.

She had made a table of it in her exact hand. At the head stood the word, underlined once, and under the word his five counts in the order he had given them: *keeps station at forty-five yards; makes no sound; follows the buckle with its head; will not cross the gravel fan; answers nothing, having been given nothing.* Beneath those stood the figure, *110*, and after it, in smaller writing, *of C.'s strides, gate to stone.* She had gone out to the well in the heat of noon and paced a hundred and ten of her own along the inside of the fence, which came to a good deal less, so as to know in her legs how far it was.

"It stops short," she said. She was sitting on the top box with the leaf on her knee. "Short of what? I can think of four answers, and I've no way of choosing among them. It may stop short of the fence, or of the people behind the fence. It may be that something lies under the ground at that distance and nowhere else. Or a hundred and ten is simply as near as it ever comes to anything, and the stone is an accident."

"Which do you think?"

"I don't. I've a noun and six numbers." She looked at the page with an exasperation that was very nearly affection. "I haven't a single verb. I don't know one thing that it *does*."

Seln went out by the gate at noon on the forty-fifth day, when the sun stood straight over the valley and there was no shadow under the posts.

He had waited for the hour, and he went without his Path, as he had gone everywhere since a crossroads fire; Shadow was a declaration like any other, and he had told four people to make none. Nobody had given him the reason for the hour, and he had not sought it; but a carter of twenty-two years kept that rule, and so did a hold that had stood behind this fence for longer, and he had not lived to his age by supposing himself wiser than the people of a place. The man on the platform watched him lift the bar. He did not call down. Seln had the feeling of being entered in a column, correctly, by somebody who would not trouble him about it afterward.

He walked to the white stone. It was a hundred and four of his paces; the boy's stride was a little shorter than his own.

The ground beside it was soft, and it had kept everything.

There were four prints, set square, where a thing had stood. Each was as broad as his palm and deeper at the toe than at the heel, and that depth told him how long. A creature that stands a moment leaves a mark like a seal in wax. One that stands until its weight has settled forward leaves a mark like these, and he judged that it had been there from the time a gate opened until the time an arrow went back into its case.

It had not circled or lain down. He could find no place where it had shifted its feet. When it went, it had turned in its own length, and gone back down the valley on the line it had come by, at a walk; he followed that line for two hundred yards with the grass over his head, and the prints lay as evenly as stitches. He cut across to the ruts at three places and paced the gap. It was forty-five yards at each, as nearly as his legs could tell him.

He stood in the grass for some time with that.

He knew what he was looking at, and it was not anything that he knew about animals, of which he knew very little. It was his own trade. A man set to follow another is given his distance. He is told to keep it, and neither close nor fall back; and it is chosen so that the ordinary noise of the person in front will cover him, and so that one particular thing, if it should happen, will reach him across the gap. He does not act until it does. He may walk behind a subject for a year and never be given leave.

Seln had stood at that distance outside a great many doors. He had stood there for two years below a bluff, in effect, with a clerk's pen in his hand.

*Subject animal kept station on the party at forty-five yards for something over half a mile, without approach. Halted one hundred and ten yards from the hold's gate and remained until the gate was barred. Withdrew at a walk by the line of its coming. It was waiting upon a signal. The signal was not given. This officer does not know what the signal is.*

That was the form, and it had made itself without his leave, as it did. He read the last sentence over. He had written *nothing further to report* at the foot of seven hundred returns, and he had never before written that he did not know a thing. It had always been possible to leave such matters out.

There were three ways out of Thornwater. He had known that from the loft door on the first morning, and he had taken them one at a time, at this hour only.

The first was the road they had come by. The second went north from a wicket in the fence, up to grazing, and ended at a stone hut with nobody in it. The third left by a gate on the eastern side and went on up the valley, over a low place in the hills. He had walked two hours along that one, yesterday, with the sun on his head. It was a road like the others. It had ruts, and the ruts had been used, though not lately, and nobody had told him that it was different from the rest and nobody had told him that it was the same. He turned back at the two hours because he had promised himself that he would be within a fence, any fence, well before the light began to go.

He had now eight roads in his head, and the manner of an animal's waiting. It was the fullest return he had ever composed.

He considered, walking back up the ruts to the gate, whether any part of it bore upon the day in hand. That was the rule he had made for himself, and he applied it with care, since he distrusted rules that he found convenient. They were inside a fence. They would go down this valley again behind a cart, at noon, with the sun over them. On that day there was nothing in his eight roads that five people needed in order to put one foot before the other, and so he would keep it, as he had kept the rest.

It would bear, some day. He was nearly sure of that. Somebody would be going to this stretch at the wrong hour and for a reason, and then he would lay the whole of it out on a table without being asked, down to the depth of a toe-print.

In the meantime he walked about with it, and listened for the level voice. *What did you see?* The woman of the hold did not ask it. She had watched him go out at noon and she would watch him come in, and she plainly thought that a man's errands at that stone were his own. The archer did not ask. The boy looked at the dust on his boots each evening, and at the direction it had come from, and wrote down, Seln was fairly certain, only that there had been dust.

He put his hand on the bar of the gate. Above him the man on the platform shifted his weight, and said nothing, and went on looking down the valley.

Seln found that he liked him very much, and went in to carry sacks.

The carter was back on the fourth day, an hour past noon, with the mule in a lather.

He had gone out at noon on the first full day of their stay, with the witnessed slip in his hat and nothing in the cart, sitting on the tailboard as he had not once sat while the grain was on it; and an empty cart behind a rested mule, it seemed, could do in one day what a loaded one did in two. He had brought salt up with him, and a keg of nails, and a bolt of blue cloth that three women came out of the house to look at.

He had also brought a scrap of bark. He took it out of his hat and held it out to Cael between two fingers, as though he were not sure that he ought to be touching it.

"From the one-armed man. He's wrote it in the book, and that's it copied. He says you're to read it, and if it's wrong you can tell him so when you're down, and he'll say it over for the meadow after." The carter rubbed his neck. "He never sends silver up a road. It's in his box."

It was in the square black hand. Cael read it standing in the yard, with the other four at his shoulders.

*Thornwater road, the stretch. Walked ahead of one cart, one carter, one mule. Delivered at the fence. No losses. Nine marks, paid.*

"He's counted the mule," said Brom.

"He would. It was in front of you the whole way."

"I'm not complaining. I'd have counted it myself."

Lira read it twice with her lips moving, and then looked up.

"Is it wrong?"

Cael went through it once more, a word at a time, as he would have gone through an instrument. There had been one cart. They had walked ahead of it. Everything that had gone in at the mouth of that valley had come to a fence. He could not find a word in it that he would have changed, and what struck him harder was that he could not find one that said anything about him at all, beyond what he had done on a particular evening.

"No," he said. "I think it's the first thing anybody ever wrote about me that I'd have put my name to."

"*No losses,*" said Karis. She had it already, on her own leaf. "On the ferry he wrote *nothing lost*. That was casks. Here the noun has changed, and it's because of what there was to lose. He is very exact. I wonder whether he knows how exact he is."

The carter had been looking at his off wheel while they read.

It had held, all the way down the valley and all the way back, and it was an ugly piece of work: a thorn peg for a pin, and a rim wound round with wire like a mended pot. Brom had been looking at it as well. He had looked at it every time he crossed the yard for three days, with the expression of a man who has left a knot tied wrong.

"I'd like to do that properly," he said. "If there's seasoned ash on the place, and an iron pin."

There were both. They did it that afternoon in the cart-yard, the two of them, with the wheel off and laid across a pair of trestles. Brom cut the split timber out of the rim and shaped a new one to the curve with a drawknife, taking a shaving at a time and trying it and taking another, and the carter held what he was told to hold. It took them until the shadows were long. When the wheel was on again Brom spun it, and stood with his head bent, listening; it ran round four full turns and never ticked once.

The carter looked at the wheel for a while. Then he went to the front of his cart, and reached in under the seat board, and came back with a roll of something pale tied round with a thong.

"I don't pay silver for a wheel," he said. "I'd not insult you. That's a thing I've got that you haven't."

It was a calfskin, scraped thin and gone the colour of cream. Karis had it out of Brom's hands before he had finished untying it. She unrolled it on the tailboard, and held the corners down with her spread fingers, and for a moment did not breathe.

It was a map.

It had been drawn in oak-gall ink and gone over, here and there, with a burnt stick. There were roads on it, each a double line, as a wheel sees a road. Three wavy strokes stood for a ford and a square with a dot in it for a hold. A ring with a bar through it lay on a river in the west, and Cael knew the ferry. Between one square and the next ran rows of small ticks, and he had counted the ticks from the ring to a square at the head of a valley before he understood that they were days. Roads went off the hide's edge to the north, and the south, and the east. Wherever a wheel had no business, he had put down nothing at all, and the skin was bare.

"That's twenty-two years," said the carter. He sounded almost embarrassed. "I've the same in my head, so I'll not miss it. You've been going about with a clever girl and three boxes of paper and not a road among you."

"I shall keep it dry," said Karis. Her voice had gone very small. "I shall keep it flat, and dry, and I shall not let anybody put a cup on it."

"It's a hide. You can put what you like on it." He scratched his jaw. "Roads are dry now, right through. That's the season open. There'll be work on every one of those lines from this week till the snow shuts the high ones."

He went to see to his mule.

---

Cael wrote in the loft door that evening, with his legs hanging over the yard and the last of the sun on the page.

He had not written much at Thornwater. The first night he had not trusted his hand, and since then he had drawn water, and carried what Brom could not, and helped Karis to pace out a number along a fence. He was eighteen, and for three days he had been a hired man on a grain hold, and it had suited him better than he would have guessed. But there was a scrap of bark in the pocket of Hesk's book now, where two letters had lain, and it seemed to him that the book ought to know.

*Forty-sixth day past the Line. Thornwater: a grain hold at the head of a valley, two days east of the board. Fourth line, in Pike's hand, copied and brought up by the carter. Nine marks, in his box. Earned, eighteen. In hand tonight, a few coppers and a map.*

*On the forty-third, at dusk, a thing the hold calls a stillhound went beside us for the last half of a mile. Five counts and a distance, given to Karis; she has the name and I have the numbers and neither of us has a reason. Nobody declared anything. Nobody drew. I put all nine down at every stride and they kept getting up.*

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

He stopped there and looked out over the fence.

The carter had said it as he might have said that the hay was in. *That's the season open.* He had meant the roads, and the weather on them, and a board in a meadow with slips enough on it for every crew that could walk. He could not have known what the word had been to the person he said it to.

Cael had lived by seasons since he was fourteen, and he had not owned one of them. The first had been the registry's, a round of assessments in a district office, and he had been carried through it like a parcel that nobody could find the right shelf for. After that it had been a circuit's, and a tournament's, with a bracket drawn by other people. Then it was a house's, an academic year, with a bell in it; and the last had been a seat's, under a hood, fourteen sittings long. Each of them had opened on a day that somebody else appointed. Each had closed with a paper.

This one had opened because the mud had dried.

*The carter says the roads are dry and the season's open: work on every line of his map from now until the snow. I have been in somebody's season since my Kindling. The registry had one, and the circuit, and the house on the bluff, and the seat at Ostrand. I never chose the day any of them began, and each one ended with a ruling about me.*

*This one belongs to a one-armed man's book, and the weather. It has four lines in it. I can read all four.*

*Note the date. Season's open, and for once it isn't theirs.*

He read the last line over, and shut the pencil in the book.

Down in the yard Brom was spinning a wheel again, for the pleasure of hearing nothing, with a row of geese drawn up behind him like a jury. Lira sat on the well's coping, binding the hilt of her knife. On the platform by the gate the bowman stood as he always stood, with his bow at the length of his arm, looking down the valley at a white stone.

Cael looked where he was looking. There was nothing beside the stone, and the light was going. He buckled the book into the satchel and climbed down the ladder, to draw the evening's water before it was dark.

They stayed six days at Thornwater, and went down on the forty-eighth morning in front of an empty cart.

The carter was going back for his next load, and the boxes rode with him again, on a bed of folded sacks, since nobody had been asked to pay for them the first time. He took the stretch at noon. The frogs creaked from one end of the valley to the other and did not stop anywhere.

They came over the saddle above Lowmarch in the afternoon of the forty-ninth day, and he knew before they were off the hill that the meadow had changed.

There were twice the tents, for the dry roads had done it, as the carter had said they would: crews had come in from every quarter while the five were away, and the grass along the river was trodden brown, and three fires burned where there had been one. "Yes."

"Is it wrong?"

"No."

Pike nodded, and turned his shoulder to the meadow, and said it out over the heads of the people at the plank in his level, carrying voice, so that it went as far as the ash.

It was strange to hear. "North road. Four carts of salt and iron walked to the upper holds. Delivered at the last gate. One mule lamed. Fourteen marks."

"Lame when I took him," said the woman.

Cael had been written in a good many books. The man after her was a big one, in a new coat, and he was pleased with himself.

He had brought fleece down from the high fold. Cael remembered the slip, for it had hung on the plank the day they came: eleven bales, at a mark the bale, delivered dry. "Fleece, down from the high fold. Eleven bales carried. Ten delivered dry, and one wet. Ten marks."

"Eleven," said the big man.

"Ten dry."

"Ten," he said.

"I know."

"That's nothing. Anybody can write what somebody said."

*Thornwater ford. Clearance. Forty marks.*

"So you do more, and you come back from it. That's all the sheet is."

"Eleven," he said.

The door of the lower inn faced the river, and the west, and the sun had come down until it stood on the ridge they had crossed forty days ago. *Forty-ninth day. Lowmarch. The Thornwater line was said for the meadow and paid: nine. Earned, eighteen. In hand, about ten, after one jug, which the board bought and Brom allowed. The reserve is not counted here.*

*Thirteenth of thirteen, and Pike gave the reason: it's a good book and a short one. Eleven lines between ours and the top one. The top one is a name, Teague, and it is also on a slip for Thornwater's ford, forty marks, with HOLD beside it. He goes out on the cold road at first light for a week.*

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

He looked at that line, which he had written forty-eight times. Then he looked down the room.

Brom was pouring the last of the jug into five cups, with great fairness, a little at a time. Lira had gone back to the sheet and was standing in front of it with her arms folded and her chin up. Karis was copying a ring with a bar through it onto a leaf of her own. Seln had his eyes shut.

Nobody in the room was watching him. He checked it, as he checked it everywhere, and the count came out at nothing, and for the first time it did not go round again by itself.

He had been turning one sentence over since a frame-house door, and he had not written it, because he had not been sure that it would stand. He had waited to see whether the country would take it back. It had had forty-nine days in which to do so. He put the pencil to the page.

*No instrument out here reads me. No Arbiter station, no assessment panel, no registry seat with a seal on it. The board reads what we did. He read it over, and his hand had been steady.

A shadow came across the page.

Teague counted his three on the sill, before he looked at the room.

He did it without turning his head. He had done it so for longer than the boy had been able to lift a pack, at every door and gate and ford, and he no longer knew that he was doing it until it was done. The spear was on his left, half a pace back, grounded. The bow was on his right and a full pace back, standing on the good leg, as the bow always stood when there had been a day's walking. The boy was between and behind, where he had been told to be, and by the sound of him he was already looking for the table with the food on it.

Three. Whole, and fed, and none of them carrying anything they had not set out with.

That was the first thing, and the second was the job.

It was a week, and he had the whole of it in his head already, a stage at a time, as he would have had a load. Salt went up to the top holds before the frost shut the pass, and it went on mules, and the mules went on a track that was a ledge for two miles of its length with a wind across it. He had done it nine times, and the price was fair. The bow's leg would stand a week of that if he set the pace for the leg and gave some other reason; and the boy would carry more than his share and have to be stopped, and the spear would say nothing at all for seven days and miss nothing.

The third thing was the plank, and he liked it less.

Forty marks, and Thornwater's mark at the foot. He had stood in front of that slip yesterday for longer than he stood in front of most, and then he had asked Pike to hold it. Pike had looked at him in that way of his, twice, and written the word.

Two carts gone, a month back. He knew the stretch, and what that mile asked of a person's hands, and he knew that the ford on that road was a worse place than the grass. It wanted clearing, and it wanted clearing by somebody who would not lose a man doing it; and there were, by his reckoning, two crews on the sheet who could, and the other was up the north road until the new moon.

So it was his, and it must wait a week, since frost would not. He had weighed the one against the other and chosen, and he did not go back over a thing he had weighed. But a held slip was a kind of debt. If that ford took anybody while he was on a ledge with a string of salt, it would be on his name, whether Pike wrote it there or not.

He set it down, as he set down a pack at a door, and looked at the room.

His eye went to the sheet from habit. He did not read the top of it. He knew what was at the top. He read the bottom, where the ink was new, and it was as he had been told at the ferry: Pike had written a list. There were five names on one line. In all the time he had been looking at that sheet there had never been a line on it with more than one.

He had heard their four said, or heard of them. A boat, a fence, the hide-woman's wagon. And today the stretch, walked at dusk, by the carter's own telling, with a hound pacing them for half the mile; and not one of the five had so much as lifted a hand.

That was not nothing. He had known Silvers who could not have done it.

They were at the boards nearest the door, on his right, with a lamp and one jug. He took them as he took ground.

The girl was standing at the sheet, and he read her from the feet. She had her weight on the balls of them, even standing still, even with her arms folded, and her heels hardly marked the rushes. Somebody had spent years teaching those feet to leave a floor in a hurry. They had been taught on a chalked floor, too, with someone watching to give a mark, for she stood as if she were being scored. She was fast, and proud of it, and she had not learned yet that out here nobody was going to tell her so; he thought that it would cost her something before she did.

The big one he did not read at all. He was a fact, like a ford or a gradient, and one did not read a fact so much as allow for it. His hands were the hands of somebody who had lately been on a rope. He was pouring out a jug into five cups as though it were a thing that could be done wrong.

The small pale one had a notebook open on the table, in an inn, among cups, and a skin of some sort pinned under her fingers. As he watched she copied something from the one to the other, and looked at what she had written, and ruled a line beneath it with real satisfaction.

Teague felt the corner of his mouth begin to go, and stopped it. He had seen that look before, on people who kept tallies, and he had always got on with them.

Four.

He stood on the sill and knew that something was wrong with the number before he knew what. The wall said five. He had never miscounted a room in his life, and a man who could not count a table of strangers had no business counting his own on a ledge.

He went over them a second time, slowly, from the left.

There was a man at the end of the bench. He sat a little back from the lamp, in a dark coat, with a strap across his chest and a cup in both hands, and his eyes were shut. Teague's look had gone across him the first time as it would have gone across a patch of plaster. He was the same height sitting as everybody else. He was the same colour as the wall. He had chosen the one place at those boards that the light from the door did not quite reach, and Teague did not believe for a moment that he had chosen it by chance, or that he was asleep.

*That one first*, he thought. He ought always to have been first. The one a man finds on his second pass is the one he should have looked for on the first, and Teague entered it against himself, as a thing to be remembered on a road.

And then there was the fifth, who was looking straight at him.

He was the youngest of them. He had a book in front of him, shut on a pencil, and he sat quite still in the long light from the door with his hands on the cover; and he was not staring, as boys stared who wanted a place in a crew. He was reading. Teague could see the order of it. The boots. The wrist, and the leather on the wrist. The three in the doorway behind, one at a time, left to right. He was going over a stranger as Teague himself went over ground, and he was not troubling to hide it, any more than Teague was.

Teague could not tell what he was.

That was the part worth a price. A Force stood one way and a Wind another, and anybody could tell a Skin by how he came through a door. This one sat like none of them. There was nothing in the set of him to say what he had in his hands, and nothing to say that they were empty.

He had no word to give them. A man did not speak to a bottom line that had cost him nothing. If they were what four lines said, then the board would put them across his road before the snow, and there would be something to say then, and something to price.

He stayed on the sill a breath longer than he needed, all the same, and let himself be read to the end. It seemed only fair.

Cael read him back, a thing at a time.

He was a man of forty or so, of the middle height, heavy in the forearm, with hair cropped close and gone grey over the ears. He had stopped on the sill before he came in. That was the first item, and Cael put the most weight on it. Three people had stopped behind him in the same instant, at the same distances, without a word; and for the length of a breath the man's eyes had gone nowhere at all, while he listened to them be there. He had counted his own before he counted the room.

The right wrist was strapped with a band of leather four fingers wide, worn black, and laced on the inside, where a man can lace a thing himself. Nobody wears that for show. There was an old hurt under it, or one that he expected, or both.

He carried a hand-axe in his belt, over the coat, with the edge forward. His boots had been soled more than once, and the coat had been mended at the shoulder, neatly, by somebody else.

He had read Lira, and it had taken him three breaths. Brom had hardly taken him one. He had looked at Karis ruling her line, and something had happened at the corner of his mouth and been put down again, quickly, like a hand.

Then he had stopped, and Cael had watched him do it. The man's eyes had finished with the table and come to rest, and a small crease had come between them, and they had gone back to the left and begun again. Cael had seen officers, and watchers, and a hooded seat, and a Gold on a stone floor look at the five of them, and every one had counted four. This one had found, on a second pass, a mild man in a dark coat with his eyes shut.

That was the item he could not rule on. He set it apart.

And last the man had come to him, and stayed.

It was not the registry's look, which he knew in his sleep; that one went to the place where a designation should be, and then it went through him. This look stopped at what was there. It took his hands, and the book under them, and how he sat. It was asking the question that a one-armed man asked of every slip, and it was content to wait for the answer.

He did not know what the answer would be. He thought that he would rather be asked it by this man than by most.

Neither of them spoke. The man on the sill moved his head, perhaps the width of a finger, at nothing, and Cael did not nod.

The sun was on the ridge. It came in past the man in the doorway, low and red, round the edges of him and of the three at his back, and went the whole length of the room to the plaster by the hearth. It lay across the sheet there in bars, where the benches and the people broke it.

One word stood at the top, and five names at the bottom, and between them eleven lines of light.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

```
PATH DECLARATION — IRON RANK 3
[Edge Instinct] — Passive. Your reflexes respond to drawn steel within 6 meters before
conscious thought. Movement penalty negated in first exchange of any combat.
```

Declarations are private by default — only the practitioner sees them. Sharing them is possible but considered intimate.

### City Access by Rank

**LOCKED:**

| Rank tier | City district access |
|---|---|
| Unranked / [SHATTERED] | Unranked Districts only; cannot legally enter inner city |
| Copper | Outer districts; limited market access |
| Iron | General city access; guild district access with credentials |
| Bronze | Full city access; administrative district entry |
| Silver | All districts; inter-city travel credentials |
| Gold | Continental access; diplomatic consideration |

## The Guilds Compact

**LOCKED (existence); PROVISIONAL (internal structure)**

The continent's dominant institutional power. Ostensibly a confederation of professional Path guilds — Blade Guild, Storm Guild, Ember Guild, etc. — that standardizes Path training, certification, and inter-city commerce. In practice, the Guilds Compact controls the Compact Registry, sets advancement evaluation standards, and has had quiet administrative authority over the Arbiter system for the past two centuries.

**SECRET:** The Guilds Compact has been falsifying Path classifications for political control since its founding generation. Practitioners who would naturally develop abilities threatening to Compact interests are reclassified into lesser Paths. The falsification is subtle, hard to detect, and has been operating for so long that most current Compact officials believe the system is legitimate.

**SECRET:** The Compact does not know about the Architect. Their control of the Arbiter system is a second-order effect — they learned to manipulate the interface, not the underlying architecture.

### Archmarshal Vastin

**PROVISIONAL (character arc)**

The Compact's senior enforcement officer. Age 51 at Book 4. Silver-tier, Iron Wall Path — exceptional defense and institutional authority. Appears in Book 5 as a legitimate authority figure, becomes the Compact's direct antagonist in Book 6, and by Book 9 has switched sides when he understands what the Architect's will is actually doing.

His arc: the man who enforced a corrupt system for legitimate reasons, and what he does when he understands the system is far more corrupt than he knew.

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## The Arbiter System

**LOCKED (existence); SECRET (true nature)**

Arbiters are experienced as personal spiritual entities — small glowing sigils, unique to each practitioner, that appear at Kindling and remain accessible throughout a practitioner's life for advancement evaluation and Path guidance.

**SECRET:** Arbiters are not spiritual entities. They are interface nodes to the Architect's underlying system — an ancient constructed architecture that pervades Valdris below the level of human perception. The Architect designed and deployed this architecture before recorded history. Every Arbiter in Valdris is a terminal to a single system.

**SECRET:** The Arbiter system has a deliberate flaw: it cannot evaluate [UNBOUND] / [SHATTERED] architecture. The Architect built in an automatic flag and elimination protocol rather than an evaluation pathway — they did not believe a Fractured Path practitioner could survive long enough to require one.

## The Quieting

**PROVISIONAL (mechanism); SECRET (source)**

First observed in Arc 3. Ancient sites in the edge territories where Path abilities cease functioning — where Arbiters go silent, where Path declarations cannot be invoked, where practitioners experience their potential as inaccessible. The Quieting spreads across Arc 4.

**SECRET:** The Quieting is not a natural phenomenon. It is the Architect's preserved will beginning to prime Valdris for a systemic reset — a reversion of the Path architecture to its original design parameters, which would eliminate all current practitioner classifications and rebuild the system from scratch. The Architect's reset protocol treats current practitioners as acceptable collateral.

**SECRET:** The Quieting cannot affect Cael's Fractured Path because the Fractured Path does not run through the Architect's system. It is prior to it.

### Observable mechanics — PROVISIONAL, on the page from Book 7 (Void Roads), 2026-09-04

What the edge territories call *quiet ground* and what Karis names *the Quieting* (B7 Ch15). Everything below is shown, measured or paced in Book 7's prose; nothing about SOURCE is disclosed or theorized there.

- **Perimeter:** a perfect circle. Paced with cord at the Fallow Ring: sixty-two cords of twenty metres and twenty strides — a two-hundred-metre radius (B7 Ch10). Same radius at the Long Stair and the Drowned Hall (the Compact's own survey ledger carries the radius line). **The fourth site is twice the radius — four hundred metres — and cordoned in numbered Compact iron a pace apart; not entered (B7 Ch23).**
- **The line is sharp, not a gradient:** a stride inside, the Arbiter is dark and no declaration renders; a stride outside, it is back — practitioners describe the return as a clock resuming (Lira's clock). Every Arbiter in a party fails identically, at the same stride, one way and back the same, regardless of Path or tier (B7 Ch10–11, Ch18, Ch21).
- **Inside**, a practitioner is exactly what body and training make them: Lira still runs, Brom is still large, Karis knows where a lattice would go and cannot ignite it, Seln is still a professional, a Tide healer can set a bone and cannot mend one.
- **The Fractured Path is unaffected** (LOCKED consequence, shown never explained): all ten fragments function inside; five companions know; the Compact does not (the Stair's rim saw a declaration on the way out, nothing inside — B7 Ch19).
- **Central structures:** fused grey stone floors — circular, level, lipped, seamless, older than any masonry Karis can date — each with a different superstructure: a ring of broken uprights (the Fallow Ring); a stair descending to a ten-stride carved chamber (the Long Stair); a hall half-drowned in a spring (the Drowned Hall); the fourth, larger, standing, unseen whole. Carved lines on every one, copied (52 sheets by book's end) and unread.
- **Sites lie on one alignment.** Three centres to a stride and a fourth near enough sit on a single straightedge; a Compact surveyor's bearing runs the same line ("Three points make a line. Four make an argument." — B7 Ch23). "Someone made this pattern." (Karis, B7 Ch24) — unanswered on the page.

- **Fauna den inside** because prey that walks into quiet ground cannot answer (stillhounds, B7 Ch10–11); the Hall's water holds a thing that hunts what stays (the wold-wyrm, B7 Ch21).
- **Locals** know it as folklore-with-teeth: the void roads skirt it, carters carry crossbows through it, nobody has mapped it.

## The Architect

**LOCKED (existence by Book 8); SECRET (nature and intent until Book 13-14)**

The entity — or long-dead person's preserved will — who designed the Path system. The Architect built the Arbiter architecture, deployed it across Valdris, and has been dormant in it for centuries. The Architect did not die. They converted themselves into the system's deep-layer governance protocol.

**SECRET:** The Architect's motivation was not malevolent. In the era before the Path system, Fractured Path practitioners — [UNBOUND] — were extraordinarily dangerous. Their unlimited potential, without structure or classification, produced catastrophic conflicts. The Architect designed the classification system specifically to prevent Fractured Paths from ever forming again. The system worked. For four centuries.

**SECRET:** The Architect's error was categorical, not motivational. They believed the problem was [UNBOUND] potential. The actual problem was [UNBOUND] potential without any framework for understanding it. The Path system did not solve the problem — it suppressed the symptoms while eliminating anyone who could have addressed the root cause.

**OPEN:** Whether the Architect, confronted by Cael in Books 13-14, is capable of recognizing this distinction.

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture. Where Ember declares ignition and Iron Skin declares density, Tide declares *current* — the perception and redirection of internal energy flow, the practitioner's own and, at contact, another's. Civil face: healing (restoring flow so the body does the rest). Diagnostic technique: **the reading** — contact, both hands, the healer's current run through the patient's architecture ("listening with my hands to how you're built"). Two modes, taught before either is load-bearing (B7 Ch7): the *surface* reading (hands on the hurt, seconds) and the *deep* reading (both hands, whole architecture, minutes, patient still and uninjured, at a mending's cost). Costs and limits: contact range only; healing spends the healer's reserve at roughly the rate of the damage repaired — no crowds, no self-healing; a Tide practitioner **cannot heal what she cannot read**; combat use is a *stall* (a hand on a wrist, a breath, the declaration doesn't render) at brutal cost — Oryn has done it three times in her life. Inside quiet ground: nothing.

**Cael's Tide-adjacent fragment (B7 Ch13):** partial; Iron; self and contact; the first fragment that does nothing in a fight; engagement field *clinical*; **the standing rule, in Oryn's hand and his: ask first, every time.** Consistent with B2's "Tide-adjacent" anomaly label and Book 15's "Oryn's Tide Path burns out."

## Continuity Rules

1. **The Compact Registry is the authority on Path classification.** Any scene involving official rank must be consistent with what the Registry would show for that character at that point in the story.

2. **Path declarations follow standard format.** Only Cael's fragment notices deviate. All other practitioners receive standard declarations.

3. **Tier advancement is not instant.** No character advances a tier in a single scene. Advancement is earned across multiple books for major characters.

4. **The Architect's will is not omniscient.** It can detect [SHATTERED] signatures and issue systemic pressure, but it cannot directly perceive or target individuals until Book 11 when it becomes actively deployed.

5. **The Quieting spreads from ancient sites outward.** It does not appear suddenly in cities. It begins at edge-territory ruins and expands. This gives the companions time to investigate before it becomes a continental crisis.

6. **Cael's ability integration has limits.** He cannot integrate an ability he has not witnessed in use. Seeing a written description does not qualify. The ability must be performed in his presence.

7. **The found family is permanent.** No companion exits the series without narrative justification. The loss in Book 12 is a choice, not a death — the companion is still alive, their relationship with Cael changed.

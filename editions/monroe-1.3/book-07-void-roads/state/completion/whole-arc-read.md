## Verdict

**PASS — RATIFY.** The book holds as a complete novel. It begins by making freedom from institutional measurement feel materially true: the board records deeds, not rank, and Cael can write `I'm free` without irony (ch7:27–103, :269–295). It then makes that freedom morally insufficient. Quiet ground turns his old absence into unique power (ch27:181–239; ch29:243–309), the Ring lets him become the only effective practitioner in the party (ch31:211–289; ch32:139–301), and he uses that advantage to take from Oryn without consent (ch36:65–225; ch37:141–240). The second half does not absolve him. It builds a rule through refusal, explicit permission, repayment and restraint (ch38:103–229; ch47:97–129; ch48:7–105; ch55:221–287; ch56:153–205). The ending therefore earns both claims at once: nobody out here can reduce him to an instrument, and the place where only he works still requires other people, their boundaries and their evidence (ch58:219–229; ch60:31–41, :247–265).

The calendar, deployment count, money, injuries, site geometry, names, knowledge limits, protected wording, Reader Standard and both book seams hold. Movements 3 and 7 earn their excess over the planning guide. The Hall adds a new opponent rule and a new cooperative solution rather than proving the Ring again (ch53:229–339; ch54:91–107, :191–323). The source-distance audit finds no surviving unprotected source-shaped passage. No line-level correction is warranted. The only recommended work is a controlled two-lane same-author texture pass: the book's hand/count/accounting motifs are appropriate, but its retrospective glosses, measured pauses and repeated evaluative looks make the surface more patterned than the events require.

## 3. The consent thread

The thread meets the Reader Standard without qualification. Before the taking, Cael states that Oryn cannot consent, admits that his real motive for silence is fear of her refusal, hears four people distinguish permission to read him from permission to take from her, and writes that he has chosen her cost (ch36:65–225). In the act, he knows that stopping is the right road and reaches anyway (ch37:141–171). The text neither excuses the choice nor disguises it as a misunderstanding.

Oryn then names the wrong precisely: he took her asking, reversed the terms of her table, and cannot appoint his own punishment or forgiveness (ch38:103–229). His new rule is not merely `ask`; Oryn adds purpose, forbidding him to use a willing body just to see whether he can (ch39:281–291; ch44:145–213).

Millrace tests the rule twice. He asks to read the boy, receives `no`, and steps back (ch47:97–129). No sentence attaches forgiveness to that permission.

The debt is paid through three self-readings, telling Oryn first and whole, and asking again before the second outward read (ch55:169–287). Payment is not absolution: Oryn's later viewpoint says she has not forgiven him (ch60:121–125). At Teague's wrist Cael neither reads nor asks, because curiosity is not a sufficient purpose (ch56:153–205). A thirteen-year-old is left in no doubt that the taking was wrong, Cael knew it, Oryn set the terms, and the body-owner rule now governs him. No sentence reads as endorsement, retroactive consent or forgiveness.

## 4. Book-wide repetition — the worst ten

Counts are case-insensitive occurrence pools across the sixty manuscript files. They identify what a human texture pass should inspect, not words to delete indiscriminately.

1. **Hands — 1,508 occurrences (687 in ch1–30; 821 in ch31–60).** Hands are the book's moral and practical image—work, reading, healing, taking, restraint—but they become the default stage business too (ch1:39–67; ch33:91–119; ch48:17–23; ch56:153–205). Preserve bodily-consent and craft uses; thin neutral placement and handling.
2. **Count-family — 608 (279/329).** Counting is character, mechanics and ethics, but almost every kind of attention becomes a count (ch1:5–49; ch32:163–215; ch47:145–153; ch59:191–199). The last third risks making tactical, medical and emotional attention sound alike.
3. **`That was` explanatory codas — 334 (155/179).** The narration often gives an image or statement and then interprets it through `That was...` (ch1:63; ch15:57–71; ch38:223–225; ch55:179). This is the most audible new sentence move across movement boundaries.
4. **Retrospective `as [person] had...` comparisons — 315 (125/190).** Present action is repeatedly pulled back through a prior scene (ch1:123–241; ch33:165; ch54:91–107; ch60:69–83). The callbacks aid continuity but, by the Hall, make new action feel pre-glossed.
5. **Accounting lexicon — enter-family 112, price-family 105, cost/bill-family 147.** The ledger language is the novel's design, yet it spreads beyond logs into ordinary thought and gesture (ch4:91–109, :183; ch34:23; ch46:259–285; ch55:275–287). Keep actual logs, contracts and costs; thin metaphorical bookkeeping where the scene already shows the price.
6. **`Which was` — 115 (46/69).** This clause often adds an immediate interpretive gloss after a concrete fact (ch1:29, :199; ch40:53; ch55:179). It is unobtrusive locally and conspicuous in aggregate.
7. **`As if / as though` — 90 (52/38).** Many comparisons clarify unfamiliar work, but the same hinge also explains faces and silences that could stand unaided (ch2:149–183; ch37:33–61; ch50:293).
8. **`At once` — 89 (43/46).** It is used for recognition, simultaneous Path response, sudden light and immediate speech (ch1:237–247; ch3:229; ch40:123; ch51:41). The physical uses are clear; the speech/realization uses can be varied.
9. **Measured pauses — 89 (42/47).** `For a moment/while`, `after a while`, `for some time`, and breath/count-length pauses give nearly every silence a ruler (ch2:27–29; ch3:91–163; ch35:335; ch59:193). The form suits this book but becomes predictable.
10. **Repeated evaluative looks — 69 (44/25).** Characters look once, twice or again to verify a person, document or count (ch2:149; ch3:253–275; ch11:321–327; ch60:57–69). Pike owns this as a signature; outside Pike and deliberate verification it is over-shared.

The Book 6 watch list is much improved. Exact `squared` occurs twice; quarter-turns do not occur; `for a moment/while` plus `after a while` occurs eleven times; `did not look` four; `nobody said` three; `did not ask` eight; `He found that` four; and the approximate `not X but Y` pool is nineteen. ## 7. Reader Standard

A fluent thirteen-year-old can follow the calendar, site rules, tactical geography and moral argument in one pass. Abstract rules are taught through demonstrations before they matter: the stillhound hypothesis before the ford (ch9:181–317), Path loss before the Ring attack (ch29:3–237), Anchor distance before the Stair release (ch50:261–295), and `hunts what stays` before the Hall rescue (ch53:229–339; ch54:91–107). The Ring's attack remains spatial and choice-driven even at maximum intensity (ch31:211–289; ch32:3–321). No one-pass comprehension failure survives. The densest passage is the five-finding sequence in ch41–43, but Karis's distinction between what is seen and what is feared is clear (ch43:57–111). The consent sequence is clearer still and never asks a young reader to infer whether the taking was wrong (ch36:65–225; ch38:103–229).

# Chapter 7 — Thirteenth

They stayed six days at Thornwater, and went down on the forty-eighth morning in front of an empty cart.

The carter was going back for his next load, and the boxes rode with him again, on a bed of folded sacks, since nobody had been asked to pay for them the first time. He took the stretch at noon. Cael walked the mile of it with the sun on the crown of his head, in the left-hand rut, with his knife on his belt and all nine of his hands held down; and he saw grass, and a split cart-shaft, and a white stone with nothing beside it. The frogs creaked from one end of the valley to the other and did not stop anywhere.

They came over the saddle above Lowmarch in the afternoon of the forty-ninth day, and he knew before they were off the hill that the meadow had changed.

There were twice the tents, for the dry roads had done it, as the carter had said they would: crews had come in from every quarter while the five were away, and the grass along the river was trodden brown, and three fires burned where there had been one. A knot of people stood at the plank's near end, under the long eave, as people stand at a counter when a clerk is giving out results.

Pike had the book open and the stone on it. He looked up as they came along the eave, and looked again, and put his finger on a leaf without having to turn to it.

"You've read it," he said. "He gave it you."

"Yes."

"Is it wrong?"

"No."

Pike nodded, and turned his shoulder to the meadow, and raised his level, carrying voice over the heads of the people at the plank, so that it went as far as the ash. The Thornwater line went out exactly as it had come to them on bark.

It was strange to hear. Cael had read those words in a hold's yard, and thought them his; and now they belonged to a woman with a board of loaves on her head, and a man mending a trace, and an old carter under a tree, who had lifted his chin at the word *stretch* and held it there to the end. Several of the people at the plank turned and looked at the five of them. They looked once, at their boots and their steel, and then went back to their own affairs.

Pike unlocked the iron box and counted nine coins onto the oak, in threes. Karis took them up, since the purse was hers to keep. She did not count them a second time in front of him, which Cael knew she would do later.

Pike did not wait for her to finish. Another slip lay ready under the edge of his stone, where somebody had left it for him earlier in the day. He drew it out with two fingers, and read it to himself, and read it again, and he was giving it to the meadow before the last of their silver was off the oak.

"North road. Four carts of salt and iron walked to the upper holds. Delivered at the last gate. One mule lamed. Fourteen marks."

"Lame when I took him."

It came from behind Cael's shoulder, in no louder a voice than Pike's own. He turned, and found a lean, burnt woman with a strip of red cloth tied round her hair, standing a little way out from the eave with her arms folded. He had taken her for one of the crowd.

Pike looked at her, in his careful double fashion.

"Which foot?"

"Near fore. He was pointing it in the yard before a pack went on. His own carter will tell you."

Pike lifted his head and called a name across the grass, and a man at the second fire stood up with a bowl in his hand. He was asked one question, and he answered it with a nod and sat down again.

Two men and a girl got up off the bank then, where they had been sitting with their packs between their feet, and came to stand at the woman's back. Cael saw that it was a captain who had spoken, and that her crew had left her to say it alone until it was proved.

"One mule lame at taking," said Pike, and dipped his pen.

He wrote it so, and when the ink was on the page he straightened, and turned his shoulder, and said the whole line through once more, from *north road* to *paid*, with the four words changed. Only then did he go to the box.

"Why twice?" said Lira.

She had held it in since the fourteenth day, and Cael supposed that five weeks was a fair trial of anybody. Pike did not seem to mind, and went on counting silver for the woman with the red cloth.

"Once before it's in, so she can say it's wrong. Once after, so it's said."

"Said to who? It's written. It's in your book."

Pike looked past her, down the length of the meadow. There were perhaps two hundred people in it that afternoon, cooking and mending and arguing over mules, and Cael watched him consider them.

"Half this meadow can't read. The book's for me. The saying's for them."

He pushed the last coin across.

Cael had been written in a good many books. There was one in a district office with a single word against his name, and one that a mild man had filled for two years, and one on a shelf somewhere with slips in its pocket that no officer would own; and the thing they had all had in common was that they were shut. People had read them about him in rooms he was not let into, and he had stood on a carved number for fourteen sittings and never once been shown the page.

This book lay open on a plank in the weather, and its keeper stood up and shouted it at anybody who was frying dough.

---

The man after her was a big one, in a new coat, and he was pleased with himself.

He had brought fleece down from the high fold. Cael remembered the slip, for it had hung on the plank the day they came: eleven bales, at a mark the bale, delivered dry. The big man laid it down with a slap. Somebody at the landing had put a row of strokes on it for a witness, eleven of them, and through the last a short bar had been drawn.

Pike read it twice, and then he gave it out.

"Fleece, down from the high fold. Eleven bales carried. Ten delivered dry, and one wet. Ten marks."

"Eleven," said the big man.

"Ten dry."

"It rained on us the last mile. You can't call that the carrying." He had a good loud comfortable voice, and he looked round at the people by the plank as he used it, inviting them in. "Eleven bales went up on my mules and eleven came down to the landing. Eleven bales, eleven marks. That's the slip."

Pike had the pen in his hand. He did not put it down, and he did not dip it. He looked at the big man once, and then a second time, and spoke quite mildly.

"Say it again and I'll write it."

Nobody at the plank moved.

Cael did not understand it for the space of a breath, and then he did, and the hair stood on his arms. It was no threat, for Pike would write it, exactly, as he wrote everything. He would put down that a captain said eleven dry, and beside it that a witness at the landing had marked ten; and then he would stand up and say both to two hundred people, and say them again when the ink was on. It would be in the book for as long as there was a book, and every hirer who ever asked about that crew would be told that its captain had once stood at this plank and argued with a mark.

The big man had got there as well. His eyes went over the faces, and the fires, and came back to the pen.

"Ten," he said.

"Ten delivered dry, and one wet. Ten marks." Pike wrote, and said it over, and paid, and the man in the new coat went off down the meadow with his neck red.

"He'd only have written what was said," Lira whispered. "That's all he was going to do."

"I know."

"That's nothing. Anybody can write what somebody said."

"I've known a great many clerks," said Cael, "and I can think of two who would have."

The plank was nearly full. The dry weather had brought slips in from everywhere, and there was hardly a hand's breadth of bare oak between the posts; he read along it by habit, the bark and the hide and the pictures drawn for people who had no letters. At the right-hand post his eye stopped.

It was a piece of real paper, the first he had seen there since the waybill. There was a mark at its foot that he had watched a woman make with a charred stick, at a gate, by lantern light: a thorn twig, three spines to a side, and a wavy line under it.

*Thornwater ford. Clearance. Forty marks.*

Under that, in the square black hand, in capitals, stood a word that was on no other slip, and after it a name.

*HOLD. Teague.*

"Forty," said Lira, beside him. Her voice cracked on it. "Forty *marks*. That's more than twice everything we've got."

"Clearance of what?" said Brom.

"I know of what," said Karis. "I have the word for it underlined."

Cael said nothing, for he knew the road that ford was on, having walked it twice now, at a mule's pace, with his mouth shut.

"What's HOLD?" said Lira, across the plank.

"It means it's spoken for." Pike did not look up. "He's asked me to keep it by till he's back, and I've told him I would."

"Who has?"

Pike reached out with the feather end of his pen and touched the name.

"Top of the sheet."

"Can anybody ask for that?"

"Anybody can ask." He blotted a line. "I've said yes to one."

"And when's he back? It's only that it says forty."

"He's not gone yet. He goes out at first light, up the cold road, and that's a week's work or near it. Then the ford's his." Pike considered the slip, and something crossed his face that Cael could not read. "It'll keep a week. Carters know the hour for that road."

Lira was frowning at the name, and Cael knew the frown, since he had seen it turned on every card she had ever stood under.

"What is he? He must be a Silver at the least, to have a board keep work waiting for him."

"Couldn't tell you."

"You must know. You know everything about everybody here."

"I know what his paper said the year he walked in. Copper, the same as mine." Pike dipped. "That's a good while back. There's no station nearer than nine days west, and he's never gone to one to be looked at again. By what I've seen him do I'd call him Bronze, if I'd to call him anything. He's not had it written. I've not asked him to."

"But why would anybody not *want* it written?" Lira's hand had gone to the breast of her coat, where a certificate was. "I'm Iron, Rank One. I bled for that. I'd have it on my forehead."

"Good for you," said Pike. He meant it, and it was plain that it settled nothing for him.

"Copper," said Brom. He had not been asked, and he said it as a man lays down a thing he has been carrying since a gate. "For good. There's a finding that says otherwise, on a table, and I'll never sign it."

Pike looked at him twice, with the same look that had followed him down the meadow on the fourteenth day.

"Same as me, then," he said. He set the stone a little higher on the page. "I'll tell you what I tell every one that comes over that hill with a number in his coat. A Bronze who lost my carter is worth less than a Copper who brought him home. Out here that's arithmetic. In there it was heresy."

He wrote for a little, and then, without lifting his head, he said one thing more, and it was to Cael.

"You never told me yours."

"No."

"I never asked."

"No," said Cael. "I noticed that."

---

Brom's rule allowed the jug, and it was Brom who said so.

He had turned it over all the way down from the saddle, Cael thought. The rule was that they ate what the board paid, and the board had that day paid nine marks into Karis's hand, and there was flour in the bag and bacon. He stood in the trodden grass outside the long eave and worked it through with his lips, and then he held up one finger.

"One," he said. "Of whatever's thinnest. And we drink it sitting under that sheet, since she'll give us no peace until we do."

So they went in, for the first time in forty days.

It was a low room, longer than it was wide, with beams gone black and a hearth at the far end that a man could have stood in. Benches ran down both sides, with thirty or forty people on them, by crews, each crew at its own boards, and the noise dropped a little as the five came through the door and then rose again. He was looked at once, and not twice, by people who had heard a line said about him two hours ago and wanted nothing more just yet.

The wall was beside the hearth.

It was one sheet, as Lira had told them: a piece of sized sailcloth as tall as a child, pinned to the plaster at its four corners, and written from top to bottom in the square black hand. He had said that he would read it when there was something on it that he had done. He went and stood in front of it now, with his thumbs in his belt, and began at the top, as he began everything.

*Teague.*

It was the one word, and it had a line to itself. Below it came the rest, twelve of them, one under another, each a name with a number of people after it, or a trade where a name would not serve. He read them all, and they meant nothing to him yet, though one crew, about the middle, seemed to be entered under the name of its mule.

The last line was longer than any of the others. It had five names on it, set down in the order that they had been spoken at a plank, and the ink of it was a little fresher than the rest.

He stood there for some time, since a board in Ardenmere had carried him, once, with a question mark where the letters and the number should have been, and he had felt that question mark between his shoulders in every street of the district. This said only that there were five of them, and where they came in a list of thirteen. He looked for what it thought of him and could not find that it thought anything.

Pike came in at dusk with the book under his arm.

He had a place, on the hearth bench, under the sheet, and nobody was sitting in it. Somebody put a cup in his hand before he was fairly down. Cael watched him drink off half of it, and then, because the thought had been with him since a scrap of bark in a hold's yard, he took his own cup and went over.

"Did you ever hear of a ledger-keeper called Vell?"

Pike thought, and gave it a fair trial, with his eyes on the fire.

"No."

"She keeps a yard in Ardenmere, a fighting yard. She had me in a book before any registry would write a figure against my name, and she never once got me wrong."

"Where's Ardenmere?"

Cael opened his mouth to say, and found that he had no way of telling it from here. It was west, beyond a line and a hill and a river and the whole of a country, at the far end of everything he knew; and the best keeper of a straight book that he had met out here had never so much as heard the town's name.

"A long way," he said.

"Then I'd not have." Pike drank. "If she keeps it straight, it doesn't want me to have heard of her."

Lira had come up on his other side. She had been standing in front of the sheet, as he had, a good deal longer and a good deal less quietly.

"We've four lines," she said. "Not a mark against any of them. That man today lost a bale and argued about it, and he's sixth. I counted. Why are we at the bottom?"

Pike looked up at her from the bench, twice over, and Cael thought that he liked her.

"Four lines. Nobody lost, nobody hurt, nobody knows you." Pike held up his one hand and folded the fingers down as he went. "A boat. A fence. A wagon out of a ditch. A mile behind a mule. I'd hire you tomorrow, myself, for any of the four. But a man comes to me from a hold with something that's eating his stock, and asks who he ought to have. I can't give him a crew that's never yet had anything go wrong." He let the hand fall. "It's a good book. It's a short one."

"So we do more."

"So you do more, and you come back from it. That's all the sheet is."

Brom had not come over, and sat at their own boards with the jug in front of him, looking up the wall with his lips moving. He started at the bottom and went to the top. Then he did it again, from the top down.

"Eleven," he said.

---

Karis moved the jug before she would unroll the hide.

She put it on the bench, a full arm's length from the table, and she wiped the boards with her sleeve; then she laid the calfskin out between them and held its corners down, and all five of them leaned over it. It was the first time Cael had been able to look at it with a lamp.

"There was a slip," said Lira. "On the plank, by the left post, on a shingle. Did you see it? A chest to be carried to the turf fire. *Eleven marks.*" She put her finger on the ring with the bar through it and ran it off the edge of the hide, toward the door. "That's a road we know. We know both fords on it. We know where the bad water is, and which keeper counts hay twice. It's nine days and nobody on it but deer."

"It's eleven marks," said Brom slowly. "That's more than the stretch paid."

"That's what I'm saying."

Seln had been sitting a little back from the lamp, as he sat a little back from anything. He leaned forward now.

"The turf fire is a chartered house," he said. "Its keeper holds a station's brass, and he marks whatever is put in front of him. A chest has a carrier. A carrier has a name, which goes on a sheet, and the sheet goes down a hill in a bag."

He had told them, on a shelf of turf, that he knew a certain phrase, having copied it onto other people's files; and none of them had asked him then what it kept in its drawer. He put one finger down on the hide's western edge, on bare skin, an inch beyond the place where the carter's ruts ran out, and he kept it there.

"West of that, the inventory executes the hour any of us shows paper. Pending jurisdiction means armed. Nobody goes west."

"Nobody wants to," said Brom.

"Nobody can," said Karis. "Different sentences."

She said it in her small dry voice, as she would have corrected a citation. Lira took her finger off the map.

That was all. Nobody at that table spoke of it again, that night or for a long time after. Seln lifted his hand from the edge of the hide. Then he took the skin by two of its corners and turned it on the boards, a half circle, so that the ring with the bar through it lay nearest the door, and everything else that a carter had drawn in twenty-two years ran away from them toward the hearth.

It lay with the east uppermost, and Karis put her fingers back on the corners.

"There are five roads out of that ring," she said, after a moment. "I've counted the ticks on each. Three of them run off the hide before they come to anything."

"I have walked the first part of every one," said Seln, mildly, and picked up his cup.

Cael looked at him. Seln looked into the cup.

---

He wrote the day at the table, while there was light.

The door of the lower inn faced the river, and the west, and the sun had come down until it stood on the ridge they had crossed forty days ago. It came in level across the meadow and through the doorway, the colour of the inside of a peach, and laid a long bright shape down the middle of the room. He turned the book to catch it.

*Forty-ninth day. Lowmarch. The Thornwater line was said for the meadow and paid: nine. Earned, eighteen. In hand, about ten, after one jug, which the board bought and Brom allowed. The reserve is not counted here.*

*Thirteenth of thirteen, and Pike gave the reason: it's a good book and a short one. Eleven lines between ours and the top one. The top one is a name, Teague, and it is also on a slip for Thornwater's ford, forty marks, with HOLD beside it. He goes out on the cold road at first light for a week.*

*Asked Pike whether he had heard of Vell. No. Seln put a finger on the west edge of the map and said what's there. Once. The map is turned east now.*

*Deployment since the Line: none.*

He looked at that line, which he had written forty-eight times. Then he looked down the room.

Brom was pouring the last of the jug into five cups, with great fairness, a little at a time. Lira had gone back to the sheet and was standing in front of it with her arms folded and her chin up. Karis was copying a ring with a bar through it onto a leaf of her own. Seln had his eyes shut.

Nobody in the room was watching him. He checked it, as he checked it everywhere, and the count came out at nothing, and for the first time it did not go round again by itself.

He had been turning one sentence over since a frame-house door, and he had not written it, because he had not been sure that it would stand. He had waited to see whether the country would take it back, and it had had forty-nine days in which to do so. He put the pencil to the page.

*No instrument out here reads me. No Arbiter station, no assessment panel, no registry seat with a seal on it. The board reads what we did. Since my Kindling I've been the case the instruments couldn't parse, and it turns out the cure was a country with no instruments. Write it plainly, because it's true: I'm free. First time.*

He read it over, and his hand had been steady.

There was a thing in the grass on the Thornwater road that had gone beside them for half a mile, waiting to be told something. A whole country kept its Paths in its pockets whenever it set foot on a road, and would not say why. He had those two facts side by side in his head, as Karis had her noun and her six numbers, and he had the strong feeling that they were the two ends of one sentence and that nobody had yet given him the middle.

He did not put that under the entry. It was true as well. It could go on its own leaf, when he knew what it was.

A shadow came across the page.

---

Teague counted his three on the sill, before he looked at the room.

He did it without turning his head. He had done it so for longer than the boy had been able to lift a pack, at every door and gate and ford, and he no longer knew that he was doing it until it was done. The spear was on his left, half a pace back, grounded. The bow was on his right and a full pace back, standing on the good leg, as the bow always stood when there had been a day's walking. The boy was between and behind, where he had been told to be, and by the sound of him he was already looking for the table with the food on it.

Three. Whole, and fed, and none of them carrying anything they had not set out with.

That was the first thing, and the second was the job.

It was a week, and he had the whole of it in his head already, a stage at a time, as he would have had a load. Salt went up to the top holds before the frost shut the pass, and it went on mules, and the mules went on a track that was a ledge for two miles of its length with a wind across it. He had done it nine times, and the price was fair. The bow's leg would stand a week of that if he set the pace for the leg and gave some other reason; and the boy would carry more than his share and have to be stopped, and the spear would say nothing at all for seven days and miss nothing.

The third thing was the plank, and he liked it less.

Forty marks, and Thornwater's mark at the foot. He had stood in front of that slip yesterday for longer than he stood in front of most, and then he had asked Pike to hold it. Pike had looked at him in that way of his, twice, and written the word.

Two carts gone, a month back. He knew the stretch, and what that mile asked of a person's hands, and he knew that the ford on that road was a worse place than the grass. It wanted clearing, and it wanted clearing by somebody who would not lose a man doing it; and there were, by his reckoning, two crews on the sheet who could, and the other was up the north road until the new moon.

So it was his, and it must wait a week, since frost would not. He had weighed the one against the other and chosen, and he did not go back over a thing he had weighed; but a held slip was a kind of debt. If that ford took anybody while he was on a ledge with a string of salt, it would be on his name, whether Pike wrote it there or not.

He set it down, as he set down a pack at a door, and looked at the room.

His eye went to the sheet from habit. He did not read the top of it, since he knew what was at the top; he read the bottom, where the ink was new, and it was as he had been told at the ferry: Pike had written a list. There were five names on one line, and in all the time he had been looking at that sheet there had never been a line on it with more than one.

He had heard their four lines said at the plank, or heard of them: a boat, a fence, the hide-woman's wagon. And today the stretch, walked at dusk, by the carter's own telling, with a hound pacing them for half the mile; and not one of the five had so much as lifted a hand.

That was not nothing. He had known Silvers who could not have done it.

They were at the boards nearest the door, on his right, with a lamp and one jug. He took them as he took ground.

The girl was standing at the sheet, and he read her from the feet. She had her weight on the balls of them, even standing still, even with her arms folded, and her heels hardly marked the rushes. Somebody had spent years teaching those feet to leave a floor in a hurry. They had been taught on a chalked floor, too, with someone watching to give a mark, for she stood as if she were being scored. She was fast, and proud of it, and she had not learned yet that out here nobody was going to tell her so; he thought that it would cost her something before she did.

The big one he did not read at all. He was a fact, like a ford or a gradient, and one did not read a fact so much as allow for it. His hands were the hands of somebody who had lately been on a rope. He was pouring out a jug into five cups as though it were a thing that could be done wrong.

The small pale one had a notebook open on the table, in an inn, among cups, and a skin of some sort pinned under her fingers. As he watched she copied something from the one to the other, and looked at what she had written, and ruled a line beneath it with real satisfaction.

Teague felt the corner of his mouth begin to go, and stopped it. He had seen that look before, on people who kept tallies, and he had always got on with them.

Four.

He stood on the sill and knew that something was wrong with the number before he knew what. The wall said five. He had never miscounted a room in his life, and a man who could not count a table of strangers had no business counting his own on a ledge.

He went over them a second time, slowly, from the left.

There was a man at the end of the bench. He sat a little back from the lamp, in a dark coat, with a strap across his chest and a cup in both hands, and his eyes were shut. Teague's look had gone across him the first time as it would have gone across a patch of plaster. He was the same height sitting as everybody else, and the same colour as the wall. He had chosen the one place at those boards that the light from the door did not quite reach, and Teague did not believe for a moment that he had chosen it by chance, or that he was asleep.

*That one first*, he thought. He ought always to have been first. The one a man finds on his second pass is the one he should have looked for on the first, and Teague entered it against himself, as a thing to be remembered on a road.

And then there was the fifth, who was looking straight at him.

He was the youngest of them. He had a book in front of him, shut on a pencil, and he sat quite still in the long light from the door with his hands on the cover; and he was not staring, as boys stared who wanted a place in a crew. He was reading. Teague could see the order of it. The boots. The wrist, and the leather on the wrist. The three in the doorway behind, one at a time, left to right. He was going over a stranger as Teague himself went over ground, and he was not troubling to hide it, any more than Teague was.

Teague could not tell what he was.

That was the part worth a price. A Force stood one way and a Wind another, and anybody could tell a Skin by how he came through a door. This one sat like none of them. There was nothing in the set of him to say what he had in his hands, and nothing to say that they were empty.

He had no word to give them, for a man did not speak to a bottom line that had cost him nothing. If they were what four lines said, then the board would put them across his road before the snow, and there would be something to say then, and something to price.

He stayed on the sill a breath longer than he needed, all the same, and let himself be read to the end. It seemed only fair.

---

Cael read him back, a thing at a time.

He was a man of forty or so, of the middle height, heavy in the forearm, with hair cropped close and gone grey over the ears. He had stopped on the sill before he came in. That was the first item, and Cael put the most weight on it. Three people had stopped behind him in the same instant, at the same distances, without a word; and for the length of a breath the man's eyes had gone nowhere at all, while he listened to them be there. He had counted his own before he counted the room.

The right wrist was strapped with a band of leather four fingers wide, worn black, and laced on the inside, where a man can lace a thing himself. Nobody wears that for show; there was an old hurt under it, or one that he expected, or both.

He carried a hand-axe in his belt, over the coat, with the edge forward. His boots had been soled more than once, and the coat had been mended at the shoulder, neatly, by somebody else.

He had read Lira, and it had taken him three breaths. Brom had hardly taken him one. He had looked at Karis ruling her line, and something had happened at the corner of his mouth and been put down again, quickly, like a hand.

Then he had stopped, and Cael had watched him do it. The man's eyes had finished with the table and come to rest, and a small crease had come between them, and they had gone back to the left and begun again. Cael had seen officers, and watchers, and a hooded seat, and a Gold on a stone floor look at the five of them, and every one had counted four. This one had found, on a second pass, a mild man in a dark coat with his eyes shut.

That was the item he could not rule on. He set it apart.

And last the man had come to him, and stayed.

It was not the registry's look, which he knew in his sleep; that one went to the place where a designation should be, and then it went through him. This look stopped at what was there: it took his hands, and the book under them, and how he sat. It was asking the question that a one-armed man asked of every slip, and it was content to wait for the answer.

He did not know what the answer would be, but he thought that he would rather be asked it by this man than by most.

Neither of them spoke. The man on the sill moved his head, perhaps the width of a finger, at nothing, and Cael did not nod.

The sun was on the ridge. It came in past the man in the doorway, low and red, round the edges of him and of the three at his back, and went the whole length of the room to the plaster by the hearth. It lay across the sheet there in bars, where the benches and the people broke it.

One word stood at the top, and five names at the bottom, and between them eleven lines of light.

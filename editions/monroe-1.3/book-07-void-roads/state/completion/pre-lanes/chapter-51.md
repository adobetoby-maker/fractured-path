# Chapter 51 — Declaring

He let Shadow go while he lay in the ditch, with his arm still round a bar of the registry's iron.

It went out of him as it had come. The sound of his own breath returned, loud and ragged, and after it the creak of frozen grass under his hip and the grey mule shifting her feet above the stone. He was a boy lying in an old rut at dusk, under the turf's lip, where nobody on that brow could have seen more of him than a shoulder; and whatever they had looked at for the last quarter of an hour, it had not been him.

He drew his arm back. Both his hands were dead to the elbow, with a cold that had nothing to do with the frost, and when he pushed himself up on them it was like leaning on two posts.

He stood.

The six on the rim were where they had been all day: two on the brow above him, two away to the south, one north, and one far off across the low ground against the last of the light. The two on the brow were on their feet; the one with the bow had it up across his chest, spanned, and the other was holding his own hands out before him and looking into them.

Cael stepped out of the rut and walked across the edge, between the old bar and the stone.

He did it slowly, since he wished it seen. He set his boots down on the outside of a line that five people could not cross that morning, and turned his face up the old road to the brow, and stood there with his arms hanging. It was forty paces. In that light he was a dark upright thing against paler grass, and the man above him had hit a shoulder at five paces more.

The bow came round.

He had priced this on the stair. There was one bolt in that groove, and when it was gone, a boot must go into a stirrup and a hook onto a string, and a back must straighten; he had watched that man do it once, from a frozen road, and it had taken him something over twenty counts. He had been told on a bluff never to spend the Wind near a thing whose single blow he could not price. He could price this one to a hair.

He set his feet as Lira had taught him to set them.

*Front. Back. Shoulders.* He loaded nothing on the third. He stood through it, with Wind lying quiet in his soles, and he watched a hooded shape forty paces up a hill. A girl had come down off a gate-walk eight days ago with a sore palm and three words, which a lean man had given her. Shooting was stopping. The stock on the brow settled into a shoulder. The shape behind it grew smaller, as a person does who is letting out his breath. And then it was entirely still.

*Four, and.*

The air cracked like a whip over the stone, and the frost went up off the grass in a white fan. He was twenty feet to the north, in pale stems to the knee, and something had gone through the place where he had been with a sound like cloth tearing. He heard it strike the frozen road, a long way down behind him.

The landing locked.

*One.* Everything that he owned went dumb. For the length of that count there was a boy standing on a hillside who could not have shifted a foot to save his life; and he spent it looking up at the brow, since his eyes were the only part of him that would answer. The man with the bow had not stood to watch his bolt. He had the nose of the thing on the turf already, and his boot going into the stirrup.

Then the count was done, and Cael ran.

He went up the slope on a slant, toward the old road, with the frozen grass breaking under him and forty paces of hill before him. He counted as he ran, and it was the bowman's count that he kept and not his own: at seven the hook was on the string, and at eleven the back was beginning to come up.

At twelve yards short of the brow he reached the lip of the old rut, and he gave up the ground.

It was like shutting an eye. The pace and a half that had been under his soles since a stair went out from under them. He let Storm, the heaviest thing that he owned, have the air instead, and he laid the lane up the rut.

It was a corridor as long as a cart and team, set down in that trough with its high end at his feet and its low end up the hill, and everything in it went one way at once. Frost went, and the seed that hung heavy on the grass at either lip, and chaff, and the dust of a hundred dry days; it went up that channel in a grey flood and burst out over the brow, twelve yards on, into two hoods.

He let it go, and began to pay.

---

For ten counts after a lane, everything that he had came to him a beat late.

He had run up a ditch on the Fallow in that lag, with hounds behind him, and he knew it; but he had not known what it was to close with a man in it. His boots struck the turf, and then he was told that they had struck. The brow came up to meet him. There was a hooded shape in a cloud of chaff with one arm across its face, and it had let the string go half drawn and caught the bow up by its stirrup end.

He saw the stock begin to move. Then he saw where it was, which was a good deal nearer.

It was coming round at his head like a flail. He got his right arm up. It was a dead arm, and it was late, and for the first beat nothing in him answered at all; a pound of ash wood and horn met the bone of his forearm with the whole of a strong man's swing behind it. His knees began to go.

Then Compression, which a fighter had put through him on a circuit, long ago, arrived and did what it was for.

There was only one road open to it. The blow went down the bone into his shoulder, and down his spine, and through both his heels into the hill; and the hill took it. The stock stopped against his arm as it would have stopped against a gatepost. He felt it ring from the wrist to the elbow, and he knew that he would be paying for that for a week.

The hooded man had both his hands stung off the bow. It fell between them.

Cael did not strike him. He could have; the man stood open, a yard off, with his cloth blown half across his hood and grass-seed in the folds of it, and there was nothing in that hood that Cael could have sworn to afterward but that it was the height of a person. He looked at it for the length of one late breath, and turned away from it, to the south.

The other stood two yards off.

He had not run, and he had not reached for anything, since he carried nothing to reach for. He stood with his feet apart on the trodden ruts, and both hands held out before his chest, bare, with the palms turned toward Cael and the fingers a little spread. They were quite still.

Cael knew that stillness. He had knelt under it once in a chalk cut, and it was the stillness of threading a needle. In another breath there would be something set against the front of his coat, and told to stay.

He did not let Anchor look. He had no need to; he had the hands.

They were a man's hands, broad, and short in the finger. That was all that he was ever to know of their owner. There was a pale band round the third finger of the left, where a ring had been taken off before the work began.

His own left hand had found his knife. He could not feel the hilt. He drew it, and held it out at the full length of his arm, with the point a hand's breadth under those two open palms; and he asked Ember for the one thing that it had.

A point of fire stood on the knife's tip.

It was no bigger than a candle's. It lit the palms from beneath, and the grey cuffs, and nothing above them. He held it there. He said no word, and he did not move the blade nearer by the width of a hair.

A person cannot thread a needle with a flame under his hands.

He saw the fingers want to. He saw them hold their stillness for one breath, and for a second, with the heat coming up into them. Then they closed, slowly, and the hands drew back, and went down to the man's sides.

The man took one step backward. He took another. He did not turn his hood away from the flame until he was five yards off; and Cael did not follow him by a single stride.

It was the bowman who ended it. He had got the chaff out of his cloth, and he bent, without haste, and took up the bow from the turf. He did something to the lock of it with his thumb, and a small dark thing like a walnut came away into his palm and went into his coat. He drew a short knife across the string, and then he laid the bow down again in the ruts, carefully, on its side, as a workman lays down a tool that was only lent him.

The two of them went north along the carters' road, side by side, at a trot. They did not run until they were sixty yards off.

Cael turned to the low ground, and lifted the knife over his head.

The lag had gone out of him. He stood on the brow in the last of the dusk, with a point of fire at the end of his arm where anybody on that rim could see it, and he looked for the other four.

To the south there were two shapes still. One of them, the one that had stood all day with its arms folded, raised a single arm above its hood and let it fall. Then both stooped, and straightened, and went away over the rim to the south, with nothing in their hands.

When he looked to the north, there was nobody there at all.

The west was the last. The sky there was green yet, low down, and the rim stood against it as black as a cut edge. A small upright shape had stood on that edge since first light and had never once loosed. It bent now, and laid something down. It walked away; and at the top of the rim, where another step would have taken it out of his sight, it stopped, and turned itself round, and looked back across five hundred yards of grass at a boy with a light.

He saw the hood against the sky. There was the shape of it, and the shoulders under it, and inside it there was nothing whatever. Then that was gone too.

Six. He counted them off that rim as Seln had counted them onto it. He let the fire go out, and walked down forty paces to the grey stone, and called one word down the old road into the dark.

"Back."

---

They came up out of the low ground in file, as they had gone down.

He heard them long before he saw them. There was no need for quiet now. Brom's breathing came first, heavy and careful, and then a shuffle of boots on frozen turf; and at last five shapes grew out of the dusk on the old road, with a small one in front that made no sound at all.

Seln stepped over the stone, and stood a breath on the outside of it. Cael saw his shoulders come down by the width of a finger, as a man's do when a Path that he has done without all day is handed back to him.

Karis was next, and she had said that she would never grow used to it. She crossed, and stopped, and put out her right hand in front of her in the dark. A point of light stood on the air above her palm, as small and steady as a star.

"I have a hand," she said, to nobody. Her voice shook. "I have a hand again."

She set the point on the wick of her lamp.

The healer came over between two of them, with Brom's good arm across her shoulders and his weight on her; and Cael watched her do what he had seen her do at a rock on the Fallow, eight times in one grey morning, when she thought that nobody saw. She took one stride past the stone. She stopped dead, with her eyes shut and her lips moving, and her free hand closed on Brom's wrist.

The count went to four, and on, and did not stop.

"There. Back," she said. It came out of her on one breath, hoarse, as a person speaks who has been holding it since the morning. She opened her eyes and looked at her own left hand in the lamplight, back and palm. "The clock started again."

Then she was a healer.

"Down. Here, on the blanket, on your left side. You, bring that light and hold it where I point. You, the pannier, the one that's on the grey." She had Brom on the turf before any of them had moved. "And somebody give me a knife that'll cut ash."

It was Seln's. She had him kneel behind Brom's shoulder and cut through the shaft below the iron head, a notch at a time, while she held the wood still in both her fists so that it should not turn. It took as long as it takes to say a line over twice; then the head came away, and she threw it on the grass, and put one hand flat on the front of the shoulder and took hold of the feathered end with the other.

"Breathe out," she said. "All of it. I'll not lie to you; you'll know that it's leaving."

She drew the shaft back out the way that it had come, in one pull.

Brom made no sound that could be heard at ten yards. His boots moved on the turf. Her two hands were round the shoulder before the wood was clear, one above the place and one below; her eyes shut; and Cael, who had sat all day in a stone room and heard her count that man's heart with two fingers, saw her lips reach three and stop counting, since she had what she wanted.

It was an hour, by the stars. Lira held the lamp for all of it with her one hand, sitting on the grey stone, and did not once let the light shake.

"That's one," said Oryn at the end of it. She sat back on her heels. Her face was the colour of the frost. "It's shut. It went through the meat and missed everything that matters, and you were born lucky. You'll carry nothing on that side till I've said so."

"I've another side," said Brom.

"I've seen it. Be quiet." She turned on her knees. "You. Give me that arm, and give me the lamp to somebody else."

Lira gave the lamp to Karis. She held out the sling without a word, and the healer undid her own knot, and unwound a strip of Cael's shirt that had gone stiff and dark, and put her two hands round the forearm.

It took a quarter of an hour. When she had finished she did not let go of the wrist at once.

"It held a stair. It's not going to hold anything else this season."

"It did hold it," said Lira. Her voice was small. "I'd have gone down the other twenty on my head."

"I know that it held. I felt where. I'm not scolding the arm." Oryn drew the wool up and tied it, shorter than before, so that the hand lay high against the collar-bone. "That's two, on a road, after three at a bench yesterday. There isn't a third in me tonight for anybody, and I'd thank you all to stay whole till morning."

---

He paid the rest of his own bill sitting against the old iron, with his knees drawn up.

It came all at once, as it had come at the stone on the Fallow. Across his left palm there was a white weal as long as a finger. He had held a knife with a flame on its point in a hand too cold to know that the hilt was hot, and he had found it out only when he tried to open that hand again. He could not stop shaking. He had had a week of barley sacks and tables to rest in, and he had spent the whole of what it gave him between a stair and a brow, in less time than a kettle takes.

The healer looked at the palm from her stride and a half.

"I can't touch that. You know why." She threw him what was left of Lira's tape. "Wind it round, and keep it out of the frost. It'll close faster than it ought to. Yours always do."

Seln had gone to the mules.

He went round the three boxes under their blanket with the lamp held low, and stooped over each cord in turn, and touched nothing. When he came back his face was as it had been all day.

"I tied those to be read. There is a stem of grass laid under every knot, and each is where I laid it." He set the lamp down. "Six people with a quarter of a mile of rim apiece do not leave it to look into a mule's load."

"What did they see?" said Cael. "Tell me as you'd write it for them."

"A fence that failed at dusk, at its knot, which was set on an iron bar that stands upon the very edge of the place; and directly afterward a boy, who stood up out of the old road beside that bar and declared." Seln folded his hands. "Nobody on that rim saw anything between the stair and the bar. I asked Lira what she saw from the thirty-fourth step, and she says that she saw a stair. They will write that he crept up a rut in bad light while they were watching a hole, and that a knot tied a hand's breadth from such an edge is a knot which nobody should have trusted. A man who lays fences for his living would far sooner put that on paper than the other thing, and he has no cause to think of the other thing at all. It holds."

He turned, and went up the old road to the brow by himself.

When he came down he had a crossbow in his hands.

He brought it into the lamplight and turned it over, slowly, as he turned over any paper. It was a good bow, heavier than the healer's, with a prod of horn and sinew, and its string hung cut from one end. He ran his thumb along the underside of the stock, and under the stirrup, and across the butt.

"There was a maker's stamp here. It has been taken off with a file, and the place oiled. There is no armoury number. It was bought for one day, by somebody who meant from the first to leave it lying." His voice had not risen. Cael had never heard it so quiet. "I walked past the place where that man lay, twice. I have sat in a hole since sunrise with his bolt on the stair. I have his bow. And I could not tell you tonight whether he was tall."

He took the thing by its two ends.

Cael had known him for two years. He had seen him carve a goat, and wax a seal, and stand in a doorway with his back to a room, and he had never seen him do a thing in anger. Seln lifted the crossbow over his head and brought it down across the grey stone with the whole of his narrow strength, and the stock broke in two at the lock. He stood over it, breathing.

"No faces."

He laid the two pieces on the turf, side by side, and went and sat down by the mules.

Cael opened Hesk's book on his knee. Lira had put it into his lap when she came over the stone, with the strap done up, and had said nothing; and he held the pencil in his bandaged left hand, since the right would not close.

*Hundred and fourteenth day, night. Outside the stone. All six out. Brom: a bolt through the right shoulder, drawn and mended, an hour. Lira: the arm opened on the stair wall and is shut again, a quarter. Thirteen bolts loosed at us in the day, and one hit. Ours: five, and none shot.*

He ruled a line, and put down what he had spent, one thing to a line, as he had on the Fallow.

*Anchor: looking, at the two bars, first light. I saw the fence go up. I stood two counts to see it, and Brom had those two counts with a bolt in him.*

*Shadow: once, from the fourth step to the old bar. Lira saw it go, from below. Seln turned his back. Three were round the door. The six were two hundred and sixty yards off, with a lip between; not one had a line to me. Let go in the rut. It holds. Dead to the elbows; I had to watch my own hand.*

*The read: the ground, from the hole to the bar. All my eyes. I looked up once, and it left me.*

*Anchor: looking, for the knot. I can't find what that costs. Entered as not found.*

*Anchor: the unbinding. Hand on it, from inside. Once.*

*Wind: one burst, off the stone. The lock.*

*Storm: one lane, up the old rut. Ten counts late.*

*Compression: in those ten. The right arm.*

*Ember: one point, on the knife. The left palm.*

He counted them with the pencil's end, and there were nine.

*Nine. With the reading on the road, ten since Thornwater. Deployment since the Line: twenty-three.*

He looked at that for a little. Then he wrote the rest beneath, as he had it.

*Second nameless team. Same hand. I own a piece of the first one and I used it on the second. The account's still unpayable. It's getting longer.*

---

The light came down the old road when it was full dark.

It was a lantern, and it was carried high, at the length of somebody's arm, as a person carries one who wishes it understood from a long way off that he is not creeping. Seln was on his feet before Cael had seen it. Lira had the bow across her knee.

"Four," said Seln, presently. "One with a spear. One who favours a leg."

They came over the brow in file, a crew's length apart, and the man in front stopped where the ruts turned, beside a thing that lay on the turf there. He held the lantern over it. Then he came on down, alone, and the three behind him stood where they were.

He was of the middle height, and heavy in the forearm, and there was a band of black leather laced on his right wrist. He stopped at the edge of their lamplight, and Cael saw his eyes go round it once, slowly. They went to a big man lying on his left side under a healer's coat, and to a girl whose sling was tied a hand higher than it had been at a certain door. They went to two pieces of a crossbow lying side by side by a grey stone. They came to Cael, and to his hands.

"You're late back," said Teague.

"We were kept."

"I saw some of it. We were on the ridge when the light went." He set the lantern down on the turf between them. "I'll say what I saw, so that you've no need to wonder what I'm carrying. There's a line down there. I've walked round it for years, and I know where it is by the ruts. I saw a man come over it from the inside."

He paused. He was choosing the next thing as he chose everything, by weight.

"You walked out of the Quiet declaring," he said.

"Yes."

Teague's eyes did not leave him, and Cael waited for the rest of it: how, and with what, and what a boy was who had a Wind's crack in him and a fire as well. He watched the man weigh each of those and set it down again, as he had set down a question at an inn's door two nights before.

"Then you're not on the board anymore. You're something the board doesn't have a column for."

It was the second time in three days that Cael had been told so. A one-armed man had said it of a line in a book. This man said it of him, to his face, across a lantern, with no heat in it and no fear; he might have been reading out the weight of a load.

"I've three with me, and a light," said Teague. "They'll want that rim walked before any of you sleeps. I'll have it walked."

He took up his lantern, and went back up the old road to where his three were standing.

---

It had been the hundred and thirteenth morning, a day and a half before, when Teague took a slip that he did not want.

He had been at the plank before the mist was off the river. The five had gone by it in the dark, two hours earlier, and he had heard the mule. Pike was setting out his book; and there was a shingle on the oak, six along from the left-hand post, which had hung there for nine days because no crew of any standing would trouble with it.

*Millrace. Three stakes of the weir, gone in the last high water. To be set before the ground shuts. Four marks.*

Teague drew its tack and held it out.

Pike looked at the shingle, and at him, and at the shingle again.

"That's a boy's job."

"It's on my road."

"You've no road south. You've forty marks of fence east, with my capitals on it, and you've a day's posts still to set."

"Then it'll want its capitals a day or two longer."

Pike said nothing more. He wrote the name. But he kept his one hand on the shingle for a breath after the ink was dry, and Teague knew that he had been read; and he found, somewhat to his surprise, that he did not mind it.

He had told a boy at a door that his crew would be at Millrace in two days, and it had been true when he said it only because he had decided, between one sentence and the next, to make it true. He had been turning that over ever since. A man does not walk up to another crew and hand it the hair on his own neck.

But he could be half a day from where a certain paper pointed, with a spear and a bow and a boy, on honest business. Nobody could say that it rested on nothing, since it rested on three stakes and four marks.

They walked at noon, and slept at the fork above the mill, without a fire, a crew's length apart; and before he lay down he counted his three by their breathing, as he did every night of his life. The bow's was the short one, since that hip had not liked the ridge.

It was the boy who asked, at that fork, before the blankets were down. He had a heel of bread half way to his mouth, and he stopped it there, as he stopped eating whenever his captain was about to speak.

"Is it the five? That we're up here for?"

"It's a weir. You heard the slip said."

"I heard it. I never knew you take four marks."

Teague looked at him for some time. He had made it a rule, nine cold terms ago, that he would not walk three people up a road for a reason that he was ashamed to give them; and the rule held on a ridge at night as it held at a plank.

"There's a paper that I hung back on a nail," he said. "There's a crew that took it down. I think that it's a bad paper, and I can't tell you what's bad in it. I said to them that if they were late, we'd come and look."

"Look for what?" said the bow. He was easing his leg out along the turf, a little at a time.

"I'll know when I see it."

"That's not a price."

"No," said Teague. "It isn't. I've none to give you. If any one of you would sooner set stakes tomorrow and go home, he can say so now, and I'll think no worse of him."

The spear said nothing, which was what he said to most things. The bow grunted, and went on easing his leg. The boy put the bread into his mouth.

They were at the weir by first light on the hundred and fourteenth, and the woman who kept the ford told him the news before he had asked for it. Five had come off the ridge the day before, at noon, with a mule; and the healer had gone up again with them, and had taken the mill's jenny, and had left word with a carter for a child at some gate to the north.

"She's not one to leave her bench for a walk," said the woman. "Thirty rounds I've counted her through this ford. She never once went up that ridge."

He set two stakes with the spear and the boy, in water to the thigh, while the bow sat on the bank and sharpened the third. He did the sum as he drove them: it was an afternoon up to that place, by the ruts, and a morning to go in and bring a case out, if it was a walk and a carry, so that they would be down at the ford again by the middle of the day.

The middle of the day came. He stood in the mill water with a maul in his hands and looked up at the notch in the ridge, and nobody came through it.

He had not said how late, and he saw that now: he had said *if you're late back*, as though late were a thing that would announce itself.

He gave it one hour more by the shadow of the wheel.

Then he laid the maul down on the bank, beside the third stake.

"That's not set," said the boy.

"No. It'll cost us the four marks; she'll not put her mark to two stakes of three." He was wringing out his breeches. "Get your bread. We're going up."

Not one of the three asked him why. The spear took his spear. The bow got up off the bank, on the good leg first, and slung his stave; and Teague, who had been about to tell him that he might stay with the mill and the mules, saw his face and held his tongue.

---

They were on the ridge at dusk, on that same day, half a mile short of where the road tips over, when he saw it.

There was a light first. It was small, and low down, where the dark ground was; a point of fire that did not flicker as a brand flickers. Before he had fairly taken it in, there was a sound that he knew as well as his own name, since he had walked eleven cold terms beside a Wind, long ago, in another crew. That crack was a Wind's.

He put up his hand, and his three stopped behind him.

Then there was a noise in the low ground that he did not know at all. It was like a squall caught in a ditch. Something pale went up the slope in a column, where the old road ran, and burst.

And then the light again, in the same place. It was held still. It was held up, by one arm, as high as that arm would go.

He stood on the ridge with the wind in his face and counted what he had. A Wind was one thing. An Ember, who could make fire stand on the air, was another, and he had worked beside two such; and whatever had gone up that ditch was a third. He had seen all three within the space of forty breaths, at one spot, and when he looked for the people who had done them he could find only one upright shape, very small, with its arm raised.

He was too old to tell himself that he had miscounted.

There were others moving. He saw them along the far edge of the low ground, against what was left of the sky: two together, going away south, and one to the west. They did not go as beaten men go; they went as people go who have practised leaving. He had seen a crew break like that only once, and he had not liked that crew.

"We'll not run in on it," he said. "Give them the time to be gone. Then we light the lantern, and we carry it where it shows."

He read the ground by that lantern as they came down, since it was the thing that he could do.

On the brow, where the ruts turned, two people had lain all day, and the frost showed him the two shapes, two yards apart, each a man's length. There was no ash of any fire, and nobody had dropped a crumb there, or a thread, or the paring of a nail. Beside the northern shape he found the round print of a stirrup pressed into the turf again and again in the one place. He counted, and there were eleven prints, and a twelfth half made.

That was a crossbow. He had expected a bow of some kind. He had not expected that every sign of its owner would stop at the height of a boot.

He said his piece to the boy at the stone, and kept it short: that he had seen a man walk out of the Quiet and declare. He did not say three, since he could not price three, and he put no question about what else there was. A captain who asks a question that he has no column for has only given himself something to carry, and he had three to carry already.

He sent the spear south-about by the ruts with the lantern, and the boy with him. They were gone the better part of two hours. They came back by the north, having walked the whole of the ruts, with a crossbow each and a third across the boy's shoulders.

"Lying down," said the spear. He was not a man of many words. "On their sides. String cut. Nothing in the lock."

"And nothing else?"

"Nothing. I looked."

The bow had the three of them across his knee before they were fairly on the grass. He turned each to the lantern, and tried each empty lock with his thumb, and shook his head over them like a man at a horse fair.

"Three makers, and not a name left on one of them." He looked up. "They're a deal better than mine."

Teague did not answer him. He was looking down the old road, to where a lamp burned beside a grey stone, and a boy sat against a bar of iron with a book on his knee.

He had a rank that he had never sent west. He had earned it on a ledge in the cold, where there was no station to see it, and had let the registry go on writing the old one; it had seemed to him then that what a man had done was worth more than what was put down about him.

So he knew a little about being something that a book had no line for. It was a very little, for he had kept back one tier, and that boy had no tier to keep back. Whatever he was, it was not written anywhere at all.

It made Teague uneasy, and he did not pretend otherwise. But six people had lain on this rim with their faces covered and their bows unmarked, and had shot a man from behind; and one boy had stood up in front of them with a light held where everybody could see it. He knew which of those two things he would sooner have on a road beside his three.

He did not sleep much. A while before the light he got up and woke them.

Nobody had asked it of him. The boy at the stone had asked him for nothing, either at an inn's door or across a lantern; he had only said *noted*, and *yes*.

But a crew that has been shot at from four sides through the whole of a day will keep looking up for a month afterward, and Teague had seen what that does to people. If there was a thing to be fetched out of that hole, they would fetch it better without having to watch the sky.

He put the spear on the southern rim, where two had stood, and sent the boy west, since the boy had the legs for it. The bow he set on the north, which was the shortest walk, and he took the brow himself.

When the sun came up over the ridge behind him, there were four on the rim, and every one had his face bare.

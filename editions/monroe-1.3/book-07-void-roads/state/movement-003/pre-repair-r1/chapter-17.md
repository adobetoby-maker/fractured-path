# Chapter 17 — Thirty Head

There were thirty of them, and he counted twice to be sure.

Twenty-six were full grown. Four were half the size, with plates of a paler grey that had not yet gathered any green, and those four kept to the inside of the shelf, against the hill. Each of the grown animals was as long as the cart and a good deal broader. It stood low on four legs like the stumps of trees, and its back and both flanks were roofed over with horn, in slabs that overlapped as slates overlap on a house, the largest along the spine and each with its keel. Lichen grew on the old ones in rings. When one of them stood still, which was nearly always, it was a heap of the mountain's own stone, and his eye slid off it.

The head was small for such a body, and carried at the height of a man's knee. It ended in something like the beak of a tortoise, and with that the animal scraped the rock. He could hear it, now that he knew to listen: a dry patient rasping, thirty times over, like a yard full of masons dressing stone a long way off. Under that there was another sound, whenever one of them shifted its weight, for the plates moved on each other with a low clatter, as a slate roof settles when the sun goes off it.

"They're eating the hill," said Lira, close to his ear.

"They're eating what grows on it," said Karis, who had come out from the buttress on her toes. "Look at the rock behind them. It's a different colour where they've been."

It was. Along the upper shelf, east of the last animal, the stone was scoured pale in a band as wide as the shelf itself, and ahead of the first it was dark and furred. They were mowing it.

He looked for the leader, and he did it without thinking, as he had done it at a ford with the light going. A pack had a first mover, and a crew had a captain, and every yard he had ever sat above had held one fighter round whom the rest arranged themselves. He went along the line from the east end to the west for the animal that the others watched.

There was none. No head was turned toward any other. Each of the thirty faced the rock in front of its own beak, and the gaps between them were so nearly equal that he could have laid Karis's cord in any one of them and found the same knot in the next.

"We go under them," said Brom, and it was not quite a question.

"The shelf does," said Cael. "For the next ten hundreds, by the look of it, and it's the only shelf."

"He said never below."

"He said also that the salt crews do it twice a year, and walk." Seln had appeared at the cart's tail, as he did. "I have been as far as the fifteenth of your hundreds this morning, and they did not lift a head for me. I should add that I made no request of them."

Cael weighed it. Thirty animals stood three hundred and fifty yards up a slope of loose plates, and the five of them had a cart, and a rule in a one-armed man's words. To go back was to go down the fan, and to go on was to do for one afternoon what carters did every year.

"We go on," he said, "at a walk. Lira runs the hundreds as before, but she runs them under the wall, and Karis measures. Nobody calls a figure across the shelf; if you want me to hear a number, you come and say it in my ear."

They did twelve more lines that day, and it was the quietest work he had ever done in company.

He paced, and wrote. Karis laid her brass on the rock and bent over her thread, and came to him with each figure as a clerk brings a paper to a desk. Brom leaned into his rope on the up-slope side, with his eyes on the cart's near wheel and never once on the hill, like a man passing a dog that he has been told will bite if it is stared at. The dry hub ticked at every turn, and up on the face the rasping went on without a break. Each time he raised his eyes from the page the grey heaps were where they had been.

At the twentieth hundred he looked up and found them behind his left shoulder, and he had not seen one of them take a step.

---

Seln had found the second overhang, and he had found it directly beneath the middle of the herd.

"It is the only one in the next mile," he said, before anybody could remark on it. "I looked for another, and I should have preferred another."

It was deeper than the first, a long low cave of a place where a bed of softer stone had weathered back, and from its mouth the whole line of the upper shelf could be seen against the evening sky. He had swept it, and laid his ring of stones, and put nothing in the ring.

Karis stood looking at the ring for some time.

"I want a fire," she said.

Lira turned round. Brom had been lowering the tail-board with both hands under it, and he stopped with it halfway.

"Hear me first. The country's rule has three things in it, and Pike's *loud* has the same three, since it's the same rule. For the hounds we found out which one mattered, and it cost four of us a month. I don't know which matters to those." She tipped her head at the roof, and at what was above it. "I shall have to find out, because the last column of his table is mine, and a carter who comes over this bench with a Force at his tail-board ought to be told what he may do with him. Of the three, there is one that I can test this evening and then take back."

"Fire," said Cael.

"It's struck with a flint. It will be the size of my two hands, under a roof of rock, at three hundred and fifty yards; and if one head comes up, Seln puts his coat over it, and we've lost a coat." She folded her arms, carefully, on account of her palms. "A Path I can't take back, and a shout I'd sooner not try on purpose. But I should like to cross one of the three off tonight, while it's cheap."

"And if it isn't cheap?" said Brom. "If it's the one?"

"Then we learn it with a fire that a coat can put out, and not next week with one we need."

Cael had the ruling, and he did not hurry it. He had lifted the rule from tinderboxes once before, above a wash, on the strength of a page that had been tested at a ford; and this was tested by nobody. He turned her reasoning over for the place where it would break, as she had once asked four people to do in a loft, and what he found was that she had already chosen the one test whose failure they could best afford.

"Hands," he said.

There were five. Brom's came up last, and he set the tail-board down on its chains first, so gently that no link spoke.

Seln struck the flint, and Lira lay on her stomach at the mouth of the overhang, where the roof stopped, and watched the upper shelf with her chin on her fists, since her eyes were the best of the five. The tinder caught. A thread of smoke went up and flattened under the rock and crept out along it into the dusk, and when the first twigs had taken there was light on all their faces.

"Well?" said Karis. She had Hesk's clock open on her knee.

"Nothing." Lira kept her head where it was. "They're scraping. The third from this end has lain down; it folded up its front legs first, like a cow."

"Did it look this way?"

"It looked at its own feet."

They gave it a quarter of an hour by the clock, and then another, with the fire built up to the size of a hat, and thirty beaks rasped on in the dark overhead. Karis shut the clock.

"One," she said, and wrote it. "Fire: lit and fed for half an hour, in plain sight, at three hundred and fifty yards, and not a head." She looked up. "I shan't call it struck off for good. I call it struck off at that distance, and at that size. But I'm a good deal warmer than I was."

"Twenty-two years," said Brom, to the flames, in a fair imitation of a carter, and Lira laughed into her sleeve.

They ate hot food for the first time since the wash. Afterward Karis sat with her notebook shut on her finger, looking at the black line of the upper shelf, and Cael knew what she was going to ask before she drew the breath for it.

"I want tomorrow, all of it. We do no hundreds, and I sit on a rock in front of this place with the clock and look at them from first light until I can't see." She turned to him. "It will cost a day's lines, and I know what that is in flour."

"It isn't a day off the map," he said. "It's the map. The column says whether it takes a cart, and why, and those are part of why."

"That's what I'd have argued."

"Then I've saved you a sentence."

"I had four ready." She opened the notebook, and her eyes had the brightness in them that he had seen over a charcoal river. "I shall want a second pair of eyes, since I can't watch both ends of a line three hundred yards long; and a hand, for when mine stops."

---

She took her seat at first light, on a slab ten yards out from the overhang's mouth, with her knees drawn up and the clock beside her on a folded stocking.

Cael sat on her left with the notebook, and Lira lay on the rock at her right, on her stomach again. Brom stayed under the roof with the cart and the boxes, mending a strap, and Seln had gone on westward before any of them woke. There was ice on the slab. The sun had not yet reached into the gorge, and thirty heaps of grey stone lay along the upper shelf with frost on their backs.

"The east end is mine," said Karis, "and you have the west. Lira takes whichever of them does something. Nobody is to tell me what he thinks it means; I want what it did, and when."

The first thing she had from them was the spacing, and she had it within the hour.

"Count the gaps, in lengths. I've no means of taping them from here."

He counted. Between the westernmost animal and the next there was room for one of them and half another, and between that one and the third it was the same, and so along the line as far as he could see it clearly. Where a half-grown calf stood inside its dam the two of them made one place, and the gap was kept from the pair.

"A length and a half," he said. "Everywhere."

"I make it the same. Watch the sixth from your end; it's about to come forward, for it's scraped out what's in front of it."

The sixth moved up a pace. It did so with great deliberation, one thick leg at a time, and a breath afterward the seventh did the same, and the eighth after that. He watched the gap close and open again down eleven animals, like a pulse going along a rope, and when it had passed, every gap was a length and a half once more.

"They don't look at each other to do that," said Lira. "Their heads are down, and they can't see past their own beaks."

"Write that she said so," said Karis, "and the time."

The second thing took her until the sun was on the shelf. High on the face, a little west of the leading animal, a vein of white stone ran down the slope as broad as a door, and at the first hour by the clock the leader's beak had been a cart's length short of it. She had him enter that. At the second hour the white vein was behind the leader's tail, and the whole line, thirty animals and all their gaps, lay forty yards farther along the mountain than it had.

"Did you see one of them walk?" she said.

He thought about it honestly.

"I saw the sixth come up a pace, and the ones behind it, and twice more I saw a leg lift. I never saw the line go anywhere."

"Nor I, and I watched one animal for a count of three hundred without blinking more than I could help. It took four steps in that time, each of them while it was scraping." She rubbed her eyes with the back of her wrist. "They move as the hand of this clock moves. One looks away, and it's later."

The third thing came down on them out of the sky at about noon.

The sun had been on the crags above the upper shelf for an hour, and whatever the frost had been holding there it let go. He heard a crack, a long way up, and then a hollow knocking that grew; and a rock the size of a keg came down the face in great slow bounds, turning over in the air, and went straight through the line.

It passed between the ninth animal and the tenth. It struck their own shelf between them with a sound like a mallet on a cask, and burst, and the larger half of it leaped out again and went on down the slope toward the bench, so that he felt the blow come up through the slab under him. The piece crossed the lower shelf two hundred yards west of where they sat, taking a spray of plates with it, and dropped into the gorge. A good while afterward the far wall gave the whole of it back, the crack and the knocking and the burst.

Karis had not flinched, and her eyes had never left the line.

"Lira."

"Two of them moved," said Lira, and her voice shook a little. "The ninth and the tenth. Each one took a step sideways, away from where it went through. The ninth's gone back to scraping already, and the tenth's looking at the ground."

"The rest?"

"Not a head, and that went by as close to them as I am to you."

"A step, then, from the two it nearly hit, and a step isn't flight." Karis let out her breath. "Give me the notebook; I want to put this one in my own hand." She took it on her knee and wrote slowly, with her lips tight. "*Sound: a rock the size of a keg, striking the shelf between two animals, loud enough to be returned by the gorge. Felt in the slab at three hundred and fifty yards. Two animals moved one step each. No other response.*"

"That's two of the three," said Cael.

"That's two." She gave him back the book, and flexed her fingers. "And it's the two I could have guessed, since the carter lit his fire at the wash and nothing came. I'm only being told what I knew."

---

The fourth thing came when the sun had gone well round into the west, and it was over in about the time of a breath.

He had the west end, and he had it faithfully. His eyes were on the last animal of the line, a big one with a broken plate on its near shoulder, which stood a little beyond a rib of the hill, so that from where he sat he could see it and it could not have seen its own fellows at the eastern end. He had been counting the slabs along its spine to keep himself awake, and he had got to seven.

It stepped uphill.

It did so suddenly, and nothing on that shelf had moved so quickly in two days of his watching. Its off foreleg came up off the shelf and went down on the slope above, and the hind leg followed, and then both again, three steps, with a clatter of horn that reached him a moment later. Beside it the next did the same, and the next. He swept his eyes east along the shelf, and there was not one grey back in the whole line that was where it had been; all thirty had gone up the hill by the length of their own bodies, the calves scrambling inside them, and stood now on the open slope with their heads lifted.

Then he heard it. It was a long grinding rush, like a load of slates shot off a cart, and it came from the east end.

"There," said Lira, who was up on her elbows. "Under the last one, at this end. Look."

A piece of the upper shelf had gone. He could see the scar, pale and sharp-edged, where a slab as big as a barn door had been lying along the lip, and the slab itself was sliding still, far down the face, breaking as it went. The easternmost animal stood above the place with its hindquarters low and its legs spread wide. It had been standing on the thing when it went.

He turned that over while the last of the plates ran out below. He had been watching one animal, on purpose, at the far end of a line three hundred yards long; and he had not seen the cause of what it did, and it had not seen the cause either, and it had moved all the same.

"What did you see?" said Karis. Her eyes were still on the hill, and her voice was very quiet, and it had something in it that made the hair lift on his arms. "Each of you, in order. Cael."

"The west end went up, three steps. I was counting plates on the last animal, and it moved before I heard anything at all."

"Before."

"I'd swear to it. I heard its own horn first, and then the slide."

"It can't see this end," said Lira. "There's a piece of hill in the way. I've been looking at that rib all day."

"Lira. What did you see?"

"The plate tipped, and I saw the edge of it lift. The one on it went down behind, as if somebody had kicked its legs away, and it scrabbled." She swallowed. "And they all went up. Karis, they all went up at once. Not one of them turned to see what had happened; they were already going."

"Which went first?"

"None of them."

"There must have been—"

"There wasn't. I've got good eyes, and I've watched a starting line since I was small. I know what it looks like when one goes and the rest follow, because you can see it run along the row, and this didn't run along." Lira sat up. "It was like one animal thirty carts long."

Karis looked down at the clock on its stocking.

"I saw the plate tip, and I began to count when it tipped. I saw them step in the same instant, both ends, as near as I can judge; and I had said *one*, and begun to say *two*, before the noise of it got to me here." She laid one finger on the glass. "It had three hundred and fifty yards to come to us. It had the best part of that again to go along the shelf to his animal, round a rib. His animal ought to have heard it a count after this one did, and it moved with the rest."

Up on the slope the thirty stood a little longer with their heads high. Then one beak went down to the rock, and another. They did not go back to the shelf they had left; they had begun to scrape where they stood, a body's length above it, and already the gaps between them were closing and opening, settling toward a length and a half.

---

She had Lira's copy of the finding out of her coat before the last head was down.

It was the leaf with the struck dot on it. She laid it on her left knee, and the new page on her right, and sat between the two of them with a pencil in her fist.

"I'm going to say it aloud, and I want it broken if it can be. Brom, come out here, and bring your objections."

Brom came out from under the roof and crouched, enormous, at the edge of the slab.

"At the ford, seven heads turned at once to a point the size of a seed. Something reached all seven together, across a river, and it wasn't the light, for six of them never turned for a hammer on steel." She touched the left-hand leaf. "Then they did a second thing. They waited for one of themselves to walk, and they followed it, *toward*. That's clause four: no leader, only whichever goes first."

She moved her finger to the other knee.

"Here, something reached thirty together. It wasn't sight, for there was a rib of the hill between and their heads were down. It wasn't sound, for the far end moved before the sound could have got to it, and a rock made a greater noise at noon and shifted two."

"The ground," said Brom. "That slab going. They'd feel it in their feet, the same as I would."

"I thought of the ground. The rock at noon struck their own shelf hard enough for me to feel it here, through this." She rapped the slab with her knuckle. "Two of them stepped. So it isn't a shake either."

"Then what did the one on the plate do, that a rock didn't?"

"It was frightened," said Lira.

She had said it slowly, and she was looking at her own right foot.

"It nearly went over. You could see it. Everything in it was trying to be somewhere else."

"Yes." Karis's eyes rested on her, and then on Brom. "I have watched Iron Skin start at your wrists when a rope snatched, and go down again, and you told me in a loft that something leaves you when it comes. A burst went off by itself on a gravel bar, in our first week, because a foot slipped."

"They've no Paths," said Brom. "They're cattle with roofs on."

"I don't say that they have. I say that I know of one thing in this country that reaches every animal of a kind in the same instant, through rock, and it's the thing those hounds turned to. I'm not entitled to invent a second until I've used up the first." She drew a line across the new page. "Whatever a frightened shale-back gives off, the herd has a sense for it, and that's the same sense, or its cousin. Now look what they did with it."

"They went away from it," said Cael.

"Uphill, and all together, with no first animal to wait for. The hounds go to the thing, and these go from it." She wrote four words and underlined them. "*Same sense. Opposite answer.* And there is Pike's word to lay beside it, which is *loud*; and when we asked a ford what a carter meant by that, the answer was a Path."

She wrote for some minutes, and when she read it out it was shorter than he had expected.

"*Shale-back. Seventy-fifth day. One: it does not answer fire at three hundred and fifty yards, nor sound, nor a blow on its own shelf. Two: it answers something given off by one of its own in fright, at once, the whole herd together, by moving away. Three: by that, and by the carters' rule, it will answer a declaration in the same way, and a declaration is a great deal larger than a frightened animal.*" She stopped. "*Derived. Not tested.*"

"Why not tested?" said Brom. "You watched it."

"I watched them do it to each other. I haven't seen one of them answer a Path, since nobody has declared on this mountain, and I'm thankful."

"You could test it small," said Lira. "One of your points, a seed, for half a breath, as you did on the bar. That moved seven dogs."

Karis lifted her eyes to the slope. Thirty animals, each the weight of a loaded cart, stood on loose plates above a shelf that had dropped a piece of itself an hour ago under one of them; and below them there was a roof-pitch of the same stone, and at the bottom of that a bench, and five people.

"On a slope, flight is a landslide with legs."

She said it without any weight, as she would have read out a width.

"A frightened calf moved them three steps, and that was enough to finish what was left of that plate. If I'm right, I find out with the hill coming down on the cart. If they don't stir, I've learned nothing, since a seed might only have been too small; there is no size of that test that's worth its answer." She turned the pencil over. "So it stays derived, and I write that word on it, and anybody who reads it knows exactly how far to trust me."

Cael looked up at the grey heaps on the mountain, and he saw what the page meant for him.

At the ford he had found a seam. He had sat on a gravel bar with four hurt people and gone over what he had counted, and there had been a first mover to give the pack, and a place to put a knife behind it. There was no seam in this. Nothing on that hill went first or could be led, and there was no cut dog; if those thirty ever ran, there would be no opponent to read, only a slope, and the single question of where a person ought to be standing.

"Write it in yours, too. I'd like it in two hands."

It was Karis, and he looked round at her.

"The other one I gave you because I couldn't hold a pencil. This I can write myself, and I have. But it's derived, and a derived thing in one hand is an opinion." She was looking at the leaf, and not at Hesk's book, which she had never read. "You had the far end of the line. If you'll put down what you saw there, in your own words, then it's two people's."

He took out the book and wrote it, with the day at the head: the plates he had been counting, and the big animal stepping before any sound had reached it, and that he had looked for one that went first and found none. He wrote her three clauses under that, and her two words under the clauses. It was the first time that anyone had asked him to keep a finding because his hand would make it worth more, and he wrote it as small and as straight as he could.

---

Seln came back at dusk, and they had a fire again, since one of the three had been paid for.

He sat down a little behind the rest and held out his hands to it, and he let a full minute go by before he spoke, which was his way of saying that the thing bore.

"The shelf narrows," he said. "I have been within sight of the end of it. For the next eight of your hundreds it is much as you have had it. After that there are places where the hill comes down to the edge and leaves less room, and I counted eight of them. The eighth I did not like."

"What's wrong with it?" said Brom.

"I should prefer that you looked at it with your own eyes, since it is a question about a load. I say only that it is wide enough." He turned his hands over. "Beyond the eighth there is a bay, with water, and beyond the bay a ledge, and at the end of the ledge the mountain stops. One sees a valley from there, and a road in it."

"Oxhollow's side," said Karis.

"I take it so."

Lira had been lying on her back with her right foot propped on a box. She sat up.

"How wide's the ledge?"

"Six feet, in places. It is level, and it is rock. I did not go out on it." He considered her. "I thought that it was yours."

She lay down again without answering, and there was the beginning of a smile about her mouth.

Brom had said nothing since the slab. He had been working something out, with his big hands hanging between his knees, and he brought it up now as he brought up a stone from a well, slowly and whole.

"So it needn't be on purpose," he said.

They looked at him.

"If she's right. A rope snatches, and the Skin starts before I've told it. Or somebody's foot goes." He kept his eyes on his own hands. "It only has to be a fright."

"It hasn't gone off since that first week," said Lira, to the roof. "Not once. I'd a dog at my knee, and it didn't."

"No," said Karis.

"I'm only saying."

"I know what you're saying. I've written that as well." Karis had the new leaf on her knee still, and she turned it to the firelight. "It's the reason I asked for two hands. If the page is right, then for as long as we are under them the hold isn't a discipline any more. It's the floor we're standing on."

The fire settled. Overhead, thirty beaks went on scraping at the dark.

"I'll run under the wall from now on," said Lira. "The whole way, where there's most rock under me."

# Chapter 15 — Ground

The paper was on the oak before the mist had left the river, and Lira read it with one boot on and the other in her hand.

She had gone up to the eave on purpose to be first. Cael came after her at his own pace, which on level grass was anybody's pace now, and he took his inventory on the way, as he took it at the start of anything. The calf was a leg again on the flat; he had asked it the evening before, on the saddle road, and it had told him nine strides uphill and then shut. Brom's forearm was closed along the whole of its seam and wrapped by Brom's own choice, and it was to pull nothing. Lira had wound a strip of linen round her right ankle, over the stocking, and she walked on the whole sole of that foot. Karis had two pink lines across each palm and held a pencil for a quarter of an hour at a time. The nine fragments lay in him as they had lain since the ford, idle, each with its rent paid.

There were fifty-two marks in the purse, and the name at the top of the sheet was still up the north water on two holds' slips.

"It's a mountain," said Lira.

She did not turn round to say it. She stood before the left-hand post with her head a little on one side, reading a second time, and her lips moved at the figure.

"It isn't a ford at all. The ford's only where they pay you." She put her boot on at last, hopping. "He let me say I'd had enough of fords, and he knew what was on it the whole time."

"I told you to read it first," said Pike.

He had come out with the book under his arm and his coat on, and he set the flat stone on the page before he so much as glanced at the new slip. He had read it by lamplight when the carter brought it, and had no need to look.

It was real paper, which was rare on that plank, and the hand was a careful one that did not write often. Beside the words somebody had drawn a horned head, broad between the horns, over three wavy strokes. Cael knew the strokes. A carter had drawn every ford on a calfskin with those three lines, and Karis had them by heart.

*The high traverse, Oxhollow to Thornwater. To be walked with a cart, and mapped. Paid at Oxhollow, on the map: seventy marks.*

Pike read it out for the meadow, and then he read it again from the first word, with his shoulder turned to the tents. There were not many to hear. The dry roads had emptied the grass to seven fires, and at most of those people were still asleep.

"Seventy," said Brom. He had come up behind them without a sound, as a large man can who has spent sixteen days with nothing in his hands. "That's more than the six lines we've got. All of them put together."

"By eight," said Karis.

"What's a high traverse?" said Lira. "In a carter's mouth. I know what it is on a hill."

"It's the same thing with a wheel on it." Pike laid his one hand on the paper. "Oxhollow's six days north-west of this plank, and Thornwater's two days east of it. A cart from the one that wants grain from the other comes round by my ferry, and it's eight days, and eight home. There's high ground between the two holds. The salt crews say there's a shelf along the top of it, a bench, with a gorge down one side. A mule string went over it once, before I'd this book, and came down in three days." He looked at the horned head, twice. "Somebody's told the ford so. The ford wants to know whether it's true before it sends a wheel."

"The ford," said Lira.

"Oxhollow's a ford with a fence round it. The keeper takes a copper a wheel, the same as my ferryman, and it's the keeper's paper. He'd sooner his wheels came back."

"And he pays at his end," said Karis. "He's left nothing in your box."

"It's six days of road, and he'd not trust seventy marks to the inside of a carter's hat. I'd not, either. That's what *on the map* means. You put the map in his hand, and he puts seventy in yours, and his mark goes on that paper under my strokes. Then you bring the paper here, and I say it."

Karis had her notebook out, and her pencil was already turning in her fingers.

"What does he call a map?" she said. "I've a registry sheet in the middle box with the whole continent on it, printed, and a calfskin that a carter drew for his own wheels. They aren't the same kind of thing. Which does he want?"

"He wants to know where a wheel goes and where it doesn't. He's a ford-keeper. He'll have no more letters than he needs to count a toll." Pike thought about it. "Draw it so a man can follow it with his finger. Put the numbers where he can find them if he's a mind to."

"A strip, then, with the shelf drawn down the middle of it, and a table beside. A line to every stretch." She was writing as she spoke. "I can rule that tonight."

Cael read the slip through once more, for the thing that was not in it. There was no name on it but the hold's. Nobody had asked to have it kept.

---

He went along the whole plank afterward, from the left-hand post to the right, as he had gone along it on the ninth day and on the forty-ninth.

There were fourteen slips. He gave each the same attention, the bark and the hide and the two shingles, and he sorted them as he read, as he had once sorted a board in Ardenmere by what each card would cost him. There was a fence for somebody on the north water, at five marks. A string of mules wanted walking to the turf fire, westward, and that one he passed over without stopping. A carter on the south road wanted a wheel fetched. There was hay to be got in at the upper inn, for a mark and the hay-loft to sleep in.

When he had done, the paper with the horned head was at the top of the heap in his mind, and it had got there so quickly that he distrusted it. He made himself say why, in items, as he would have made a stranger say it.

The first was the plainest: it paid. Seventy marks was more than the book had ever counted out to them, and the crew was still four hurts short of whole.

The second was that nobody's finger was on it. The last large slip they took had carried a captain's name in capitals. A one-armed man had struck that name with his own pen before a meadow, and they had gone down to a river under the weight of it. This one had been posted for anyone at all. If they took it they would take it off nobody, and the first line on the sheet could read it when he came down and find nothing in it that was his.

He was longest over the third item, since it was not a reason of the same kind.

It was *ground*. Nothing in those three lines of careful writing had teeth. He looked for the opponent in it, from a habit as old as his reading, and found a shelf, and a gorge, and a question about a wheel. Somebody wanted a mountain looked at closely and written down, with numbers, by people who would say what they had counted and no more. The paper named no tier and no rank. It asked for a cart and a count.

He had done that for nothing since before he could remember. He had catalogued a harness room and a painted floor and sixty feet of sprung oak, and two years of other people's fights from the east tier of a yard; and all of it had been held against him at one time or another, as the habit of a person who watched because he could do nothing else. Now a stranger with a ford was offering seventy marks for it.

It was work, besides, that four half-mended people could do. A man with a bitten calf could pace a level shelf and write. A girl with a stiff ankle could tell him whether a hundred yards of it would bear her, and a researcher who could hold a pencil for a quarter of an hour had a cord, and a clock, and a head that kept numbers as a jar keeps water. Nobody would have to be fast, or hard, or to set anything alight; the job asked for no Path from any of them, and for no fragment of his.

He wanted that job more than he had wanted the forty. It surprised him, and he entered it as a want, since Hesk had taught him that a figure left out of a sum did not thereby cease to be owed.

"Does it need the cart?" he said. "We've none. We could walk your bench and measure it. She has a cord, and I can pace a yard."

Pike considered him in his double fashion.

"You want to tell a ford it holds a cart, you'd better have had one on it."

He said it level, and then, because he said everything of weight twice, he gave it again a little differently.

"A cord'll tell him how wide it is. It'll not tell him what a wheel does on it. The paper says *with a cart*, and he'll ask you at his gate where yours is, and I'd have the answer ready."

"What's up there?" said Lira. "Besides a shelf."

"Weather. It's cold on that top a month before it's cold here." Pike dipped his pen. "And shale-backs. The salt crews see them from the cold road, across the valley, on the high ledges. Bigger than a cart, and they eat moss."

"Do they eat anything else?"

"Not that I've heard."

"Then why did you say it like that?"

"I said it as I say everything." But he had not, quite, and Cael saw Seln's mild eyes rest on the bald brown head for a breath longer than the answer needed.

"Hands," said Cael.

Lira's went up, and Brom's, and Karis's with the pencil in it. Seln lifted two fingers from his folded arms. Cael put up his own and took the paper off its tack, and Pike wrote five short strokes at its foot, side by side, and said the whole of it over to the grass with *taken* at the end.

---

The other four went down the meadow to find the smith, and Seln did not go with them.

Cael looked back from the ash. The eave was sixty yards behind him, and under it two men stood at the far end of the plank, where the oak ran out past the right-hand post. One of them had a book under his arm. The other had his hands clasped behind him and his head bent, as a clerk stands who has come to ask for the loan of a file. He was talking, low, and Pike was listening with his chin down.

It was the first question that Cael had ever seen Seln put to anybody in that meadow.

He knew what it would be. A word had been dropped at that plank a quarter of an hour ago, in a voice that was not quite Pike's ordinary one, and Seln collected such things as other men collected debts. He would be asking what a shale-back was, and who had been under one, and what had become of them, and he would get it in fewer sentences than anybody else could have.

Cael could have walked back, and nobody would have thought it strange. He was the nearest thing that five people had to a captain, and there was a mountain in it, and he wished very much to know.

He had told that man at that same plank, ten days ago, that nobody was coming to take his report off him. It had been easy to say, and he had learned since that the saying was the smallest part of it. The promise had to be kept at exactly such moments as this one, when the report was sixty yards off and plainly worth having, and when nobody but himself would ever know that he had turned his back on it.

He turned his back on it, and went after the others.

The smith was at his door with a rasp. He was the same square silent man who had bought a cart from them in their first week, and he heard Brom through to the end without putting the rasp down. Then he walked round the corner of his shed, and they followed him.

It stood under a lean-to roof with its shafts on the ground.

"That's ours," said Lira.

"It was," said Brom.

Cael knew every board of it. They had loaded it in a yard on a bluff with a coach standing by, and it had gone under a gate and down a road and over a ridge, and its bed had carried three boxes and a locked case under one canvas for the whole width of a province. The smith had done what he said he would. The axle had been cut and the hubs set in, so that the wheels now stood a hand's breadth inside the line of the bed on either side. The cart looked narrower in the hips, and a little surprised.

Brom went all round it slowly, as round a horse he had sold and now met on a road. He set his right hand on the off wheel and rocked it, and stooped to look along the new axle-tree, and lifted the tail-board on its two chains and let it down again without a sound.

"He's done it well," he said. "The iron's good. He's kept my seat board."

"Six," said the smith.

It was the first word he had spoken.

"A day?" said Karis.

"Against its coming home." He jerked his head at the shafts. "You lay six on my bench and take her. When she's back under this roof, that's the hire, and we're square. If she's not back, it's six more, and I'll want to hear how."

"Twelve, for a cart with a cut axle," said Lira.

"I gave you more than twelve for her."

"You did," said Brom. "It's fair. It's cheap." He glanced at Cael, and Cael nodded, and Karis wrote it.

There was the matter of the boxes, and it was settled before anybody had the chance to argue it. The ferryman's boy had come up from the round shed with a message and stood twisting his cap. The hay was coming in from the river meadows that week, he said, against the cold, and his master wanted the whole loft over the drum, every foot of it, and was sorry.

"It was dry room while we were near," said Karis. "That was the bargain, and he's kept it. We shan't be near. It's a day and a half to the foot of this, and then a mountain, and six days home from the other side." She had gone a little pale. "I can't leave them three weeks in a meadow under a net."

"Thornwater has a loft," said Lira.

"Thornwater's loft is full of the east carts' grain, and it's half a day past the place where we turn off."

"Then they come," said Brom. "It's what she's for."

So three numbered boxes went onto the cart they had come east on, left and middle and right, with the corner that was worn white turned outward where Karis could see it; and at the bottom of the third, under the masonry chips, the reserve lay in its canvas with five thumbs in the wax. Seln came down the meadow as the canvas was being roped. He looked at the cart and seemed to recognise it, and put his hand for an instant on the place under the seat where a locked case had ridden from a bluff to a river. The case was on its strap across his chest. He said nothing whatever about a plank, and Cael kept his eyes off his boots.

---

They paid the six at first light on the seventy-first day, on the smith's own bench, and Karis said the new figure aloud so that everybody should have it.

"In hand, forty-six."

The cart went up to the saddle between two people. It had no beast, for there was not a mule in Lowmarch to be hired for a mountain by a crew that might not bring it back. Seln took the near shaft and Cael the off, and they leaned, and the narrow wheels went round. On the flat of the meadow it was nothing. On the hill Cael counted nine strides and felt the calf begin to close, and at the tenth Lira's shoulder came in beside his without a word, and took the shaft, and he fell back to the tail and walked with his hand on a box until the leg forgave him.

They changed so all the way up, by nines, and nobody remarked on it.

Brom walked behind with his arms hanging.

He bore it as far as the dead tree. Then he stopped the cart with a word, and took the long rope from under the canvas, the ferryman's, which had drawn a wagon out of a wash and a boy's worth of bad air out of a well. He cut nothing. He doubled a bight of it, and knotted it, and passed the loop over his head and one shoulder so that it lay across his chest like a bandolier, and he made the two ends fast to the cart's frame on the near side, low, behind the wheel.

"What's that?" said Lira.

"A strap."

"You're not to pull."

"I'm not pulling. Look." He walked forward until the rope came taut, and then he simply went on walking, with both hands clasped behind his back like a schoolmaster, and the cart came after him. "That's my chest. She said nothing about my chest. There's not a finger on it."

"The hold's woman said not to lift."

"I've lifted nothing. I'm leaning." His whole plain face had lightened, for the first time since a river. "I leaned on a mule for five days at Thornwater and nobody stopped me."

Karis walked round him once and examined the knot, and the lie of the rope over his breastbone, and both wrists, which were behind him and idle.

"I can find nothing in it to forbid," she said. "I shall enter that I looked."

So he drew, and the two in the shafts had little to do but steer. They went over the saddle with the sun behind them and down into the grey scrub by a road they had walked five times, past the wash, which was dry and red, and they made their camp at the thorn stump on its eastern lip, as they had on the fifty-ninth night.

Seln lit the fire with his flint. It was the fourth they had had on a road, and Cael noticed that not one of them watched the first smoke go up; the thing had become ordinary in twelve days, and he thought that a carter with cold feet would have been glad to see it.

Karis ruled her table before the light went, on a long leaf that she had pasted up out of four. She drew two lines down the middle of it for the edges of a shelf that none of them had yet seen, and beside them six narrow columns, and she wrote a head over each in her small exact hand: the number of the stretch, its width, its pitch, the footing, water, and a last one that she left wide.

"What goes in that?" said Lira.

"Whether it takes a cart. Yes or no, and why." She blew on the ink. "It's the only column he's paying for. The other five are there so that he can believe it."

"And who fills it in? You can't write for more than a few minutes."

"I measure, and I say the figure aloud. He writes." Her pencil pointed across the fire, at Cael. "He writes small, and he doesn't argue about a stop, and he has never in his life put down a number that he didn't count. I've had three pairs of hands since the river, and I know which one I want for a table."

He wrote by its light.

*Seventy-first day. The east road, above the wash. Taken at the plank yesterday: a hold's slip, Oxhollow's, for the high traverse between that hold and Thornwater. Walked with a cart and mapped. Seventy marks, paid at Oxhollow on the map. We walk it from this end and come down on his.*

*The cart is our own, hired back from the smith for six against its return. In hand, forty-six. The boxes are on it, since the ferryman wants his loft.*

*I took this one because it pays, and because nobody had asked to keep it, and because it is ground. I've put the third down last. It weighed the most.*

*The nine: held. No Path has been lifted by any of us since the ford. Deployment since the Line: one.*

"You've left out the strap," said Brom, who had read none of it and knew.

"It's a rope."

"It's a strap when it's on me." He turned a piece of bacon on a stick. "Put that I thought of it."

He put it.

---

They left the Thornwater road in the middle of the next morning, at the lower of its two crossings, and none of them had ever looked to the left there before.

A second water came into the river at that place from the north. It was no wider than a cart, and brown, and it ran out of a fold in the hills between banks of alder, and beside it there went a track. Wheels had made it, a long time ago. The ruts had grown over with a short wiry turf, and here and there a sapling as thick as a wrist stood up in the middle of the way. Seln, who was thirty paces ahead, bent each one down with his boot as he passed, and the axle went over it.

"Who came up here?" said Lira.

"Somebody who wanted stone," said Karis. "Or timber. I can't tell from a rut." She was walking at the off wheel with her eyes on the ground. "It was a loaded cart that made these, coming down. They're deeper on the low side."

The hills closed in as they climbed. The alders gave way to the red-barked trees with the paired needles, and then those thinned as well, and there was grey rock on either hand with heather growing in its cracks. The stream had gone down out of sight on their right. He could hear it, a steady rushing from somewhere below the level of the track; and then, after they had gone another half mile, he began to hear something else.

It was the cart. The off wheel had a dry place in its hub that ticked once at every turn, and he had been hearing it since the saddle without attending. Now he heard it twice. It ticked beside him, and then, about as long after as it took to draw a breath, the same small sound came again from across the valley and a little above, thin and exact.

Lira had stopped.

"Say something," she said.

"What?"

*What*, said the hill opposite, when he had almost ceased to expect it.

"It's late," said Lira, delighted. "Listen to how late it is. I've had an echo before, in the long hall at Fenmark, and it came straight back at you like a slap. This one goes away and thinks about it."

"There's a wall over there," said Seln, from the bend. He had not raised his voice, and the far side gave back only the hiss of it. "A high one. I should say three hundred yards across, and I should not care to guess how far down."

They came round the bend and saw what he was standing in front of.

The valley ended. It did not close gently, as valleys did in the country below; the two hillsides simply drew together until they were walls, and between the walls there was a slot full of blue shadow, with the stream coming out of the bottom of it white. The far wall went up sheer and dark for as high as he could tip back his head. The near one, on their own side of the water, was broken.

It was broken in one place only. For two hundred yards along its foot the mountain had been shedding its skin for longer than there had been carters. A slope of loose stone came down there out of the sky, grey and flat and steep as the roof of a barn, spreading as it came, like corn poured out of a sack; and it ended at their feet in a litter of thin slabs, none thicker than a hand, that rang under the cart's wheel like broken crockery.

He followed it up with his eyes. It narrowed as it rose, and at the top, a very long way above the red trees, it stopped against a level line. There was a shelf up there. He could see the edge of it against the light, running away westward along the gorge's lip as straight as a ruled margin, until the rock took it.

"That's the bench," said Karis softly.

"And that's the road to it," said Brom.

He had slipped the rope from his shoulder, and he stood with his head back and his mouth a little open, looking at two hundred yards and more of stone that would slide if a goat sneezed on it. Cael knew that face. He had seen it turned on a wagon in a wash with three wheels and a river coming.

"Can it be done?" said Lira.

"I don't know yet. I've not put my foot on it."

Seln had not looked up at all. He was standing a little way out on the foot of the slope, where the slabs lay thickest, and he had bent and lifted one of them in both hands.

It was grey, like the rest, and the size of a shield, and somewhat curved. He turned it to the light. The others had broken edges, sharp and pale. This one's edge was rounded and thick, and along its upper face there ran a low ridge like the keel of a boat, and on the ridge a green crust of lichen was growing.

He rapped it with his knuckle, and it did not ring.

"Karis," he said. "I think that you will want to see this. It is not a stone."

# Chapter 16 — Floats

Karis took the grey thing on her two forearms, since her palms would not bear the weight of it, and held it up to the sky.

"It's horn," she said.

She turned it edgewise. It was as thick as two fingers in the middle and thinned toward the rim, and it was built in leaves, one upon another, like the hoof of a horse or the bark of the red trees.

"It grew. Something laid this down a layer at a time, and carried it for years; there's lichen on it as old as any on that rock." She tipped it, and the pale underside showed, smooth and faintly hollowed. "And then it was shed, as a stag sheds. This lay on a back."

"A broad one," said Brom.

"If these overlap, as I think they must, then a broad one indeed." She looked up the slope, at two hundred and sixty yards of thin grey slabs, and Cael saw the same thought reach all of them together. A person could not have told, from where they stood, how much of that hillside was stone.

"It bears now," said Seln.

He had dusted his fingers, and he stood with his hands behind him.

"I asked a question at the plank, two mornings ago, and I have carried the answer for a day and a half, since it concerned nothing that we had yet seen. I asked what a shale-back was, and whether a crew had ever come to harm by one." He inclined his head toward the horn. "He gave me the carters' rule first, and he gave it twice, and I shall give it as he did. 'Never be below them. Never be *loud* below them.'"

"Below," said Lira. She glanced up the fan, and then along the edge of the shelf at the top of it, and her mouth went thin. "That's a bench. It's got a mountain on one side of it. Where else is there to be?"

"I put that to him also. He said that the salt crews go under them twice a year on the cold road, and walk, and are let alone. The animals are as mild as cattle. A man may pass beneath a herd all day."

"And if he's loud?"

"There he had two tales, and I asked for both." Seln looked at the ground while he told them, as he did when he was reading from memory. "The first is his own time. A salt string was going under a ledge herd on the cold road, and a block had come down across the track; and a Force at the tail of the string shifted it with his Path, since that was quicker than unloading. The hill came down, with the herd upon it. They found two mules."

Nobody spoke.

"The second he had from a carter, who had it from his father. Men used to come up a side water off the Thornwater road with carts, for roofing stone. It lies ready split at the foot of a great slide, and need only be loaded. One year they did not come back down, and the next year nobody went up to learn why." He lifted his eyes to the wheel-ruts under their feet, with the turf grown over them. "I had not supposed, until this morning, that I should be standing in the second tale."

"Which loud?" said Karis.

Her voice was sharp with attention.

"He said *loud*. A carter says that of a Path, and of a shout, and I've heard one say it of a fire. Which did he mean?"

"I asked him that in those words. He said, the kind a man would not be on a road." Seln spread his hands a little. "He means all three, and he does not know which. It is the rule that Thornwater keeps, for the reason that Thornwater gave us."

Cael looked at the slope for a long count. Nothing on it moved. A small wind came down the gorge and stirred the heather, and across the water the far wall gave back the sound of the stream, late.

"The hold stands," he said. "No Path and no fragment, for any of us, here as on a road; that's not new. What's new is that until somebody can tell me which kind of loud, we keep every kind of quiet as well. Low voices. No iron on iron. And nobody goes under anything with a back until we've looked at it first."

"We're about to drag a cart up a hill of plates," said Lira.

"Then we'll do it when there's nothing above us. He's looked." He turned to Seln. "You have looked?"

"For a quarter of an hour. I should not say so otherwise."

"Then how do we haul?" said Brom. "A rope wants calling. Somebody has to say when to heave and when to stand, or a man loses his fingers."

"With an arm," said Karis. "One arm up is *walk*. Both is *hold*. I can't pull on anything, so I shall stand where everybody can see me and be the voice." She looked at her two pink palms. "It's the first use I've found for them since a river."

"And if you want to say more than two things?"

"Then I shall come down and say them quietly. I was brought up in reading-rooms. I know how far a sentence needs to go."

Brom walked out onto the fan, and it moved under him.

It did not slide. The whole surface gave a little, with a dry clinking, as a shingle beach gives under a boot, and settled again about his ankles. He stood in it with his arms out. Then he climbed ten yards, slowly, and each step sank and slipped back half its length, so that he seemed to be wading upstream.

"It's loose to the depth of my arm," he said, when he came down. "Farther, maybe. There's no floor to it that I can find."

They tried the cart loaded all the same, since he wished to see what it would do. Seln and Cael took the shafts and Brom went into his rope, and they put her at the slope and leaned.

She went up four yards. Then the wheels stopped turning. Cael felt it through the shaft before he saw it; the rims had sunk into the plates to the depth of a hand, and they were no longer rolling over the slope but pushing a wave of it ahead of them. Brom leaned harder, and the wave grew. The cart stood still for a breath, with three people straining at her. Then the stones under her let go all together, and she came back down the four yards with a long sigh of slate, a little sideways, and stopped where she had started.

Brom stepped out of his rope and regarded her.

"She floats," he said.

"She what?" said Lira.

"Floats. A wheel's for a road. It wants something hard under it to turn on. There's nothing hard in that." He pushed his boot into the fan and watched the plates close round it. "She'll not roll on it. She sits in it, like a boat in a pond, and she goes where it goes, and it only knows the one way."

"Then it can't be done."

"I never said that. You don't row a boat up a weir. You put a line on her bow from the top of the weir, and you lift her head, and you let her swim up."

He would have her empty first, and nobody disputed it.

The boxes went up by hand, and it took what was left of the day. Seln and Cael slung the left-hand box between them from its two rope beckets and set off, and it was the worst climb that Cael had made since a scree above a valley with no name. He had nine strides. At the tenth the calf shut its fist, and he stood on a slope that crept under his boots, with half a box hanging from his right hand, and counted twenty while the gorge breathed cold on his neck. Seln waited beside him each time without a word, looking at the view, as a man waits for a door to be unlocked.

It shut on him three times before the top, and each time it let go a little sooner.

Brom took the middle box by himself. He knelt, and had Lira set it on his back, and passed his rope over it and across his chest. Then he stood up under it and walked onto the fan with his hands clasped before him, as a porter walks under a chest.

"That's lifting," said Karis.

"It's carrying. It sits on me. There's nothing hanging off a hand." He did not turn his head, since the box would not let him. "The hold's woman said the wrist would be wet if I'd used it. Look at it when I come down."

Karis looked at it when he came down, and it was dry, and she wrote that as well.

He took the third box after it, in the same manner, while the light went yellow on the far wall. Lira carried the stores. She would not be told that a bound ankle had no business on loose plates; she went up with the flour on one shoulder and the kettle in her hand, two steps and a slide, and came down for the blankets, and went up again. Karis took what could be carried on a forearm or hung from an elbow, which was more than Cael would have guessed, and made four journeys of it in her small determined silence.

By the last of them the cart stood at the foot of the fan with nothing in it but its own seat board, and looked like a thing that had been robbed.

They slept that night at the top, on the end of the shelf, because she would not have three boxes left alone on a mountain and nobody cared to be the one to tell her that there was no one within a day's walk to steal them. It was too dark by then to see what they had climbed to. He lay with his back against the third box and heard the stream a long way down on his right hand, and a second stream, fainter, that was only the first one coming back from the wall.

---

The cart went up in the morning, by six stages, and Brom built every one of them.

He had found his weir. Islands of solid rock stood up out of the fan here and there, the bones of the hill showing through its skin, and the highest was a smooth grey knob at the very lip of the shelf, as round as a bollard on a quay. A rope laid over bare rock would have sawed itself through in an hour. So he had Seln cut a length of red trunk, as thick as a thigh, and peel it. He bedded that across a notch in the island, with a flat stone at each end to keep it from walking, and laid the ferryman's rope over it.

"It turns," he said. "The rope doesn't rub. The log goes round under it."

One end of the rope went down to the cart. He had lashed her shafts together and made a bridle to her axle-tree, so that a pull from above would lift her nose. The other end came back down the slope beside her, and at that end he put himself.

It was the neatest thing Cael had ever seen him think of. He stood in a loop that went round his chest and under both arms, facing downhill, with his hands behind his back; and when Karis, up at the log, raised her arm, he walked down the mountain. Seln and Cael went down with him, hauling hand over hand. For every yard that three people descended the cart rose one, nose first, half buried, with the plates parting round her and closing behind as water closes behind a punt.

"Hold," said Karis, in the low carrying voice that she kept for a reading-room.

They held. Lira scrambled across below the cart and wedged slabs under both wheels, and the rope was carried up to the next island, and the log after it.

The first stage went as he had planned it, and the second did not.

Along the gorge-ward edge of the fan there ran a gully, a chute as wide as a road, where the loosest of the stone went down toward the drop; and the whole skin of the slope leaned that way, like a floor in an old house. Twenty yards into the second stage the cart began to lean with it. She did not slide back. She went sideways, quite gently, a foot for every yard she climbed, floating off the line of the rope toward the chute; and up at the island the peeled log began to creep along its notch after her.

"She's going to the right," said Karis. "The log's walking. I can't stop it with my hands."

"Lira," said Brom.

She was there already. They had knotted the box-lashings into a second line, thirty yards of it, and made one end fast to the cart's near side. Lira had carried the other up the slope and to the left, away from the chute, to a boulder as big as a barrel. She sat down behind it now with both boots against the rock. She passed the line round her back, under her arms, and took it in her two fists in front of her.

"It's a thing I can do sitting," she said. "Walk."

They walked. The line came taut across the slope and the cart's head swung, a little, back toward the island.

Then the fan under the near wheel let go.

It was a small slip, a barrowful of plates running out from under the rim, and the cart dropped a foot and snatched. Cael saw the line take Lira. It lifted her off her seat and dragged her a yard up the boulder's face, with her heels scraping, and he saw what happened in her calves, because he had been watching for it for sixty days. Every sinew in her stood ready to be somewhere else. It was the burst, asking, with the whole of her Path behind it.

In his own feet Wind leaned with hers, as it always did.

She sat down. She did it on purpose, hard, as one sits on a lid, and drove both boots against the rock again, and the line burned through her fists for a hand's breadth and stopped. The cart's nose came round.

"Walk," she said, through her teeth.

He walked. Nine fragments had come up at the snatch, and he went on down the slope with the rope in his hands, putting them down one at a time. Compression had felt the jerk come along the hemp and had offered to take it through his frame into the hill. Anchor had been asking about the island since first light. *There?* A point fixed against that notch, and told to stay, would have been a better thing than any log; it would not have walked, or worn, or needed a man's weight to hold it. He owned the craft of it. He told it that the log was Brom's, and to leave it.

The Iron read was harder to refuse, because it offered the thing he was being paid to know.

It could have told him the weight of that slope. Within a pace and a half of his boots it would have given him every plate, and which way the whole loose skin meant to go, a breath before it went; he would have felt the slip under the near wheel coming, as he felt a punch. He was a surveyor today, by the terms of a slip, and he carried an instrument that no surveyor west of the Line could have bought, and he kept it shut.

He turned that over through two more stages, since his hands were busy and his head was not.

It was not only the hold. A reading taken with that thing could be checked by nobody. He might write that the fan was sound at a certain place, and be right, and a ford-keeper six days off would have nothing but his word for it; and Cael had lived since his Kindling under figures that could not be checked by the person they concerned. What Karis did with a knotted cord, any carter could do after her, at the same place, and get the same number or call her a liar. That was the map the man was buying. The other would only have been a boy on a hillside saying that he knew.

They brought her over the lip at noon. The last yard was rotten rock, and four of them lifted while Brom got his back under the tail and straightened his legs, with his hands on his knees and nowhere near a rail.

"Carrying," he said, to Karis, before she could open her mouth.

---

He saw the bench properly for the first time with the cart standing on it.

It was a road, or the stone that a road might have been made on. It ran west from the top of the fan along the wall of the gorge, as level from end to end as a shelf in a shop, thirty feet broad where they stood; and no hand, by the look of it, had ever cut it. The mountain rose on the left in a long grey slope of plates, steep as a roof, to a skyline that he had to lean back to find. On the right there was an edge, and beyond the edge blue air, and the far wall three hundred yards off with a white thread of water hanging on it.

Only one thing was wrong with it. It was not flat from side to side. The whole shelf tipped outward, toward the drop, gently, like the top of a desk.

"Pitched," said Brom. He had seen it at once. "Like a roof. Everything that falls on this from up there is meant to roll across it and go over." He set a pebble down. It lay still for a breath, and then it trundled away from him, quite slowly, the whole thirty feet, and dropped out of sight without a sound.

Karis had Hesk's clock in her hand.

"Four," she said. "And a piece. I heard it land, and then I heard it land again." She looked at the far wall with respect. "I shall have to take the first."

She laid out her method on the spot, as she would have laid out instruments on a table.

Lira was to run. She would go out a hundred of her own strides along the shelf, and stand a stone on its end where she stopped, and come back and say what she had found. Nothing with a wheel was to go on any stretch until she had been over it at speed.

"Why running?" said Brom. "She could walk it and see more."

"Because a thing that'll bear me running will bear her walking," said Lira. She was looking down the shelf, and not at anyone. "And if a piece of this is going to go, I'd sooner it went under me than under three boxes and the four of you tied to them. I can get off it."

Cael said nothing to that. He had stopped counting her runs on a gravel bar, and he did not begin again; but it cost him something to hear her set her own ankle against a mountain as though it were a price she had already agreed.

He was to pace. A hundred of his strides made a hundred yards, as near as mattered, and that was the unit of the map; where his hundredth fell, Karis measured. She had her cord for the width, knotted at every foot. For the pitch she had made a thing out of the brass straightedge and a pebble hung on a thread, and she laid the brass across the slope and read off how far the thread hung from its mark. For the drop she had a pocketful of stones and the clock.

"And you write it," she said. "One line. I'll give you the figures in the order of the columns, and I shan't give them twice unless you ask."

Lira went, with her Path where she had kept it since the ford. She ran lightly, well in from the edge, and her right foot came down a shade behind her left, and he looked at the gorge until she was a small figure standing a stone on end. She walked back.

"Slab," she said. "Good slab, all the way. Dry. It rings a bit hollow about halfway, on the outside, so I kept in."

He paced it, and his hundredth stride fell eleven yards short of her stone.

"Mine are bigger," said Lira, with satisfaction.

Karis measured. It took her the better part of a quarter of an hour, and she spoke each number once.

"One. Twenty-six feet. A hand and two fingers in the yard, outward. Slab; hollow at fifty on the outer third. No water. Cart: yes, keeping in."

He wrote it across six columns in his smallest hand, and beside the table he drew a short length of shelf between her two ruled lines, and marked the hollow place with a cross.

Lira had come to look over his arm.

"How many of those will there be?" she said. "Before the other end."

"If the salt crews are right about three days for a mule," said Karis, "then sixty. Perhaps seventy."

"Seventy lines." Lira began to laugh, low, mindful of the rule. "Seventy marks. He's being paid by the line. A mark a line." She pointed at the book in his hand. "You've written in one of those every night since a lane in Ardenmere, and I've watched you do it, and nobody ever gave you a copper. Now there's a man with a ford who'll give you a mark every time you rule one off."

"It's her line," said Cael. "I only hold the pencil."

"It's in your hand. That's whose it is; ask Pike."

He looked at what he had written. It was twenty words and four numbers, and it said what a hundred yards of mountain was like, and whether a wheel could go there. He had kept a log through every term since his Kindling, and filled a notebook with other people's fights, and all of it had been called watching. Nobody had ever before called it work, and it was the first time in his life that a line of his had been worth a coin to a stranger.

"What's the rate for a strap?" said Brom.

---

They made eight hundreds before the light failed, and at the end of the eighth Seln was waiting for them.

Cael had not seen him go. He had been at the near shaft through the first three stretches, and then at some point the shaft had had Lira on it instead. Now, where the shelf bent a little round a buttress, there was a mild man in a dark coat sitting on a stone with his hands on his knees.

Behind him the wall leaned out. It made a roof as deep as a room and twice as long, dry underneath, with a floor of fine grit; and somebody had swept that floor clear of plates with a branch of heather, and laid a ring of stones for a fire, and set a full water-skin in the shade.

"There is a seep forty yards on," said Seln. "It fills a cup in the time one takes to say so. I should not drink from the first pool; something has."

"You found this," said Lira.

"I was ahead."

"We've been measuring that hill for six hours. When were you ahead?"

"For most of them." He seemed to feel that more was owed. "It is the one part of the day's work that I can do better than any of you; I cannot run, or carry, or count by a clock. I can go on in front and learn where a person would be glad to lie down."

They ate cold. Nobody lit the fire, and nobody proposed it; it lay there in its ring with the rule over it. Brom let the tail-board down by its chains as gently as he could, and one link slipped and struck the frame, a small clear note, like a spoon on a cup.

It came back off the far wall when he had already begun to apologise for it.

They listened to it go. Up on the dark slope above them nothing whatever happened.

"Well," said Brom, very low. "That's one thing it doesn't mind."

"It's one thing that slope doesn't mind tonight, with nothing on it that we can see," said Karis. "I shan't write more than that."

He wrote his own page with his back to the wall, by the last grey light, since there was to be no lamp.

*Seventy-third day. The bench, at the end of the eighth hundred. The cart came up the fan this morning in six stages, on a rope over a log, by Brom's reckoning and nobody's Path. Eight lines in Karis's table, all of them mine to write. Lira says that I am paid by the line. Each of the eight says yes.*

*Above us there is a mountain of plates, and by Pike's rule we are not to be loud under whatever lives on it. We have seen nothing yet but one piece of horn.*

*The nine: held, on a hill where three of them would have been of use. Deployment since the Line: one.*

There was ice on the kettle in the morning, a skin as thin as a leaf, the first that any of them had seen past the Line. Lira ran the ninth hundred with her breath smoking. He paced it, and wrote, and they drew the cart on with Brom leaning into his rope on the up-slope side, where the weight of him kept her from drifting toward the edge. Wherever a gully came down the face it had laid a tongue of loose plates right across the shelf, and on those she floated, as he had said she would. He walked against the strap there with his whole body slanted, like a man in a gale.

The tenth was slab, and the eleventh was slab with a crack.

Lira went out for the twelfth, and she did not come back at a run.

He saw her stop, sixty yards off. She stood quite still for as long as it would have taken to count ten. Then she set no stone on its end; she turned, and walked back toward them along the inside of the shelf, slowly, close under the wall, and her feet made no sound on the rock.

She did not speak when she reached them. She took Cael by the sleeve and drew him three steps out from under the buttress, and pointed with her chin, up and to the west.

He looked where she showed him, and saw the mountain.

A quarter of a mile along, and far up the slope, another shelf ran across the face. It was narrower than theirs. Along it, for the length of a long field, the grey plates of the hillside lay in a row, humped and overlapping, each heap about the size of the cart; and there was a gap of bare rock between each heap and the next, and all the gaps were the same.

He counted the heaps, since he could no more have helped it than breathing, and he had reached twenty-two when one of them lifted its head.

# Chapter 17 — Thirty Head

There were thirty of them, and he counted twice to be sure.

Twenty-six were full grown. Four were half the size, with plates of a paler grey that had not yet gathered any green, and those four kept to the inside of the shelf, against the hill. Each of the grown animals was as long as the cart and a good deal broader. It stood low on four legs like the stumps of trees, and its back and both flanks were roofed over with horn, in slabs that overlapped as slates overlap on a house, the largest along the spine and each with its keel. Lichen grew on the old ones in rings. When one of them stood still, which was nearly always, it was a heap of the mountain's own stone, and his eye slid off it.

The head was small for such a body, and carried at the height of a man's knee. It ended in something like the beak of a tortoise, and with that the animal scraped the rock. He could hear it, now that he knew to listen: a dry patient rasping, thirty times over, like a yard full of masons dressing stone a long way off. Under that there was another sound, whenever one of them shifted its weight, for the plates moved on each other with a low clatter, as a slate roof settles when the sun goes off it.

"They're eating the hill," said Lira, close to his ear.

"They're eating what grows on it," said Karis, who had come out from the buttress on her toes. "Look at the rock behind them. It's a different colour where they've been."

It was. Along the upper shelf, east of the last animal, the stone was scoured pale in a band as wide as the shelf itself, and ahead of the first it was dark and furred. They were mowing it.

He looked for the leader, and he did it without thinking, as he had done it at a ford with the light going. A pack had a first mover, and a crew had a captain, and every yard he had ever sat above had held one fighter round whom the rest arranged themselves. He went along the line from the east end to the west for the animal that the others watched.

There was none. No head was turned toward any other. Each of the thirty faced the rock in front of its own beak, and the gaps between them were so nearly equal that he could have laid Karis's cord in any one of them and found the same knot in the next.

"We go under them," said Brom, and it was not quite a question.

"The shelf does," said Cael. "For the next ten hundreds, by the look of it, and it's the only shelf."

"He said never below."

"He said also that the salt crews do it twice a year, and walk." Seln had appeared at the cart's tail, as he did. "I have been as far as the fifteenth of your hundreds this morning, and they did not lift a head for me. I should add that I made no request of them."

Cael weighed it. Thirty animals stood three hundred and fifty yards up a slope of loose plates, and the five of them had a cart, and a rule in a one-armed man's words. To go back was to go down the fan, and to go on was to do for one afternoon what carters did every year.

"We go on," he said, "at a walk. Lira runs the hundreds as before, but she runs them under the wall, and Karis measures. Nobody calls a figure across the shelf; if you want me to hear a number, you come and say it in my ear."

They did twelve more lines that day, and it was the quietest work he had ever done in company.

He paced, and wrote. Karis laid her brass on the rock and bent over her thread, and came to him with each figure as a clerk brings a paper to a desk. Brom leaned into his rope on the up-slope side, with his eyes on the cart's near wheel and never once on the hill, like a man passing a dog that he has been told will bite if it is stared at. The dry hub ticked at every turn, and up on the face the rasping went on without a break. Each time he raised his eyes from the page the grey heaps were where they had been.

At the twentieth hundred he looked up and found them behind his left shoulder, and he had not seen one of them take a step.

Seln had found the second overhang, and he had found it directly beneath the middle of the herd.

"It is the only one in the next mile," he said, before anybody could remark on it. "I looked for another, and I should have preferred another."

It was deeper than the first, a long low cave of a place where a bed of softer stone had weathered back, and from its mouth the whole line of the upper shelf could be seen against the evening sky. He had swept it, and laid his ring of stones, and put nothing in the ring.

Karis stood looking at the ring for some time.

"I want a fire," she said.

Lira turned round. Brom had been lowering the tail-board with both hands under it, and he stopped with it halfway.

"Hear me first. The country's rule has three things in it, and Pike's *loud* has the same three, since it's the same rule. For the hounds we found out which one mattered, and it cost four of us a month. I don't know which matters to those." She tipped her head at the roof, and at what was above it. "I shall have to find out, because the last column of his table is mine, and a carter who comes over this bench with a Force at his tail-board ought to be told what he may do with him. Of the three, there is one that I can test this evening and then take back."

"Fire," said Cael.

"It's struck with a flint. It will be the size of my two hands, under a roof of rock, at three hundred and fifty yards; and if one head comes up, Seln puts his coat over it, and we've lost a coat." She folded her arms, carefully, on account of her palms. "A Path I can't take back, and a shout I'd sooner not try on purpose. But I should like to cross one of the three off tonight, while it's cheap."

"And if it isn't cheap?" said Brom. "If it's the one?"

"Then we learn it with a fire that a coat can put out, and not next week with one we need."

Cael had the ruling, and he did not hurry it. He had lifted the rule from tinderboxes once before, above a wash, on the strength of a page that had been tested at a ford; and this was tested by nobody. He turned her reasoning over for the place where it would break, as she had once asked four people to do in a loft, and what he found was that she had already chosen the one test whose failure they could best afford.

"Hands," he said.

There were five. Brom's came up last, and he set the tail-board down on its chains first, so gently that no link spoke.

Seln struck the flint, and Lira lay on her stomach at the mouth of the overhang, where the roof stopped, and watched the upper shelf with her chin on her fists, since her eyes were the best of the five. The tinder caught. A thread of smoke went up and flattened under the rock and crept out along it into the dusk, and when the first twigs had taken there was light on all their faces.

"Well?" said Karis. She had Hesk's clock open on her knee.

"Nothing." Lira kept her head where it was. "They're scraping. The third from this end has lain down; it folded up its front legs first, like a cow."

"Did it look this way?"

"It looked at its own feet."

They gave it a quarter of an hour by the clock, and then another, with the fire built up to the size of a hat, and thirty beaks rasped on in the dark overhead. Karis shut the clock.

"One," she said, and wrote it. "Fire: lit and fed for half an hour, in plain sight, at three hundred and fifty yards, and not a head." She looked up. "I shan't call it struck off for good. I call it struck off at that distance, and at that size. But I'm a good deal warmer than I was."

"Twenty-two years," said Brom, to the flames, in a fair imitation of a carter, and Lira laughed into her sleeve.

They ate hot food for the first time since the wash. Afterward Karis sat with her notebook shut on her finger, looking at the black line of the upper shelf, and Cael knew what she was going to ask before she drew the breath for it.

"I want tomorrow, all of it. We do no hundreds, and I sit on a rock in front of this place with the clock and look at them from first light until I can't see." She turned to him. "It will cost a day's lines, and I know what that is in flour."

"It isn't a day off the map," he said. "It's the map. The column says whether it takes a cart, and why, and those are part of why."

"That's what I'd have argued."

"Then I've saved you a sentence."

"I had four ready." She opened the notebook, and her eyes had the brightness in them that he had seen over a charcoal river. "I shall want a second pair of eyes, since I can't watch both ends of a line three hundred yards long; and a hand, for when mine stops."

Cael sat on her left with the notebook, and Lira lay on the rock at her right, on her stomach again. Brom stayed under the roof with the cart and the boxes, mending a strap, and Seln had gone on westward before any of them woke. There was ice on the slab. The sun had not yet reached into the gorge, and thirty heaps of grey stone lay along the upper shelf with frost on their backs.

"The east end is mine," said Karis, "and you have the west. Lira takes whichever of them does something. Nobody is to tell me what he thinks it means; I want what it did, and when."

"A length and a half," he said. "Everywhere."

"They don't look at each other to do that," said Lira. "Their heads are down, and they can't see past their own beaks."

"Write that she said so," said Karis, "and the time."

The second thing took her until the sun was on the shelf. High on the face, a little west of the leading animal, a vein of white stone ran down the slope as broad as a door, and at the first hour by the clock the leader's beak had been a cart's length short of it. She had him enter that. At the second hour the white vein was behind the leader's tail, and the whole line, thirty animals and all their gaps, lay forty yards farther along the mountain than it had.

He thought about it honestly.

"Nor I, and I watched one animal for a count of three hundred without blinking more than I could help. It took four steps in that time, each of them while it was scraping." She rubbed her eyes with the back of her wrist. "They move as the hand of this clock moves. One looks away, and it's later."

The third thing came down on them out of the sky at about noon.

The sun had been on the crags above the upper shelf for an hour, and whatever the frost had been holding there it let go. He heard a crack, a long way up, and then a hollow knocking that grew; and a rock the size of a keg came down the face in great slow bounds, turning over in the air, and went straight through the line.

It passed between the ninth animal and the tenth. It struck their own shelf between them with a sound like a mallet on a cask, and burst, and the larger half of it leaped out again and went on down the slope toward the bench, so that he felt the blow come up through the slab under him. The piece crossed the lower shelf two hundred yards west of where they sat, taking a spray of plates with it, and dropped into the gorge. A good while afterward the far wall gave the whole of it back, the crack and the knocking and the burst.

Karis had not flinched, and her eyes had never left the line.

"Lira."

"Two of them moved," said Lira, and her voice shook a little. "The ninth and the tenth. Each one took a step sideways, away from where it went through. The ninth's gone back to scraping already, and the tenth's looking at the ground."

"The rest?"

"Not a head, and that went by as close to them as I am to you."

"A step, then, from the two it nearly hit, and a step isn't flight." Karis let out her breath. "Give me the notebook; I want to put this one in my own hand." She took it on her knee and wrote slowly, with her lips tight. "*Sound: a rock the size of a keg, striking the shelf between two animals, loud enough to be returned by the gorge. Felt in the slab at three hundred and fifty yards. Two animals moved one step each. No other response.*"

"That's two of the three," said Cael.

"That's two." She gave him back the book, and flexed her fingers. "And it's the two I could have guessed, since the carter lit his fire at the wash and nothing came. I'm only being told what I knew."

---

The fourth thing came when the sun had gone well round into the west, and it was over in about the time of a breath.

He had the west end, and he had it faithfully. His eyes were on the last animal of the line, a big one with a broken plate on its near shoulder, which stood a little beyond a rib of the hill, so that from where he sat he could see it and it could not have seen its own fellows at the eastern end. He had been counting the slabs along its spine to keep himself awake, and he had got to seven.

It stepped uphill.

It did so suddenly, and nothing on that shelf had moved so quickly in two days of his watching. Its off foreleg came up off the shelf and went down on the slope above, and the hind leg followed, and then both again, three steps, with a clatter of horn that reached him a moment later. Beside it the next did the same, and the next. He swept his eyes east along the shelf, and there was not one grey back in the whole line that was where it had been; all thirty had gone up the hill by the length of their own bodies, the calves scrambling inside them, and stood now on the open slope with their heads lifted.

Then he heard it. It was a long grinding rush, like a load of slates shot off a cart, and it came from the east end.

"There," said Lira, who was up on her elbows. "Under the last one, at this end. Look."

A piece of the upper shelf had gone. He could see the scar, pale and sharp-edged, where a slab as big as a barn door had been lying along the lip, and the slab itself was sliding still, far down the face, breaking as it went. The easternmost animal stood above the place with its hindquarters low and its legs spread wide. It had been standing on the thing when it went.

He turned that over while the last of the plates ran out below. He had been watching one animal, on purpose, at the far end of a line three hundred yards long; and he had not seen the cause of what it did, and it had not seen the cause either, and it had moved all the same.

"What did you see?" said Karis. Her eyes were still on the hill, and her voice was very quiet, and it had something in it that made the hair lift on his arms. "Each of you, in order. Cael."

"The west end went up, three steps. I was counting plates on the last animal, and it moved before I heard anything at all."

"Before."

"I'd swear to it. I heard its own horn first, and then the slide."

"It can't see this end," said Lira. "There's a piece of hill in the way. I've been looking at that rib all day."

"Lira. What did you see?"

"The plate tipped, and I saw the edge of it lift. The one on it went down behind, as if somebody had kicked its legs away, and it scrabbled." She swallowed. "And they all went up. Karis, they all went up at once. Not one of them turned to see what had happened; they were already going."

"Which went first?"

"None of them."

"There must have been—"

"There wasn't. I've got good eyes, and I've watched a starting line since I was small. I know what it looks like when one goes and the rest follow, because you can see it run along the row, and this didn't run along." Lira sat up. "It was like one animal thirty carts long."

Karis looked down at the clock on its stocking.

"I saw the plate tip, and I began to count when it tipped. I saw them step in the same instant, both ends, as near as I can judge; and I had said *one*, and begun to say *two*, before the noise of it got to me here." She laid one finger on the glass. "It had three hundred and fifty yards to come to us. It had the best part of that again to go along the shelf to his animal, round a rib. His animal ought to have heard it a count after this one did, and it moved with the rest."

Up on the slope the thirty stood a little longer with their heads high. Then one beak went down to the rock, and another. They did not go back to the shelf they had left; they had begun to scrape where they stood, a body's length above it, and already the gaps between them were closing and opening, settling toward a length and a half.

---

She had Lira's copy of the finding out of her coat before the last head was down.

It was the leaf with the struck dot on it. She laid it on her left knee, and the new page on her right, and sat between the two of them with a pencil in her fist.

"I'm going to say it aloud, and I want it broken if it can be. Brom, come out here, and bring your objections."

Brom came out from under the roof and crouched, enormous, at the edge of the slab.

"At the ford, seven heads turned at once to a point the size of a seed. Something reached all seven together, across a river, and it wasn't the light, for six of them never turned for a hammer on steel." She touched the left-hand leaf. "Then they did a second thing. They waited for one of themselves to walk, and they followed it, *toward*. That's clause four: no leader, only whichever goes first."

She moved her finger to the other knee.

"Here, something reached thirty together. It wasn't sight, for there was a rib of the hill between and their heads were down. It wasn't sound, for the far end moved before the sound could have got to it, and a rock made a greater noise at noon and shifted two."

"The ground," said Brom. "That slab going. They'd feel it in their feet, the same as I would."

"I thought of the ground. The rock at noon struck their own shelf hard enough for me to feel it here, through this." She rapped the slab with her knuckle. "Two of them stepped. So it isn't a shake either."

"Then what did the one on the plate do, that a rock didn't?"

"It was frightened," said Lira.

She had said it slowly, and she was looking at her own right foot.

"It nearly went over. You could see it. Everything in it was trying to be somewhere else."

"Yes." Karis's eyes rested on her, and then on Brom. "I have watched Iron Skin start at your wrists when a rope snatched, and go down again, and you told me in a loft that something leaves you when it comes. A burst went off by itself on a gravel bar, in our first week, because a foot slipped."

"They've no Paths," said Brom. "They're cattle with roofs on."

"I don't say that they have. I say that I know of one thing in this country that reaches every animal of a kind in the same instant, through rock, and it's the thing those hounds turned to. I'm not entitled to invent a second until I've used up the first." She drew a line across the new page. "Whatever a frightened shale-back gives off, the herd has a sense for it, and that's the same sense, or its cousin. Now look what they did with it."

"They went away from it," said Cael.

"Uphill, and all together, with no first animal to wait for. The hounds go to the thing, and these go from it." She wrote four words and underlined them. "*Same sense. Opposite answer.* And there is Pike's word to lay beside it, which is *loud*; and when we asked a ford what a carter meant by that, the answer was a Path."

She wrote for some minutes, and when she read it out it was shorter than he had expected.

"*Shale-back. Seventy-fifth day. One: it does not answer fire at three hundred and fifty yards, nor sound, nor a blow on its own shelf. Two: it answers something given off by one of its own in fright, at once, the whole herd together, by moving away. Three: by that, and by the carters' rule, it will answer a declaration in the same way, and a declaration is a great deal larger than a frightened animal.*" She stopped. "*Derived. Not tested.*"

"Why not tested?" said Brom. "You watched it."

"I watched them do it to each other. I haven't seen one of them answer a Path, since nobody has declared on this mountain, and I'm thankful."

"You could test it small," said Lira. "One of your points, a seed, for half a breath, as you did on the bar. That moved seven dogs."

Karis lifted her eyes to the slope. Thirty animals, each the weight of a loaded cart, stood on loose plates above a shelf that had dropped a piece of itself an hour ago under one of them; and below them there was a roof-pitch of the same stone, and at the bottom of that a bench, and five people.

"On a slope, flight is a landslide with legs."

She said it without any weight, as she would have read out a width.

"A frightened calf moved them three steps, and that was enough to finish what was left of that plate. If I'm right, I find out with the hill coming down on the cart. If they don't stir, I've learned nothing, since a seed might only have been too small; there is no size of that test that's worth its answer." She turned the pencil over. "So it stays derived, and I write that word on it, and anybody who reads it knows exactly how far to trust me."

Cael looked up at the grey heaps on the mountain, and he saw what the page meant for him.

At the ford he had found a seam. He had sat on a gravel bar with four hurt people and gone over what he had counted, and there had been a first mover to give the pack, and a place to put a knife behind it. There was no seam in this. Nothing on that hill went first or could be led, and there was no cut dog; if those thirty ever ran, there would be no opponent to read, only a slope, and the single question of where a person ought to be standing.

"Write it in yours, too. I'd like it in two hands."

It was Karis, and he looked round at her.

"The other one I gave you because I couldn't hold a pencil. This I can write myself, and I have. But it's derived, and a derived thing in one hand is an opinion." She was looking at the leaf, and not at Hesk's book, which she had never read. "You had the far end of the line. If you'll put down what you saw there, in your own words, then it's two people's."

He took out the book and wrote it, with the day at the head: the plates he had been counting, and the big animal stepping before any sound had reached it, and that he had looked for one that went first and found none. He wrote her three clauses under that, and her two words under the clauses. It was the first time that anyone had asked him to keep a finding because his hand would make it worth more, and he wrote it as small and as straight as he could.

---

Seln came back at dusk, and they had a fire again, since one of the three had been paid for.

He sat down a little behind the rest and held out his hands to it, and he let a full minute go by before he spoke, which was his way of saying that the thing bore.

"The shelf narrows," he said. "I have been within sight of the end of it. For the next eight of your hundreds it is much as you have had it. After that there are places where the hill comes down to the edge and leaves less room, and I counted eight of them. The eighth I did not like."

"What's wrong with it?" said Brom.

"I should prefer that you looked at it with your own eyes, since it is a question about a load. I say only that it is wide enough." He turned his hands over. "Beyond the eighth there is a bay, with water, and beyond the bay a ledge, and at the end of the ledge the mountain stops. One sees a valley from there, and a road in it."

"Oxhollow's side," said Karis.

"I take it so."

Lira had been lying on her back with her right foot propped on a box. She sat up.

"How wide's the ledge?"

"Six feet, in places. It is level, and it is rock. I did not go out on it." He considered her. "I thought that it was yours."

She lay down again without answering, and there was the beginning of a smile about her mouth.

Brom had said nothing since the slab. He had been working something out, with his big hands hanging between his knees, and he brought it up now as he brought up a stone from a well, slowly and whole.

"So it needn't be on purpose," he said.

They looked at him.

"If she's right. A rope snatches, and the Skin starts before I've told it. Or somebody's foot goes." He kept his eyes on his own hands. "It only has to be a fright."

"It hasn't gone off since that first week," said Lira, to the roof. "Not once. I'd a dog at my knee, and it didn't."

"No," said Karis.

"I'm only saying."

"I know what you're saying. I've written that as well." Karis had the new leaf on her knee still, and she turned it to the firelight. "It's the reason I asked for two hands. If the page is right, then for as long as we are under them the hold isn't a discipline any more. It's the floor we're standing on."

The fire settled. Overhead, thirty beaks went on scraping at the dark.

"I'll run under the wall from now on," said Lira. "The whole way, where there's most rock under me."

# Chapter 18 — The Eighth

The first narrowing came at the thirty-third hundred, and Karis gave it a number of its own.

"One," she said, into his ear. "Of his eight. I shall keep them in a separate column, since they're what a carter will ask about first."

The hill had put down a foot there. A buttress of the grey slab came out of the face and ran right across the bench, and where it reached the edge it had left a shelf eleven feet broad, by the cord, where there had been twenty-five. It was room and to spare. Even so the cart looked different on it, with the wall at Brom's left elbow and nothing at all beyond the off wheel.

They had measured her at the top of the fan, as they would have measured any other thing on the mountain. She was four feet between the wheels since the smith had cut her, and five from hub to hub, and Karis had written both figures at the head of the table in a box by themselves.

"Because every line under it means *this* cart," she said. "A farm wagon from Oxhollow is a foot wider, for all I know. I can tell him what the shelf is. I can't tell him what he'll put on it."

It was the seventy-sixth day, and they were making up for the one she had spent on a rock. Lira ran each hundred close under the wall, where the slab was thickest, and came back to say what was in it. He paced and wrote. The table had grown down its long pasted leaf a line at a time, and the drawn shelf beside it had begun to look like the thing itself, with its crosses for hollow places and its small hatched tongues wherever a gully had laid loose stone across the way.

On those tongues the cart still floated, and Brom still walked against his strap.

He had it to a craft by now. He could tell from the sound under the near wheel when the plates were about to carry her outward, and he would lean a breath before they did, with his whole weight slanted toward the hill and his hands idle at his back; and the cart would hang on the rope like a boat on a mooring, swing a little, and come on. He never spoke while he did it, and afterward he would look at his left wrist, briefly, as a man glances at a door that he has been told to keep shut.

Cael watched him at it through most of that day, between one line and the next, and thought that he had never seen Brom so content. He had stood idle in a yard for five days and in a meadow for eleven. Here he had found a way of putting his whole weight to use that a woman with a needle could not have forbidden, and he had found it by himself. Iron Skin had not once risen to meet a load on that shelf; and when the cart came safe across a tongue of plates he never glanced round for anybody to have noticed.

Cael had his own things to keep down on those tongues. Compression rose whenever the rope snatched, and Wind leaned at every yard that Lira ran, and he paid the rent on both fragments and spent neither.

The second narrowing was nine feet, and the third nine and a half. The fourth was seven, on a pitch of two full hands to the yard, and there Lira walked backward in front of the off wheel for the whole length of it, laying a flat stone under the rim at every turn so that it should have something to bite.

"Four," breathed Karis. "Seven feet. Two hands, outward. Slab under grit. Cart: yes, with a man at the outer wheel and dry weather. I shouldn't care to say it of a wet day."

"Then say that."

"I have. It's in the last column."

He wrote it. That column had been growing more careful all day; on the first afternoon every line of it had said a plain *yes*, and now each *yes* dragged a condition after it like a cart with a pole lashed under its corner.

Overhead the rasping went on. He had ceased to hear it, as he had once ceased to hear frogs, and he caught himself at that and made himself listen. The line had been above the second overhang at first light. By noon it lay over the fourth narrowing, and they had not got ahead of it by a yard.

"They're following us," said Lira at the next halt, with her mouth almost on his collar.

"They're going where the moss hasn't been eaten," said Karis, who had heard. "And so, as it happens, are we, since that's west. I shan't write *following*. I've nothing under it."

"It feels like following."

"A good many things do."

The fifth narrowing was eight feet, and beyond it Seln had found them a place to sleep that was not much more than a brow of rock with a dry floor under it. He was sitting in it with the water-skin when they came up, and he had swept it.

They had a small fire, and Cael wrote by it, briefly, since his eyes were tired from the table.

*Seventy-sixth day. The bench, beyond the fifth narrowing. Forty-seven lines. Every one says yes, and most of them now say yes on a condition, which Karis holds to be the more honest sort. The herd is over us still. It has come as far west today as we have, and she will not let me write that it follows.*

*The calf is a leg on this shelf, since the shelf is level. Brom has not once looked at the hill. Deployment since the Line: one.*

---

The sixth was six feet and a hand, in the middle of the next morning, and the seventh was the one that Cael remembered afterward.

It came at the fifty-fourth hundred. The buttress there was not slab; it was a rib of darker stone, and it came out so far that the bench seemed to stop against it. Lira had gone round the corner at a walk and stood a long time out of sight. When she came back she held her two hands apart in front of her, a little wider than her shoulders, and then she shrugged.

Karis measured it twice. She lay down on her stomach to do it, with her chin over the edge and one end of the cord pinned under her elbow.

"Five feet nine," she said, when she had wriggled back. "At the narrowest, for four yards. The cart is five. That is a hand to spare beyond either hub, if she goes through exactly in the middle." She brushed grit from her chin. "And the outer edge is not a good edge. It's sound for the first foot. After that I think it's a crust."

He went to look, since the line would be in his hand.

The shelf under his boots was whole. But at the very rim, where the dark rib had weathered, the stone had come away underneath in a long hollow, and what was left on top was a lip no thicker than a plank, with daylight below it. A wheel that strayed nine inches to the right would be standing on that.

Something in him had found the lip before his eyes did. Anchor had asked at every sill and coping since a river, and at every one of seven narrowings, and it had never asked as it asked now. *There?* It showed him the place, under the crust, where a fixed point would sit: one point, set against the sound rock and told to stay, and that plank of stone would have carried a loaded wagon. It would have cost him nothing that he could not have paid by supper.

He looked up the slope at thirty grey heaps, and thought of a page in his own book that had her three clauses on it.

*No.*

It was the plainest refusal he had made on the mountain. The reason was above him, eating moss. But he noticed that he would have refused it without them. A point of his under that lip would have made the seventh narrowing safe for one cart on one morning, and then he would have had to write *yes* in a column that a ford-keeper was paying to believe; and the next wheel over it would have found only the crust.

"I want to measure it again," he said. "Myself."

Karis looked at him. Then she held out the cord.

"That's what the second hand is for."

He lay where she had lain, with the edge of the mountain under his breastbone and a long blue emptiness in front of his face, and he drew the cord tight from the foot of the rib to the last sound inch of the rim. He found the knot. It was five feet nine.

He had expected nothing else, for he had never known her figure and his own to differ. But he understood, getting up, what she had given him at the slab two days before. She had not asked for his hand because hers was tired. She had asked for it so that a number on that table could be doubted by somebody before a wheel was put on it; and it was an odd use to be wanted for, by a person who had once kept an index of everything he could do, and he liked it better than any of the rest.

They took her through an inch at a time. Seln knelt at the off wheel with his cheek nearly on the rock, watching the rim against a scratch that Karis had made, and lifted one finger for every hand's breadth of travel. Cael had the shafts alone, so that there should be one pair of feet to steer by. Brom leaned on the strap. It took a quarter of an hour to go four yards, and the only sounds were the dry hub and five people breathing through their mouths.

When the tail was clear, Lira let go of the frame and wiped both palms on her breeches.

"Seven," whispered Karis. "Five feet nine. A hand and a half, outward. Sound for a foot at the rim and crust beyond. Cart: yes. This cart, with a man at each wheel who has nothing else in his hands." She waited while he wrote. "And under that: *a wagon of five feet six between the hubs, no.* I want him to have the number where the yes stops."

---

Seln had said that he did not like the eighth, and when they came to it Cael saw why he had refused to say more.

It was wide. That was the first thing, and it was nearly a disappointment after the seventh; the shelf ran out from the wall a good nine feet, sound slab from the face to the rim, and the pitch was no worse than a hand.

It was simply not all there. A crack went across it from the wall to the edge, as straight as a sawn board, and the two sides of the crack stood two feet apart.

He looked down into it. The sides went down smooth and close, without a ledge, and where they passed out of the light there was no floor that he could see. Karis let a pebble go, and it clicked from one wall to the other for a count of three and was still clicking when he lost it.

"It's clean," said Brom. He had got down on one knee and laid his right hand on the western edge, and then on the eastern. "Look at that. It's not crumbled. The frost's got into a joint and opened it like a book." He sat back. "That's the same height both sides, to a finger. If there was a plank across it you'd never know."

"We've a tail-board," said Lira. "And his seat."

"Elm, and three feet long, with a wheel on the middle of it? She'd go through them like bread." He shook his head. "There's nothing on this shelf to fill it with, and nothing to bridge it. A wheel that size'll drop a hand into a two-foot gap and come up against the far lip, and the far lip's a knife."

"Then it stops here," said Karis. Her voice had gone thin. "At the fifty-ninth hundred. With the end in sight."

"I never said that." Brom was looking at the cart as he had looked at a rung of ash in a barn. "It wants carrying."

"No," said Karis.

"It's two feet. It's one stride."

"You are not to lift. A woman with a needle told you so, and I wrote it down."

"She told me not to pull, and that the wrist would say if I had. I carried a box up a fan on my back, and you looked at the wrist yourself, and it was dry." He got up. "A cart's a box with wheels on. It sits on me. There's nothing hanging from a hand."

Karis opened her mouth and shut it. She turned to Cael, as people turned to him when there was a ruling to be given.

He had it ready, and it was not the one that she expected. On any floor he had ever stood on he would have decided this himself, and in a breath, since the plan was always his. But the thing in front of him was a weight and a gap, and whether a back could take the one over the other; and there was a person on that shelf who had known such sums since he was twelve years old, and it was not Cael.

"It's a load," he said. "Loads are his. If he says it goes, it goes, and he tells us where to stand."

Brom gave him a slow look, as if a thing had been set in his arms that he had not thought to be handed. Then he nodded, and began to give orders, very low.

The boxes went first, passed across the crack from two pairs of hands to two more. He had the cart emptied to her boards. He folded his coat into a pad and laid it on the back of his neck, and he had Seln and Cael step over the gap and take a shaft each from the far side.

"When I say, you lift the shafts to your shoulders, and you walk. Don't pull her. I'm bringing her. You're only keeping her nose up." He looked round. "She'll want to tip back when I've got her, and that tail-board'll swing."

He did not say what was to be done about that. He crouched, and went in under the bed between the wheels, backward, until the axle-tree lay across his shoulders on the folded coat. He set his hands on his knees.

"Now."

Cael lifted. He felt the shaft come light in his hands, and then lighter, and he looked back along it and saw the wheels leave the rock.

Brom had stood up under her.

He had not straightened altogether; he was bent like a man beneath a low beam, and the cords of his neck stood out, and every part of the cart that was not on two shoulders at the front was on his. It was the whole of her. Cael watched the two wrists, on the two knees, and waited to see Iron Skin start at them, as he had seen the grey start at a ferry and a wash whenever a weight came on suddenly. Thirty animals were scraping on the hill above, and there was a page in Karis's coat that said what a start might be worth.

The wrists stayed the colour of wrists.

Brom took two short steps to the edge. Then he took one long one, with his left boot going out over two feet of nothing and coming down on the western lip, and the cart went over the crack on a man's back. The tail-board swung out on its chains as she tipped.

It did not strike. Lira had a hand under it on the near side, and Karis had the back of her wrist against it on the off, and they walked it across between them and laid it against the frame as one shuts a door on a sleeping house. Nobody had told either of them to be there. He had been looking at Brom, and had not seen them move.

The wheels came down on rock. Brom backed out from under her on his knees and stayed there, breathing.

"Carrying," he said, when he could.

"I've stopped arguing," said Karis. "I'm only counting now." She had his left arm turned to the light already. The cloth was dry at the wrist, and she let it go. "Eight. Nine feet. Broken across, clean, two feet wide, no bottom found. Cart: taken over on one man's back, with four to steady her." She stopped. "And then I shall write that a carter by himself does not cross it, and that two planks of oak might serve, eight feet long, and that I haven't tried them."

"Put that it didn't bang," said Lira.

"That isn't a measurement."

"It's the best thing I've done all week. Put it."

Beyond the eighth the mountain let go of the bench at last, and it opened like a hand.

He had lived for five days on a shelf that he could have thrown a stone across. Now the wall on his left fell back in a long curve and came round again two hundred yards on, and between the wall and the drop there lay a bay forty yards deep, floored from side to side with fine loose shale that had come down the face and spread. The slope above it was the same roof-pitch of plates, but lower; the upper shelf ran across it not three hundred yards up. He did not need to count what was on that shelf, and he counted it all the same, and it came to thirty.

Seln had a fire laid, and he was sitting beside it with his hands on his knees.

He had chosen the eastern corner of the bay, hard against the eighth narrowing, where a slab leaned out from the wall and water ran down the rock behind it into a basin the size of a hat. The cart stood on sound stone there, and that was as well, for she would have floated on every yard of the bay's floor.

"You'll have seen the rock," said Seln.

Cael had. Nobody could have missed it. Two-thirds of the way along the bay a blade of pale stone stood up out of the shale, quite alone, halfway between the foot of the face and the edge. It lay square across the fall of the ground, like a wall across a field, and it was as long as a barn and no thicker than a garden wall. At either end it was no higher than a man's waist, and in the middle it was twice his height, and the evening sun lay along its upper side and left the lower black.

"I walked round it this afternoon. The shale is heaped against the upper side of it to the height of my chest, and it has gone round both ends, as water goes round a pier. On the lower side there is bare floor, a piece as large as this camp, with nothing lying on it at all." Seln turned his hands over to warm the backs. "That has not happened once. A slide has come down this face and gone round that rock a good many times, and never yet filled the place behind it. I should have slept there, if it had water. I do not care to carry a skin seventy yards across loose stone in the dark."

"A fin," said Karis. She was shading her eyes. "It's a harder bed, standing on its edge. I've seen it drawn."

# Chapter 19 — The Loud Thing

There was frost on the bay at first light, and Brom wanted the cart across it before the sun came.

The narrow wheels cut two clean lines in the rime. The hub ticked, and the far wall gave it back.

He had walked a mountain for seven days with nine things shut in him, writing down what a cord and a clock and a girl's feet could tell him, and not one line of it had wanted anything that he was. He had chosen every place they slept on that mountain, and he had not wished to sleep in this one's middle.

She was. She went out along the ledge under its roof of rock, close in, and from that distance there was nothing to show that one foot came down later than the other; she looked like a thing that the mountain had been keeping that shelf for. He made himself look at the fin. It stood ahead of them and a little to the right, pale in the half light, with the frozen shale banked along its upper side, and he judged that they were thirty yards short of its nearer end.

He said it as he would have told somebody that a horse was lame. Then he ran back for it.

"Carried," he said, to nobody. "Both of them. It was the lifting them out did it. I pulled."

"Here," he said. "She is here. Come when you can, and not before."

He could, after a fashion. He got his back against the stone and worked along it, a foot at a time, with his left arm clamped to his side and his left leg leaving a mark on the clean floor. The middle of the wall went by over his head, tall and pale. It took him as long as it had taken thirty animals to come down a mountain.

She had almost reached it. She lay on her front in the grey spill of the other stream, with one arm stretched toward the rock and her cheek on the shale, and from the knees down he could not see her. A plate of the mountain lay across her legs. It was as long as a door and as thick as his wrist.

Her eyes were open, and they were looking at him.

She had liked the ledge from the moment she saw it.

She would not have told the boy so. He would have written it down. But she had stood at the end of that bay in the evening light with her arms folded, looking at three hundred yards of clean level rock, and something in her had sat up, as it used to sit up at the sight of a chalked lane.

It was not the same thing, and she knew that. Nobody was going to call a time. There was no slate, and there was no girl in the next lane to be beaten by a tenth. She would run to the far end of a shelf and come back and say *slab, dry, six feet*, and a boy with a pencil would write it in a column. A carter she would never meet would put a wheel there or not, on her word.

She had done it sixty-two times. Somewhere about the thirtieth she had noticed that she no longer wanted him to count.

That had surprised her more than the mountain. For a week after a fire above a wash she had gone about with a question in her like a stone in a boot, the one she had put to four people and nobody could answer. She had not got an answer. She had got a job. Each morning there was a hundred yards of ground that nobody alive had tested, and she was the one who went onto it first, at speed, to learn whether it would bear; and when she walked back and said that it would, four people and three boxes moved forward onto the place where her feet had been.

It was the only use she had ever found for being fast that was not somebody else's ladder.

So she bound the ankle in the dark, on the seventy-eighth morning, and ate nothing, as she had eaten nothing before a card. The foot was what it was. It bent less than the other and later, and she had stopped being angry with it somewhere on the bench and begun simply to allow for it, as one allows for a wind.

She set her feet at the ledge's mouth, and went.

The rock was a gift. It was dry under its roof, and rimed along the outer half where the roof stopped, so she kept in, close under the wall, as she had promised a fire that she would. Her left foot struck and her right came after it. She had the count in her legs. Forty. Fifty. The gorge went by on her right hand, blue and enormous, and she gave it none of her attention; she was reading the shelf five yards ahead of her toes, for cracks and for colour, as she had read a sprung floor.

She saw no crack. She would say so afterward to anyone who asked, and nobody ever did.

At eighty the wall on her left hand moved away from her.

That was how it seemed. She had been running a hand's breadth from it, and between one stride and the next there was a gap there the width of her arm, with daylight in it. The shelf under her had come off the mountain in one piece and was leaning out.

She did not decide anything.

She had been told at Fenmark that a burst was a choice, and at Norhold she had made a rank of choosing well. For sixty days on gravel bars and roads she had refused it a stride at a time, on purpose, and she had been proud of how good she had got. Not one of those refusals was anywhere near her now. The ground went, and her body did what a body with Wind in it does when the ground goes, and it did not send up to ask.

The air broke round her. She felt it in her teeth.

Then there was rock under both feet, and she was standing on it, twenty feet back, with her arms out and her heart going like a mill. Behind her the ledge roared down into the gorge. She had not chosen where to come down, either. Her feet had found sound stone under the roof by themselves, and stopped dead on it, as she had spent a month on a river bar teaching them to stop.

*I'm alive*, she thought, with real surprise.

Then the far wall answered her.

It was her own burst, coming home. It crossed three hundred yards of empty air and came back from the cliff late and clean, and it did not sound like a thing she had done. It sounded like a whip in somebody else's hand.

She understood it then, in the pit of her stomach, before she had turned her head.

There was a page with a comma struck out of it. She had been lying on a slab when it was written. *It will answer a declaration in the same way.* She had asked for a test, a seed, half a breath; and a small pale person had looked up a hill and declined.

She looked up the hill.

Thirty heads were lifted, all of them, turned away from her.

---

She ran.

There was nothing else to do with what she had done. The cart was a toy on the white floor of the bay, with four specks beside it, and one of the specks had its arm out. She could not hear what it said. She did not need to, for the arm was pointing at the long pale rock.

It was sixty yards of ledge to the bay and forty more of shale to the wall's near end, and she had run farther than that to catch a dog.

Wind said *now*.

It said it at the second stride, quite reasonably. It had just saved her life and it could not see why it was not wanted again. Twenty feet at a time, five times, and she would be behind that stone before the first of them was halfway down.

*No.*

She knew what a second one was. She had sat in a loft and heard it laid out with a charred stick. *Never two at once.* A burst would crack over that bay like the first, and thirty animals that were running from one loud thing would have another to run from, and it would be going off beside the only shelter on the mountain. She had been the loud thing once this morning without being asked. She would not be it a second time by choice.

She refused it at the third stride and the fourth. It was harder than it had been at the ford, where she had only been afraid of a dog.

The right foot came down late. She allowed for it. The ledge poured back under her, and the hill on her left was no longer a hill; it was brown, and moving, and the sound of it came down ahead of it and pressed on her ears. She came off the rock onto the shale and it broke under her like the crust on snow.

Thirty yards.

A small figure stood at the wall's far end with its hands out. She saw it across the whole length of the pale rock, no bigger than a doll, and she knew whose hands they were.

The mountain caught fire.

She had watched those points go down in barns and annexes and on the end wall of a whitewashed room, a dozen at a time, each no bigger than a barley grain. She had seen them joined in threes, and once, at a sitting, sixteen. She had never seen a road. It stood up across the whole face above the rock, two straight burning lines that met in a point high on the slope, as wide as a street and as long as a field; and the animals coming down on it split like a log under a wedge.

*She's staked the whole page on it*, thought Lira, running. *With us underneath.*

Twenty yards. The near arm of the fire came down the slope ahead of her and stopped a little beyond the wall's end, and on this side of it, on her side, the right-hand stream was coming.

She was outside the line. She saw that with great clearness. Ten yards more would put her under the rock, and she did not have ten yards; she had perhaps six, and a foot that was a beat behind.

*Now*, said Wind, for the last time, and it was not reasonable any longer. It was begging.

She told it no, and ran the six yards on her feet.

Something the size of a cart went behind her so close that the wind of it turned her half round. She kept her legs. She was three strides from the stone, with her arm already out for it, when the shale that the thing had brought down with it took her ankles from under her like a wave on a beach, and she went onto her face.

She felt the plate come.

It was not quick. It slid up over her calves on the moving stone as a lid slides onto a box, and settled, and the weight of the hill behind it leaned. Her left leg was in a hollow and it only pinned. Her right was lying across something hard.

It went below the knee, with a duller sound than the ankle had made at a river. She knew at once that it was a different thing.

After that she lay with her cheek on the mountain and watched the stream go by. It took a long time and no time. Grey backs passed at the level of her eyes, and stones ran over her outstretched hand without hurting it, and none of it seemed to have much to do with her. The fire went out. She noticed that, and thought, *She's done*, and was sorry.

Then it was quiet, and the gorge was giving something back.

She could not have said how long it was before the boy came. He came along the lower side of the rock, on his feet and hardly so, with one arm shut against his ribs and his left leg dragging; and he was the colour of the dust. He got down beside her by letting the wall take him. His eyes went over her a piece at a time, as they always did. They reached the plate, and stopped.

She had one thing to say, and this time she did not pick it with any care.

"That was me," said Lira. Her mouth was full of grit. "The loud thing."

He did not tell her that it was not. She watched him not tell her. He had never once made her a present of a thing that was untrue, at a fire or anywhere, and she saw him go through what had happened in its order, as he would have gone through a bout.

"It was the ledge. The ledge went. Then it was you."

She turned that over, with her face on the stone. It had the ledge in it, first, where the ledge belonged. It had her in it second, and it did not leave her out.

"Then it was me."

So Seln drove the ash haft of the bill under the slab's edge and bore down on it, and Brom knelt in the shale and got his right shoulder beneath the corner that rose. "Now," said Brom, through his teeth. "Whoever's got her."

Cael had her wrists. It was all that was left for him to do, and he could do it sitting, with his back against the pale rock. He drew, and she pushed with her elbows, and she came toward him over the plates a foot at a time with her face shut like a fist.

The left leg came out whole, and the right came out without its boot.

He saw the boot for a breath, down in the dark under the slab, with the lace still tied. Then Brom let the corner go, because he had to, and the plate settled, and the shale ran in round it and over it with a long sigh. By the time the dust had gone there was nothing in that place but hill.

"That was a good boot," said Lira. Her voice was very small. "I'd only just got it to stop rubbing."

They carried her round the end of the wall onto the bare floor. Nobody remarked on the leg until she was lying flat, and then there was no need to. Below the knee the stocking was whole, and under it the shin took a small wrong turn, a hand's breadth down, where no shin has a joint.

"It's not come through," said Brom. He had looked at a good many hurts on a good many yards, and he said it as the one thing in sight that was better than it might have been. "I'll not pull it straight, for I don't know how, and I'd do it badly. But it wants keeping as it is, till there's somebody who does."

He chose the splints himself, and they were the mountain's own; he found two plates in the bank above the wall, each as long as her shin and no thicker than a slate, and he laid one down either side of the leg. They had no canvas to tear, for the canvas had gone with the cart. Cael's coat had lost its left sleeve at a river. He got the knife into the seam of the right one, and Seln cut that as well, and Brom bound the two plates on with it in three places, one-handed, with Seln's fingers for the knots.

"There," he said. "You've a roof on."

"Like them," said Lira, and shut her eyes.

Cael took his inventory then, because it was the start of something, and because he could not think what else to do with the fear.

There were three ribs. He knew the number without touching them; he had heard it. They were low on the left side, and they let him breathe in to a certain depth and then they stopped him, quite firmly, as a hand stops a door. The calf had opened along the line of the old bite. Seln had taken off his own neckcloth and bound it, and the cloth was already dark.

Brom's forearm had parted at the wrist for the length of a finger, where it had parted once before above a wash.

Karis sat where she had sat down, and she had spoken twice since then and no more. When Seln went to her and put his coat round her shoulders she let him, and her eyes followed his hands as though they were a long way off.

There were three boxes. He counted them a second time, since they had cost what they had cost. In the third, under the masonry chips, there was a bag that had not been opened since the twelfth day, and it would buy nothing whatever on this shelf.

Then he counted what they had to eat, and that took less time. Brom had slung the bread bag on his own back that morning, from habit, and it held a loaf and a half and the hard end of the bacon. Seln had his flint, a twist of salt, and a water-skin a third full. Everything else had been on the cart.

---

Karis could not light the lamp.

It was her own, the small brass reading lantern that lived in the left-hand box with her pens, and when the dusk came down she asked for it. They were the first words she had said since morning. Seln set it in front of her with the flint and steel beside it, and she took one in each hand, as she had taken them a thousand times.

Her hands would not do it. He watched her bring the steel down on the flint, and there was no strength in the stroke at all; it slid off, and no spark came. She tried again, with no better result, and the third time the flint dropped out of her fingers onto the floor, and she sat looking at it.

"I've set nothing, and I want that understood," she said. "I didn't try a point; I only looked for the place I'd have set one from." She turned her right hand over. "It's like feeling in a coat for a pocket, and finding that somebody has taken the pocket out."

"It'll come back," said Brom.

"You don't know that, and I don't know it either. I've never spent it all before; nobody at Ternhall would have let me." Her voice had no fright in it, and that was worse than fright. "Nine points and eight channels, held for eight seconds across sixty yards. I should be able to give you the cost of that to a decimal. I can't find the figure. I can't find where I keep figures."

Brom picked up the flint and lit the lamp for her, with two strokes, and set it where the light fell on her knees. She thanked him. Then she lay down on her side with her back to the wall, in Seln's coat, and was asleep before he had drawn his hand away.

It was a cold night, and they had no blankets; the frost came down out of a clear sky, and they lay close under the rock in a row, the hurt ones in the middle, and kept one another warm as cattle do.

Cael did not sleep much. He could not lie on his left side, and on his right the ribs hung, and so he sat against the stone and watched the bay by starlight.

The herd was still there, though he could not have said how many of it. They stood here and there on the level, among the spilled shale, grey on grey, and through the first hours of the dark he heard them moving, a few slow steps and a clatter of horn and then quiet. By the middle of the night the sounds had spread themselves out along the foot of the face into something very like a line, and toward morning one of them began to scrape.

They had lost their hill and some of their number, and by the next light they would be mowing what was left. He could not hate them for it. They had run from a noise, as anything runs.

Above the wall, in the dark, a plate let go by itself and slid, and stopped; and a little later another.

"It's still coming," said Lira.

He had thought her asleep. She was lying on her back with her right leg laid straight in its two slates and her face turned up to the stars.

"A bit at a time. I've been counting them, and that's eleven since the lamp went out."

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes." She thought about it. "It's a different hurt from the ankle. That one was a thing I'd done, and this is only a thing that's there."

He knew what she was telling him. He had given her the order of it at the wall's end, the ledge and then herself, and she had been turning it over ever since to see whether it would bear.

"Karis turned thirty animals with a page she'd not tested," said Lira presently. "And Brom carried two boxes he's not allowed to lift. And you went back for the third and stood under one of those things with your hands shut. I saw the marks where you came down." A plate slid, somewhere above. "I ran away from it, and I was the only one who did what I was supposed to."

"You ran a hundred yards on a late foot without bursting. With a herd coming."

"I know what I did. I'm saying it's a queer morning when the one who made the noise is the one who kept the rule."

"That's twelve," said Cael.

She breathed out, and it was nearly a laugh.

---

It rained on the seventy-ninth day, a thin cold rain out of the west, and nobody wrote.

He had the book, for Hesk's satchel had been on his shoulder when the mountain came, as it was always on his shoulder, and it had gone under him when he fell and taken no harm. He opened it once, in the grey of the morning, and found the page with the last entry. It ended on the word *Sleep*, and above that he had written that the job had no fight at the end of it.

He read it through, and he was not ashamed of it, for it had been true when the pencil made it; but he could find no sentence to set beneath it that was a thing counted, and he would not put down one that was only a thing felt. He shut the book, and that day got no line either.

Karis made no move toward her notebook either. She slept, and woke to drink, and slept again; and each time she woke she looked at her two hands before she looked at anything else.

Brom had the bread.

He had taken charge of it without a word, and he made a ceremony of the dividing that was plainly meant to give five people something to watch. He cut the loaf and a half into fifteen with Lira's long knife, on the lid of the middle box, and laid the pieces out in three rows.

"That's today, and tomorrow, and the day after. Five a row." He pointed with the knife. "The bacon's for whoever's walking. I've done it in my head six ways and that's the one that comes out even."

"And the day after that?" said Lira.

"The day after that somebody's walked."

It was Seln who would walk, and they all knew it. He was the only one of the five with two legs, two hands and his wits about him, and he had spent the first night without a coat, saying nothing of it.

He went for water first. The seep was at the bay's eastern corner, a hundred and thirty yards from the rock, and every yard of the way lay under the stripped face, where the plates were still coming down singly, with no warning but a small dry tick. Cael watched him cross it without once hurrying. He stopped whenever a slide began, and judged its line, and stood still until it had gone by, sometimes within a few feet of him; and he came back in the rain with the skin full and grey grit to his knees.

"I should not send anybody else," was all he said. "It wants a kind of patience that is unpleasant to learn."

In the afternoon he went the other way.

He was gone three hours. When he came round the end of the wall the light was failing, and he sat down on his heels in front of Cael, and Cael understood that he was being given a report, unasked, since it bore.

"The ledge is whole for eighty yards from the bay," said Seln. "Beyond that it has gone, for thirty-one paces; I counted them, since I had to place a foot for each. What is left there is not a shelf. It is a lip of rock, where the shelf was rooted in the wall; four feet wide at the best of it, and tilted outward, and covered with what fell on it."

"Does it go?"

"It went for me, and I crossed it twice. Some of the stones on it are bedded and some are waiting to leave, and I could not tell the one from the other by looking; so I tried each with my weight before I trusted it, and I scratched a mark on every one that bore." He took out the narrow dull knife and showed its point, which was bright. "There are forty-four. A careful person may go over on those, facing the wall."

"A person with a leg in slates?" said Lira.

"No."

"Or three ribs?"

Seln considered Cael, and did not soften it.

"One at a time, perhaps, with a hand on his belt. Not this week." He put the knife away. "Beyond the lip the ledge is sound again to the shoulder, and from the shoulder a track goes down, an old one, steep. I went a little way. There is a road in the valley and I saw nobody on it. I judge it a day and a half to the road for a man alone, and I cannot say how far along the road to a roof."

So it was settled as Brom's bread had settled it. Seln would go down at first light with the bacon, and bring back whatever he found that had hands. The four would stay under the rock. Nobody said aloud how many rows there were on the lid of the box.

Lira's leg was hot that night. Cael felt it through the stocking when he reached across in the dark to find the water for her; the skin between the two slates burned like a stone that has lain in the sun, and she was shivering with her teeth shut.

"Don't count them tonight," he said.

"I'm at six," said Lira. "It keeps my mind off."

---

He saw the mule before he saw the woman.

It was the middle of the eightieth day, and the rain had gone through, and he had got himself propped at the western end of the rock, where he could watch the ledge. He could do that much. He had been watching it since Seln went out along it in the first grey light, a narrow dark figure under the overhang, and passed from sight where the shelf was broken.

Now something was coming back.

It was Seln, and he was walking in front, at an easy pace, with his hands clasped behind him; and behind Seln there were two long brown ears, and under the ears a small neat mule with a pack on it as high as its own back. And beside the mule, on the outer side of it, between the animal and three hundred feet of air, there walked a woman.

Cael read her as he read anybody, from the feet.

She walked at a carter's pace. It was the pace that he had fallen in behind on the fifth day, which is neither quick nor slow and can be kept from dark to dark; she did not lengthen it when the bay opened in front of her, or when she saw four people lying under a rock. She had one hand laid on the mule's shoulder. She kept herself on the side where the drop was and the beast on the side where the wall was, and when the ledge widened she did not change over.

She was not tall, but she was square in the shoulder and broad in the hand, and she wore a man's coat of some thick brown stuff that had been made for a bigger person and taken in. Her hair was cropped as short as a boy's. He thought that she was perhaps ten years older than himself, and he could not have said why he was sure it was not more.

On top of the pack, lashed along it in an oiled cover, there was something with the shape of a crossbow.

She brought the mule across the shale to the end of the wall and stopped it with a word, a stride and a half from where Lira lay. Her eyes went over the five of them a single time, and it did not take her as long as it takes to count five. He saw her eyes go to the slates on a leg, and to a dark cloth at a wrist, and to the way that he himself was sitting, with one arm shut against his side; and they rested longest on Karis, who had nothing wrong with her that could be seen.

"Who's worst?"

It was a low voice, and a little hoarse, like a voice that was not much used on the road.

She had not asked who they were, or what had happened to them, or how five people came to be lying under a rock at the top of a mountain with three boxes. Cael noticed that. He had been met at gates and counters and tables for as long as he could remember, and at every one of them the first question had been what he was; this woman had walked a day and a half up a hill to put a different one, and it was the only one she appeared to want answered.

"Her," said Brom, and pointed at Lira.

"I'm fine," said Lira.

"You're not. I can see your leg from here." The woman was already kneeling. She had Cael's coat sleeve unknotted in the time it took to say it, and she lifted off the upper slate and looked at what was under it without touching. "Who put these on?"

"I did," said Brom.

"They've kept it. That's all they were asked to do." She glanced up at Lira's face, as one looks at the head of a horse before going round behind it. "Name's Oryn."

"Are you a healer?" said Lira. Her teeth were knocking together. "A woman at a gate said there wasn't one. She said the nearest walked a route."

"Tide. Healer."

"She said the rest of you keep to the country where somebody ranks you."

"Most do."

"Do they rank you?"

The woman had laid two fingers on the shin, a hand's breadth below the knee, as lightly as on the string of an instrument.

"Iron. Four, if anybody's keeping a book up here."

"Nobody is," said Lira.

"I shall be," said a faint voice from under Seln's coat, "as soon as I can hold the pencil."

---

She took off the second slate, and then she put both hands round the leg and shut her eyes.

Her lips moved. Cael watched them, having nothing else to do, and found that she was counting; she went to ten, slowly, and stopped, and opened her eyes.

"It's broken through, clean, a hand below the knee," she said. "The one bone, and the other's whole. The ends have slipped by each other the width of my thumb. I have to bring them back to where they belong before I mend it, or I mend it short, and you walk on that for the rest of your life." She looked at Brom. "You. Behind her. Take her under the arms and don't let her come with me."

Brom got down on the floor and took Lira against his chest.

"That's the part you'll mind."

She said it to Lira, plainly, as a ferryman says that the next step is wet. Then she set one hand above the place and one at the ankle, over the dirty linen, and leaned back with her whole weight, steadily, as one draws a bucket.

Lira made one sound, and it was not loud. Thirty yards off, out on the level, a beaked head came up and went down again.

"There," said Oryn. "That's it over. You'll not get worse than that from me."

She did not let go. She shifted her hands until both of them lay round the shin where the wrong turn had been, with the fingers overlapping, and she settled on her heels, and her face went quiet.

There was nothing to see. He had half expected a light, or a colour, as there was grey in Iron Skin and yellow in a point; and there was a square brown woman kneeling in the dust with her hands on a girl's leg, breathing slowly. A minute went by, and another, and the only thing that changed was that the sweat came out on her forehead, though the air was cold.

"What are you doing?" said Karis. She had got up on one elbow. "I want it described, for I can't write, but I can remember till I can."

"Finding where it's stopped."

"Where what has?"

"What she mends with. Everybody's got it. It goes round you like water round a mill, and when you break a bone it does the knitting, if you leave it be for six weeks." Oryn spoke in pieces, between breaths, with her eyes shut. "At a break it stands, since it can't get by, and it piles up, and that's the heat you felt in this last night. I find where it's standing. Then I open the way with mine, and hers goes through, and I hold it open while it does the six weeks."

"All of the six weeks?"

"All of them. That's what I pay. It doesn't come from nowhere."

"Cold," said Lira suddenly.

Her eyes had opened wide, and she was staring at the pale rock over her head.

"No. It's going along."

"Then lie still and let it," said Oryn.

It took the greater part of an hour, and he counted it by the shadow of the wall, which crept the width of two hands across the floor. After the first of it the hollow was silent. Brom held Lira as he would have held a full pail, and Seln stood at the mule's head with the rein in his fingers; and Cael watched a stranger's face, and saw it grow a little older while he looked.

When she took her hands away, the shin was straight.

Lira lifted her head to see it. She moved the toes of her right foot, in the torn stocking, all five.

Oryn did not get up. She turned herself about on the floor, slowly, and let herself down with her back against the rock, a little at a time, as a carter sets down a load whose weight he has known for years.

"That's one," she said.

"One what?" said Brom.

"One mending. A Tide has a reserve, like a purse, and it's spent at the rate of what it mends. Mine holds three in a day, if I've slept under a roof and somebody's fed me, and on a road it holds two." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "After that the reserve's gone, and I'm a woman with a mule."

"Could you do his from where you sit?" said Lira. She was looking at Brom's wrist. "So you needn't move."

"Hands on. That's the only way it works."

She was unlacing her own right boot as she spoke. She drew it off, and the stocking after it, and turned the foot to look. The heel had been rubbed raw in a patch the size of a coin, and over the patch there was a strip of some stiff linen stuck down smooth.

"And I can't do it to me."

She pressed the strip smooth with her thumb and put the stocking on again.

---

She ate before she touched anybody else.

It was her own bread, out of the mule's off pannier, a dark close loaf from some hold's oven, and she broke it in six and gave five pieces away before she bit into hers. There was a cheese as well, and four onions. Brom looked at the three rows on the lid of the box, and then at the pannier, and swept the rows back into his bag without a word.

Then she had his arm across her knee.

"How old's this?"

"Twenty-seven days. I counted the stitches in and I counted them out."

"Then it should have closed in a month and hasn't, because you've pulled on it."

"I carried," said Brom. "Mostly."

"You pulled this week. I can feel where." She had her hands round the wrist already, and it took her a quarter of the time that the leg had taken. When she let go there was a seam on it, pink and dry, from the heel of the hand to the middle of the forearm, and it lay closed along its whole length. "That's two, and that's my day. You'll want telling what it's good for, and I'll tell you tomorrow, when I can think."

She went to Karis last of the three, on her knees, and read her as she had read the leg, with two fingers on the inside of a thin wrist and two on the side of the neck. Her lips moved to ten.

"Ember. And nothing. You're empty."

"I know the word. I didn't know that it could be felt."

"It's like a well you drop a stone in, and hear it land on the bottom dry." She sat back. "What did you do?"

Nobody answered at once. Then Seln lifted his chin at the slope above the wall.

The shale still lay banked there, in the shelter of the rock, where neither stream had carried it off; and across it, from a point high up down to either hand, ran two straight black lines. The plates along them had been burnt to the colour of iron.

Oryn looked at that for some time.

"How long did you hold it?"

"Eight seconds," said Cael. "I counted."

"Hm."

It was all she said of it, and she turned back to Karis.

"That isn't mine to mend, for nothing's broken. It fills again or it doesn't, and it fills quicker asleep and fed. Don't ask it for so much as a spark for a week." She got to her feet with a hand on the wall, and her eyes went to Seln. "Those were your scratches, on the lip."

"They were."

"They were right. I put the mule's feet on four of them."

Seln inclined his head. He looked, Cael thought, like a man who has had a report read by somebody competent, and it had been a long time since that had happened to him.

"Can I stand on it?" said Lira. She had been flexing the foot all this while, a little at a time, with the wonder of a person who has been given back a thing she had already begun to grieve for.

"Tomorrow, with somebody's arm. It's knit, and it's sound, but it's new, and new bone's like new bread; it wants a day before you cut it." Oryn looked down at the leg as a wheelwright looks at a spoke he has just driven. "It'll walk in a week as well as the other. You'll not run on it for a month, and I can see by your face that you were about to ask."

"I wasn't," said Lira, who had been.

---

She came to Cael when the others were eating.

"You sat up to watch me come in," she said. "So you weren't worst. Coat."

He got it open with one hand. She knelt on his left, and her fingers went in under the shirt and lay along his side, low, where the three small sounds had been; they were warm and very steady, and her lips began to move.

He counted with her. *One. Two.* It was the first time since he crossed the Line that anybody had laid a hand on him to learn what he was. *Three. Four.*

At five the reading stopped.

She took both hands away and held them palm upward in front of her, and studied them. It was the look of a carpenter whose rule has just given him a length that cannot be. Then she wiped them on her coat, and breathed, and put them back on his side, with the fingers spread wider; and this time she did not count at all.

He watched her face, and he knew what it was doing, for he had seen a brass plate in a district hall wait eleven seconds over a boy of fourteen before it lit. He had seen assessors look at a panel with that same small stillness, and a hooded seat, and a clerk with a form that had no box for him. It was what an instrument looked like when it reached for the thing it was made to read, and closed on air.

She was not an instrument, and that was the difference. She took her hands away a second time and sat back on her heels, and she looked straight at him.

"Your ribs are broken," she said. "I can feel that they're broken. I can't feel *you*."

Behind her nobody was eating any longer. Lira had come up on both elbows, and Brom sat with the bread halfway to his mouth; Seln's mild eyes were on the back of the healer's cropped head, and under his coat Karis had gone perfectly still.

"Whatever you are, I can't read it."

He waited for the next thing, since he knew the order in which it came. First the instrument failed, and then somebody asked him what he was, and wrote the answer down, and the answer became a word on a file.

The question did not come.

"I've never said that to anyone. Tape them. They'll heal on their own."

She was frowning at his side, as at a sum that had come out oddly, and she spoke the rest as though she were reading it off the bone.

"They always have, haven't they?"

"Yes," he said.

"That's not an answer," Oryn said. "That's a *finding*."

He knew that word. It was Karis's, and she kept it for a thing that had been looked at carefully and written down with nothing added. He had been a good many words in his time. He had been a flag on a board and a level on an advisory, and one word in capitals that a registry had settled on when it could find no tier; and every one of them had been a verdict, given by people who were angry with him for not fitting. This woman's hands had come back empty twice, and she had only said so, and put a name on the emptiness that made no claim about him at all.

She got up and fetched clean cloth from her pannier, and a flask; and she unwound Seln's neckcloth from his calf and washed what was under it, and bound it again in her own linen, tight and neat. She put no hand round it to count.

"I can't mend what's in you, since I can't find where it's standing," she said. "So you get what a carter gets. Cloth, and tape in the morning." She knotted the end. "A man at the foot of this hill told me the mountain had come down two days back, and that a crew off the Lowmarch plank had gone up it with a cart and not come off the other side. He said you were late."

"We were," said Brom.

It was dusk before Cael opened the book, and it was easier than it had been in the rain. He had five things that were counted.

*Eightieth day. Lira's leg is straight. The boxes are ours. The cart is in the gorge. A healer came up the road because a carter said we were late.*

He looked at that, and added what belonged with it.

*The herd's dead: heard, not sworn, which is Karis's wording. She cannot hold a pencil again, and will not be able to set a point for a week.*

*The healer put her hands on my ribs and could not read me. She told me so to my face, and asked nothing.*

*Deployment since the Line: one.*

On the far side of the hollow the healer was asleep already. She slept sitting up, against the mule's pack, with one boot off and her hands turned palm upward on her knees.

She taped him at first light, before she had eaten, and she talked the whole time to somebody else.

He held. She drew the strip from his breastbone round under the arm and on to the spine, firmly, in one pull, and smoothed it down with the heel of her hand before he had finished being surprised. The second went on above it, lapping the first by half, and she laid five in all.

"Breathe."

He breathed in; and at a certain depth, a little short of where the ribs had stopped him yesterday, the tape drew tight across his side like a hand laid there.

"That's all it does," she said, still to Karis. "I want you to have that right, if you mean to write it. It mends nothing, and it doesn't hold the bone, for you can't hold a rib; it's got to move, or he stops breathing. People take it for a splint, and it isn't one."

"Then what is it for?" said Lira. She was propped against a box with her right leg stretched out in front of her, bare from the knee, and she had not stopped looking at it since she woke.

"It tells the ribs where the edge is."

Oryn bit off the linen and tucked the roll into her coat.

"He'll go to draw a full breath, or turn to pick something up, and that pulls before the bone does, so he stops there. He'll have learned it by tonight, and he'll hate it by noon tomorrow, and it stays on till I take it off." She looked at him at last. "Twenty-one days from the morning they went, and I want to be the one who lifts it. Can you count that far?"

"He can count anything," said Brom. "It's stopping him that's the work."

"Why tape for him," said Karis slowly, "and hands for her? You had two mendings yesterday and you've two this morning, and a rib is a bone like a shin."

Cael had been waiting for that question, and he saw that the others had too, for Lira's eyes came up from her leg.

"Because I mend what I can read," said the healer, without heat. "I read her leg at a count of ten and I knew what it was, and how she was built round it, and where the thing that knits had stopped; and then I knew where to put mine. I had my hands on him for three times as long and I got bone. I'd have got that much with my eyes shut, off a dead sheep." She showed her palms, briefly, as she had shown them to herself. "Past the bone there's nothing I can lay hold of. I'd be pushing in the dark, and I don't do that to anybody."

She turned to Brom then, and had his left arm out of its sleeve before he could offer it. The seam was dry and pale. She wound a strip of plain linen round the wrist, four turns, no tighter than a cuff, and tied it.

"That's not holding anything either. It's closed, and it's sound, and for a week it's the weakest thing on you, so this is to remind the hand." She tapped the cloth. "It'll take what sits on it. It won't take what hangs from it. No rope, no rail, and no hauling anybody up anything; and if you find it shut on something that's pulling away from you, let go."

"A week from yesterday, at the middle of the day," said Brom. "I'll know the hour."

"I believe you would."

Cael let his shirt down over five bands of linen. It was a strange thing to have somebody else's judgment fastened round his chest. He had always priced his own hurts, alone, and decided what each would bear, and he had been wrong often enough to carry the marks. This one had been priced for him by a person who could not read him, and she had put the price where he would feel it at every breath.

---

The lip was eighty yards along the ledge, and it looked worse in the morning than Seln had made it sound.

Cael stood at the end of the sound rock with the others and saw where a girl had been running three days ago. The shelf stopped as if it had been bitten. Beyond the break there was only the wall of the gorge, and along that wall a scar where the fallen stone had been rooted: a strip of raw pale rock four feet wide, tipped outward like the sill of a window, and heaped with broken plates. It ran on for thirty yards or so, and then the ledge began again, grey and level under its roof, as though nothing had happened. Under the scar there was a great deal of air, and he looked at that once.

On the stones of the lip, here and there, a small bright scratch showed, each the length of a finger.

"There are forty-four," said Seln, "and they are not in a line. Do not go by the look of a stone; go by the mark. Several without one look a good deal better than their neighbours."

He went over first, to stand at the far end, and he went as he did everything, without seeming to hurry and without a glance at the drop.

Oryn had loaded the mule in the hollow. Two of the boxes hung from the pack-saddle, one on either flank, and her own panniers were lashed on top with the crossbow in its cover. She took the animal by the cheek-strap and walked it to the edge of the break, and there she changed her hand and put herself on the outside.

"You're on the wrong side," said Lira. "If she slips she goes that way, onto you."

"If she slips with me on the inside, she goes that way with nothing to stop her. This way she's got something to lean on." Oryn clicked her tongue. "She's a mule, and she'd sooner lean than fall. Most things would."

They went out onto it together, the brown beast with her near flank brushing the wall and the square woman at her shoulder on the open side, a boot's length from the rim. The mule set each small hoof down and tried it. The woman's eyes were on the scratches; and where a marked stone lay under the animal's line she moved its head a little with her hand, and the foot came down there. At the far end Seln received the cheek-strap, and the boxes came off, and she brought the mule back as steadily as she had taken it.

"Now you," she said to Lira. "Up."

"I can walk it. You said tomorrow, with an arm, and it's tomorrow."

"I said you could stand. That leg's a day old; it'll bear you on a floor, and I'll not have it asked to catch you on that." She had made a step of her two hands already. "Up, and sit loose, and keep your knee off the wall."

Lira got up. She sat the pack-saddle as she would have sat a fence, sideways, with her new leg hanging and her bare foot turned in; and she fixed her eyes on the roof of rock over the far ledge and kept them there while she was taken across between a wall and a healer. When Seln lifted her down she said something short to him that Cael could not hear, and Seln bowed.

Brom had the third box on its end at the brink. He knelt in front of it and reached back over both shoulders, and stopped, since the healer was regarding his wrist from the far side of thirty-one paces.

"Carrying."

He said it across the gap, to her, before she had opened her mouth. Oryn considered the box, and the rope that went round it and over his chest, and his two hands, which were open.

"Not pulling."

It took Cael a breath to understand that she had agreed with him. Brom understood it at once. He stood up under the box like a man who has had a long account settled in his favour, and went over the lip sidelong, breast to the wall, placing his big boots on the scratches as daintily as a cat on a shelf of plates.

Seln came back for Karis. He put two fingers through the back of her belt and walked behind her, and she went across with her lips moving; and when she reached the sound ledge she turned round and studied the scar.

"Thirty-one," she called softly. "I make it the same."

Then there was nobody left on the near side but himself. He had known that it would be so, and had not let himself think of it. He set his left hand on the wall and his right foot on the first scratch.

The tape spoke at once. He had reached out a little farther than a man reaches who is walking on a floor, and the five bands drew across his side and said *there*; so he took the breath shallower and the reach shorter, and it let him be. He learned that he could go so, at half a stride, with his face to the rock.

Anchor asked, as he had expected. It had asked at every sill and coping since a river, and at seven narrowings, and here was a rim thirty yards long with a broken boy on it. *There? There?* Each stone under his boot was a place where a point might be fixed and told to stay, and at each one he said no, and moved his foot to the next mark.

There was something else that wanted to help him, and it was harder to turn away. The Iron read would have given him every plate on that lip within a pace and a half: which was seated on rock and which on its fellow, and which meant to go. He would have known the ground as he knew his own weight. He had refused it on a fan, when all that hung on it was a map.

At the twentieth pace he came to a place where his eyes and the scratches disagreed.

A broad slab lay directly before his right foot, level, as big as a hearthstone, and there was no mark on it. The mark was on the stone beyond, a mean tilted thing no wider than his boot. To reach that he must stretch, and the tape had already told him what it thought of stretching.

He stood still, with the gorge breathing cold up his back, and he knew what he was choosing between. On the one hand was what he could see for himself. On the other was a line the length of a finger, cut with the point of a knife by a mild man who had come this way in the rain and stood on forty-four stones, one at a time, to learn which would have him. Cael had not asked for that report. He had been given it, since it bore.

He stretched. The tape drew, hard, and three ribs said what they had to say, and his right boot came down on the mean stone and it did not move. Behind him, as his weight left the edge of the broad slab, it turned over like a page and went out into the air without a sound.

At the thirty-first pace there was a hand in his coat, and it was Brom's, the right one.

"Thirty-one," said Cael, when he could.

"So everybody says." Oryn was looking at his colour, and then at his side. "You breathed to the tape the whole way, which is more than most do. Most don't believe it till they've cried out once."

He turned round, since the others had. From the western ledge the whole bay lay open behind them. He could see the pale rock standing across its floor, and the two black lines above it that came to a point, and the wide scoured strip of the hill; and along the foot of the face, in a row, with a length and a half between each, the shale-backs were mowing.

He counted them, as he had not been able to before, and there were twenty-five. That was five fewer than thirty, and it was not the same thing as saying that five were dead.

---

They went down the western side of the mountain for the rest of that day and half of the next, and he had a great deal of time in which to watch the healer walk.

The track had been cut for mules. It left the shoulder by a notch and dropped in long slants across a hillside of brown grass and broken rock, turning back upon itself at every hundred yards, and the valley came up to meet them so slowly that the pale thread of the road seemed no nearer at dusk than it had at noon. Going down was worse for him than the level had been. Each step jarred, and the tape told him so, faithfully, some four thousand times; and he catalogued her, since it took his mind from his side.

She never hurried and she never stopped, and that was the first item. She had the carter's pace so deep in her legs that he thought she could not have walked at any other. On the steepest of the slants she shortened her stride and kept the beat, and where the track ran level she lengthened it again, and the time between one footfall and the next did not change by a hair. He tried to walk at it himself and could not, for he went in fits, quick and then slow, as he had always gone.

The second item was the side she kept. On every slant she was below the mule. When the track turned, and the fall of the hill went over to the other hand, she passed behind the animal's tail without breaking step and came up on the low side again; and she did it at every turn of that hillside without once seeming to consider which side was the low one.

The third he did not see until the first halt, and it was that she limped. It was very slight, a shade of favour to the right foot, and she had built it into the pace so thoroughly that it showed as a rhythm. When she sat down she had the boot off at once, and pressed the strip on her heel with her thumb, and put the boot on again.

"How long has that been raw?" said Karis.

"Two rounds. It closes when I'm three days at a hold, and I'm never three days at a hold."

"And you can do nothing for it, with all that you've got in your hands."

"I can tape it." Oryn pulled the lace tight. "I told you on the hill. Tide runs from the healer into somebody else, and there's nowhere in me to run it from. A healer with a broken leg lies in a barn six weeks like anyone, and hopes there's another healer on the next road. There isn't, out here."

Lira rode. She had been put up on the pack-saddle at the shoulder, on top of the panniers, between two boxes, and for the first hour she sat as stiff as a person on a throne that she expects to be taken away. She had not been carried anywhere since she could walk. She looked straight in front of her, over the long brown ears, and when the mule stopped to consider a turn she looked at the sky.

"How is she?" said Brom, on the second slant. He was walking at the tail with the third box on his back.

"Who?"

"The mule."

"I've not noticed a mule."

"You're on one."

"I'm on a saddle. I was told to get on a saddle, and I did, and I've nothing to say about what's under it." She shifted her weight, with some care. "It's very sure-footed, for a saddle."

"She's got a kind eye," said Brom.

"I wouldn't know. I can't see her eye from up here," said Lira, and then she shut her mouth, and Brom walked on behind with a look of deep content.

They slept that night beside a seep of good water, halfway down, in a fold of the hill where somebody had once built a wall of loose stones against the wind. Seln had found it, and he had a fire lit when they came up, and enough dead heather gathered to keep it till morning.

---

It was at the noon halt on the second day that she took Lira's leg across her knee.

They had come off the hillside at last, onto a floor of turf beside a stream, and the road was in sight, half a mile off, two pale ruts going north-west. Oryn lifted the girl down without asking leave and sat her on a stone. She put both hands round the shin, and her lips moved to ten.

"It's knitting, and it's ahead of where I'd have looked for it." She pressed with her thumbs, up and down the bone. "You've good legs. Somebody's taken trouble with them."

"A good many people," said Lira. "They kept lists."

The healer's right hand had gone on down. Cael saw it reach the ankle, where the dirty strip of linen was still wound over the bare skin, and stop there; and he saw Lira's face go still, as a person's does who has carried a question a long way and comes suddenly upon the door.

Oryn unwound the linen. She took the heel in one palm and the top of the foot in the other and moved it gently, up and down, and in and out, and she counted again. It took her a good deal longer than ten.

"Who saw to this?"

"A woman with a barn, who bound it to a stave. She said it would set stiff, and stiffer." Lira's voice was very level. "She said a healer would have had it straight in an hour."

"So I would, that week." Oryn turned the foot outward, and it went a little way and stopped. "This is four weeks old. It isn't a break. It's the cords that hold the joint, torn, and they've grown back short, and across each other. It's knit wrong, but it's knit, and it's strong."

Lira said nothing for the space of three breaths. Then she asked, and Cael could hear what it cost her to do it in front of four people.

"Can you mend it?"

"I could."

"Will you?"

Oryn held the foot and looked at it, and she did her sum aloud, plainly, as Pike did his.

"To put that right I'd have to take it back to the day it went, and undo the whole of what's grown, and then bring it up again straight. That's two mendings, or three; I'd not know till I was in it. Then you're on your back for a week while it takes." She laid the foot down on her knee. "I've two in me on a road. There's a table at that ford that hasn't seen me for twenty-five days, and I don't know who's lying on the bench beside it. It could be nobody. It could be a man with his chest stove in, who's been waiting on me since the day I went up your hill."

She shook her head, once.

"That's a tape and a season. It'll set."

Lira looked at her own right foot, lying in a stranger's lap, and Cael watched her take it. He had seen her take a result before, on a painted floor, with a sheet of figures to tell her how far she had fallen short. There was no sheet here. There was a square woman with tired eyes, who had spent what she had on the leg above that ankle, and had said to her face what the rest was worth and to whom it was owed.

She had asked it once, and she let that be the whole of her asking.

"Then show me the tape," said Lira. "What's on it now I did myself."

"I could tell, for it's been holding your stocking up." Oryn had the roll out of her coat. "Watch, and then you'll take it off and do it, twice. I'm not going to be there every morning."

She showed her. It was a craft, like any other, and Cael watched it as he would have watched a new guard. One turn went round the shin above the ankle, for an anchor; then a strip ran from the inside of that, down under the heel, and up the outside, pulled hard.

"Always that way, up the outside. That's the side it went over on, so that's the side that's slack, and you're lending it a cord." She laid a second beside the first, and then a long one that went over the top of the foot and under the arch and crossed itself in front, like a figure of eight. "That one stops it rolling. Now stand."

Lira stood, on turf, with her weight on both feet. She rocked forward onto her toes and back, and something went over her face that was a long way from joy and yet was on the road to it.

"It doesn't bend any more than it did."

"No, and it won't."

"But I know where it is." She tried it again. "It's been a beat behind since the river. I'd put it down and not be sure of it till it was down, and now I can feel the edge of it."

"That's all tape ever does," said Oryn. "Take it off. It's your turn."

---

They reached the road before dusk, and there was a halting-ground beside it, with a ring of chock-stones and the black place of other people's fires.

It was the second night down, and the first on which nobody was too cold or too sore to talk. Seln had the fire. Brom had made a bread of the healer's flour on a hot stone, and it had come off black on both faces, and everyone ate it.

Karis had been studying the healer across the flames for some time, with her notebook on her knee. She could hold a pencil again, for as long as it took to write a line, and she had spent her line that evening on the foot of the long leaf. Cael had seen it: *Sixty-three. The ledge. Gone, for thirty-one paces. Cart: no.*

"How long is your round?" she said.

"Twenty days," said Oryn.

She was sitting on a chock-stone with her boot off and her heel to the warmth.

"Round. Two at a hold, near enough, and the road between, and the road's the part that doesn't move."

"And how many holds?"

"Seven. I've been about them thirty-odd times, and I stopped counting rounds a while back, for what I count is days late." She turned the heel to the fire. "I've been late eleven times, and this is twelve."

"That's us," said Brom.

"That's a carter at the foot of a hill who had a split hand, and told me while I was looking at it that a crew was late off the top. I said I'd do his hand that evening, and then I went up to you." There was no reproach in it. "He's got it in a rag still. He hauls to Thornwater, and I'll find him."

"What do they pay you?" said Lira. "The holds."

"They feed me, and they feed her." She nodded at the mule. "One of them shoes her. One gave me this coat, the year I came, off a peg that nobody was using any more. I get a bed for two nights and a table to work at, and nobody's put a coin in my hand since I crossed the Line."

"Then what do you owe them, if there's no price?"

"The day." Oryn looked into the fire. "I said I'd be at a gate on a day. There's a child three stops on with a chest that I listen to every round, and most rounds it's nothing. One round it won't be nothing; and if I'm five days late for that one, then that's what five days cost."

She shrugged, heavily.

"That's what late is. It's not a number. It's who I don't know about."

Cael turned that over. He had counted a great many things in his life, and it had never once occurred to him to count them so.

Karis had waited as long as she was able.

"But you keep a record," she said. "You must. Seven holds, and every one with its sick; what you found, and what you did, and what you said you'd do next time. I should like to see how you've ruled it, for I've never seen a healer's book."

"I've not got one. No ledger, and no roll."

Karis's mouth came open. She looked as she had looked in a cart-yard, when a woman dropped three sentences in passing and drove away.

"Then how do you know who's owed?"

"I remember who I've had my hands on," said Oryn.

She held them up to the firelight, the broad backs and the short nails.

"That's the book. A healer who needs a page to know who's owed has stopped looking at them."

Karis sat with her pencil lifted over nothing, and Cael saw the two halves of her at war: the one that could not bear a thing unwritten, and the one that knew a primary source when it sat down across a fire from her.

"I have a rule about not asking," she said at last, rather faintly. "I made it at a fence, and I've never wanted so much to break it."

"Ask what you like. I'll not write the answer down for you."

It was Lira who asked. She had her taped foot stretched to the fire beside the healer's bare one, and she had been quiet since noon.

"You're Iron, Rank Four; you said so on the hill. That's three above me, and I was thought good." She was picking the words. "A Tide of that rank could sit in any city on the continent and have people come to her. Why are you walking round seven fences with a sore heel?"

Oryn was slow to answer. When she did, her voice had not changed, and yet Cael thought that it was the first thing she had told them which she had not said a hundred times before.

"A healing house keeps a list, every morning, of who's waiting and for what. Do you know what order it's in?"

"Worst first," said Brom.

"That's the order I keep. A house keeps it by tier, with Gold at the top, if there's a Gold, and then Silver, and so down. There were six of us on the staff, and I got what the four above me didn't want." She held her right hand out, palm down, at a level. "A Bronze comes in from his practice yard with a bruise the size of an egg, and he's seen that hour. There's a carter on the bench outside with his foot gone under a wheel, and he's no tier at all. He's seen when the list's done; and the list is never done, so mostly he gets up and goes home on it."

She let the hand fall.

"In the city I healed Bronzes with bruises. Out here I set legs. I'm not going back and I'm not stopping. The route is the only thing I own."

The fire ticked. Lira was looking at her with an expression that Cael had not seen on her since a sheet of sailcloth by a hearth.

"Did you mind?" she said. "Leaving it, and not being told any more what you were."

"I know what I am. I'm twelve times late." Oryn reached for her boot. "Go to sleep. That leg knits at night."

Cael lay awake a while after the others. He had supposed, for eighty days, that he was the only person past the Line who had walked out from under a measure, and that the rest had merely been born beyond it. Here was another. She had not been thrown out, and she had not run; she had looked at a list that was kept in the wrong order, and gone to find a place where she could keep it in the right one, and all she had asked of that place was a road to do it on.

He had a book, and she had a route, and he did not think that either of them owned much else.

---

Oxhollow lay in the bend of a broad shallow river, and they came down to its ford in the middle of the eighty-fourth morning.

The road had brought them along a valley for a day and a half, through alder and rough pasture, with the mountain standing over their right shoulders the whole way. From below it was a long grey wall with a notch in it, and he had looked back at the notch a good many times. On the evening of the eighty-third day he had seen a line of smoke ahead, and in the morning a fence.

The hold stood on the far bank, in a hollow of the hill, behind a palisade of split logs with a gate in it, and between the gate and the water there was a low stone house with a turf roof. The ford itself was a hundred yards of pale gravel under a foot of quick water, wider than any he had crossed past the Line. On the near side a yoke of red oxen stood in the shallows with a chain between them, chewing.

"For the wheels," said Oryn. "He hooks on and draws them through, at a copper a wheel, and nothing for feet."

She had not slackened her pace for the river. She walked the mule into it on the low side, with Lira up, and the water went round her knees.

A man had come out of the stone house. He was old, and bent, and brown as a nut, and he had a hazel stick in his hand with notches cut all down one edge. He watched them cross as he would have watched a cart cross, counting. He nodded to the healer as to somebody who was owed that and more; and then his eyes went along five people in torn clothes, and a mule, and three boxes, and came back to Cael.

"You'll be the Lowmarch crew that took my paper. A carter said the one-armed man had given it to five." He looked past them, at the empty road, and at the river. "Where's your cart?"

Pike had said that he would ask it at his gate, and Cael had had the answer ready for six days.

"In your gorge."

The old man took that in without any change of face. Then he jerked his head at the door of the stone house, and they followed him in, all but the healer, who had gone on up the bank toward the palisade with her hand on the mule's shoulder.

It was one room, with a hearth and a bench and a scrubbed board under the window. Karis unrolled the long leaf on the board, and weighted its four corners with the old man's own tally-sticks, and stood back.

He could not read. Cael saw that in the first breath, from where the brown eyes went; they passed over the six columns to the two ruled lines down the middle of the leaf, and to what was drawn between them, and a thick cracked finger came down on the foot of a fan and began to climb.

"That's loose," he said, at the first hatched tongue.

"A wheel floats on it," said Brom. "It wants a man on a rope, up the hill side, leaning."

"And that's a cross."

"Hollow under," said Lira. "Keep in."

He went on. They told him each mark as his finger reached it, in a word or two, as one names the stops on a road; and Karis, at his elbow, read him the number of each narrowing from her separate column. He stopped a long while at the seventh. He stopped longer at the eighth, where Cael had drawn a short black bar across both lines.

"Two feet, clean through," said Karis. "He took our cart over it on his back." She touched Brom's sleeve. "No carter crosses that by himself."

The finger moved on, over the bay, and came to the last three hundreds. The two ruled lines ran out there to the edge of the paper, and between them Cael had drawn nothing.

"And here?"

"It isn't there any longer," said Cael. "It went on the morning we came to it. There's a lip of rock thirty-one paces long that a mule can be led over, by somebody who knows how, and there's no road." He put his own finger on the margin, where Karis had written small. "And that's the herd. They're shale-backs, on the face over the bay. If anybody uses a Path beneath them, they run, and the hill comes with them, and that's how your cart went into the gorge. She's written that it's derived."

"I have written that it was derived on the seventy-fifth day, and used on the seventy-eighth," said Karis. "I haven't written that it's tested, since I couldn't see both ends."

The old man straightened, as far as he could. He looked at the leaf from end to end once more, the whole length of a mountain on a board.

"Then they go round," he said.

He went to the hearth and lifted a stone out of it, and brought back a leather bag.

"That's worth seventy to know before the first cart's in the gorge. I'd sooner pay it for this."

"There's one in it," said Brom.

"Aye, and it's yours." The brown eyes were not unkind. "I'd have sent three of mine over that top at the next dry spell, with a Force walking behind to shift what fell, for he's the only strong back I've got. I'll not, now."

He counted it onto the board in tens, seven heaps of thick worn silver, beside the foot of the fan. Then he took a burnt stick and made his mark at the bottom of Pike's slip, pressing hard: a horned head, broad between the horns, over three wavy strokes.

Karis had the purse open in her two hands, for she could hold it now.

"Earned, a hundred and thirty-two," she said. "In hand, a hundred and sixteen, and six owed to a smith."

"Sixty-three lines," said Lira. She was leaning in the doorway on her taped foot, looking at the heaps. "And seventy marks. That's a mark a line, and seven over. What are the seven for?"

"For the last line," said Karis. "It's the only one that says no."

# Chapter 22 — Nine Days

The table stood under a lean-to roof against the gate-house wall, and nobody in Oxhollow had put so much as a cup on it for twenty-five days.

Cael could tell that from the wood. It was a slab of ash on two trestles, long enough for a man to lie on, and it had been scrubbed so often that the grain stood up pale and clean, and round its feet the earth was swept. A bench ran along the wall beside it. Eight people were sitting on the bench when the five came up from the ford with seventy marks, and it was plain that some of them had been there since the mule was first seen on the valley road.

The healer was not at her table. She was in the long house, eating, and when she had eaten she climbed the gate-house ladder and slept.

"There's people waiting," said Lira, to the woman who kept the gate. "There's a boy on that bench with his arm in a scarf."

"She's a rule. She'll not lay a hand on anybody till she's had a meal and two hours on her back, not if the hold was burning." The woman said it with a kind of pride. "They've waited on her twenty-five days. They'd sooner wait two hours more and have the one who can count to three."

She came down when the shadow of the palisade had reached the well. She washed her hands to the elbow in a bucket, and then she went along the bench.

It was the thing he had seen her do under a rock, and he understood it better for seeing it done eight times. She stopped in front of each of them at her stride and a half and looked. Then she put two fingers on a wrist, or a hand on the hurt place, and her lips moved to ten, and she passed on to the next without a word. An old man with a cough. A child on a lap. A woman who held her left forearm away from her body, wrapped in a cloth that had been wet and dried.

At the end of the bench she turned round.

"You," she said to the woman. "Then him, with the foot. Then the boy. The rest of you I'll see after, and none of you is going to die of it."

Cael sat on an upturned trough with his back against a post and watched the whole of it, since the tape would not let him do much else.

The arm was a scald, a week old, and it had gone bad. He saw a red line running up the inside of it from the wrist toward the elbow, and the woman's eyes were too bright. Oryn laid her on the ash and put both hands round the forearm above the cloth, and shut her eyes, and it was as it had been with Lira; there was nothing to see but a square brown woman breathing slowly, and the sweat coming out along her cropped hair. It took half an hour. When she lifted her hands the red line was a faint pink thread, and the woman on the table had begun to cry quietly from relief.

She said the number to nobody, under her breath, and drank a cup of water standing.

The foot belonged to a man who drove the oxen, and one of them had stood on it. The boy had gone off a hay-rick onto his shoulder. She set that bone with her thumbs before she mended it, and told him first that he would mind it, and he bit his scarf.

After the third she did not get up at once. She let herself down onto the end of the bench, a little at a time, with one hand on the wall.

"That's three."

"And those?" said Lira. She was on the trough beside Cael, with her taped foot on her knee, and she tipped her head at the five still sitting.

"A tooth, which the smith can draw. A cough that's had him every cold term for twenty years. The child's fat, and it's cross, and there's nothing the matter with it." The healer's voice had gone hoarser. "I've looked at every one. That's what the bench is for. It isn't the ones I mend who need me to come on the day. It's the ones I have to tell from the others."

Lira watched her flex her fingers.

"Does it do anything else? Tide. Could you fight with it?"

Oryn was slow to answer, and Cael saw her mouth go hard.

"There's one thing, and you'll hear of it from somebody, so hear it from me. A hand on a wrist, for a breath, at the moment the other one means to declare; and whatever was coming doesn't come. I'm not opening a way then. I'm shutting one." She looked at her right hand as though it were a tool that she kept in the bottom of a chest. "It costs me a day's mendings, and the next day's, and it mends nobody. I've done it. I'd give a good deal not to know how."

---

She called him to the table when the yard had emptied.

"You. Up here, and your shirt."

He got onto the ash by stages. The light was going yellow, and the others had drawn in without being asked; Karis sat on the end of the bench with her notebook shut, and Brom stood behind her, and Seln was a darker place against the gate-post. Lira had not moved from the trough.

Oryn laid her hand along his left side, over the five bands of linen, and counted.

He counted with her. It was the second time, and he knew now what to watch for. At six a crease came between her brows. At ten she left the hand where it was and shifted her thumb, a little, from one rib to the next.

"They're knitting," she said. "Six days, and they've done the work of ten." She pressed, very lightly. "That's not what I mind. I've known bone knit fast in the young. What I mind is that when a rib knits, I can feel the thing that's doing the knitting. It's there in a goat. And these are closing up under my hand, as neat as you like, with nothing doing it."

She took the hand away.

"Give me your wrist. The right one; there's nothing wrong with that."

He gave it. She laid two fingers on the inside of it, and two of the other hand on the side of his neck below the jaw, as she had laid them on Karis, and this time she shut her eyes.

He lay still on the scrubbed wood and looked at the underside of a turf roof. Nine things lay in him as quiet as he had ever known them; he had held them for eighty-four days, and it had become very like breathing. He could not tell what her fingers were reaching for. He knew only that none of the nine had stirred, and that if one had, he would have felt her feel it.

It went on a long time past ten.

"No," said Oryn.

She opened her eyes and stepped back, and she rubbed her hands together slowly, as a person does who has had them in cold water.

"That's three times. The first I thought it was the break; a bad one can shout over everything. So I went where you're whole. There's nothing wrong with that wrist, or that neck, and there's still nothing I can read." She looked at Karis, who was the one writing it down, or would be. "It's like putting my hands in a river in the dark. Something's moving. I can't find the banks, so I can't read it, so I can't touch it. That's the rule. I didn't make it."

"What are the banks?" said Karis. Her voice was not quite steady, and Cael did not think that the healer could know why.

"Everybody's got them. What moves in a person moves in a bed, the same bed all their life, and the shape of the bed is their Path. I put my hands on you and I felt the banks before I felt anything else, and I said Ember, and I was right. I'd know a Wind from a Skin with a glove on." She jerked her chin at the table. "In him it's moving, and it hasn't a side anywhere that I can find."

Nobody in the yard breathed. Lira's eyes had come round to Cael, and Brom's, and he kept his own on the turf.

"You counted ten the first time," said Karis. "And just now you didn't count. Are those two different things?"

"They're the same thing, done twice as hard. That's the short one, and a healing house calls it the surface reading. Hands on the hurt, or on a wrist, and a count of ten. It tells me what's broken and where it's standing, and it costs me nothing I'd notice; I did eight of them along that bench." Oryn leaned on the edge of the ash. "There's a long one, which they call the deep reading. Both hands, and all of me, and it isn't a count, it's minutes. I go through the whole of how a person's built, as you'd go through every room of a house with a lamp. It costs what a mending costs. And the one under my hands has to lie still for it, and be whole, since a break shouts."

"Have you run it on him?"

"I don't run it on a road. I've not the day to spare for it, and he's three ribs short of whole." She turned back to Cael, and for the first time since a rock he saw something in her face that was neither tiredness nor arithmetic. It was appetite. "When you're not broken, I run the long one. It won't read you either. I want to feel it not read you properly."

He heard *minutes*, and *both hands*, and *all of me*. He knew what else those words described, and so did four people in that yard, and one of them had gone white to the lips over a shut notebook.

"Get down," said Oryn. "I've three things to ask you, and then I'm done."

---

She asked them while she was strapping the panniers, with her back half turned.

"You've broken things before. There's an old one in that left forearm that I could see from the bench. Did they knit where they should?"

"Yes."

"Slower than other people's, or faster?"

He thought of a stone floor, and a Gold, and a bone that an infirmary had given six weeks.

"Faster."

"I'd have laid money on it." She pulled a buckle through. "Has a healer ever got anything from you? A Tide, anywhere, with hands on?"

He thought of a bed in that infirmary, and two voices in a corridor deciding what his ribs meant.

"No."

"No." She said it after him, with a kind of grim content, as a carter says the weight of a load he has guessed right. "That's the visit."

She did the rest of them in the time it took to load a mule. She looked at Lira's foot, and at how the long strip crossed in front, and said that it would do. She had Brom's wrist out of its cuff and into it again. She told Karis to eat, and to ask nothing of herself for three days more, not so much as a spark to see by. To Seln she gave a nod, as one gives it to a man of one's own trade, and Seln returned it exactly.

Cael stood by the well with his shirt laced and a sentence ready.

He had been building it since the halting-ground. *We go south in the morning, six days, to a town with a board. There are five of us, and in one month we have been bitten, and broken, and emptied, and the nearest hands were a day and a half down a mountain. Come south.* It was a reasonable sentence. He could have priced it for her: what a crew would pay, and what the board paid a crew.

He looked at the table. It was bare again already, and somebody had swilled it down; it would stand so for twenty days, scrubbed, with its bench against the wall, and on the twentieth there would be people sitting there before it was light. There were six more like it. There was a child with a chest, three stops on, and a carter with his hand in a rag.

She had told him what late was. He would have been asking her to be late to all of it at once, and to call that a wage.

He did not say it, and it was not like the things he had left unsaid at a fire; he was not sparing himself. He had no right to the sentence, and that was a different sort of silence.

She had the cheek-strap in her hand. The gate stood open on the north side, and beyond it the road went up a long brown hill.

"Thornwater's on my route in nine days. You'll be on the board by then. Come find me. I want to read you when you're not broken."

"Nine days."

She nodded, once, as to a thing entered. Then she clicked her tongue to the mule, and went out under the lintel at a carter's pace, on the low side.

---

Oryn walked out of that gate with her hands in the pockets of a dead man's coat, and she kept them there for the first mile.

She did not often do that. A healer's hands are her eyes, and she carried hers open, as a rule, swinging, where the air could get at them. This evening they felt as they had felt once in her first year on a house's staff, when she had been set to wash a hundred bandages in a trough in the cold and had lost her fingers to the wrist; she could move them, and they would not tell her anything.

She had put them on that boy three times.

She had spent three mendings at the table since noon, the whole of a Tide's reserve for a day, and every one had been what it should be. The scald had stood in the arm like water behind a board. The ox-man's foot had been four small bones and a great deal of noise. She had gone into each of them as she went into a house she knew, and found what was stopped, and opened it, and come out tired. That was her trade. She had not once, since the morning her Path woke, laid a hand on a living body and had it come back to her empty.

Three times, now. Once on a mountain, over three broken ribs, and twice on a scrubbed table.

She was not a person who guessed. She had been taught by a woman who struck her knuckles for guessing, and she had come to be glad of it. So she went over what her hands had found, as she would have gone over a patient for a second healer, and she kept strictly to that.

There was bone. Bone answered to anyone; she had felt the breaks as a blind man feels a step. There was movement, past the bone. She was sure of that, and it was what kept her fingers stupid. A dead thing she could have understood. This was more alive than most, and it ran, and she had felt it running; and she could no more have said which way, or between what, than she could have drawn the wind.

*No banks.* She had told the small pale one so, since the small pale one was writing it down. It was the truest thing she had, and it was not much.

She tried her hands on the mule, at the first turn of the hill, as a person tries a lamp that has gone out once. She laid her palm on the warm brown neck, under the mane, and counted. It was all there. A beast's bed is broad and shallow and runs slow, and she had felt this one's every day for thirty rounds; it told her of a sound heart, and a near hind hoof that would want the smith inside the month. Her hands were as they had always been.

That ought to have been a comfort, and it was only half of one. If the fault was not in the instrument, then it lay in the thing the instrument had been put to, and she had no place to keep a thought of that kind.

The road went up the hill in a long slant, and she went up it below the mule.

Four people were coming down.

She knew the first of them, as anybody on those roads did. It was Teague, off the north water, with a string of two mules behind him and his three; and she gave them what she gave everything on legs, which was a look from a stride and a half as they drew level. A spear, carried upright and grounded at each step, by somebody who never hurried. A boy who was all wrists. And a bow, on the back of one who walked a hair short on the left, at the hip.

Her fingers woke for that. She had not touched it, and she did not need to. It was old, ten years or more; the joint had been out and gone back in by itself, a little wrong, and grown a seat there. She knew what it would cost to unmake that. She knew, too, that the captain had stopped his walk by half a pace to let her see it.

"You were looked for up the water," he said. "On the eighty-first."

"I was up a hill, after a crew that was late."

He took that in, and she saw him keep it. He had a level voice. "Is it a thing you'd do?"

"Not on a road. Not in a night." She did not soften it; he was not a man one softened things for. "It's two days of me and a week lying down after, and I'm five days behind. Ask me on a round when I'm not."

He nodded. He had asked, and been told the price, and she saw him put it by.

"They're in the yard," she said, as she passed. She could not have said why she told him. "Your five."

"They're not mine."

"No," said Oryn. "They're not anybody's. I noticed that."

---

She was at the next gate on the eighty-sixth, five days after she had said.

It was the hold that had given her the coat. She did there what she always did. She ate, and slept her two hours, and walked the bench; and at the end of it sat a man whose shoulder had come out of its place a week before, hauling a calf. His brothers had put it back for him, kneeling on his chest, since there was nobody else. It was in. It was not right. She spent two on it that should have been one, and she did not need to be told whose five days those were.

There was a woman on that bench, too, with a breast-fed child that would not thrive, and an old herdsman who wished to be told that the pain in his side was nothing. She told the one what to do and the other the truth. Neither of them cost her a mending, and both of them had needed her on the day; and that was the part of the round that nobody at a healing house would have understood, for a house counted what its hands did, and never what its eyes had spared them.

She gave that hold a night and a morning, and went on. They had put a loaf and a cold fowl in the off pannier without being asked, as they did every round, and the smith's girl had looked at the near hind hoof. It was how the coat had come to her. Nobody at any of the seven gates had ever spoken of a price, and nobody had ever let her leave with less than she came with.

The route was the only thing she had ever made. She thought of that on the road, where there was time. She had not been given it. There had been no round when she crossed the Line, only a rumour in a ferry-town that the holds had no hands; and she had gone to the first gate, and mended what was there, and asked where the next fence was. It had been four holds, the first year. She had put the fifth on in her third round, and the sixth for an old woman's sake. The seventh she had added because of a child.

So she knew how a stop was put on. It was not done lightly. Every stop was two days, and every two days was a day that six other gates waited; she had sat on a chock-stone with a pencil in her teeth, the night she added the seventh, and worked it through until the round came out at twenty.

The child was at the next gate.

She got there on the eighty-ninth evening, and she did not eat first. That was a rule broken, and she knew it, and she went straight through the yard to a house under a pear tree and put two fingers on a small wrist and two on a neck.

Her lips moved to ten.

The banks were where they had always been, narrow and clear, a little ragged on the left side where the breath came hard in the cold. It was running. It was no worse than it had been twenty-five days ago, and perhaps a shade better.

She sat down on the floor of that house, and the child's mother put a bowl in her hands.

It had been nothing. She had been five days late, and for five days she had not known, and it had been nothing, this round. She ate the whole bowl. Her hands had told her the truth as easily as they ever had; they had gone in and come out full, and she sat and looked at them in the firelight with a relief that she would not have owned to anybody.

It was not her hands, then. She had half thought, on the hill out of Oxhollow, that something in them might have begun to fail.

It was he.

She left that hold at first light on the ninety-first, with two days of road in front of her and the mule going well. And somewhere on the first of those days, between a ford and a stand of the red trees, she found that she had been doing a sum for the better part of a morning without having set herself to it.

Three ribs, broken on the seventy-eighth. Twenty-one days to knit, in anybody; less, in him, by what she had felt. She would be at Thornwater on the ninety-third, and he would be fifteen days from the break. Not whole. She could not run the long one over a break. She would have her two days at that gate, and the carter's hand, which she owed, and whatever was on the bench; and then the round would take her on.

The hand troubled her more than she liked. A split across the palm is a small thing on the day, and she had left it clean and wrapped, and told the man to keep it out of a rein. He would not have kept it out of a rein. He had a load for Thornwater and one pair of hands, and he had given her the news of a late crew when he might have held his tongue and had his mending. By the ninety-third that hand would be fifteen days old. She owed it to him as she owed nothing else on the round, and she meant to pay it before she sat down.

And he would be whole a week after she had gone.

She walked a mile on that.

She was not a person who wanted things. She had wanted one thing, once, which was to keep a list in the right order, and she had got it, and it had filled her days so full that there was no room left over. She had not thought that there was anything else her hands were hungry for.

They were hungry for this. She had spent her life reading what people were built like, and she had been proud of never meeting a body she could not go into. Now she had met one. She did not want to mend him; there was nothing to mend that a strip of linen could not see to. She wanted to lay both hands on him, whole and still, for as long as it took, and feel exactly how it was that he could not be read.

What he was she left alone. That was a question for people who kept books.

On the morning of the ninety-second, on a level stretch with frost in the ruts, she did the thing she had done three times before.

She took it out of the round. Not two days; she had not two. One. She would find it somewhere, off a road, or out of her own two hours, and six gates would wait a day longer on her account, and she would know every one of the people she was making wait.

She put a day on the route.

Then she clicked her tongue to the mule, and went on at the same pace as before, on the low side, toward a gate that was still a day's walk off.

---

Teague had counted his three before the healer's mule was out of hearing.

They were behind him on the hill. The spear had not changed pace for the stop. The boy had both mules by one rein and was looking back up the road after the woman, with his mouth a little open, as he looked after everything. The bow had stood square on both legs for as long as the healer's eyes were on that hip, and now the weight had gone over to the right again.

Three. Whole, and fed at noon, and one of them no better off than a week ago.

They had done two holds' work in four weeks, a drain cut and a byre roofed, and there was nothing in either that would be talked of at a plank. He did not mind that. Most of what kept a crew at the top of a sheet was work of that kind, done to the day and brought home without a mark on anybody; a man who wanted his lines to be stories did not keep his people long.

It was the bow's hip that he minded, and he put it first, since it was the cost.

He had timed the second of his two slips to end at the upper hold on the eighty-first day. That was the day the healer's round should have brought her to its gate. He had said nothing of it to the bow; he had only found a day's more work on a byre roof that might have waited, and stayed. She had not come, and nobody at that gate could say why. He had the reason now from her own mouth, in one sentence on a hill: she had been up a mountain after a crew that was late.

He did not blame her. Worst went first; he would have kept that order himself.

But it was a day he had spent and a thing he had hoped for; and if the crew was the one he supposed, he had both of them to set down against five names. Pike would have said that it was the ledger. He had struck a ford off Teague's hold and given it to them, and that had cost nothing. Teague had weighed that slip against frost on a pass and chosen the pass, and a man could not be robbed of what he had put down himself. This was the first time they had cost him anything, and they did not so much as know it.

The road came round the shoulder of the hill, and Oxhollow lay under him.

He read the yard through the open gate as he went by it, without turning his head more than a walking man does.

There was no cart. That was the first thing, and it was the largest. He had heard on the road that they had hired the smith's, and there were three boxes standing against the gate-house wall on the bare ground, and a crew that has wheels does not stand its goods on the ground.

The mild one was by the gate-post. Teague found him at once, this time, and was pleased with himself for a breath, until he saw that the man had been looking at him since the shoulder of the hill.

The big one sat on the well's coping with a box-rope coiled over his knee. His left wrist had a cuff of clean linen on it, tied with a knot that Teague knew, since he had seen it on his own people. Mended, then, and under orders. The girl was on a trough. She had one boot, and the other foot was bare and strapped, crosswise, by somebody who had been shown how. It was on its whole sole. He had read those feet in an inn as the quickest thing in the room, and he would not have laid a copper on them now.

The small pale one was asleep sitting up, with her head on a box and a book shut under her hand.

There were four of them, and he had counted them rightly.

The old keeper was on his bench by the stone house, with a tally-stick, and he gave Teague the rest in the time it took two mules to pass.

"It's a no," he said. "Over the top. I've paid seventy to be told so, and I'd pay it again. They put their own cart in my gorge finding out."

"All five came down?"

"All five. On her mule, the one."

Teague went on down to the water.

So he had it, and there was nothing in it that a man could not have read off a yard and a keeper. They had cleared a ford with five, in a way that nobody on those roads had cleared one before, and they had walked a mountain and brought back a true answer; and they had lost a cart doing it, and come home on a healer's charity with three of them marked.

He had four things to say to that boy. He priced each of them, on the bank, as he would have priced a stage.

The first was the two jobs, set side by side with the same number on each. It would cost him nothing, and it would tell the boy that he was being read, and by what. That was fair. The boy had read him across a room and not troubled to hide it.

The second cost Teague something, and he had known that he would pay it since the fifty-seventh day. The ford had been good work. To say so aloud was to say that the slip had gone where it ought, and that he had no claim left on it, even in his own head. It was true, and a true thing was owed.

The third would land hardest, and he turned it over longest. He did not mean it unkindly. A cart was not a carter. Nobody lay in that gorge. But a crew that went out with another man's wheels and came back without them had done a thing that the board would enter, and it was better that they heard the word for it from him, on a river bank, than read it on a wall.

The fourth was the only one that he might have kept. It gave something away. It told five people at the bottom of a sheet that the name at the top expected to stand on a road beside them before the cold term was out; and it set a term on that, which was his to set, since he had three behind him who would be standing there too.

He looked back, once. The spear. The boy. The bow, on the good leg.

He decided to say it. A man who meant to share a contract owed the other crew the warning.

The boy was sitting on the gravel at the water's edge, alone, with a book on his knee. He held one arm in a little against his left side, and under the shirt, when he breathed, something drew a line across his ribs. There was a healer's tape on him. It would be three weeks, then, from whenever the mountain had done it.

Teague still could not tell what he was. A Force stood one way and a Wind another, and there was no Path in the set of that boy that a man could name, and no want of one. He went down the bank.

---

Cael heard the mules on the gravel and shut the book on his pencil.

He had known who it would be. A crew had come down the north road at a walking pace, in single file, without a word passing among them; and the man in front had a band of black leather laced on his right wrist and a hand-axe in his belt. He came to a stand a stride and a half from where Cael sat. It was the second time that day that somebody had chosen that distance.

He gave no name, and he wanted none.

"You cleared Thornwater's ford with five and lost a cart on the high bench with five."

"Yes."

It was the first word that Cael had ever said to him. There was no other to put with it. He could have told the man about a ledge that showed no crack, and a page marked *derived*, and sixty yards of fire held for eight seconds by a person who had never held a third of it. All of that was true, and all of it was his to know and nobody's to plead.

"The ford was good work. The bench was a cart."

There was no contempt in it. He said it as Pike said a line, level, so that it could be called wrong if it was wrong; and he waited, as Pike waited.

Cael went through it for the place where he could say so. He thought of a smith in Lowmarch, and six marks owed, and a tail-board that had not banged. He thought of what a ford-keeper had counted out, and what the ford-keeper had said he was paying for.

He had no argument. The man had not said that they had failed. He had said what the bench was, on a board that wrote down outcomes, and it was that.

"The board's going to have you and me on the same contract inside a season. Be good enough that I'm glad."

He nodded, the width of a finger, as he had nodded once from a sill. Then he walked on into the river, and his three came after him, each at the same distance from the next.

They crossed without a sound. Cael watched it as he would have watched a thing well made. The one with the spear went second and grounded it at every step. The one with the bow walked a little short on one side and kept the pace regardless. A boy of sixteen or so brought the two mules through on a slack rein, and not one of the four looked down at the water. They had kept the hold on roads since before he knew there was one.

He opened the book again while there was light.

*Eighty-fourth day. Oxhollow, at the ford. The map is delivered: sixty-three lines, and the last one says no. Seventy marks, counted. Earned, a hundred and thirty-two. In hand, a hundred and sixteen. Six owed to the smith for a cart that is in a gorge.*

*Mine: three ribs taped, twenty-one days from the seventy-eighth; the calf bound in her cloth. Lira: a leg that is new, an ankle that is taped and will not be mended. Brom: mended, and not to pull. Karis: empty, and filling. Seln: nothing.*

*The nine: held, on a mountain and off it. Deployment since the Line: one.*

*Teague priced us. He's right about the cart. Oryn read me three times and got nothing three times and wants a fourth. Two people out here measuring me, and neither of them has a seal. I keep waiting to mind.*

He read the last of it over, and it stood.

On the far bank four people and two mules were going up out of the river into the dusk, quiet, a crew's length apart. He turned his head the other way. The north road climbed the brown hill behind the hold in one long slant, and a mile up it, small and steady, there was a mule, and on the low side of the mule a woman, walking at a pace that she could keep until dark.

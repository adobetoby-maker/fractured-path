There was frost on the bay at first light, and Brom wanted the cart across it before the sun came.

"It's stuck," he said, low, with his boot on the shale. The plates did not creep under him as they had the evening before; they held, with a small dry crackle. "The cold's glued it. She'll roll on this for an hour, and by noon she'll float on every yard of it. I'd as soon have her on the far side waiting for the word."

So they had broken their camp in the grey, without a fire, and drawn her out from Seln's corner onto the open floor. Seln had the near shaft and Cael the off. Brom walked against his rope on the left, toward the hill, and Karis went at the off wheel with her cord looped over her shoulder and the long leaf rolled in her coat. The narrow wheels cut two clean lines in the rime. The hub ticked, and the far wall gave it back.

Lira had gone on ahead. She had not eaten. She had wound the linen round her right ankle by the first light there was, and tried her weight on it, and walked away along the foot of the wall with her arms swinging loose. By the time the cart was a hundred yards out she was a small dark figure at the place where the bay shut.

He watched her stop there and set her feet.

He did not count. He had the book open against the shaft, with the leaf turned to the sixty-third line, and he had written the number and nothing after it. Above them the slope was in shadow still. Thirty grey heaps lay along the upper shelf with white on their backs, and not one of them had yet begun to scrape.

It was the last morning of the job, and he let himself know it. Three lines remained of a table that had sixty-two. After the ledge there was a shoulder, and a hillside going down into a brown valley, and a man at a ford who would count seventy marks into Karis's purse. He had walked a mountain for seven days with nine things shut in him, writing down what a cord and a clock and a girl's feet could tell him, and not one line of it had wanted anything that he was. He thought that he would be sorry to come down.

"It is very quiet," said Seln.

"That's the frost," said Brom. "They'll not eat till the sun's on the moss. Nor would I."

"No doubt."

But Seln went on looking up the slope, with his mild face lifted, and Cael saw that he liked the quiet no better than he had liked the eighth narrowing. He had chosen every place they slept on that mountain, and he had not wished to sleep in this one's middle.

"She's off," said Brom.

She was. She went out along the ledge under its roof of rock, close in, and from that distance there was nothing to show that one foot came down later than the other; she looked like a thing that the mountain had been keeping that shelf for. He made himself look at the fin. It stood ahead of them and a little to the right, pale in the half light, with the frozen shale banked along its upper side, and he judged that they were thirty yards short of its nearer end.

"Stand here," said Brom. "This'll do. There's slab under this bit; I can feel it."

They set the shafts down. Karis took the cord from her shoulder and began to free the first knot, since the ledge would want measuring as soon as it had been run. Seln straightened his back with both hands. It was very still. The stream was only a breath of sound from far below, and the cold had laid a quiet on the hill that Cael had not heard there on any other morning.

He looked west again in time to see the ledge go.

It made no noise at first. That was what he remembered.

A length of the shelf simply tilted outward, thirty yards beyond the place where Lira was running, as a table-leaf drops when its bracket is drawn. Then the piece nearer to her did the same. She was on the third.

He saw it leave the wall under her feet, with a puff of pale dust along the join; and in the same instant the air over the gorge cracked.

It was Wind, and it was hers, and it was the whole of it. He had not heard that sound from her since a painted floor at Norhold, and she had never, there or anywhere, let it go so hard. She was not on the falling stone. She was twenty feet east of it, in the air, and then she was standing on sound ledge with both arms flung wide. Behind her a hundred feet of shelf was going down into the blue with a roar that grew as it fell.

Wind stood up in his own feet to answer hers. He put it down.

Her declaration crossed the gorge. Its crack went with it. He had time to see Lira turn her head toward the mountain, and then the far wall gave it back, late, as clear as a whip.

Thirty heads came up.

They came up together. There was no first. One breath there were thirty heaps of frosted stone on a shelf, and the next there were thirty small beaked heads lifted at the height of a man's knee, every one of them pointed east, away from the place where the air had broken.

"Karis," said Cael.

"I see them."

They went. He had watched them for five days and had never seen one of them hurry, and they went along that shelf now like barrels loosed down a ramp, all thirty, shoulder behind shoulder, with the calves between; and the clatter of their plates reached him across three hundred yards of slope like hail on a slate roof. They were running from her. They were running east, along a lip of rock that had dropped a slab under one frightened animal at a walk.

It held them for as long as he could have counted four.

Then the shelf began to come away in front of the leaders. He saw the edge of it sag, and a grey back lurch outward, and after that there was no shelf and no line, only the whole upper face of the bay beginning to move. The animals had turned downhill, since downhill was the only way that the ground was going. They came in a front, eighty yards wide and three or four deep, and the mountain's loose skin came with them.

He did the sum as he would have done it on any floor. It was three hundred yards of roof, and they were carts with legs.

He had his arm out at the fin before he spoke.

"There. Thirty seconds. Lee side."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Seln had Karis by the elbow before the second word and was running her over the rimed shale as one runs a child across a road. Brom ripped the rope over his head. Cael ran, and for a wonder the leg ran with him; the ground was level and frozen, and he had twenty strides in him before it thought to complain.

They came round the eastern end of the rock, where it was no higher than his belt, and into the black strip behind it.

It was just as Seln had said. The wall stood over them, twice Brom's height in the middle, and at its foot there was bare clean floor, twelve feet deep. On the far side of it the shale lay banked to a man's chest.

Karis had not crouched. She had stopped at the low eastern end, where she could see over, and she was looking up the hill with her lips moving.

"It will stop the stone," she said. "That's what it's stopped before. It won't stop them. That bank's a ramp, and there are thirty of them, and they'll come straight over the top of this and down on us."

"Can you turn them?" said Cael.

She did not answer at once. He saw her eyes go to her coat, where a leaf was, and come back to the slope.

"Derived," she said. "I've nothing else."

Brom had turned round.

He was looking back the way they had come, at a cart standing by itself on a white floor with its shafts down and three boxes under one canvas. The frost about its wheels had begun to crack. Cael could see it from where he stood: the whole apron shivering, as the skin on a pail of milk shivers when the pail is kicked.

"Cart's gone."

He said it as he would have told somebody that a horse was lame. Then he ran back for it.

He went like a bull at a gate, and Karis made a small sound and did not call him back.

She had no breath to spare for it. She had both hands lifted a little in front of her, the palms with their pink lines turned toward the hill, and she had begun to do the thing that Ternhall had taught her before she was fifteen. Points first, always. Joins last.

Cael could not see them. Nobody could; a set point was a promise and gave no light. But he had watched her lay them for two years, in a whitewashed room and on a district floor, and each one had always cost her the same small dip of the right hand, as a person dots a page. He counted the dips, and followed her eyes. The first went down eleven paces up the slope, straight above the middle of the wall; the second eight paces from that, down and to the left; and then the third.

She was drawing an arrowhead on a mountain, with its point uphill.

Thirty yards off, Brom let the tail-board fall. It banged, and nobody was there to stop it. He dragged out the left-hand box and got it onto his shoulder, and the middle one after it onto the other. Then he turned with a hundredweight on either side of his head and came back over the cracking shale at a trot.

There were three boxes.

Cael had known it as he ran, and had kept it out of his mind until the others were behind the rock. The third was the right-hand one. It had a third of everything that Karis had ever copied in it, and at the bottom, under the masonry chips, a canvas bag with five thumbs in the wax.

"Twenty," said Seln quietly. He was counting as well.

Cael went.

He heard Seln say his name, once, and then he was round the end of the wall and out on the open floor with the mountain coming down on his right hand.

He gave it no more than a breath's looking, and that was enough. The front had come a third of the way, and it was no longer grey; it had a brown smoke of dust standing over it, and out of the smoke came the sound, a deep steady grinding with the hail of the plates on top. He passed Brom going the other way, and Brom's eyes went to him and to the cart and he did not waste his wind.

The cloth at Brom's left wrist had gone dark.

The box was heavy and its beckets were stiff with frost. He got it to the edge of the bed and his right shoulder under it, as he had seen it done, and straightened. The cart moved beneath his hand as he let go of her, like a boat that has been pushed off.

He ran with it. It was thirty yards, and he had a count of ten.

All nine of them were up. He had never felt them so, on the stretch or at the ford; there the offers had come one behind another, and here they came in a single shout. Wind wanted his feet and had the whole distance measured. Storm showed him the air over the bay, torn already, as a lane. He put them down as he ran, without words, since there was no time for words, and they got up again at every stride.

The slope lit.

He saw it at the edge of his left eye and could not help turning his head. Across the face of the mountain, above the pale rock, a line of fire had stood up out of the shale. It ran from a point high over the wall's middle down and outward on either hand, straight as two rules, yellow-white, the height of a man's knee; and it was sixty yards from end to end. She had joined them. Nine points and eight channels, no join longer than eight paces, laid on frozen stone in the time it takes to lace a boot, and every one of them burning.

He felt her discharge go over him like the door of a furnace opening. He had stood near that Path in archives and annexes for two years. It had never been a quarter of this.

The front met it.

He could see the animals now, the foremost, with their heads down and their plates flying; and he saw them feel it. The middle of the line broke. The ones dead above the arrowhead's point flung themselves left and right as if the ground there had been snatched away, and the ones beside them went with them, and in the space of two breaths a front eighty yards wide had torn down the middle into two streams. Eleven paces above the rock there was nothing coming at all.

A bird over the bay would have seen it plainly: the pale rock lying east and west across the floor, and above it an arrowhead of fire, point up the hill, with one foot burning a little beyond each end of the rock. The herd had broken on that point into two streams, one pouring down outside the eastern foot and one outside the western, and everything between the two feet was in shelter. Cael was coming from the east with a box, so the eastern stream was the one that could reach him; Lira was coming off the ledge from the west, and the western was hers.

He was eight yards short of the wall's end.

He was outside her line. Its eastern foot stood a little ahead of him and uphill, burning, and the eastern stream was pouring down past the end of it, and he was in the edge of where that stream was going to be.

He had perhaps a count of two, and he spent the whole of it pricing.

Compression had the thing measured already. It had waited since a river for a blow worth taking, and it showed him how: a plated shoulder at a run, met square, and sent down through his frame into the slab beneath the shale. He would have stood. Anchor offered him the other way. There. One fixed point against the rock under his left boot, and told to stay, and a boy with a box would have been a post that a river goes round.

Either would have saved his ribs, and either was a declaration.

It would have gone off in front of animals that were running from one already. They were pouring away from her fire, down and east; if a second loud thing stood up in their road, the leaders would check, and the rest would pile on them from behind, and a stream that is dammed goes over its bank. Its bank was sixty yards of fire held by one small person who had never held a third of it. They would come back through her line and over the rock.

The whole split stood on Karis. One more loud thing would turn it.

No, he told the pair of them, and he gave it his left side.

He had learned that from Lira on a bar of gravel: the side that is not wanted afterward. He turned the box away, and the innermost animal of the stream came by him at the length of an arm. It did not so much as see him. Its shoulder caught him below the left armpit as a cart's tail catches a man in a lane.

He heard three small sounds, low on that side, one after another.

Then he was in the air, going the way he had chosen, with both arms still round the box. He came down on his left leg in the shale at the foot of her fire, and slid, and the edge of a plate opened his calf along the old line as a thumb opens a pod.

There were hands in his coat. They were Seln's, and then Brom's, and he was hauled over the low end of the rock on his back, with the box on his chest.

He lay where they put him and counted, since it was the one thing he could do.

She was above him, at the wall's end, on her feet. Her two hands were out in front of her and they shook as a rope shakes under a load. Her face was as white as paper and quite calm.

Five, he counted. Six.

The noise was on both sides of them, a river in flood going by either end of a pier. Plates were coming over the top of the wall in the middle and bursting on the floor.

Seven.

At the eighth second Karis sat down.

She did it all at once, like a puppet whose strings have been let go, with her back against the rock and her hands open in her lap. The light went off the mountain.

It did not matter. The last of them were by.

He got himself onto his right elbow, with a care that he had never before had to take over breathing, and looked out past the low end of the wall to the east.

The stream had spread as it reached the level. He saw the backs of perhaps a dozen animals, wheeling on the bay's floor in a fog of dust, with the shale still running between their legs like water; and beyond them, alone, in the place where five people had stood a minute ago, the cart.

She was floating.

Brom had named it at the foot of a fan, and Cael had heard the word a dozen times since, and he had not understood it until now. The whole apron under her had let go. It was moving toward the edge in one piece, without haste, as ice goes out on a river, and she rode on it with her wheels buried to the hubs and her canvas still roped. She turned once, slowly, as a leaf turns in an eddy. Her shafts swung round and lifted.

She went over the rim tail first, with the two shafts standing straight up behind her like the arms of a person wading into deep water.

He did not hear her land. He heard the slide going over after her, tons of it, in a long hissing fall; and after that, at intervals, three or four heavier sounds, dull and separate, that were not stone and not a cart.

The far wall gave all of it back, late.

Nothing in the hollow moved. Dust came down on them out of the brown air as flour comes down in a mill, and settled on the boxes, and on Karis's open hands. Above the wall the face of the bay was a different mountain. The roof of plates had gone from a strip eighty yards wide, scoured to the pale slab beneath, and along both edges of that strip the stone was sliding still, a plate at a time, with a sound like somebody clearing a table in another room.

Out on the level the animals had stopped. They stood about in twos and threes among the wreck of their own hill, with their sides heaving and their heads low; and already, he saw, they had begun to shuffle a little apart from each other, feeling for a length and a half.

"How many?" said Karis.

It was hardly a voice. She had not moved, and her eyes were open and fixed on nothing.

"I couldn't see," he said. "I heard something go. I wouldn't swear to it."

"Heard." Her lips shaped the rest. "Not sworn. Write that."

"When I can."

Brom was kneeling between two boxes with his left arm held across his chest. The cloth on it was wet from the heel of the hand to the middle of the forearm, and he was looking at it with a kind of weary respect, as at a debt that had come due on the day it said it would.

"Carried," he said, to nobody. "Both of them. It was the lifting them out did it. I pulled."

Seln was on his feet and whole, with grey dust in his hair. He had gone to the western end of the rock and was standing there very still, looking out.

Cael counted the hollow. It was a habit, and it was all that he had. There was a large man, kneeling. There was a small one, sitting. There was a mild one, at the end of the wall. There were three boxes, left and middle and right, and one of them was lying on his own legs.

That made four people, and one of them was himself.

"Lira," he said.

Seln had not turned his head.

"Here," he said. "She is here. Come when you can, and not before."

He could, after a fashion. He got his back against the stone and worked along it, a foot at a time, with his left arm clamped to his side and his left leg leaving a mark on the clean floor. The middle of the wall went by over his head, tall and pale. It took him as long as it had taken thirty animals to come down a mountain.

She was three strides beyond the western end.

She had almost reached it. She lay on her front in the grey spill of the other stream, with one arm stretched toward the rock and her cheek on the shale, and from the knees down he could not see her. A plate of the mountain lay across her legs. It was as long as a door and as thick as his wrist.

Her eyes were open, and they were looking at him.

She had liked the ledge from the moment she saw it.

She would not have told the boy so. He would have written it down. But she had stood at the end of that bay in the evening light with her arms folded, looking at three hundred yards of clean level rock, and something in her had sat up, as it used to sit up at the sight of a chalked lane.

It was not the same thing, and she knew that. Nobody was going to call a time. There was no slate, and there was no girl in the next lane to be beaten by a tenth. She would run to the far end of a shelf and come back and say slab, dry, six feet, and a boy with a pencil would write it in a column. A carter she would never meet would put a wheel there or not, on her word.

She had done it sixty-two times. Somewhere about the thirtieth she had noticed that she no longer wanted him to count.

That had surprised her more than the mountain. For a week after a fire above a wash she had gone about with a question in her like a stone in a boot, the one she had put to four people and nobody could answer. She had not got an answer. She had got a job. Each morning there was a hundred yards of ground that nobody alive had tested, and she was the one who went onto it first, at speed, to learn whether it would bear; and when she walked back and said that it would, four people and three boxes moved forward onto the place where her feet had been.

It was the only use she had ever found for being fast that was not somebody else's ladder.

So she bound the ankle in the dark, on the seventy-eighth morning, and ate nothing, as she had eaten nothing before a card. The foot was what it was. It bent less than the other and later, and she had stopped being angry with it somewhere on the bench and begun simply to allow for it, as one allows for a wind.

She set her feet at the ledge's mouth, and went.

The rock was a gift. It was dry under its roof, and rimed along the outer half where the roof stopped, so she kept in, close under the wall, as she had promised a fire that she would. Her left foot struck and her right came after it. She had the count in her legs. Forty. Fifty. The gorge went by on her right hand, blue and enormous, and she gave it none of her attention; she was reading the shelf five yards ahead of her toes, for cracks and for colour.

She saw no crack. She would say so afterward to anyone who asked, and nobody ever did.

At eighty the wall on her left hand moved away from her.

That was how it seemed. She had been running a hand's breadth from it, and between one stride and the next there was a gap there the width of her arm, with daylight in it. The shelf under her had come off the mountain in one piece and was leaning out.

She did not decide anything.

She had been told at Fenmark that a burst was a choice, and at Norhold she had made a rank of choosing well. For sixty days on gravel bars and roads she had refused it a stride at a time, on purpose, and she had been proud of how good she had got. Not one of those refusals was anywhere near her now. The ground went, and her body did what a body with Wind in it does when the ground goes, and it did not send up to ask.

The air broke round her. She felt it in her teeth.

Then there was rock under both feet, and she was standing on it, twenty feet back, with her arms out and her heart going like a mill. Behind her the ledge roared down into the gorge. She had not chosen where to come down, either. Her feet had found sound stone under the roof by themselves, and stopped dead on it, as she had spent a month on a river bar teaching them to stop.

I'm alive, she thought, with real surprise.

Then the far wall answered her.

It was her own burst, coming home. It crossed three hundred yards of empty air and came back from the cliff late and clean, and it did not sound like a thing she had done. It sounded like a whip in somebody else's hand.

She understood it then, in the pit of her stomach, before she had turned her head.

There was a page with a comma struck out of it. She had been lying on a slab when it was written. It will answer a declaration in the same way. She had asked for a test, a seed, half a breath; and a small pale person had looked up a hill and declined.

She looked up the hill.

Thirty heads were lifted, all of them, turned away from her.

She ran.

There was nothing else to do with what she had done. The cart was a toy on the white floor of the bay, with four specks beside it, and one of the specks had its arm out. She could not hear what it said. She did not need to, for the arm was pointing at the long pale rock.

It was sixty yards of ledge to the bay and forty more of shale to the wall's near end, and she had run farther than that to catch a dog.

Wind said now.

It said it at the second stride, quite reasonably. It had just saved her life and it could not see why it was not wanted again. Twenty feet at a time, five times, and she would be behind that stone before the first of them was halfway down.

No.

She knew what a second one was. She had sat in a loft and heard it laid out with a charred stick. Never two at once. A burst would crack over that bay like the first, and thirty animals that were running from one loud thing would have another to run from, and it would be going off beside the only shelter on the mountain. She had been the loud thing once this morning without being asked. She would not be it a second time by choice.

She refused it at the third stride and the fourth. It was harder than it had been at the ford, where she had only been afraid of a dog.

The right foot came down late. She allowed for it. The ledge poured back under her, and the hill on her left was no longer a hill; it was brown, and moving, and the sound of it came down ahead of it and pressed on her ears. She came off the rock onto the shale and it broke under her like the crust on snow.

Thirty yards.

A small figure stood at the wall's far end with its hands out. She saw it across the whole length of the pale rock, no bigger than a doll, and she knew whose hands they were.

The mountain caught fire.

She had watched those points go down in barns and annexes and on the end wall of a whitewashed room, a dozen at a time, each no bigger than a barley grain. She had seen them joined in threes, and once, at a sitting, sixteen. She had never seen a road. It stood up across the whole face above the rock, two straight burning lines that met in a point high on the slope, as wide as a street and as long as a field; and the animals coming down on it split like a log under a wedge.

She's staked the whole page on it, thought Lira, running. With us underneath.

Twenty yards. The near arm of the fire came down the slope ahead of her and stopped a little beyond the wall's end, and on this side of it, on her side, the western stream was coming.

She was outside the line. She saw that with great clearness. Ten yards more would put her under the rock, and she did not have ten yards; she had perhaps six, and a foot that was a beat behind.

Now, said Wind, for the last time, and it was not reasonable any longer. It was begging.

She told it no, and ran the six yards on her feet.

Something the size of a cart went behind her so close that the wind of it turned her half round. She kept her legs. She was three strides from the stone, with her arm already out for it, when the shale that the thing had brought down with it took her ankles from under her like a wave on a beach, and she went onto her face.

She felt the plate come.

It was not quick. It slid up over her calves on the moving stone as a lid slides onto a box, and settled, and the weight of the hill behind it leaned. Her left leg was in a hollow and it only pinned. Her right was lying across something hard.

It went below the knee, with a duller sound than the ankle had made at a river. She knew at once that it was a different thing.

After that she lay with her cheek on the mountain and watched the stream go by. It took a long time and no time. Grey backs passed at the level of her eyes, and stones ran over her outstretched hand without hurting it, and none of it seemed to have much to do with her. The fire went out. She noticed that, and thought, She's done, and was sorry.

Then it was quiet, and the gorge was giving something back.

She could not have said how long it was before the boy came. He came along the lower side of the rock, on his feet and hardly so, with one arm shut against his ribs and his left leg dragging; and he was the colour of the dust. He got down beside her by letting the wall take him. His eyes went over her a piece at a time, as they always did. They reached the plate, and stopped.

She had one thing to say, and this time she did not pick it with any care.

"That was me," said Lira. Her mouth was full of grit. "The loud thing."

He did not tell her that it was not. She watched him not tell her. He had never once made her a present of a thing that was untrue, at a fire or anywhere, and she saw him go through what had happened in its order, as he would have gone through a bout.

"It was the ledge. The ledge went. Then it was you."

She turned that over, with her face on the stone. It had the ledge in it, first, where the ledge belonged. It had her in it second, and it did not leave her out.

"Then it was me."

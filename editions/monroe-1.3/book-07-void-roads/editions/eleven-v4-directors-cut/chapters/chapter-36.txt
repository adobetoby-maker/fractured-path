Chapter 36 — Written Before

He drew the ladder up himself, hand over hand, and laid it along the edge of the loft.

There was no need of it. Nobody in that hold would have come up uninvited; and the one person whom he did not want at its foot had gone to her bed at dusk, across the yard, behind a small window with no light in it, like somebody who has work in the morning. He drew it up all the same. He had asked four people among the barley sacks to hear a thing, and he wished to have done something with his hands before he began.

They had come, and had looked at his face, and had sat down without talking. The two boxes that had waited in that loft stood against the gable, and the third, with the corner that was worn white, had been set down between them and not yet opened.

"She runs the long reading at first light," said Cael. "On me, in the back room, with the door shut."

Nobody moved.

"She called it that at Oxhollow. Both hands, and all of her, for minutes, with the one under her hands lying still and whole. You were all in that yard, and I saw four people hear it."

"We heard it," said Brom.

He was on the boards with his knees up, and his hands lay open on them, one of the two pale across the pads of the fingers.

"And none of you said what it was, and nor did I. I've had fifteen days in which to say it, and a ring; and I'm saying it now with one night to go, which is late." He looked at the flame of Karis's lantern. She had held up one finger when she lit it, to show him how much oil an hour at a stone had left her, and he meant to have said all of it before that was gone. "It's the thing that I take them in."

It was out, and it lay on the boards among them. He went on, since he had built the rest with some care and wished them to see every joint of it.

"I've nine, and you know how each of them came. There was somebody with a Path, who used it on me or against me for long enough, and meant it; and there was something that mattered to both of us in how it came out, whether that was a card, or a ruling, or only which of us was left standing. That's the whole of what any of them had in common, and Karis has a page on it."

Karis lifted one finger from the notebook. Her throat was the colour of an old apple by that light, and she had spoken twice that day, a word at a time, in a voice like a rasp on wood.

Her page was a single leaf, and she had been two years in the making of it. It had a curve on it, for how quickly each of the nine had come, and a column for what each had cost him afterward; and at its head, in her small exact hand, stood three words with a rule under every one. She had never in his hearing called it more than a model. He had its three words by heart, and so had the others, since she had read them out on the night that the eighth was priced.

"So I've laid tomorrow beside her page, a piece at a time. It wants to be sustained. She says twenty minutes, with both her hands on me, and I've never stood a bout that was a quarter as long. It wants stakes, on both sides. Mine are that I'm three days off that floor, with every one of the nine still shaken by it. Hers are that she's spent three mendings on us and she's late at the top of her round, and she means to lay out a fourth on a boy who has given her nothing four times. And it wants to be in earnest."

"On you," said Lira. "Not against you."

She had the sack nearest the lantern, and her right hand lay over the sling as if to keep it from hearing.

"On me. She'll go into it with everything that she is, since that's the only way she does anything."

"That's the part that's never been so," said Brom slowly. "The others were all trying to put you on the floor. Even the captain was."

"Ephram was trying to beat me. He wasn't trying to hurt me, and the notice knew the difference; it said so, in two words that I'd never seen on one."

He had got as far as that when the pencil began to move.

Karis wrote for the length of three breaths. Then she did a thing that he had never seen her do to that book: she tore the leaf out along its stitching, carefully, and laid it on the boards in front of the lantern, facing the rest of them.

Ephram fought you in earnest and the notice said non-hostile. This is earnest and it isn't a fight at all.

They read it. She was already writing again, and the second leaf came out after the first and was laid beside it.

The model doesn't say the engagement has to be a fight. It says it has to be real.

"Why've you pulled them out?" said Lira.

Karis touched her throat and shook her head, and wrote small on the stub that was left.

Not tested. Not going into the book as though it were.

"Is it right, though?" said Brom. "Her model."

"She doesn't know, and I don't, since there's been nothing to try it on. Every one of the nine came out of a fight of some kind. There's never been a person who did a real thing to me for a long time, at a cost, who wasn't standing on the other side of a floor." He touched the second leaf. "If she's right, tomorrow is the first morning of my life on which it could happen with nobody struck. And if it does, I shall have a piece of what a healer reads with."

He heard how that sounded, in a loft, with a girl's arm tied up six feet from him; and he did not soften it, since that was the very thing that he had brought them up a ladder to hear.

"It isn't the model that I wanted you for. It's what's under it. She doesn't know what I am."

Seln's head had turned. He had put himself at the loft door, where he could hear the yard, and until then he had not looked at the lantern at all.

"I've told her nothing. She has put her hands on me four times and found no banks, and she watched me go up a hill with fire in my fist, and in all that time she has never asked me one question that wasn't about a hurt. She thinks that she is going to lay me on a table and read me. If something is taken from her while she does it, she'll not have been told that it might be. She can't say yes to it, and she can't say no."

The lantern ticked, and for a breath that was the only sound in the loft.

"Then tell her," said Lira. "Tonight. Before."

She had not raised her voice; she looked straight at him across the flame, as she had looked on a gravel bar when he gave her a plan with a lock in it.

"She's across a yard, and there's a door. You knock on it, and you say that there's a thing you do, and what it is, and that it might do it to her. And then she says what she says."

"I've been at that since the fork in the road," said Cael. "I'll give you what I've got, and there are three reasons."

"Go on."

"The first is that if I tell her, it's a different morning. She would be reading a patient that she'd been warned about. She might keep some of herself back, to see; or she might go in looking for the very thing I'd named. I don't believe that either of those is the whole of her, and the page says that it has to be real. I'd have turned it into a trial."

Karis's pencil made a small mark on the stub, and stopped.

"The second is that it isn't all mine to tell. I can't say what I am without saying that I've nine, and where a thing of that kind comes from; and then she is within one question of the one that's sealed. She talks to every carter on seven roads. She would never mean any harm by it."

"No," said Seln.

He said it quite gently, as he had said it at a stake to a small person holding up a leaf.

"I shall not be a reason. You shut that thing for my sake, and you have kept it shut at a cost that I watched you pay in a river. But I have had four nights in which to consider who was looking in a lane, and I find that I should sooner have her told what may be done to her than be used to keep her ignorant. Strike the second. It is not available to you."

Cael looked at him. In two years he had never been given anything by that man that was not a report, and he did not at once know what to do with his face.

"Struck," he said, and his voice was not quite steady.

"That leaves the first and the third," said Lira. "What's the third?"

"That there's nothing I could put in front of her which she could weigh. I'd be telling her a thing with no name that she has ever heard: that I take pieces of people when they come at me hard enough and mean it, and that there may be room in me for a tenth. What does a person say to that, at night, through a door?"

"I said something," said Lira. "In a lane, when I'd never heard of it either. You told me on the day you knew, and I didn't run."

"You had watched it happen. You'd felt it go."

"She's felt you not be there, four times, and that's more than I had." She leaned forward, as far as the sling would let her. "Those are reasons, and they're good ones; you never bring bad ones. Which of them is the real one?"

It was the question that she had put by on a shelf at a stone; he saw that this was the same question, cut to a smaller size, and that she had every right to this one tonight. He had known the answer since the fork, and had gone round it for the rest of that day as a carter goes round a ring.

"I don't know what she'd say."

Nobody in the loft breathed.

"If I tell her, she may say no. She's a person who says no; I heard her say it to a girl on a mule. And then there is no morning. She goes north in a day or two, and I never lie on that table, and the one time that it could have been tried is gone." He made himself finish. "I want it, and that's under all three. I have been a thing that instruments couldn't read since a brass plate went dark over me, and she's the first that ever tried it with her hands and told me so to my face. On that hill I was the only thing that worked, and I can't tell any of you why. If I had a piece of what she reads with, I might be able to look."

"That's honest," said Brom. "I'll say so much for it."

He had not shifted from his place on the boards. He was looking at his own open hand, where a healer had drawn four new lines.

"What's it cost her?"

"I don't know."

"You must know something of it. You've done it nine times."

"I know that it's never shown, and I've looked. Lira was no slower in the week after Ardenmere, and you were no softer; Karis set sixteen points for a panel inside a month of hers. Not one of the people that they came out of has ever said that they felt it go." He turned his palm up. "But every one of them was whole when it happened, and fighting. She will be spending. It costs her a mending to do this at all; so if it comes, it comes out of a purse that she has open, and I've nothing whatever to tell me what that does."

"So it might be nothing. Or it might take the legs from under her, and she's a round to walk."

"Yes."

"And you'd find out which by doing it."

"Yes."

Brom nodded heavily, several times, as he nodded over a load that he did not like.

"Then it's wrong," he said. "I'd not stop you; I'd not know how, and it isn't mine to. But somebody ought to say it who's glad of what you carry, and I am. I was glad of it on a ridge and at a ford, and three nights back, for her arm." He jerked his chin at the sling. "It's wrong to do that to a person who climbed a mountain for five strangers on a carter's word."

"I know that it is."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so that it's been said by more than you."

Karis had turned the notebook round. She had written four lines on a fresh leaf, and this one she had not torn out.

It is wrong. I want the result more than I have ever wanted a result. Both of those are mine. Entered against me, in the book, tonight.

Then she opened her mouth, and he saw what it cost her. The word came out cracked across the middle, and it was the third that she had spoken since a lane.

"Wrong."

She looked at him to be sure that he had it, and shut the book.

"I'd tell her after," said Cael. "That is what I've built in place of the before. On her table, before I have got up off it, whatever has happened and whatever hasn't; all of it, from the brass plate on, with the nine named for what they are. And then it's hers to deal with as she likes. If she says that I'm never to come within a mile of her round again, then that's the price of it, and I walk south."

"That's not consent."

It was Seln. He had come away from the loft door, and he stood at the edge of the light with his hands behind him.

"It's the nearest thing to it the log's ever had, and it's still not it. I'd enter both."

"Both," said Cael.

"That it is the nearest, and that it is not the thing itself. A person who writes down only the first has begun to forgive himself." The mild eyes rested on him. "You wrote such a leaf for the captain, the night before, and you did not tell him, and you have carried that. But he had asked you for a fight, and a person who asks for a fight knows that it will cost him something. She believes that she is the only one in that room who will be doing anything."

"She asked for it," said Cael.

He heard that it was the weakest thing he had said all night, and he said it regardless, since it was true and he wished to have it weighed.

"I'm not to make one come. That's the first rule, and it's kept; I never put this in her mouth. She said it herself, at a ford, before I had so much as thought of it. When you're not broken, I run the long one. She put a day on her route for it."

"She asked to read you," said Lira.

Her voice was very quiet.

"She didn't ask for the rest. Those aren't the same sentence."

It lay on the boards beside the two torn leaves. He had nothing to put against it, since there was nothing that could be put.

He looked at the four of them in the last of the oil. Not one had told him that he might, and not one had offered to stand in a door. Each of them had called the thing by its name, in turn, as Pike said a line to a meadow so that anybody might call it wrong; and the four names had been one name, which was wrong, and he had not a word to set against it.

He called for no hands, since it was not a matter that hands could carry.

"I'm going to do it," he said. "I've heard every one of you, and you're right. The right thing is to cross that yard tonight; or, failing that, to tell her in the morning before she has laid a hand on me. I know that. I am going to lie down on that table and say nothing, and tell her the minute that it's over. I want you to know that I chose it with all of this in front of me. Nobody talked me into it, and nobody was kept from talking me out."

"Then enter it tonight," said Seln. "By your own rule: before, and at its full weight. An entry made tomorrow will have learned how the morning went."

"I had meant to."

"I know that you had. I wished it said aloud."

Lira got up off her sack. It took her a breath to do, on that foot, with one hand. She stood over him, and for an instant he thought that she was angry, and then he saw that it was something else.

"You could have done it and told us after," she said. "You could have told nobody at all. You carried it up a ladder and let four people call it what it is." She touched the top of his head once, with two fingers, as she might have touched a card for luck. "It's still wrong. Go and write it down."

The oil lasted him long enough to write it, with the others asleep or pretending to be.

He did not put it under the day's heading. He had written two such leaves before this one: the first in a week of rain, with four people's initials at its foot, and the second at a sill, on the night before a bout with a friend who was not to be told. He turned to a clean page for the third, and began it with the two words that were the point of the whole.

Written before. Tomorrow morning, the back room, the door shut. Oryn runs the long reading. Both hands, all of her, all of me, twenty minutes. I lie still. I'm still and whole for the first time in front of her; she said so in the yard, with the tape off.

The conditions, as I have them. Sustained: twenty minutes at contact is longer than any bout I've stood. Stakes: I'm three days off the Ring and the nine are still settling; she's three mendings down and her route's late at the far end and she's spending a fourth on a patient who's given her nothing four times. Earnest: she'll do the thing she does as well as she does it. Karis: the model says real, not fight. I don't know if the model's right. Nobody does. Tomorrow's the first time it could be tested and I'm the test.

The price. She doesn't know what I am. She can't consent to what she can't know about — not won't; can't. There's nothing I could put in front of her tonight that she could say yes or no to, because the thing doesn't have a name she's heard. I've decided not to tell her before. My reasons are good, and they're the reasons of somebody who wants it, and the real one is that I don't know what she'd say. Entered.

If it takes what she's using on me, it takes it from the one person on this road who came to help. I don't know what it costs a source. It's never shown. Tomorrow's the first time it could, and if it does, she pays, and I chose that for her.

After: I tell her. In full. On her table, before I'm off it. Whatever it costs me, including the route walking north without me on it.

I want it. Write that too.

Write it before, not after. That's the whole difference between me and the system.

The flame had gone down to a blue bead, and he read the leaf through once by it, quickly, from the top.

It was not a good page. He had hoped a little that it would look better written; that somewhere between the pencil and the paper it would turn into the kind of thing that a careful person does. It had not. It read as what it was: a boy wanted something, and had found that he could not have it honestly, and was going to take it and pay afterward; and he had set down the price first, in a hand that would be dry by morning, so that he could never claim to have been surprised.

That was all that the last line meant. A registry had read him for years, and written down what it found, and had never once told him beforehand what it was reaching for or what the reaching might cost him. He was about to reach for somebody else in the same way. The one thing that he had which that office had not was a leaf with tonight's date at the head of it.

It was a thin difference, and it did not make the thing allowed. He was exact with himself about that, since he was trying to be exact about all the rest. To cross the yard now and knock would be right. To say in the morning, before she had so much as washed her hands, what those hands might lose, would be right. Four people had shown him both doors. A leaf written first opened neither of them; it meant only that when he walked past them he would be doing it with his eyes open, and could never afterward tell anybody, himself least of all, that he had not known.

He held to that much, since it was all there was.

The bead went out. He lay down between two sacks of barley with the book under his hand; and in the dark Wind leaned a little toward where Lira was breathing, and the heaviest of the nine shifted, like a sea a long way off, and was still.

It was barely light when he crossed the yard.

There was ice on the trough. The geese were asleep in a heap by the long house wall, and on the platform by the western gate a lean man turned his head, and turned it back. Cael had eaten nothing. He had his shirt on and no coat, and his feet were bare inside his boots.

The door of the back room stood open. He stopped on the sill, since something in him was asking about the sill, and since he wished to have the room exactly; he thought that he would be remembering it for a long time.

It was small. There was one window, in the eastern wall, a square of grey with its shutter folded back, and a bench under the window. A table stood in the middle of the floor, a scrubbed plank on two trestles, long enough for a man to lie on, with a three-legged stool at its head. Along the sill, under the light, a strip of canvas had been unrolled, and on the canvas her things lay in a row: the tape on its peeled stick, a flask, shears, a hank of clean linen, a horn cup, a bone needle. He had seen them come out of a pannier in a dozen places. He had never before seen them set out in her own order, where her left hand would find each of them without her eyes.

The healer was washing. She had a basin on the bench and her sleeves pushed above the elbow, and she did not turn round.

"Shut that. Boots off, and the shirt. On your back, with your head at the stool."

He shut the door, which had a peg on a thong for a latch.

The plank was cold under his shoulders and very clean, and there was a folded sack for his head. He lay and looked at rafters with barley dust on them, and listened to her drying her hands, a finger at a time.

Nine things lay in him, and he went along them as he went along anything at the start. Wind was in his feet, and still. Pressure sat under the breastbone. The read was shut, as it had been since a rut. Compression lay in his frame, and Ember in his right hand, with nothing to look at. The bright quick one had asked about the table's edge when he lay down, and Anchor about the sill, and he had told each of them no. The heaviest lay low, like weather that has blown itself out.

The ninth had drawn itself in very small. This was the first light, and not the last; that one came up at dusk.

She had told him in a yard to hold nothing. He had spent a hundred days in learning to refuse the nine, a stride at a time; and now he was to lie here for twenty minutes and neither refuse them nor let them go, and he did not know the name of that.

She came to the table's side, smelling of pitch and cold water.

"Breathe like a man asleep."

He tried it; he had watched Brom do it often enough. The breath went in to the bottom of him, where a tape had stopped it for three weeks, and out again slowly.

"Slower than that. You're thinking. Stop."

Two fingers came down on the inside of his right wrist, and two more below the hinge of his jaw, and they were warm from the basin. Her lips moved. He did not count with her this time. He lay under it and felt the count come to five, and to six, where it had stopped on a leg at a stone; and it went on to ten.

"Nothing. As before."

She said it without any disappointment, as she would have said that a door was shut. It was the fifth time. She took her fingers away and shook both hands out from the wrist, twice, hard.

"That was the short one, so that I'd know I hadn't dreamed it. Now the other." He heard her push the stool aside with her foot, for she did not mean to sit. "I'll be a while finding my way in, and then I'll be in, and I'll not hear you. If you must move, say so first. If I say lie still, you lie still."

"Yes."

"And don't mind my talking. I talk, in a long one. It's how I keep my place."

Her left hand slid in under the back of his neck, broad and warm, and took the weight of his head. Her right came down flat on his breastbone.

It lay exactly over the place where a small bright thing had gathered on a morning in a windowless room, and looked at him for eleven seconds, and gone out. He had gone looking for that place on a hillside five days ago, with five people abreast of him, and had found it dark. Her palm was on it now. She could not have known that; it was only where a healer puts a hand.

"Still," said Oryn, and shut her eyes.

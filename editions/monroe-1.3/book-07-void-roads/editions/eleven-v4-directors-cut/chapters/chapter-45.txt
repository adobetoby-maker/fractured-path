The bar came out of Thornwater's western gate on the hundred and eleventh morning for five people, three boxes and a mule that belonged to none of them.

Brom had loaded her by the well, in the grey, with the steading's woman holding the lantern. Two boxes hung from the pack-saddle on their own ropes, one to a side, and the third rode between them on a folded blanket with the empty pannier strapped over it. He had gone round the animal three times, pulling at each knot. Then he had stood back and looked at the load as he would have looked at a wheel that he had built himself.

"She'll want to go on the low side of you," said Lira.

"On the low side of nothing. I go on the low side of her." He clicked his tongue as he had been shown, and the small brown ears came round. "I was told it once. I'd be ashamed to be told it twice."

It was the hardest cold that they had yet walked in. The ruts of the valley road had frozen as the last grain cart left them, with every hoof-mark standing up like a thing cut in iron, and the pale grass on either hand was white to the knee. Their breath went ahead of them. On the gate-walk a lean man stood with his long bow at the length of his arm, as he had stood on every morning that they had spent behind that fence; and when Lira looked up, he lifted two fingers from the stave. She had nothing in her right hand, and she lifted two of her own.

They went down the stretch at the one time of day when nothing had ever come up it. Cael walked last. He had the white stone on his left hand at a hundred and ten yards, and he gave it the look that he gave it every time. Fifty-seven evenings had gone by since anything stood there.

Lira was in front of him, and he watched how she went. The ankle was taped, and under a healer's order still, so that she set the great boot down whole at every stride, heel and toe together, and rolled nowhere. She had done it for fifteen days on turf. On frozen ruts it was a different trade. Twice in the first mile the boot skated, and twice her right arm went out and her left stayed where a knot held it; she had been told that she was to fall on her face sooner than put that hand out, and he saw that she had taught the hand to believe it.

"I can hear you counting," she said, without turning her head.

"I'm not counting."

"You're doing the other thing, then. The one where you'd catch me." She put the boot down. "Don't. If I go over, I go over. She'd sooner mend my nose."

They came to the ford a little before noon.

The row of white stones stood whole along the lip, as the hold had set it, and the water went over the sill no more than shin-deep, with a rim of ice along both beaches. Seln crossed first, and then Karis, who lifted her coat. Brom led the mule down to the edge on her off side, where he had walked all morning, and she set her forefeet on the wet rock and would not move them.

He clicked. He said a few reasonable things to her in a low voice. She regarded the far bank with her kind eye and stood like a milestone.

"Near hand," said Lira. "You're on her off side, and the water's moving. She'll not cross with anybody on her near hand."

"I'm not on her near hand."

"Seln is. He's standing on the far beach, straight in front of her left eye."

Seln, who had heard, walked ten paces up the far beach without a word. The mule went into the river as if nothing had ever been the matter. Brom, who had been leaning on the rope, went in with her a good deal faster than he had meant to, and came out on the other side wet to the thigh.

"I'd like it entered," he said, "that I did everything I was told."

"You did," said Karis. "She was told something else. You will have to learn which of you ranks."

They made their camp that night on a carters' halting-ground a little above the wash, with the mule hobbled at the windward end and the three boxes under her blanket. Seln lit a small fire with his flint. They had kept that custom since the fifty-ninth day, on the strength of a finding, and it was a comfort still.

Cael had the first watch. He sat with his back against a chock-stone and Hesk's book shut on his knee, and for some time he left it shut.

He owed a healer two readings of himself with the tenth fragment, on two different days, with a night between; and after those he owed her the whole of it at once. She had set it at a well that morning, with a pannier on the coping. Nine days had gone by since the first reading, in a loft, and he had let every one of them pass. She had seen why from a stride and a half. He had found a place in himself that did not move, and he had been afraid of it, and he had liked it better as a line on a leaf.

He could do the second tonight. Four people were asleep or near it, and the fire was low. He had his fingers on his own wrist before he saw what he was about.

It was the same thing that she had stopped at the well. He wished to have three readings done in three nights, so that he could set them down at her first bench and stand square. She had told him to read it frightened, and to read it when he was not. She had said nothing about reading it in a hurry; that was a third temper, and it was the one that he had been in when he lay down on her table.

He took his fingers off his wrist. The listening went on at the edge of his thought, quick and slow, and he let it go on, as a miller lets a mill; he would read himself on a night that was only a night, and until then the debt could stand where she had put it. It was the first that he had ever left standing without picking at it. He wrote one line, and gave the watch to Brom at the turn.

Hundred and eleventh day. The halting-ground above the wash. One reading done, two owed, and nothing tried tonight; I was in a hurry, and she never asked for that. Deployment since the Line: thirteen.

They came over the saddle above Lowmarch in the afternoon of the hundred and twelfth day, and he stopped on the crown of it to see what was there.

It was the fourth time that he had looked down on that town from high ground. He had learned on the first that it was a river, and a ferry, and two inns, and a smith, and a meadow with no wall. On the others he had learned to read the meadow as he had once read a board, since it changed with every week, and whatever was true of that country showed there first.

The cold had been at it. Where forty fires had burned when the roads were dry, he made out twelve, drawn in close about the great ash, and half the trodden grass had gone back to white. The ferry's cable stood across the water like a chalk line, with frost on it. The scow lay on the near bank. A brown gelding with one white foot was walking his circle in the capstan-shed's doorway with a blanket on his back, and Cael was glad to see that somebody had thought of the blanket. Both inns were smoking. There was a skin of ice along the eastern bank as wide as a cart, and beyond it the river ran dark and fast.

"It's shrunk," said Lira.

"It's gone home. He said that it would, in the cold term." Brom had the mule's rope, and he was looking at the smith's chimney. "Half of them have fences of their own to sit behind."

A cart was coming up the saddle road toward them, slowly, behind two mules, with a load under canvas that was white at the seams. Its driver walked at the near wheel. He was a round man, floured to the eyebrows, and he lifted a hand to them as carters did on a hill.

Lira did not wait for him to draw level.

"There's a healer walks a round out here. Which gate's she late at?"

She put it exactly as they had been told to put it, in a loft, by the healer herself. The round man took no offence at being asked a thing before he had been wished good day, and he answered her as a man answers who has been asked the same question at every door for a fortnight.

"Ours. She came in at Millrace last night, down the mill water off the hill track, with a rope on her shoulder and no beast. It's the next gate on her round after that grain hold of yours, and she's sixteen days behind at it." He jerked his chin at the canvas. "I'm the mill's carter. I left a bench full when I pulled out this morning, and my own sister's boy on it."

"Millrace," said Karis. "I have it on the hide: a square with a dot, a day down the south road from this town."

"A carter's day. Less, for legs." He looked at the brown mule with some attention, and then at the boxes, and Cael watched him decide that it was no business of his. "You'll want to be quick, if it's her you want. She's never more than two days at our fence."

He clicked to his pair and went on up the hill.

She was a day from where they stood. Cael had expected to count gates for a week.

"She said not without her," said Lira.

"She did."

"I'm only saying that it's a day south, and I don't know of anything we want to the south."

"Nor do I," said Cael. "We'll hear what's on the oak."

They went down into the meadow with the light going. The old place under the ash was empty, since a good many places were, and Brom unloaded there and rubbed the mule's back with a twist of hay while Seln walked once round the twelve fires and came back and said that there was nothing. A boy came for the standing-money. Karis gave him two coppers, which were too small a matter for her book.

The plank under the lower inn's eave was bare of its book. Pike had taken it in for the night, as he did when the dusk came early; but a line of yellow showed under the inn's door, and the noise of the room came through the wood.

"He's not said ours," said Brom. "The Fallow. He wrote it eleven days ago, and sent it up to us on a piece of bark, and it's not been said where we could hear it."

"Then he'll say it," said Karis. "He has never yet kept one back."

Cael put his hand to the latch.

Pike said it before the door was shut.

He was on the hearth bench, under the sailcloth, with the ledger open beside him and the horn pot at his elbow; and his eyes came up as the cold came in. His look went over the five in the doorway once, and then a second time, as it went over everything. Then he turned his shoulder to the room and gave it out, in the voice that he used under his eave.

It was the eighth line under their heading. Cael had read it on a scrap of bark in a grain hold, across the bones of a goat, and he knew every word; but he had never heard it said. Pike gave the recovery, and the Fallow road, and the broker's posting. He said that it was taken and witnessed. He said what had not been found there, and that the crew had come back. He gave the count of those who went in and of those who came out, which was the same count, and at the end of it he said that no price was paid.

Then he said the whole of it again.

Lira had not got the door shut. She stood with the latch in her right hand and the frost coming in round her boots, and nobody in that room told her to shut it.

It was a thin house. There were perhaps twenty people on benches that had held forty, and every one of them had heard that line on the hundred and first day, when it was written. They had not then had the five to look at. Cael felt the room turn its attention on them as he had felt a yard do it once, at an academy on a hill. But this was no registry's look. Nobody's eyes went to where a tier ought to be.

The hide-woman sat nearest the door, with a bundle of cured skins beside her on the bench end. She had given them a cake in their first week, and had told him over a wash that he was not paper. She said nothing now. She lifted the bundle off the bench and set it down between her feet, and went back to her cup.

Three old men sat beyond her, at boards of their own. Cael had seen them under the ash for a hundred days without ever learning what they did, three grey heads over a pair of dice. The dice lay on the wood between them now. The eldest had his hand half closed over them, and he neither threw nor gathered them up.

By the hearth sat the old carter who had come east with them from the first ford, and had asked Cael on a road whether he was paper. He had his chin lifted. He was looking past the five at the dark doorway behind them, steadily, as a man looks who wishes to see whether anything else is coming in.

And the boy who took the standing-money, who had got in ahead of them by the yard door and was warming his hands, was looking at their boots.

Not one of them spoke. Cael had been looked at in a great many rooms, and he knew the difference between a room that has made up its mind about a person and a room that has had to change it. This one was changing it, a face at a time, with no word said. It did not seem to be making it up again.

At the far end, on the last bench before the hearth, a man had got to his feet.

He had done it without haste. He was of the middle height and heavy in the forearm, with a band of black leather laced on his right wrist. Three people sat behind him at their distances. He stood with his thumbs in his belt and his eyes on Cael, and he stayed on his feet while the line was said the second time.

Cael had seen that man count three on a sill before he looked at a room. He had been told by him on a river bank what a mountain was worth. He had never once seen him stand up for anybody.

Pike had dipped his pen. He did not write with it. The line had been in his book for eleven days. He raised his eyes to the sailcloth over his head, and found the place.

"Eleventh. Teague's ten up."

The man with the leather on his wrist sat down.

Lira let go of the latch, and the door swung to behind her.

"Eleventh," she said, in front of the cloth. "We were tenth when we went up that road."

She had gone straight to it, on her whole sole, and stood with her nose a hand from the square black writing. Cael came up beside her. The five names were where she had said, on the eleventh line; and over them, where there had been two crews under theirs, there was one that had been written back above.

"It's the wooden foot," said Lira. "The four who never lost a mule. They've gone over us again." She turned on the hearth bench. "We went into a place that you've never stepped over the edge of, and six of us came out. You've just said so twice. And it's cost us a line."

"It has."

Pike was looking up at her from under his own handwriting, and he did not seem displeased to be asked.

"You've read my lines. Every one says what came back, and what was paid on it. That one says the things weren't there and the box stayed shut. A hold comes down to my plank with a job to give, and asks me who fetches what he's sent for. I've a crew that was sent for a thing and came home without it." He held up his one hand. "It's nothing against you. The cloth can't say whose fault a thing was. It can only say what was on the cart."

"So we're worth less for it."

"On the cloth. I never said the cloth was all of it." He looked at the fire for a breath, and then at his book, and he went on to the book, in the size of voice that he kept for things nobody had asked him. "I've had that sheet thirty years. Forty-odd crews have been on it, one time and another, and gone off it. Some went home. Some the road had. There's not one of the forty that ever went over a line and came back to tell me so; there's none that I ever heard of who tried. I've a line in here now that says it was done." His finger lay on the leaf. "Six in and six out, and the sixth a healer. I'd a good look for the column to put that in. There isn't one."

"Then make one," said Lira.

"I'll make one the day I know what goes at the head of it."

He said it mildly, and he took up his cup. Cael stood before a sheet of sized sailcloth with eleven lines on it, and found that he was not thinking of the line that they had lost.

He had been a number in other people's books for as long as he could remember. A magister had given him one every week, to the decimal. Vell had kept one for him in a yard. Every one of those figures had been somebody's best account of how much of a thing he was; and here was a book-keeper with one arm, who had never owned an instrument, saying aloud in a room that his own book had a true line in it and was too small to hold what the line meant. Cael did not know what to do with it.

It was Karis who touched his sleeve.

She was looking past the cloth, at the plaster beside its lower corner. There was a nail there, a long one, driven at the height of a man's eyes. He had seen it on other evenings and taken it for the place where a lantern hung.

"It is for what has been read and put back," said Karis, very low. "The girl at the next boards told me, on the ninetieth night. A captain takes a slip down, and reads it through, and hands it in again; and if it is a slip that weighs, he hangs it there, so that the next captain knows it has been weighed and by whom. There is hardly ever anything on it."

There was something on it now. It was a single sheet, hung by a hole in its upper margin. The firelight came through it a little, and it was cream.

Pike unhooked it himself and laid it on the bench between his knee and the horn pot.

"You'll read it whether I give it you or not. You read the last one off my plank before I'd lifted the stone." He kept one finger on its corner. "It came up from the landing the morning after I'd said your line. The clerk brought it at a trot. His master had had it lying by since the same sack as the other, sealed up inside the same wrapper, with a bag counted and a word of direction on the outside: that it wasn't to be posted till the first was answered."

"It was there all the time," said Brom. "The whole month that the other lay under your stone."

"In the broker's strong-box, with his seal on it, and him not knowing what was in it. He says so. I believe him; he's not a man who'd keep three hundred marks shut up for a month if he knew that he had them." Pike took his finger away. "I said it to the meadow, twice. Nobody scratched. Then Teague came down off his fence for flour, and read it, and hung it where you found it. That's eight days it's been on my nail."

Cael picked the paper up. It was the same weight as the other, which lay folded in Hesk's book; it had the same pricked margin, and one word standing alone at its head, with a stop after it. He held it so that the four of them could see, and read it out low, a stop at a time.

Recovery. Surveyor's instruments abandoned at a site on the Stair road. South of Lowmarch; the first day; where the road drops off the ridge and the ground opens. Three hundred marks (three hundred).

Below the bracket there was a seal in dark wax, a hook and a bar.

Three hundred marks was more than twice what the five of them had earned in a hundred and twelve days. He did the sum without wishing to, and saw Brom doing it, with his lips.

Pike had his eyes on his book.

"Nothing else ever."

"I'd not take it."

He did not raise his voice for it, and he kept his eyes on the book. He had told a man in this room, on the eighty-ninth evening, that he would have done the same with the first of those papers; and on the ninetieth he had held the first out across his plank to five people and written five strokes on it. He had not said then what he would do in their place. Cael understood that he had been given something that Pike did not give, and that it had not been asked for.

"The Stair road," said Lira. "Which is that?"

"South. Past the well you cleared, and up onto the ridge. It's a carter's day to Millrace by it, and the road forks above the mill." Pike rubbed the stump of his arm through the pinned sleeve. "One branch drops to the ford. The other keeps the ridge half a day more, and comes off the end of it, and the carts go round what's there."

"Round what?"

"I've told you what I know about ground like that. I've never been nearer to this one than the ruts."

Karis had not been listening to him. She had the paper by one corner, turned to the fire, and her lips were moving over a single line of it; and Cael saw the colour come up into her face.

"A day south, and a little aside," she said. "In his hill-hatching. I rang it on the ninety-second evening, and I wrote guess."

She looked up at the four of them.

"That's the carter's second circle."

Seln had not yet touched the sheet. He stood a pace behind Brom's shoulder, and his mild eyes went over the pricked margin from the top hole to the bottom, as they went over any paper, and came to rest on the bracket.

"Can it be read at our own fire?" said Cael.

"It can be read where you like. It's been said." Pike pushed the paper an inch toward him along the bench. "Bring it back, or bring it marked. I'll be here till the fire's banked."

Cael folded it once, along the fold that it already had.

The room let them go as it had let them come. The hide-woman's bundle was still between her feet, and the place that she had cleared on the bench end stood empty. The three grey men had not thrown. At the last bench a man with leather on his wrist watched a sheet of cream paper go out of the door in a boy's hand, and Cael could not have said what was in his face, since it was the face of somebody who had already done his own sum on that paper and did not mean to do another crew's.

Outside, the frost had come down on the meadow, and their own fire was the smallest of the twelve.

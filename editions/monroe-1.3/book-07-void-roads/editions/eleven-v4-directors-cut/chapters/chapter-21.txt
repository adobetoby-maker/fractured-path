He found the edge of it at his first whole breath.

It was grey in the hollow still. He had got to his feet by the wall, as he did every morning, to learn what the night had done to him, and he had drawn in air to say something to Brom. At a certain depth, a little short of where the ribs had stopped him the day before, a hand seemed to close across his left side and hold it.

It was the tape. She had put it on him by the lamp, before the light came and before she had so much as eaten. There were five bands of linen, three fingers wide, each drawn from his breastbone round under the arm to the spine in a single pull, while he stood with his left arm on his head and no air in him at all. He had supposed, while she did it, that he was being splinted.

"That's all it does," said Oryn.

She had seen him find it. She was winding what was left of the strip back onto its peeled stick, and she spoke across him to Karis, who was sitting up this morning in Seln's coat with her notebook open on her knee and no pencil in her hand.

"I want you to have that right, if you mean to write it. It mends nothing, and it doesn't hold the bone, for you can't hold a rib; it's got to move, or he stops breathing. People take it for a splint, and it isn't one."

"What's on it?" said Karis. "It smells like a pine wood."

"Pitch, and mutton fat, and a thing I get from a hold that keeps bees. It sticks to skin, and it comes off again when I say so."

"Then what is it for?" said Lira. She was propped against a box with her right leg stretched out in front of her, bare from the knee, and she had not stopped looking at it since she woke.

"It tells the ribs where the edge is."

Oryn tucked the roll into her coat.

"He'll go to draw a full breath, or turn to pick something up, and that pulls before the bone does, so he stops there. He'll have learned it by tonight, and he'll hate it by noon tomorrow, and it stays on till I take it off." She looked at him at last. "Twenty-one days from the morning they went, and I want to be the one who lifts it. Can you count that far?"

"He can count anything," said Brom. "It's stopping him that's the work."

"Why tape for him," said Karis slowly, "and hands for her? You had two mendings yesterday and you've two this morning, and a rib is a bone like a shin."

Cael had been waiting for that question, and he saw that the others had too, for Lira's eyes came up from her leg.

"Because I cannot mend what I can't read." The healer said it without heat. "I read her leg at a count of ten and I knew what it was, and how she was built round it, and where the thing that knits had stopped; and then I knew where to put mine. I had my hands on him for three times as long and I got bone. I'd have got that much with my eyes shut, off a dead sheep." She showed her palms, briefly. "Past the bone there's nothing I can lay hold of. I'd be pushing in the dark, and I don't do that to anybody."

She turned to Brom then, and had his left arm out of its sleeve before he could offer it. The seam was dry and pale. She wound a strip of plain linen round the wrist, four turns, no tighter than a cuff, and tied it.

"That's not holding anything either. It's closed, and it's sound, and for a week it's the weakest thing on you, so this is to remind the hand." She tapped the cloth. "It'll take what sits on it. It won't take what hangs from it. No rope, no rail, and no hauling anybody up anything; and if you find it shut on something that's pulling away from you, let go."

"A week from yesterday, at the middle of the day," said Brom. "I'll know the hour."

"I believe you would."

Cael let his shirt down over five bands of linen. It was a strange thing to have somebody else's judgment fastened round his chest. He had always priced his own hurts, alone, and decided what each would bear, and he had been wrong often enough to carry the marks. This one had been priced for him by a person who could not read him, and she had put the price where he would feel it at every breath.

The lip was eighty yards along the ledge, and it looked worse in the morning than Seln had made it sound.

Cael stood at the end of the sound rock with the others and saw where a girl had been running three days ago. The shelf stopped as if it had been bitten. Beyond the break there was only the wall of the gorge, and along that wall a scar where the fallen stone had been rooted: a strip of raw pale rock four feet wide, tipped outward like the sill of a window, and heaped with broken plates. It ran on for thirty yards or so, and then the ledge began again, grey and level under its roof, as though nothing had happened. Under the scar there was a great deal of air, and he looked at that once.

On the stones of the lip, here and there, a small bright scratch showed, each the length of a finger.

"There are forty-four," said Seln, "and they are not in a line. Do not go by the look of a stone; go by the mark. Several without one look a good deal better than their neighbours."

He went over first, to stand at the far end, and he went as he did everything, without seeming to hurry and without a glance at the drop.

Oryn had loaded the mule in the hollow. Two of the boxes hung from the pack-saddle, one on either flank, and her own panniers were lashed on top with the crossbow in its cover. She took the animal by the cheek-strap and walked it to the edge of the break, and there she changed her hand and put herself on the outside.

"You're on the wrong side," said Lira. "If she slips she goes that way, onto you."

"If she slips with me on the inside, she goes that way with nothing to stop her. This way she's got something to lean on." Oryn clicked her tongue. "She's a mule, and she'd sooner lean than fall. Most things would."

They went out onto it together, the brown beast with her near flank brushing the wall and the square woman at her shoulder on the open side, a boot's length from the rim. The mule set each small hoof down and tried it. The woman's eyes were on the scratches; and where a marked stone lay under the animal's line she moved its head a little with her hand, and the foot came down there. At the far end Seln received the cheek-strap, and the boxes came off, and she brought the mule back as steadily as she had taken it.

"Now you," she said to Lira. "Up."

"I can walk it. You said tomorrow, with an arm, and it's tomorrow."

"I said you could stand. That leg's a day old; it'll bear you on a floor, and I'll not have it asked to catch you on that." She had made a step of her two hands already. "Up, and sit loose, and keep your knee off the wall."

Lira got up. She sat the pack-saddle as she would have sat a fence, sideways, with her new leg hanging and her bare foot turned in; and she fixed her eyes on the roof of rock over the far ledge and kept them there while she was taken across between a wall and a healer. When Seln lifted her down she said something short to him that Cael could not hear, and Seln bowed.

Brom had the third box on its end at the brink. He knelt in front of it and reached back over both shoulders, and stopped, since the healer was regarding his wrist from the far side of thirty-one paces.

"Carrying."

He said it across the gap, to her, before she had opened her mouth. Oryn considered the box, and the rope that went round it and over his chest, and his two hands, which were open.

"Not pulling."

It took Cael a breath to understand that she had agreed with him. Brom understood it at once. He stood up under the box like a man who has had a long account settled in his favour, and went over the lip sidelong, breast to the wall, placing his big boots on the scratches as daintily as a cat on a shelf of plates.

Seln came back for Karis. He put two fingers through the back of her belt and walked behind her, and she went across with her lips moving; and when she reached the sound ledge she turned round and studied the scar.

"Thirty-one," she called softly. "I make it the same."

Then there was nobody left on the near side but himself. He had known that it would be so, and had not let himself think of it. He set his left hand on the wall and his right foot on the first scratch.

The tape spoke at once. He had reached out a little farther than a man reaches who is walking on a floor, and the five bands drew across his side and said there; so he took the breath shallower and the reach shorter, and it let him be. He learned that he could go so, at half a stride, with his face to the rock.

Anchor asked, as he had expected. Here was a rim thirty yards long with a broken boy on it. There? There? Each stone under his boot was a place where a point might be fixed and told to stay, and at each one he said no, and moved his foot to the next mark.

There was something else that wanted to help him, and it was harder to turn away. The Iron read would have given him every plate on that lip within a pace and a half: which was seated on rock and which on its fellow, and which meant to go. He would have known the ground as he knew his own weight. He had refused it on a fan, when all that hung on it was a map.

At the twentieth pace he came to a place where his eyes and the scratches disagreed.

A broad slab lay directly before his right foot, level, as big as a hearthstone, and there was no mark on it. The mark was on the stone beyond, a mean tilted thing no wider than his boot. To reach that he must stretch, and the tape had already told him what it thought of stretching.

He stood still, with the gorge breathing cold up his back, and he knew what he was choosing between. On the one hand was what he could see for himself. On the other was a line the length of a finger, cut with the point of a knife by a mild man who had come this way in the rain and stood on forty-four stones, one at a time, to learn which would have him. Cael had not asked for that report. He had been given it, since it bore.

He stretched. The tape drew, hard, and three ribs said what they had to say, and his right boot came down on the mean stone and it did not move. Behind him, as his weight left the edge of the broad slab, it turned over like a page and went out into the air without a sound.

At the thirty-first pace there was a hand in his coat, and it was Brom's, the right one.

"Thirty-one," said Cael, when he could.

"So everybody says." Oryn was looking at his colour, and then at his side. "You breathed to the tape the whole way, which is more than most do. Most don't believe it till they've cried out once."

He turned round, since the others had. From the western ledge the whole bay lay open behind them. He could see the pale rock standing across its floor, and the two black lines above it that came to a point, and the wide scoured strip of the hill; and along the foot of the face, in a row, with a length and a half between each, the shale-backs were mowing.

He counted them, as he had not been able to before, and there were twenty-five. That was five fewer than thirty, and it was not the same thing as saying that five were dead.

They went down the western side of the mountain for the rest of that day and half of the next, and he had a great deal of time in which to watch the healer walk.

The track had been cut for mules. It left the shoulder by a notch and dropped in long slants across a hillside of brown grass and broken rock, turning back upon itself at every hundred yards, and the valley came up to meet them so slowly that the pale thread of the road seemed no nearer at dusk than it had at noon. Going down was worse for him than the level had been. Each step jarred, and the tape told him so, faithfully, some four thousand times; and he catalogued her, since it took his mind from his side.

She never hurried and she never stopped, and that was the first item. She had the carter's pace so deep in her legs that he thought she could not have walked at any other. On the steepest of the slants she shortened her stride and kept the beat, and where the track ran level she lengthened it again, and the time between one footfall and the next did not change by a hair. He tried to walk at it himself and could not, for he went in fits, quick and then slow, as he had always gone.

The second item was the side she kept. On every slant she was below the mule. When the track turned, and the fall of the hill went over to the other hand, she passed behind the animal's tail without breaking step and came up on the low side again; and she did it at every turn of that hillside without once seeming to consider which side was the low one.

The third he did not see until the first halt, and it was that she limped. It was very slight, a shade of favour to the right foot, and she had built it into the pace so thoroughly that it showed as a rhythm. When she sat down she had the boot off at once, and pressed the strip on her heel with her thumb, and put the boot on again.

"How long has that been raw?" said Karis.

"Two rounds. It closes when I'm three days at a hold, and I'm never three days at a hold."

"And you can do nothing for it, with all that you've got in your hands."

"I can tape it." Oryn pulled the lace tight. "I told you on the hill. Tide runs from the healer into somebody else, and there's nowhere in me to run it from. A healer with a broken leg lies in a barn six weeks like anyone, and hopes there's another healer on the next road. There isn't, out here."

Lira rode. She had been put up on the pack-saddle at the shoulder, on top of the panniers, between two boxes, and for the first hour she sat as stiff as a person on a throne that she expects to be taken away. She had not been carried anywhere since she could walk. She looked straight in front of her, over the long brown ears, and when the mule stopped to consider a turn she looked at the sky.

"How is she?" said Brom, on the second slant. He was walking at the tail with the third box on his back.

"Who?"

"The mule."

"I've not noticed a mule."

"You're on one."

"I'm on a saddle. I was told to get on a saddle, and I did, and I've nothing to say about what's under it." She shifted her weight, with some care. "It's very sure-footed, for a saddle."

"She's got a kind eye," said Brom.

"I wouldn't know. I can't see her eye from up here," said Lira, and then she shut her mouth, and Brom walked on behind with a look of deep content.

They slept that night beside a seep of good water, halfway down, in a fold of the hill where somebody had once built a wall of loose stones against the wind. Seln had found it, and he had a fire lit when they came up, and enough dead heather gathered to keep it till morning.

She saw the ankle from below, walking, on the second morning.

The track had come down by then onto the last long slant above the valley, and Lira's right foot hung from the pack-saddle at about the height of the healer's shoulder, bare, and wound with the strip of linen that it had worn since a meadow. Oryn went beside it for the better part of a mile. Cael, two paces behind, saw her eyes go to it and come away, and go again, as a person's go to a thing that somebody else has done badly.

"Who put that on?"

"I did," said Lira.

"It's holding nothing. It would keep a stocking up, if you'd a stocking."

Lira did not answer for the length of one turn of the track. Then she asked, from the back of a mule, over the heads of four people, and Cael could hear what it cost her to do it so.

"It's the cords, and not the bone. A woman with a barn bound it to a stave, four weeks back. She said that it would set stiff, and that a healer would have had it straight in an hour." Her voice was very level. "Can you mend it?"

Oryn kept her pace. She put up her left hand and took the heel in her palm as it swung, and went on so, with another person's foot in her hand and her lips moving; and it took her a good deal longer than ten. She turned the foot outward. It went a little way, and stopped.

"So I would have, that week," she said. "It's knit now. It's knit wrong, short, and across itself; but it's knit, and it's strong."

"Will you?"

She did her sum aloud, walking, as plainly as Pike did his.

"To put that right I'd have to take it back to the day it went, and undo the whole of what's grown, and then bring it up again straight. That's two mendings, or three; I'd not know till I was in it. Then you're on your back for a week while it takes." She let the foot go. "I've two in me on a road. There's a table at that ford that hasn't seen me for twenty-five days, and I don't know who's lying on the bench beside it. It could be nobody. It could be a man with his chest stove in, who's been waiting on me since the day I went up your hill."

She shook her head, once.

"That's a tape and a season. It'll set."

Lira looked straight in front of her, over the long brown ears, and Cael watched her take it. He had seen her take a result before, on a painted floor, with a sheet of figures to tell her how far she had fallen short. There was no sheet here. There was a square woman with tired eyes, walking below a mule, who had spent what she had on the leg above that ankle, and had said to her face what the rest was worth and to whom it was owed.

She had asked it once, and she let that be the whole of her asking.

"Then show me the tape," said Lira, a quarter of a mile farther on. "Properly. What's on it I did myself."

"At the halt. And when I've shown you, you'll take it off and do it, twice. I'm not going to be there every morning."

They halted at noon, on a floor of turf beside a stream, with the road in sight half a mile off, two pale ruts going north-west; and there she showed her. It was a craft, like any other, and Cael watched it as he would have watched a new guard. One turn went round the shin above the ankle, for an anchor; then a strip ran from the inside of that, down under the heel, and up the outside, pulled hard.

"Always that way, up the outside. That's the side it went over on, so that's the side that's slack, and you're lending it a cord." She laid a second beside the first, and then a long one that went over the top of the foot and under the arch and crossed itself in front, like a figure of eight. "That one stops it rolling." Her thumb went on up the shin, of its own accord, and pressed along the new bone. "And this is ahead of where I'd have looked for it. You've good legs. Somebody's taken trouble with them."

"A good many people," said Lira. "They kept lists."

"Now stand."

Lira stood, on turf, with her weight on both feet. She rocked forward onto her toes and back, and something went over her face that was a long way from joy and yet was on the road to it.

"It doesn't bend any more than it did."

"No, and it won't."

"But I know where it is." She tried it again. "It's been a beat behind since the river. I'd put it down and not be sure of it till it was down, and now I can feel the edge of it."

"That's all tape ever does," said Oryn. "Take it off. It's your turn."

They reached the road before dusk, and there was a halting-ground beside it, with a ring of chock-stones and the black place of other people's fires.

It was the second night down, and the first on which nobody was too cold or too sore to talk. Seln had the fire, and Brom had made a bread of the healer's flour on a hot stone; it had come off black on both faces, as his bread did, and everyone ate it.

Karis had been studying the healer across the flames for some time, with her notebook on her knee. She could hold a pencil again, for as long as it took to write a line, and she had spent her line that evening on the foot of the long leaf. Cael had seen it: Sixty-three. The ledge. Gone, for thirty-one paces. Cart: no.

"How long is your round?" she said.

"Twenty days," said Oryn.

She was sitting on a chock-stone with her boot off and her heel to the warmth.

"Round. Two at a hold, near enough, and the road between, and the road's the part that doesn't move."

"And how many holds?"

"Seven. I've been about them thirty-odd times, and I stopped counting rounds a while back, for what I count is days late." She turned the heel to the fire. "I've been late eleven times, and this is twelve."

"That's us," said Brom.

"That's a carter at the foot of a hill who had a split hand, and told me while I was looking at it that a crew was late off the top. I said I'd do his hand that evening, and then I went up to you." There was no reproach in it. "He's got it in a rag still. He hauls to Thornwater, and I'll find him."

"What do they pay you?" said Lira. "The holds."

"They feed me, and they feed her." She nodded at the mule. "One of them shoes her. One gave me this coat, the year I came, off a peg that nobody was using any more. I get a bed for two nights and a table to work at, and nobody's put a coin in my hand since I crossed the Line."

"Then what do you owe them, if there's no price?"

"The day." Oryn looked into the fire. "I said I'd be at a gate on a day. There's a child three stops on with a chest that I listen to every round, and most rounds it's nothing. One round it won't be nothing; and if I'm five days late for that one, then that's what five days cost."

She shrugged, heavily.

"That's what late is. It's not a number. It's who I don't know about."

Karis had waited as long as she was able.

"But you keep a record," she said. "You must. Seven holds, and every one with its sick; what you found, and what you did, and what you said you'd do next time. I should like to see how you've ruled it, for I've never seen a healer's book."

"I've not got one. No ledger, and no roll."

Karis's mouth came open.

"Then how do you know who's owed?"

"I remember who I've had my hands on," said Oryn.

She held them up to the firelight, the broad backs and the short nails.

"That's the book. A healer who needs a page to know who's owed has stopped looking at them."

Karis sat with her pencil lifted over nothing, and Cael saw the two halves of her at war: the archivist, who could not bear a thing unwritten, and the researcher, who knew a primary source when it sat down across a fire from her.

"I have a rule about not asking," she said at last, rather faintly. "I made it at a fence, and I've never wanted so much to break it."

"Ask what you like. I'll not write the answer down for you."

It was Lira who asked. She had her taped foot stretched to the fire beside the healer's bare one, and she had been quiet since noon.

"You're Iron, Rank Four; you said so on the hill. That's three above me, and I was thought good." She was picking the words. "A Tide of that rank could sit in any city on the continent and have people come to her. Why are you walking round seven fences with a sore heel?"

Oryn was slow to answer; and when she did, though her voice had not changed, Cael thought that it was the first thing she had told them which she had not said a hundred times before.

"A healing house keeps a list, every morning, of who's waiting and for what. Do you know what order it's in?"

"Worst first," said Brom.

"That's the order I keep. A house keeps it by tier, with Gold at the top, if there's a Gold, and then Silver, and so down. There were six of us on the staff, and I got what the four above me didn't want." She held her right hand out, palm down, at a level. "A Bronze comes in from his practice yard with a bruise the size of an egg, and he's seen that hour. There's a carter on the bench outside with his foot gone under a wheel, and he's no tier at all. He's seen when the list's done; and the list is never done, so mostly he gets up and goes home on it."

She let the hand fall.

"In the city I healed Bronzes with bruises. Out here I set legs. I'm not going back and I'm not stopping. The route is the only thing I own."

The fire ticked. Lira was watching the healer.

"Did you mind?" she said. "Leaving it, and not being told any more what you were."

"I know what I am. I'm twelve times late." Oryn reached for her boot. "Go to sleep. That leg knits at night."

Cael lay awake a while after the others. He had supposed, for eighty days, that he was the only person past the Line who had walked out from under a measure, and that the rest had merely been born beyond it. Here was another. She had not been thrown out, and she had not run; she had looked at a list that was kept in the wrong order, and gone to find a place where she could keep it in the right one, and all she had asked of that place was a road to do it on.

He had a book, and she had a route, and he did not think that either of them owned much else.

Oxhollow lay in the bend of a broad shallow river, and they came down to its ford in the middle of the eighty-fourth morning.

The road had brought them along a valley for a day and a half, through alder and rough pasture, with the mountain standing over their right shoulders the whole way. From below it was a long grey wall with a notch in it, and he had looked back at the notch a good many times. On the evening of the eighty-third day he had seen a line of smoke ahead, and in the morning a fence.

The hold stood on the far bank, in a hollow of the hill, behind a palisade of split logs with a gate in it, and between the gate and the water there was a low stone house with a turf roof. The ford itself was a hundred yards of pale gravel under a foot of quick water, wider than any he had crossed past the Line. On the near side a yoke of red oxen stood in the shallows with a chain between them, chewing.

"For the wheels," said Oryn. "He hooks on and draws them through, at a copper a wheel, and nothing for feet."

She had not slackened her pace for the river. She walked the mule into it on the low side, with Lira up, and the water went round her knees.

A man had come out of the stone house. He was old, and bent, and brown as a nut, and he had a hazel stick in his hand with notches cut all down one edge. He watched them cross as he would have watched a cart cross, counting. He nodded to the healer as to somebody who was owed that and more; and then his eyes went along five people in torn clothes, and a mule, and three boxes, and came back to Cael.

"You'll be the Lowmarch crew that took my paper. A carter said the one-armed man had given it to five." He looked past them, at the empty road, and at the river. "Where's your cart?"

Pike had said that he would ask it at his gate, and Cael had had the answer ready for six days.

"In your gorge."

The old man took that in without any change of face. Then he jerked his head at the door of the stone house, and they followed him in, all but the healer, who had gone on up the bank toward the palisade with the mule.

It was one room, with a hearth and a bench and a scrubbed board under the window. Karis unrolled the long leaf on the board, and weighted its four corners with the old man's own tally-sticks, and stood back.

He could not read. Cael saw that in the first breath, from where the brown eyes went; they passed over the six columns to the two ruled lines down the middle of the leaf, and to what was drawn between them, and a thick cracked finger came down on the foot of a fan and began to climb.

"That's loose," he said, at the first hatched tongue.

"A wheel floats on it," said Brom. "It wants a man on a rope, up the hill side, leaning."

"And that's a cross."

"Hollow under," said Lira. "Keep in."

He went on. They told him each mark as his finger reached it, in a word or two, as one names the stops on a road; and Karis, at his elbow, read him the number of each narrowing from her separate column. He stopped a long while at the seventh. He stopped longer at the eighth, where Cael had drawn a short black bar across both lines.

"Two feet, clean through," said Karis. "He took our cart over it on his back." She touched Brom's sleeve. "No carter crosses that by himself."

The finger moved on, over the bay, and came to the last three hundreds. The two ruled lines ran out there to the edge of the paper, and between them Cael had drawn nothing.

"And here?"

"It isn't there any longer," said Cael. "It went on the morning we came to it. There's a lip of rock thirty-one paces long that a mule can be led over, by somebody who knows how, and there's no road." He put his own finger on the margin, where Karis had written small. "And that's the herd. They're shale-backs, on the face over the bay. If anybody uses a Path beneath them, they run, and the hill comes with them, and that's how your cart went into the gorge. She's written that it's derived."

"I have written that it was derived on the seventy-fifth day, and used on the seventy-eighth," said Karis. "I haven't written that it's tested. The ledge went and the declaration followed in the same breath; that's two causes in an accident, not one cause in a test."

The old man straightened, as far as he could. He looked at the leaf from end to end once more, the whole length of a mountain on a board.

"Then they go round," he said.

He went to the hearth and lifted a stone out of it, and brought back a leather bag.

"That's worth seventy to know before the first cart's in the gorge. I'd sooner pay it for this."

"There's one in it," said Brom.

"Aye, and it's yours." The brown eyes were not unkind. "I'd have sent three of mine over that top at the next dry spell, with a Force walking behind to shift what fell, for he's the only strong back I've got. I'll not, now."

He counted it onto the board in tens, seven heaps of thick worn silver, beside the foot of the fan. Then he took a burnt stick and made his mark at the bottom of Pike's slip, pressing hard: a horned head, broad between the horns, over three wavy strokes.

Karis had the purse open in her two hands, for she could hold it now.

"Earned, a hundred and thirty-two," she said. "In hand, a hundred and sixteen, and six owed to a smith."

"Sixty-three lines," said Lira. She was leaning in the doorway on her taped foot, looking at the heaps. "And seventy marks. That's a mark a line, and seven over. What are the seven for?"

"For the last line," said Karis. "It's the only one that says no."

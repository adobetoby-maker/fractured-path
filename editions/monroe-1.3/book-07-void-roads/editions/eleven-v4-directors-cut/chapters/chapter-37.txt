Chapter 37 — Minute Twenty-One

He counted breaths, since it was the only count that she had left him.

A man asleep draws twelve to the minute. He had timed Brom against Hesk's clock once, on a road, for want of anything better to do, and had kept the figure. Twelve to the minute made sixty in five, and two hundred and forty in the twenty that she had named; and he lay on a scrubbed plank with a healer's hand over his breastbone and began at one.

For the first dozen there were only her hands. They were warm, and broad, and they did not move. He could feel the short nails of the left one against the back of his skull, and the heel of the right resting on bone. He could feel her pulse in it, which was slow.

"That's skin," said Oryn.

Her voice was lower than he had ever heard it, and it came from a little way off, as a person's does who is talking in her sleep.

"That's breath. That's a heart, and it's a good one; somebody's run it hard since it was small. Those are the three I taped, and they're done. I said that they were."

She was silent while he drew six more.

"Past that, now."

He had thought about what it would be. He had lain awake over it in a loft, with the book under his hand. He had been read by a great many things, and the only one of them that he could do himself went down: it opened under his boots like an eye and took the weight of whatever was beneath, a pace and a half, straight into the ground. He had supposed that a healer's reading would do the same, and that he would feel it come down into him from her palm as a plumb-line comes down a wall.

It did not go down. It went along.

He felt it leave her right hand at about the thirtieth breath. It was not warmth, and it was not a touch; it was nearer to what a person feels when somebody else's attention comes to rest on the back of his neck. It set out from his breastbone toward his feet, and it went the whole length of him without haste, as a joiner runs his thumb down the edge of a plank to learn whether it is true. When there was no more of him to go along it turned, and came back up the other side, and he felt it arrive under the nape of his neck, in her left hand, where she was waiting for it.

Then it set out again.

He lay very still. It was not even, and that was the first thing about it that he could have sworn to. Down his right side it bore a shade more lightly than down his left, and came a hair late. Each time, the heel of her hand on his breastbone eased and settled with it, by so little that he would never have caught it without three weeks of a tape to teach him what a hair was. He knew that unevenness. He had walked behind it for a day and a half down a mountain before he saw what it was: a raw heel, taped, and favoured for so long that it had been built into everything she did.

It was only after that that he found the beat. It was slower than his heart and slower than his breath, and it did not vary. It was the pace that she kept on a road, below a mule, and she was going through him at it.

He could feel her keeping time. He had not expected that, of all things: that he would lie under an instrument and be able to tell that it was a person. A brass plate in a district hall had waited over him for eleven seconds, and he had not felt it wait; an assessor's panel had read him across a floor, and he had got nothing from it but a number on a slate. This one limped.

He had been afraid, coming over the yard, that it would be like being searched. He had been searched by a good many careful people; he knew the hands of a clerk going through a satchel, and the eyes of a panel going over a stance for the thing that was wrong with it. This did not rummage. It went down him and came back as a carter goes down his own road, looking at what was there, and it took up nothing to see what lay beneath.

"There," said Oryn.

It was at the fifty-fourth breath.

"There it is. I've got the moving. I had that much at a ford with two fingers." Her hand had not shifted on his chest. "Now I go and look for what it's moving in."

He drew the sixtieth, slowly, like a man asleep. That was five minutes, and she had only just come in.

She talked, as she had told him that she would.

He had supposed that it would be like the talk at a bench, short and to the purpose. It was nothing of the kind. It was the low running murmur of a person who is feeling her way down a passage in the dark and saying what her hands meet, less to be heard than to keep herself from being lost; and he lay under it and counted, and tried to do as he had been told, which was nothing.

It was a good deal harder than refusing.

He had refused the nine at every stride of a road, and he knew that work as he knew the weight of his own boots. He was not to refuse them now; a refusal was a holding, and she had said that she would know. He was not to let them answer either. He had to lie as a house lies when somebody goes through it with a lamp, with every door standing as it happened to stand, and he found that he had never in his life left a door alone.

Her current went down the length of him and came back.

Eighty-four. Something in his feet turned over like a sleeper who had heard his name. It was Wind. He did not tell it no, and he did not let it rise. He let it turn over, and it settled.

His right palm grew warm on the plank. He left it where it lay.

She was silent for several breaths. He felt the thing that she was doing go more slowly along his left side, where three ribs had been, and then down the leg that a hound had opened, and stop there, and go on.

"Everyone I've ever had my hands in, this is the part where I know." She said it as a finding about herself. "Iron Skin, you feel the banks first — dense, walled, everything held. Ember, the banks are a fire's, hot at the edge. Wind you barely feel the banks at all, the water's too fast. But there's always a bank. It's how you know which river." A pause, long, her current going along Ember and stopping at the ignition point and lying there as if it had found a stone in a bed. "I can't find one here. I keep putting my hand where the bank ought to be and there's more water."

He was at a hundred and eight. That was nine minutes.

He had begun to be able to tell where she was. It was like lying in a field with his eyes shut while somebody walked round him: he had her by a sense that he had no name for, now at his knee, now under his heart. And as she came to each place, the thing that he carried there did what it would have done if a stranger had come to its door.

Compression lay through the whole of his frame, and it did nothing at all; it had been built to wait for a blow, and no blow came. The read wished to open. It wished it very much. There were two hands on him and a square woman standing over a table, and it could have told him the weight of her to a pound and which way she leaned. He left it shut. He did not hold it shut. He found, after a while, that there was a difference, and that it lay in whether his jaw was tight.

"You're helping," said Oryn.

His eyes came open. She had not opened hers.

"Your neck went hard. Let it go. I don't need you to keep anything out of my way. I need you to lie there."

He let it go. It took him three breaths.

"That's it. That's a man asleep."

She went on down.

It was at about the hundred and thirtieth that she came to where the ninth lay, and that one did a thing that none of the others had done. It drew in. He felt it go from her as a snail's horn goes from a finger, small, and smaller, into some place behind the rest where even he could hardly have put a hand on it. He had not asked it to. He had not asked anything of it since a lane. It was a thing made for not being found, and somebody was looking, and it had hidden by itself, as its first owner had hidden on a back stair before he was old enough to choose.

Her current went over the place and did not check.

He breathed out. He had not known, until then, that he was afraid of what she might say.

The heaviest of them she passed last, low down, under everything. He had felt it shift in a loft the night before, and it had been restless since a ditch; it had three lanes still in it, like a sea with a swell left over from weather a long way off. He waited for it to rise at her.

It did not. It lay quiet under her hands. She went along the top of it as a boat goes over deep water, and it did not so much as lift.

A hundred and forty-four, he counted. Twelve minutes. He lay with his neck loose and all nine doors standing as they stood, and for the first time since a morning in a windowless room there was somebody in the house besides himself.

It was in the thirteenth minute that he understood what was strange about her.

He had been waiting for it without knowing that he waited. He had lain under hands before, and under eyes; he knew to the breath when it came. The thing reaching for him would find that he was not what it had been built to hold, and then it would change. A brass plate went dark. A panel's faces shut. A clerk with a form that had no box for him wrote a word in capitals, because he could find no tier to write; and after that there was a file, and a watcher at a gate.

They had all been afraid of him. He had first seen it on the morning of his Kindling, and had not had the word for it then. It had looked like anger, mostly.

She had found it eight minutes ago, and he had heard her find it: there it is, I've got the moving. She had been walking up and down the length of something for twelve minutes that was like nothing she had ever put her hands in, and her voice was the voice in which she had told an ox-driver that four small bones were broken.

She was not afraid of him.

He turned that over, slowly, at a man asleep's pace. She had watched him go up a hill with fire in his fist, in a place where she could not count to four, and she had asked him whether his calf was worse than an arm. She had read him afterward and found nothing, and said so, and left his leg in its cloth.

It was not courage, and he did not think that it had so much as occurred to her that courage was wanted. It was her trade. A healer who stood amazed at a broken leg would never set it; she had spent her working life walking up to the worst thing in a yard and asking which it was, and her craft had no room in it for being impressed. He was the strangest thing that she had ever touched, and to her that made him a difficult reading, which she was doing as well as she knew how.

He had been a case to a great many people. He had never before been a patient.

Something in him eased that had been braced for longer than he had carried any of the nine, and he let it; and she did not tell him that he was helping.

She had gone quiet. He counted through the fifteenth minute, and the sixteenth, with nothing over him but her breathing and the slow going-along; and he felt it change. It was no weaker, but it had been a walk, and now it was a search. She went down one side of him and stopped, and came back a hand's breadth, and tried it again from a little farther in, as she had tried a stone with her feet at dawn, eight times. She was looking for an edge: under his heart, and along his arms to the fingers, and in the long muscles of his back. She was not finding one, and she would not leave off.

The sweat had come out on her. A drop of it fell on his collarbone.

Two hundred and four. That was seventeen. Two hundred and sixteen.

Her right hand pressed down a very little on his breastbone, as though she were leaning on a door.

"I'm going to say what I've got." Her voice had roughened. "I say it while I'm in. It's truer."

He did not answer, since he had been told not to speak to her.

"I can feel that there's movement. More than one. I can't count them — I can't find where one stops. There's no channel. Everyone has a channel; I feel the banks and I know the Path. I can't find the banks in you. I've looked for twenty minutes. It isn't that you're broken. It's that I can't find anything shaped like the thing I read."

It was the longest thing that he had ever heard her say with her eyes shut.

More than one. She had it, and she did not know what she had. She had gone through a house with a lamp and counted more rooms than a house ought to hold, and she had stood in every one of them and could not find a wall.

He was at two hundred and twenty-eight. That left one minute of her twenty.

Her hands did not lift. He felt her draw a breath all the way down, and let it go, and there was something in the going that he had not heard in her at a bench or on a hill or beside a bar of cold iron. She was not tired; he knew what her tiredness sounded like. A person who had only ever owned one thing had put the whole of it down on a table and found that it would not buy what was there.

"Eleven years," said Oryn. "I've never had my hands in something and not known what it was."

She did not take them away. She leaned on them, and went in again.

It came then.

He knew it before it had finished coming. He had felt it nine times. The first four he had hardly known that he was there for; after that he had learned to be present. It had always come in the thick of a blow, or at the end of a bout, with his arm ringing. It had never before come into a quiet room.

There was a place in him that stood empty. He had not known of it until it was offered something.

And along the slow current that she kept, the one that set out from her right hand and came home to her left, he could feel a shape. It was not his. It was the way she went: the keeping of time, and the feeling ahead for a bank, and the patience of it, which would walk the length of a person eight times sooner than guess. It was the thing that she read with. She had laid the whole of it open on him for twenty minutes, in earnest, at a cost, and she was pressing it against him now harder than she had pressed it yet.

It stood at his breastbone, under her palm, like a hand held out in the dark.

He could stop it.

That was the first thing that he was sure of. He had refused nine things a stride at a time for a hundred days, and he was better at that one act than at anything else he did. He had only to say no, at the first stride, as he had said it on a gravel bar. Her current would go by the empty place and find it shut, as it had found the ninth; she would come to the end of her twenty minutes and lift her hands and tell him that there was nothing, as before. He would get down off her table with what he had lain down on it with. Nothing would have been taken from anybody.

He went through what came after that, as quickly as he had gone through it on a white floor with his hands on a skull.

It would be right. He did not hide that from himself, lying there. Four people had said so in a loft, and nothing that he had written afterward by a bead of oil had altered it by a hair. There was even an honest way to do it: he could shut the place, and when she lifted her hands he could tell her all the same what had stood open in him while she worked, and what he had come within a breath of taking.

That was the right road. He saw the whole length of it, and he did not want it.

There was another way to stop, and he looked at that one longer, since it was the one that he would really have taken. He could shut the place and say nothing. He would walk out into the yard having wanted a thing with his whole heart, and having climbed a ladder to say so, and having written it on a leaf with the price beside it. Four people would be glad, and he would let them be; and the leaf would turn into a page about a boy who had thought of doing wrong and had not. That stopping would be a lie, and he would not add it to the rest.

So he knew which was right, and he chose against it. He was exact about why. It was not that a leaf permitted him; a thing written down beforehand is only a thing done on purpose. It was that he wanted to know what he was more than he wanted to be in the right, and the one instrument that might tell him was standing at his breastbone. He would answer for it within the minute, on this table, to her face.

He looked at it squarely, with his neck loose and his eyes open on a rafter.

He reached. Directed. Open-eyed. Still.

It was a small thing to do. He had not known how small. He did not so much as shift a finger on the plank. He opened the one door in the house that had been shut, and something came through it at her pace.

The place behind his breastbone took on weight. It filled slowly at first and then all the way to the brim, like a cup under a careful hand; and he lay under a healer's palm and kept his eyes open through all of it.

The lines came after. They stood where they had always stood, plain, in the place that belonged to nothing else, and nobody in that room could see them but himself.

It sat under his breastbone now, as real as the other nine, and he did not try it. He did not know how. And he had a healer's hand on him still, and a debt to pay before he had any right to learn.

He counted what was new in it, since he could not have helped counting.

It was the first that had ever come to him lying down. It had come out of nobody's blow, in a room with a basin in it, from two hands that were trying to learn what he was made of. That was one.

Her hands had gone still.

They had not lifted. But the slow going-along had stopped, somewhere down by his knees, and it did not set out again. He heard her breath catch and hold.

He looked up at her. He had not done that since she shut her eyes. Her face hung over him, upside down, grey, with the sweat standing on it and the cropped hair dark; and the crease had come between her brows, the one that she wore over a sum that had come out oddly.

Her eyes opened.

She was looking straight down into his. He had seen those eyes go over a yard of people in the time that it takes to count five. They were not going over anything now.

"Something just changed," she said. "In you. While I was in there."

"Yes."

He had told her not one true thing about himself since a hollow of shale, beyond what she had asked; and he gave her that word as he would have handed her the first coin of a debt.

She did not take her hands away. He felt the right one move on his breastbone, by the width of a finger, toward the place where a cup had filled. It stopped there. She could not read him. She had said so five times. But she had been walking the length of something for twenty minutes, and she knew its gait by now as he knew hers; and it was not going as it had gone.

He watched her do what she did at a bench. She did not guess. She went over what her hands had found, strictly, and came to the one thing that a healer must ask first.

"Did I do that?"

There was no fright in it. It was the question that she would have put if a patient had gone white under her on the ash at Oxhollow.

He could have let her think so. He saw it open in front of him, as easy as a gate: it would have been a healer's hands doing something that they had never done before, to a boy that they could not read, and she would have carried it north with her and turned it over at every hold for a year.

"No. I did."

The crease deepened. She waited. She was a person who knew how to let somebody come to the end of his own load.

He had a hundred days of road behind him in which he had been asked what he was by nobody. He had thought, on a shelf above a valley, that this was freedom, and had written it down. He lay now under the hands of the one person past the Line who had tried to find out without a seal, and who had just paid for the trying with something that he did not yet know the size of; and he had a leaf in a loft that said what he owed.

"I need to tell you what I am."

He had not known that it would be so hard to say. It was only eight words.

Oryn looked at him for as long as it takes to draw one breath, and he could not tell what was in her face. It was not fear. It had never been that. Then the corner of her mouth went down, a very little, as it went when a patient on her table began to sit up before she had done.

"Then lie still while you do it. You've been still for twenty minutes. I want to see if you can manage twenty-one."

Her hands stayed where they were.

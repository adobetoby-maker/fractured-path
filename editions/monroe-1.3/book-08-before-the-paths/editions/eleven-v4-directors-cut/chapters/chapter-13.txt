The page lay where it had lain all night, on its square of oilcloth at the bare end of the deal, and the first grey light out of the one small window showed Cael that nobody had moved it.

He had slept beside it, after a fashion, on the meal-sacks. It was one line out of an old register, copied by a man who had then walked three hundred miles to lay it on that table, and it sent whoever read it to a town. He had got up twice in the dark to see that it was there, which was foolish, since a sheet of desk paper does not walk; and each time he had stood over it, keeping both hands by him, and had gone back to the sacks.

Karis came in before the smith's first hammer, with her gloves on already.

"I am packing," she said. "I said that I should count in daylight, on a table without elbows, and I have been awake since the middle of the night working out where such a table is to be had. It is not in this inn. So everything goes into the flat, in the order that he gave it, and the flat goes where I go."

She folded the oilcloth over the page along its own creases, and did not look at him while she did it. The broker's sheet went under the sixth row, and the straps drew down across a red stamp which said that the thing was to go by hand.

"You have still not touched it."

"No."

"I am not asking you to. I only enter what I see." She put the flat under her arm. "It is a hand's width that you have kept, all night. I should not have had the patience."

He followed her out through the tap-room, where the keeper was raking his ashes, and into the yard.

It was the two hundred and seventy-eighth morning past the Line, and cold, with a white mist on the river that hid the cable's middle. Under the long eave, on twelve feet of tarred oak, Pike had his book open and his stone on the leaf. He was driving a tack with the butt of his penknife into the last clear space at the plank's right hand.

"That came over in the bag," he said, without turning. "I had it yesterday, with eleven pieces to give out and a boat that nearly went down to the mill. I don't pin by lamp."

Five of them read it together from the left-hand post, since Lira and Brom had come round from the well, and Seln had been standing there already, with no wand under his arm. It was a strip of bark, like every other slip on that oak, in the square black hand. Beside the words Pike had drawn a little road going between two heaps, and on the road something with four legs.

Clearance. The cut below the shoulder hold, on the border road, two days west.

"It's twenty," said Pike. "At the hold's own gate, on the factor's thumb, when the factor's walked it. That's a hold that's lost carts. It doesn't say what to. They never do; they'd have to pay more."

Cael read the slip a second time, as he read anything, and it was the last five words that he read. Two roads left the western landing. One ran away to the south and west for three hundred miles, to a ford and a station and a house with a seal, and an old man had come down it on a stick. The other was the border road. It went up over a moor to the Line by the short way, and Cael knew what stood at the end of it, since Karis had laid her palm on a strip of oilcloth and told him; and last night he had read it again, in a square black copy, under a title.

"Two days west," said Lira, with her lips. She did not say the rest.

At the far end of the eave, beyond the wash-house door, the keeper had a settle against the wall for carters to wait on. Vastin sat there now, at the door end, with his stiff leg out before him and both hands on the head of the ash stick. He was on no slip on that oak, and on no line of that book. He had read the new one as soon as it was pinned; Cael saw his eyes go to it once and then away to the river, as a guest's go from a letter that is not his.

"Ours," said Karis.

She had said it small and clear, with the flat under her arm. Cael had known her since a library table in Ostrand, and in all that time he had never once heard her claim a job. She had put her hand up for fences and for roofs when hands were asked for, and had gone where the count sent her; she had never looked at a slip and wanted it. Pike's pen stopped over the horn.

"Ours," said Brom.

He had said that word at this plank before, of a fence, and had had it written down. He said it now as he would have set his foot on a plank to learn whether it bore. Then he looked at Cael, and so did the other three.

"You said hands," said Lira. "At the plank, in daylight."

"I did."

"Then say what they're for. I'll not put mine up for a thing with two meanings."

It was fair, and he had lain on the meal-sacks for most of a night finding out that he could answer it.

"They're for a slip. There's a cut two days up a road, with something in it that takes carts, and a hold that'll pay twenty to have it out. I can price that. I know what a clearance costs; each of you has a mark on you from the last one." He kept his eyes on the bark. "I'm not ruling on a town. I said that I'd not rule over a thing I hadn't touched, and I've still not touched it. I can't price a town. Every figure that I've got for it is somebody else's."

"It's the same road," said Brom.

"It's the same road, and I can read. I'm not going to stand at this post and pretend that I took a job without seeing where it lay, and I'd not ask any of you to pretend it either." He made himself look at them then: a big man with four yards of webbing on him, and a girl with her left hand in her coat pocket, and a small pale woman holding six years of somebody's counting under her arm. "The boxes come. So do the papers, and the kit. I'll not leave a thing of ours in a store for a week with a river between. When the cut's done we'll be two days nearer than we are this morning, with the road going on in front of us, and I'll know more about what I'm walking with than I do now. That's all that I can honestly say I've decided. It's a job. I'll not make it a vow."

"That is a ruling," said Seln.

"It's half of one."

"It is the half that can be kept. I have seen a great many of the other kind." He had his hands in his sleeves. "I should sooner follow a man up a road for twenty marks than for a principle. The twenty marks will still be there in the morning."

Lira had been considering Cael with her head on one side.

"I told you so last night. You said that you'd ask for hands, and I knew what you'd ask them for before you'd shut your mouth. You were never going to say the word. You were going to find a slip." She took her left hand out of her pocket. "I'm not complaining. It's how I'd have done it. I only want it known that I saw it first."

"It's known."

"Then hands," said Lira, and put up the left.

It went up slowly, and a little crooked at the elbow, and it shook. But it was the first time since a white floor on the Fallow that she had voted with it, and she held it there until he had seen. Brom's left went up beside it. Karis shifted the flat, and raised her right hand with the glove still on. Seln lifted two fingers, and Cael put up his own.

"Five," said Pike. He made the strokes at the slip's foot, and then he did not dip his pen again. He turned his shoulder to them. "And who's your healer?"

"We've not—"

"It's a clearance. I've written a good many of those taken, and I've written some of them closed with a line drawn under a name. In my second year here a crew of four went up the north water, and one of them bled for two days on a hurdle for want of anybody who knew where to put a thumb. Since then I don't write a clearance taken until I've a healer said. It's not the hold's rule. It's mine." He scratched his stump. "Teague's old woman goes up the cold road with him. So who's yours?"

Nobody at the post said the name. They had laid a sixth bowl for it every night since a heap of stones, and had seen it eaten out of standing; and not one of the five would have named her to a ledger without her leave.

"I can hear you not saying it."

Oryn was in the wash-house door, a yard past the plank's end, drying her hands a finger at a time. There were four people on her bench already. She looked along them, and at the river; and last, for the length of a breath, she looked toward the settle at the eave's far end, where a stiff leg was stretched out on the flags.

"I've a bench here that's done by noon, and it's had six mornings of me. The mill's my second stop. It's waited a whole cold term, and it can wait four days more while I go round by the top and come down on it from above; I've five gates up that way that I'd have had to climb to in any case." She threw the cloth over her shoulder. "I said once that somebody ought to stand at an edge with her sleeves up. I'm not having a girl with a fortnight's arm go into a cut behind something that takes carts, and hearing about it afterward from a carter. Write me. I'm a line on that slip. I'm not a name under theirs."

Pike considered her in his double fashion. Six days ago he had had no word in his book for a woman who did what nobody had posted.

"That's posted," he said slowly. "A clearance wants a healer. It's my own rule, and you've answered it, and I can write posted." The pen went into the horn. Beneath the five strokes he made a sixth line, apart from them, and said it over twice to the mist. "And a healer; of her own road; at her own price. That's the first time I've had a column for you."

"Don't grow fond of it," said Oryn, and went in to her bench.

They were on the landing an hour before noon, with the mist gone and the river a hand lower than it had been at dusk.

It took that long to leave a place where five people had lain for a hundred and forty-eight nights. Brom carried the three boxes down from the hearth's end one at a time, roped as he had roped them in the night, and slung them on the brown mule with the beckets outward, while she stood for it at the shed's door with her ears back, like a person being measured for a coat. Karis walked beside the load with the hide under one arm and the flat under the other, and would give neither of them to the mule.

Cael left the word with Pike, as he had promised a better captain that he would: the cut, two days west by the border road, and whatever lay beyond it, with no day put on the coming back. Pike wrote nothing. He said that he had a season line of theirs with no number against it, and had stopped expecting to fill it by the week.

Vastin had risen from the settle when the first box went by.

He did not come to the plank, and he asked nobody anything. Cael saw him go in to the keeper, and come out again putting up his small purse; he had paid for the second night in the loft, and it appeared that he did not mean to have a third. He took up his pack by its one sound strap. Then he stood by the gate with the stick grounded until the mule had gone through it, and Oryn after the mule, with her strip of canvas rolled under her arm. When the last of them was a dozen strides down the bank he came after, at his own pace, and kept that distance.

Nobody had asked him, and Cael noticed that nobody turned round to tell him no.

Teague's salt was going down the planks. There were forty sacks of it in a grey heap at the landing's head, and the ferryman would have no cart on his boat yet, or any bale that a man could not carry; and so it went aboard on backs. The bow took a sack at a time, and came up again easing his weight onto the right leg. The boy took two, and was told by nobody to take one. The tall man carried his on the shoulder that the spear did not use, with seven feet of ash in his other hand, shod with its steel again, and he set each sack down in the scow's waist square upon the last, as though he were laying a course of stone.

Teague stood at the post's foot with a slip in his fist, counting them by. He glanced at the mule and the three boxes, and it was enough for him.

"Which way?"

"West. There's a cut below a hold, two days up the border road, that's been taking carts. It's ours."

"The shoulder hold. I've carried salt up to them. It's a bad piece of road in the cold; it lies in its own shadow, and there's water comes down the middle of it. I never lost a mule there, since I went through at noon, on foot, with their heads held." His eyes had gone past Cael's shoulder, up the bank, to where a brown coat was coming down a stride at a time. They stayed there a stride longer than a crew looks, as they had the morning before, on a pair of hands on the head of a stick, wrapped now in strips of the keeper's linen. "He's walking with you."

"He's walking behind us. I've not asked him, and I've not told him no."

"A man that holds a thing so doesn't leave off because nobody's asked him. I'd know what I had at my back, if it were my road."

It was as near as he had ever come to giving advice, and Cael took it as he would have taken a touch called. He did not say that he knew exactly what was at his back. He had sat across four feet of table from it, once, for three days.

"My term's where I left it," said Teague.

"I know where it is."

The chin came up. Then he turned to his sacks. The tall man went by with another on his shoulder, and gave five people and a healer the length of a stride, and not a word.

The ferryman took the mule aboard last, with a sack over her eyes.

"A copper a head, and she's a head." He looked at the boxes. "They're not. They're on her."

Brom counted eight coppers out of the small money into that long sour palm. Then he looked at what he had done, and took one back.

"He pays his own," he said, low, to Cael. "He told us so at the board. I'd not shame him."

Vastin had his copper ready. He gave it with the short stiff bow, and stepped over the rail bad leg first, and went aft without being told, to the post where a new strap of stern line had been rove. He stood there through the whole of the crossing with his hands on his stick, a foot from the thing that he had held two days before with his back, and did not touch it. The ferryman, at the sweep, looked once at him and once at the strap. Cael thought that it was the nearest that man had ever come to thanking anybody.

Above the western landing a long stack lay under tarred sheets, as it had lain since the last boat of the old year.

Brom had the purse in his fist before the mule was off the scow. He had carried that debt since before the snow: sixty yards of four-strand rope, to a ferryman who would take no silver for it. A brisk man with ink on his cuffs came along the stack to meet him, with a clerk at his elbow.

"Cordage," said Brom. "Sixty yards. Four-strand."

"I've the very thing."

The broker turned back a corner of the sheet himself, and laid his hand on the nearest coil as a man lays his hand on a horse that he means to sell. Brom did not look at the tag; he took the rope's end between his finger and thumb, and bent it back on itself, and opened the lay with his nail.

"That's three."

"It's hawser-laid. It's the best three-strand that comes up this river, and it'll outwear any four that you could show me."

"I've no doubt of it. He asked for four." Brom let the end go. "A four's laid round a heart, and it keeps its round in a block, and a ferry's rope lives in blocks. You could give me this, and I'd still be owing him sixty yards of four." He put the purse inside his shirt, where the dead slips lay. "A man lent us a rope and told us what he wanted back. I'll not hand him something else and call it paid, however good it is. When you've four, there's a hundred and eighteen marks will come down this road to look at it."

The broker watched him go with his mouth a little open, like a man who has sold cordage all his life and has never before had any refused for being the wrong kind of excellent.

The border road left the river by a cart-track through alders, and at the first rise it turned its back on the south.

It was a road again as soon as the landing was out of hearing, and the hold came down on all of them as it had on every road in that country. The sigils stayed in their pockets. Cael's ten lay shut; the read asked at the first bend, civilly, what the wet ruts weighed, and was told that it need not know. Seln went ahead by thirty yards with his hands a little away from his sides. Lira came next, with the bow on her back. Brom had the mule's cheek-strap, with Karis at the off side of the load and Oryn at the near, and Cael walked behind the mule, as he always had.

Behind Cael there was a space of a dozen strides, and then a stick.

Nobody had told the man where to walk. A crew on a road has a place for each person in it, and each of those places had been found by somebody's getting hurt; nobody had yet been hurt for want of a seventh. So Vastin walked where a man walks who has been given no place, which is last, and a little too far back, and in the middle of the road.

Cael dropped back to him at the top of the rise. He had not looked forward to it. He was nineteen, and the man beside him had told people for forty years on which side of a line they stood; and Cael had now to tell him how to walk along a road.

"There's a rule out here, and you've been inside it since that landing. I'd sooner you had it from me than from the thing that it's about."

"I should be glad of it. I have noticed for twelve days that people on this road do not speak, and I took it for manners."

"It isn't manners. There's a thing that lives along the roads, grey, as high as a calf at the shoulder. The holds call it a stillhound. It'll walk beside you for a mile at forty yards, and you can talk, or drop a pot, and it only follows. What it's waiting for is a Path." He kept his voice at the pitch that the road allowed by day. "It hunts whatever goes out of a person when a Path's used. Karis has a word for it."

"Discharge," said Vastin. "It is on the form. An assessor marks it under presence, and I have signed for a great deal of it."

"Out here it gets you eaten. They come to it all together, from as far as they can feel it, and they don't leave off while it lasts. So nobody declares on a road. A carter who's Iron to the elbows will let his wheel go over a bank before he'll bring up the grey to hold it."

"And if one of them comes close?"

"Then you're a post. It goes by a silent person as it'd go by a tree. If it wants to be sure, it tries you with its teeth; and if you answer it, with anything, you've told it what you are. You give it something wrapped, and you stand. And since nobody speaks at such a time, there are hands."

He showed them as they walked. A flat hand, with the palm to the ground, was stand: wherever a person was, and as he was. A shut fist was seen, and one looked where the fist went and did not ask. Two fingers lifted were come on; that was Seln's, mostly. And a hand turned over empty, as a girl had once turned hers over to him on a hillside, was nothing: the thing had gone, or it had never been.

"Stand; seen; come; nothing." Vastin made each of them with his own right hand, in its linen, exactly, like a junior learning the seals. "I have them."

Thirty yards ahead, at a bend, a small grey figure lifted two fingers.

"Understood," said Vastin, in a clear carrying voice, to Seln's back.

The fingers stayed up a breath longer than they needed. Then they came down, and Seln went on round the bend without having turned his head.

"That's the one thing you don't do with them," said Cael.

"No. I heard it as I did it." Vastin's face had not altered, but the back of his neck had gone a dull red above the frieze. "I have acknowledged every instruction that I was ever given, aloud, and I have required it of others. I beg your pardon. It will not be the last."

They made eleven miles that day, by Brom's reckoning, which was a mile more than a certain knee could do, and nobody remarked on it.

The halting-ground lay in a fold of the moor beside a beck, with a dozen rings of stones on it, and black in every ring. No fire had been lit there since the first frost. The carters of that country ate cold on a road, and Cael had long since left off missing a flame at dusk. He saw Vastin look at the rings, and ask nothing, having been told why.

They drilled him for the last of the light, and it was Lira who did most of it. She had taught a boy from another crew to wait for a pole, and she went at an Archmarshal in the same fashion and with no more ceremony, walking backward before him down forty yards of rutted road with her right hand going. He stood for the flat hand and came on for the two fingers; and when the fist was thrown out to the left he looked left, and found a thorn-bush, and regarded it with great attention.

"That's well enough," she said, low. "Now look at yourself. No, don't move. Look where you are."

He was in the middle of the road, upright between the two ruts, with his weight laid evenly on both feet and the stick grounded, as he had stood on a landing in Ostrand and at the head of a ferry's planks.

"Now look at them."

Brom had been walking behind him, with Karis and the mule. There was nobody behind him now. The mule was nose-down in the heather at the road's edge, with a large man bent beside her as if he were looking at a hoof, and Karis had sat down on a stone. Cael could not have found Seln at all, if he had not known where to look.

"You stood like a gatepost," said Lira. "It's the finest standing that I've ever seen. But a post is a thing that nobody looks at, and I looked at you first of everything on this moor. Do it by the ditch. Do it a bit bent."

"I do not know that I can do it bent."

"Then do it by the ditch."

He did it by the ditch, four times, until the light went. He was a little too far out each time, and each time a hair less so.

They ate on the stones of a dead ring, with the beck talking.

"May I ask a thing that I ought perhaps to have asked this morning?" Vastin had his bread in his hand, and he did not lower himself onto a stone until he had the question out. "You have told me what I am to do if one of them comes at me. You have not told me what I am to do if it comes at one of you."

"The same," said Cael. "You're a post. If it's got Brom by the arm, then Brom has given it the arm. He'll be bitten, and he'll not answer, and somebody with a rock will come up on its blind side while it's finding out what he is. It was done so at a ford."

Vastin looked at his bread.

"Then I had better tell you what I can promise, since I have never yet made an undertaking that I did not expect to keep. I can be silent, and I can walk at the ditch. I think that I can be bitten. I do not believe that I can stand a stride from a person who is being bitten, and do nothing." He spoke as low as the dusk required. "I have stood in front of people for forty years. It is the whole of what I was for. I have never stood beside one and watched."

Nobody at that ring made light of it, and Cael turned it over. A man who told you before a job where he would break was worth two who found it out in the middle; a one-armed man had taught him that, with a blank left in a book on purpose.

"Then you'll not be a stride from anybody but Brom. Whatever we find up there, that's your place: a stride behind him. He's the hardest of us to hurt, and the slowest to answer. If Brom's standing still, then it's right to stand still."

"I shall endeavour to. I cannot put it higher."

"I'd not have believed you if you had."

Lira had her chin on her knee. She had been studying the man across the dead ring with the look that she had once kept for names on a card.

"What are you? On a plate. You know what all of us are. You could have told Karis's rank from the size of her flame; I watched you not say it."

"It has not been asked of me since I took a desk." He gave the question the attention that he had given a mule's stool. "There is a Path on a roll, with a tier beside it, and I have not declared the one or been paid for the other in more years than you have lived. It does not go anywhere. It is for standing still. From what I have been told today, it is the worst thing that a man could carry onto this road, and I am very content to leave it where it is."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It is as much of one as I should care to see entered against a person who had been on your road for eight hours."

With the dark, the quietest of the ten came up in Cael to the height of his knees, and he held it there, as he did at every dusk. A healer sat on the next stone with her sleeves down. She had looked at a stiff leg four times since the bread was cut, and Tide, the tenth, lay as still in him as she sat, and for the same reason: nobody had been asked.

Brom had brought seven bowls out of the pannier, and nobody had seen him count. He filled the seventh with what there was, which was cold barley and a heel of cheese, and set it on a stone; and Vastin thanked him by name. A little afterward a copper lay on that stone beside the empty bowl. Brom looked at it for a while, and left it where it was.

Cael wrote by feel, on his knee.

Two hundred and seventy-eighth day. A halting-ground on the border road, eleven miles from the river. A slip taken at the plank: a clearance, the cut below the shoulder hold, twenty. Five hands. Pike has a sixth line on it for the healer, of her own road. Vastin walks behind us. He was not asked, and he was not told no. He has the four hands, and he knows where a post stands. He says that he cannot promise to watch one of us bitten, and I believe him. The rope on the far bank was three-strand; Brom would not have it. In hand, a hundred and eighteen.

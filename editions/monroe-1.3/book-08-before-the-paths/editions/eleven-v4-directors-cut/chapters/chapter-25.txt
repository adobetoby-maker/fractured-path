Chapter 25 — The Slate Town

[Measured, watchful narration; hold the buried history beneath the practical work]

The second of the five gates was the one with the child in it, and Oryn came to it after dark on the third day, a good deal greyer than she cared to be seen.

She had walked nine miles along the hill-foot since noon on legs that had wanted a bed. She had told a carter that she did not put her hands to hard work tired; and she had then spent an hour inside his wrist and set out on a road. It was not the first time that she had broken that rule. It was the first time in a long while that she had broken it before the day's second gate, with the gate still in front of her.

The child found her before she had the mule tied.

He was three, or thereabouts, with a head of hair like a dandelion, and he had been a thing to worry over before the snow: a chest that whistled, and a mother who had walked him out to the bench twice in one round to ask whether it was the kind that passed. Oryn had said that she would hear him again before the cold. Then there had been seventeen days on a mountain, and a road shut behind them.

He ran at the mule, shouting, and was caught by the back of his smock.

"He's been doing that since he could stand," said the mother, laughing, and a little out of breath. "You never came, and I thought every night that I'd hear it stop."

"Bring him here."

She heard him on the bench, by the gate-house lantern, with two fingers on a wrist no thicker than a spoon and her ear against his back. He would not keep still, and she did not need him to. The river in him ran quick and clean. The whistle had gone down into a small low hum, a long way off, like a kettle in another room.

"He's mended himself," she said. "They do, at that age, as often as not. Then I'm sent for to be told so."

"So I needn't have feared."

"You'd every need. You couldn't know which sort it was, and nor could I, from the far side of a shut road. Fear's what you had instead of me." She let the boy go, and he went straight back to the mule. "I'm sorry that it was all you had."

It was then that the gate-house door opened.

That hold kept its keeper outside the wall. There was a room built against the wall's outer face, on the road side, with one window and a bed in it, since somebody must sleep on the wrong side of a bar to hear who knocks at night. An old woman stood in its doorway with her sleeves rolled and her hands held away from her apron.

"You're the healer," she said. "I'd have sent a boy up the road for you this morning, if I'd known there was a road with you on it. It's my son's wife. She's been at it since first light, and it's not coming. I've caught every child in this hold for thirty years, and I can feel a shoulder where there ought to be a head."

Oryn stood up. The yard went grey at its edges, and came back.

"Lying across."

"Square across. I've tried to walk it round, and I've not the hands for it any more."

She washed to the elbow at the butt, and it took her longer than it should have. The old woman watched her do it.

"You're spent," she said. It was one tradeswoman's finding to another.

"I'm spent. I gave it to a wrist this morning, and I'd do the same again, and that's no use to you at all. I told a man last night that I don't put my hands on this sort of thing tired." She dried them, a finger at a time. "A child doesn't wait on what I've told people."

The room was hot, and smelled of tallow, and there was a young man in the corner who had been told to keep out of the way and could not find any. The woman on the bed had her eyes shut. She was perhaps twenty. She opened them when the pain let go of her, and looked at a strange square face in the lantern-light.

"I'm a healer," said Oryn. "My name's Oryn; I walk this road. Your child's lying crosswise in you, with its back to the door, and I can turn it. I do it from outside, with my hands on your belly, slowly, in between; it'll hurt you some, and a good deal less than what you're doing now." She waited until the eyes had hold of hers. "May I?"

"Yes. Yes. Do it."

She laid her hands on, and counted, and went in.

There were two rivers.

She had felt it a hundred times, on a hundred benches, and it had never yet become an ordinary thing. There was the woman's, wide and driven, like a millrace with a week of rain behind it. And inside that, in a bed that was no bigger than a loaf, there ran a second, as quick as a bird's, twice as quick as anything grown, going its own way entirely. It did not know that anything was the matter. It lay across the current of its mother like a boat that has swung broadside in a stream.

She did not hurry. She could not have hurried, with what she had left in her; and a child was not a thing that she would have hurried in any case.

Her hands asked. The Path eased what they asked of, a little, so that a wall which had been set like a fist went slack under her palms for the length of a breath; and in that breath she walked the small boat round by the width of a finger. Then the pain came back, and the wall shut, and she stood with her hands exactly where they were and waited for it to pass over. She did that forty times, perhaps, and lost the count, which she never did.

Twice the quick river faltered.

Both times she stopped as she was, with nothing moving in the room but a tallow flame, and gave it what she had been keeping back for her own walk to the next gate. Both times it picked up, and ran on, and she breathed.

The head came down into its place a little after midnight. After that it was the old woman's work, and the young one's; and Oryn sat on the floor against the wall with her hands in her lap, because she could not have stood, and kept two fingers on an ankle to hear the rivers through to the end.

It was a daughter. She came at first light, in a great hurry at the last, and she was very angry about it.

Oryn heard the cry from the bench outside, where they had carried her a cup. She had not been able to lift the cup. She sat with her back against field-stone and watched the hill go from black to grey, and listened to a person who had been in the world for less than a minute telling it what she thought.

Two rivers in one body; and she had never in her life had any trouble in telling which was which. Each had its own bed. She could have drawn the banks.

She had met one body in which ten ran together with no bank to any of them. In the middle of the ten there had been a place where nothing ran at all, and everything went round. She had called it a stone, in her own head, on a carter's wrist, and she had called it a join. Sitting here with her hands as heavy as wet sacks, she knew that it had been neither of those, and that she had only reached for the two nearest words. She did not have a word. She had a child, an hour old, that had lain turned the wrong way in the dark and could not have told anybody so; and she had a boy three days south who was walking into a town with a thing in him that he could not read. She had not read it. She had sent him in with a rule about a square, since a rule had been the only thing that she owned to give him.

It was three weeks, less three days. She did not like to owe.

The old woman came out and stood beside the bench, with her arms folded, looking at the light.

"You were grey when you came," she said. "You're the colour of that wall."

"I'll be some other colour by noon. Is she feeding?"

"Like a calf."

"Then leave me be for an hour, and tell that bench that I'll see it when I've slept. And somebody's to keep the boy off my mule."

There was a white frost in the inn yard at dawn on the two hundred and eighty-fourth day, and the three boxes stood in it on their ends.

Brom had roped them again in the night, for a stair. Each had a second becket now, low down, so that it would sit high between the shoulders and leave a person's eyes clear for his feet. He went along the three of them once more in the half light, a knot at a time, as he had gone along them at a ferry's shed. Oryn's empty bowl was wedged under the lashing of his own, mouth down.

"It's coming," he said, when Lira looked at it. "She said all of it."

Karis had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other. She had the card inside her left glove, against the palm, and she had looked to see that it was there four times since the ladder.

Seln stood by the pump, with his hands in his sleeves. He had nothing on his back.

"The card has a figure on it," he said. "Three of that figure are plain, and they are standing beside their loads; and I am not the fourth. I shall say why once, since you will otherwise think it a nicety."

Nobody had asked him. Cael thought that he had been getting it ready since he drew a stair in the dust.

"A hand is a thing that a clerk looks at for the space of a count. I was in a registry's service for fifteen years, and for the most of them I was looked through; but it was clerks who looked through me, and a clerk remembers furniture. There may be one man in that yard who has seen me walk a file from one desk to another, in a house above Ostrand or in some other. If he finds such a face among the porters, he will not ask me who I am. He will ask himself what the rest of you are, to be worth it." He drew a breath. "I should cost you the count. And there is this besides: when a door has shut on five people, somebody ought to be standing where he can see who comes to look at the door. That person cannot be on a card."

"So you'll be on a roof," said Lira.

"I shall be where I ought to be. If you see me, I have done it badly."

That left the case.

It leaned against the pump's trough, tarred and black, with its strap new-stitched in three places, and it had ridden one shoulder since a fork-stone, though nobody had asked for that. A healer had forbidden it, twice, and had been waited out; and the person who kept a book had been told so on a road, to his face, and had neither given leave nor refused it.

Cael looked at the case, and so did Brom. He saw Karis begin to turn her head, and stop herself, since she had been raised to think it rude to look at a person whom one was about to ask for something.

"Yes," said Vastin.

He had said it before a mouth was open. He stood at the foot of the ladder, with both hands on his stick and the frost round his boots, and he looked at none of them in particular.

"You are one short of a figure, and I am standing here. I have carried that case for three days against a healer's express instruction, and I do not propose to hand it to somebody else at the foot of a hill because the carrying has acquired a name." He bent, hooked the strap with the stick's head, as he had at a stone, and brought it to his shoulder. "I have been counted once already in this town, by a man with beads. I found that it could be survived."

"It's not the gate," said Cael. "The gate's a thumb. It's the yard door. There's a man there with a pen, and if he looks up—"

"He will be told nothing by me. I have been practising; you may have observed it, and I am told that I do it very ill."

"If he asks who you are."

"Then I shall look at my boots, as a hand does, and somebody with more wit will answer him." The old mouth moved. "I am aware of what I am being entered as. I have signed a great many sheets with that column on them."

"Nobody's ordered it," said Brom.

"No. I heard no order. I heard a count that came out one under, and I have never been able to leave such a thing alone."

Cael did not answer. He had asked that man a great many things in his own head, across a table, over the years, and some of them in anger. It had never once entered his mind to ask him to carry.

"Twenty-two steps," said Brom. "Down."

"I can count them. It is the one accomplishment that I have brought with me."

They went up the street at the second bell.

Cael put his boot on the first of the slanted setts with a box on his back and a card going before him in a small pale woman's glove. He marked it, as he had marked a sill, since two days ago he had stood at that kerb and not stepped. It was the same stone; he had only become a different sort of thing to it.

The blue coats were waiting, as they waited for everything.

Karis gave up the card. She did it well, he thought: she did not offer it, or explain it, but only held it out and looked at the wall. The keeper with empty hands took it. He looked for the figure, as he had looked for a hundred figures since the gate opened. Then his eyes went back up the card, to its head, and stopped there.

"Lower archive," he said, to his fellow. "Here, look at this. Matter for the lower archive."

"There's no such card."

"There's one in my hand. It's his seal; the old one, with the corner gone." He turned it over, and turned it back. "I've had this coat nine years, and I've not seen one of these come up the hill."

It was not a question, and nobody answered it. There? said Anchor, of the tall arch and its two grooves. Cael told it no.

The keeper shrugged. He lifted his pen. He pointed its feather at Karis, and his lips moved; and then at a box with a big man under it, and at a box with a girl under it, and at Cael. Cael watched the feather come. It rested on him for no longer than on the others. He was a cap.

It came to the old man last, and there it stayed.

It did not stay for long. It was perhaps the length of a breath. But the keeper who held it read people for his bread, and he had in front of him a porter with a stick, and a bound leg, and the lightest load of the four, who stood up under it a great deal too straight.

Vastin was looking at his boots.

He was doing it badly. Cael could see that he was. A person who has looked at his boots all his life lets his head hang, and this one held his at an angle, with care, as a thing that he had been told the trick of; but his right hand was shut on the ash, and it did not move an inch toward his coat.

"You've a lame one," said the keeper.

That was not a question either. It was Karis who took it.

"He carries the light one."

"He'll want to. It's a hill, from here." The feather dropped. He made a stroke on his sheet, a single one, and tore the corner off the card and gave the card back. "Go on."

Vastin went under the arch with the case on his shoulder and his eyes on the setts. It was a gate that he had ridden through on four occasions, with a keeper standing at each jamb and the board held out to him, open, so that he might see for himself how it was kept; and he went through it now as one stroke in four, on the wrong side of the pen, and Cael heard him let his breath go when the shadow of the arch was behind them.

Lira went under the arch second.

Cael had her back in front of him, and a box on it, and above the box the back of her head. He could not see her face. He saw what her head did. As she came level with the board that hung from the second keeper's neck, where a line stood for every stranger who had walked through that gate today on his own paper, her chin turned toward it.

There would be names on that sheet, in a clerk's hand, each with a district and a rank; and there was a pocket in her coat with such a line folded in it, sealed, which a station at Ostrand had made out to her, and which she had worked harder for than for anything else that she owned. She had only to put the box down.

Her chin came back. She shifted the beckets, and went on up the hill.

It was quieter inside the second wall, and it was cleaner, and for the first hundred yards that was all that he could have said of it.

Then he began to see what Iron bought.

The roofs were slate, every one. The street had a gutter down each side of it, with water running, and no dung. The windows had glass in them. At a corner there was a cistern with a brass spout, and a girl was filling a jug at it; and she did not put a copper in any dish, since there was no dish. A row of children's voices came out of an upper window, chanting something all together, and he caught the shape of it before the words: it was a list, and it went up by steps, and at the top of each step there was a colour.

Nobody looked at them.

It was not as the outer street had not looked, where everybody was carrying and a box was only a thing in the way. People here were not carrying anything. They walked with their hands free, in good cloth, at their own pace; and their eyes went over four backs under loads as eyes go over a row of posts. A woman drew her skirt aside for Brom without breaking her talk.

Karis had not looked at a single slate. She walked with her face turned to the house-fronts on the upper side, where the street had been cut into the hill and the footings of the houses stood on the bluff's own rock; and twice she put out two gloved fingers, in passing, and drew them along a bed of pale stone at the height of her hip, as another person might have touched a dog.

"Left foot," said Brom, to himself, on the steep of it. He was favouring two toes.

The street climbed harder past the cistern. It had been laid in long shallow steps there, a stride to each, with a raised kerb for the grip of hooves. Behind him the stick had begun to come down out of time. Cael did not turn round; he slowed, by a little, as one slows a line for the last on it, and heard the breath at his shoulder catch up, and shorten.

"I am aware of what you are doing," said Vastin, very low.

"I'm tired. It's a hill."

"It is courteous of you to be tired at my pace."

There was a house on the right hand with a board beside its door.

He would have gone by it. It was Lira who slowed, in front of him, with her head turned; and he read what she was reading. The board was black slate, ruled in chalk, as every board in that town was, but it had no loads on it: it had hours, and against each hour a colour. First bell: buff. Second bell: blue. And at the foot, in smaller letters: Cards, after the fourth, at the side door, as the house's means allow.

There was a bench under the board. Nobody was sitting on it at that hour.

"That's hers," said Lira. She did not say whose, and did not need to. "That's her column. They've hung it on a door."

"It is the standing order for a district house," said Vastin. He had come up level, and stopped, with both hands on the stick, and he was looking at the board as he had looked at a bridge with five piers. "I have read it in the code, where it is the ninth article of a schedule. I had never seen it in chalk."

"Does it read different in chalk?" said Brom.

"It reads the same. It is a good deal harder to walk past."

They walked past it. There was nothing else to be done with it. But Cael carried it up the hill with the box, a square woman's voice saying that it was not cruelty and was only how a heap was sorted; and he thought of a girl on a kerb with a basket of eggs, and a child who had taken hold of a stick with both fists.

The third wall was the oldest, and it showed.

It was lower than the others, and thicker, and its stone had gone the colour of a tooth. The arch in it was round, and narrow, and not tall enough for a loaded wagon. There was one keeper under it. He was an old man, in a long pale coat that had been made for somebody broader, and he sat on a stool with a board on his knee.

He read the card twice. He read it as Karis read a seal, from the stamp inward.

"Lower archive," he said. He had a dry, exact voice. "I had heard that there was such a card. My father kept this gate before me, and he told me that he had passed one, once, when he was young." He looked up, at three boxes, and at a tarred case, and for rather longer at a courier's flat of waxed leather with a stamp across its face. "That is a good deal of matter."

Karis said nothing. She looked at the wall.

"It is not my business what it is. My business is how many." He lifted his pen, and it had no feather; he used the point. It went to her, and to Brom, and to Lira; it came to Cael, and he felt it on his breastbone like a finger, and it went on. It came to the stick, and the bound leg, and it did not linger there at all, since that old man had seen a great many porters, and he had long ago stopped expecting them to look like anything.

"One, and four," he said. He made his stroke, and tore the second corner off the card.

"Where do loads go?" said Karis.

It was the only thing that she had been told she might ask. She asked it of the wall.

"Not by the square. They'll not thank you for boxes on that paving, with the steps swept." He pointed with the pen, through the arch and to the right. "Under the wall. There is a lane. Follow it till it goes down, and then go down with it, and you will come to a yard. Mind your lame one on the steps. They are older than he is."

"Thank you."

"Go on."

They went under the round arch, one behind another, and it was so low that Brom bent his head.

There? asked Anchor. It asked very softly here, as a person lowers his voice in an old room. Cael told it no.

Then he was through, and he stood inside the third wall of Treswick, on the crown of the bluff, with a box on his back.

He had his eyes on the ground. He had put them there under the arch, before the light changed, and he held them there now as he would have held a rope. He saw setts, small ones, laid in a pattern of fans. They ran away in front of him between two rows of very old doorsteps. A long way up that street, eighty yards perhaps, they stopped being grey, and went white, where the sun came down upon something wide and open and paved.

There was no sound from that end, and he had thought that there would be. There was only a brightness lying on the stones, and at the top of his sight, where he would not lift it, a paleness in the air that was not the sun's.

It was a hundred paces off. He could have walked to it while a person counted to sixty. Hesk's book lay against his ribs, under the box's strap, with two sentences in it that a square woman had read with her lips moving; and he was glad, for the first time, that she had made him write them. A thing that he had only said would have been arguing with him by now.

He turned right.

A small quick shape came up on his left side as he did it, with a box on its back, and settled there, between him and the street's end. She did not look that way herself. She had her eyes on the lane, and her chin level, and she said nothing whatever; she only walked where a wall would have been, if anybody had thought to build him one.

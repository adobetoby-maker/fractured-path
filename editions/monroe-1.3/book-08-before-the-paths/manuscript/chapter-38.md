# Chapter 38 — Read Once

The tall books went back first.

The man in the dusty coat had ruled it from his chair, on the two hundred and ninety-third morning, before any of them had a load off. It was the seventh day of his week; there were twelve of the old town's tally-books lying open along a table's edge, and he would not have a tape touched while one of them was out of its bay.

"As they stood. Third bay on the left, and the shelves she had them off. The lamp can go with them; I'll not want it here." His eyes were on the floor between two boxes. "I've a light of my own for this."

So Lira went up the ladder in the eastern gallery, with a book as broad as a paving stone against her chest, and Brom stood at the foot with the ring in his left fist.

"You said that I was a wall," he said, over his shoulder, to nobody who was listening.

"A wall will do for a ladder," said Lira, from the dark. "Higher. No. Lower."

Cael had turned to go with them. He had not been four strides from that flame since the third hour of the second day, and his hand felt wrong without it.

"Stay," said Karis.

She stood by the second chair, with her notebook against her chest. She had slept; he could see that she had, and that it had been done on purpose, as a person eats before a long road; and it had not taken the white out of her face.

"I should like one of you here. I do not wish to read it to a chair, and a man who shuts his eyes." She drew a breath. "You have knelt at this for eight days, and been given a sore knee for it. I think that you are owed the end of the week."

"I've nothing to hold."

"Then hold nothing. Stand there."

He stood at the desk's left side, a hand off, where the lamp would have been.

There was a fourth in that end of the gallery. The old man sat on Brom's box by the nearest bay, six strides from the desk, with the bound leg out along its plank and both hands on the ash; he could not have followed a ladder, and he was only where he had been set down, as he was every morning.

The man reached up to the shelf.

He took the bundle in both hands; it was thicker than a loaf, and tied across both ways, and it left a clean square in the dust where it had lain. He set it upon the slope of his desk, under the good lamp, and stood looking at the knot.

"I'll not cut it," he said. "Whoever tied that meant it to be untied."

It took him some time. The tape had gone brown and stiff, and it cracked under his nails like the skin of an onion; but the knot came, a turn at a time, and he laid the two lengths out to either side as flat as rulers. Under the tape there were two thin boards of beech, and he lifted the upper one away.

Cael looked at what lay beneath, since he had been told to stand there, and since it was the thing that all of them had walked to.

It was a single leaf, and it was not paper.

It had the colour of old horn, and it was stiff, like horn, and a little curled at one corner; and where the lamp struck the curl, the light came through it, brown and clouded, as it comes through the pane of a stable lantern.

It had not been written with a pen; he saw that at once, from his side of the desk, with the head of it away from him. There was no ink that lay upon the surface. Every sign had been pressed into the skin with a point, as a thing is scored on a slate, and some dark stuff had been rubbed into the scoring afterward and had mostly gone. The lines stood in it like the tracks of a very small bird across a crust of snow.

They were the floors' letters, and he could not read one of them; but he had held a lamp above that woman's column for eight days, and he knew a hooked upright when he saw it, and a gate, and a fork.

There was a place in it that was not like the rest.

It lay a little below the middle, where for the width of four or five letters the skin was paler, and thinner, and rough, as a board is rough where a plane has gone against the grain. Something had been taken out of that line with an edge. Whoever did it had been thorough; and had stopped, all the same, a hair too soon. At the left side of the pale place a single stroke was left standing. It was upright, and as long as a letter is tall, and it led to nothing.

The thing behind Cael's eyes had come round to that leaf as it had come round to nothing since a plank of oak, and it leaned as a dog leans on a leash.

*No*, he told it. This was hers.

The man had bent over the skin. He looked at it from its foot, without a blink, and the water ran; and Cael, who had no lamp to move, found his empty hand going left of its own accord.

"One hand," said the rough voice at last. "One sitting. He ruled it first, with the same point; you can see the lines under the letters. It's a fair copy, and he didn't hurry." The square finger came to rest in the air above the pale place. "That's later. I can tell you that it's been scraped since it was written. I can't tell you by whom."

He straightened, and felt for the edge of the desk, and stood back from it by two paces with his eyes shut.

"I've looked," he said. "It's yours."

---

She did not sit.

She stood at the slope of the desk, where he had stood, with her notebook open beside the skin and her pencil upright in her right hand. The left lay flat upon the oak, in its strip of linen. Cael saw her eyes go to the first sign, at the head, in the corner that was farthest from him; and they stopped there.

They stopped for a long time.

He had watched that woman read since a reading room above Ostrand. She read as some people eat, quickly, with her whole attention, and a page was gone before another person had found its first line. She did not read so now; her eyes went along one sign at a time, and her lips were shut. Twice the pencil went to the notebook and wrote a single letter, and went back.

"There were two that I had not," she said, to nobody. "It spells them for me."

She did not take the leaf's word for either. She turned to the last of the tall books, which she had kept by her, and hunted the old clerk's column for a word with the first of the two in its middle; and when she had found one she laid her pencil along both, the brown ink and the scored skin, and nodded.

Cael counted, since there was nothing else for him to do. He counted the lines on the skin, by the pale place, and made them twenty-six. From the eastern gallery, a long way off and round a corner of rock, there came now and then the small dry knock of a book going home upon a shelf.

"The fourth line," said Karis. She had not turned her head. "I should like to know how it stands. Does it sit, or does it go?"

The red eyes opened, and the man came the two paces back to his desk, and bent, and looked along the line from its foot; and the water ran.

"Sits. The lot of it sits. There's not an order on that skin from the head to the tail." He straightened. "Somebody's telling you what was done. He's not telling you to do it."

"Thank you. I had thought so, and I did not wish to have only thought it."

He went back to his two paces, and shut his eyes.

There was one thing more, and she did it with her glove: she laid it over a word near the foot of the skin. On her notebook she wrote out what that word would have to be, sign for sign, if the letters meant at the foot what she had taken them to mean at the head; and then she lifted the glove, and looked from the one to the other, with the pencil gone quite still in her fingers.

The oil in the lamp's glass stood a finger lower than when the tape came off.

When she lifted her head, she did not look at any of them.

"I can read it."

"Then read it," said the man. His eyes were still shut.

"Aloud?"

"I've had that on a shelf ten years, a hand's breadth from my head, and I never knew a word that was in it. I'll hear it before I'm older."

"Once," she said. "I shall read it once, aloud, as nearly as I can put a dead tongue into ours. I shall not do it twice. It is the first face only; there is more on its back, which is a list, and I have not gone into that." Her voice was low, and quite steady, and it cost her to keep it so. "It has a head, and then it begins."

She set one gloved finger in the air above the first line, an inch off the skin, as a man had taught her.

"*In the years of the unmaking, when the un-bound walked in the land and no wall stood against them and no law held, and the cities of the east were emptied and the cities of the west were emptied and the rivers ran wrong*" — her breath had given out upon a comma, and she took another without lifting her finger — "*there came one who saw the shape of it, and set himself to the ordering. And he made the measure, and the seat, and the tier, and the mark that is given at the kindling, that each should know what he is and none should be what he is not. And he set the apparatus over all the land, and the land was ordered, and the un-bound came no more.*"

"*And the* — "

Her finger had stopped, and it hung over the pale place, above the one stroke that was left.

"It is scraped," she said, in her own voice. "A word as wide as four of his letters, or five. I have the first stroke of it, and nothing after."

The finger went on, to where the signs began again.

"*— set the apparatus over all the land, that the un-bound should not come again, who had near unmade the world before the ordering of it. And this is the reason of the apparatus, and this is all of its reason, and let none who come after forget it, though the un-bound be forgotten: that they should not come again.*"

Her finger had come to the foot of the first face.

She took it away, and did not turn the leaf. She stood with both hands flat on the oak, and looked at the wall of pale rock above the desk, where a pick had left its strokes.

"That is what it says," she said. "I have given it as nearly as I am able, and I shall make a copy, in our tongue, to the letter. Whoever was not here shall have it from the page." She drew a breath. "I ask one thing. I ask that nobody say it back. Not to me; not to each other, in his own words, on a stair. A thing that is said twice begins to be said differently. It is very old, and it is not ours to improve."

---

For a while nobody did anything.

The man had not opened his eyes; he stood two paces from his own desk, with his hands hanging, as still as a thing put down. Six strides off, on a box, the ash stick had been lifted a little from the rock, and was held so, and did not come down.

Nobody looked at Cael.

He marked that, and it was done three ways. The red eyes were shut, and had been since before she began. The grey ones had gone to a bay of bundles and were reading the under side of a tag. And the small pale woman, who had asked him to stand there, kept her face to a wall. He had been looked away from by one person in that gallery, two days ago, on purpose and for his own sake. It appeared to have spread.

"I shall say what I take it to be," said Karis. "I shall say it once as well, and plainly, and then I shall stop. It is not the leaf. It is only my account of the leaf, and it is worth less."

She put her hand into the breast of her coat, and brought out a square of oilcloth.

Cael knew it, having laid his own palm upon it once, on a rise, with a town in sight. She unfolded it on the desk's slope, beside the skin, and there was a sheet of plain desk paper, folded twice, in a square upright hand.

"You made it a term that I should tell you nothing of what I thought until you had looked. I have kept this from you for nine days by that term, and from myself. It is a line that a man copied out of a register, a long way from here, and carried three hundred miles." She did not look toward the box. "It sends its reader to the lower archive at this town for a thing that is held here in full. And it has a word in it, which I had never met."

The butt of her pencil went to the head of the skin, and then to the sheet.

"That word stands here. It is spelled as he has spelled it, letter for letter, in the old signs. *The Architect.*"

It was the first time that Cael had heard it spoken. He had seen it written, in fresh ink, on a table at Lowmarch; and his hand had lain a hand's width from it for the whole of a lamp.

The pencil's butt went down the skin, from its head to the pale place below the middle, and rested there.

"It's a title, not a name; the name's gone."

"It gives its own year, at the head," she said. "It is in the count that the old town's books keep, and I have had twelve of those open for a week. By that count this was written about four hundred years ago."

"And the rest?" said the man.

"The rest is what you heard. It says what the apparatus is for, and it says that there is no other reason. It was set over the land so that a kind of people should not come again." Her voice had dropped. "It calls them the un-bound. I said that word at this table on the first day, as a limit; I had only ever met it as the name of a layer in the ground. I did not know that it had been a people."

She folded the oilcloth over the sheet.

"That is my whole account. I have read no more than the first face, and I have said no more than is on it."

She looked down at her right hand, and shut three fingers of it into the palm, one at a time, as a big man had counted findings at a spring.

"Older," she said. "Made. Us."

It was three words. Cael counted them, as he counted anything, and found that he could go no further along that road than the count.

So he took the room instead.

He went over it as he would have gone over a cut on a moor, from the left. There was a desk of black oak, sloped, and a lamp on an iron arm, with the oil a finger down. On the shelf above stood a cup, which he had drunk from, and a jar; and a clean square in the dust. Two lengths of brown tape lay out to either side of a leaf of skin with twenty-six lines on it. There were two chairs, of which one had a cushion that had been carried from the other. A man stood with his eyes shut. A woman stood with a strip of a shirt round her left hand, and the hand was flat on the wood, and it had left off shaking.

He counted the uprights between the desk and the nearest bay, and there were four. On a box beyond the fourth sat a tall grey man with a stick an inch above the floor.

It was all there, and he had it to the last peg.

He knew what he was doing. He was counting a gallery so that he need not count the one thing in it which the leaf concerned. He let himself. He thought that he would be given the rest of the day to stand in this room; he would not be given it to stand in the other.

"There's more in that," said the man. His chin had gone toward the bundle. "Under the board."

---

It was paper, under the second board, and there was a good deal of it.

Karis lifted the skin away on its beech, with both hands, the bound and the whole, and set it at the head of the desk's slope. What lay beneath was a dozen leaves or more, of a thick soft paper gone brown at the edges, written in ink in a cramped upright hand. She bent to the first, and her face changed.

"I can read this at sight," she said. "It is the old town's hand. It is the hand of the tally-books, or its father's." Her pencil went down the leaf without touching. "It is a chronicle. It has the floods in it, and a murrain among the cattle, and who was reeve. Somebody of this town kept a year-book, and somebody else laid it away with that."

"Later than the skin?"

"A good deal later, I should think. It speaks of the walls." The pencil had stopped. "It speaks of him."

She read the line out before she had decided whether to. Cael saw that; her voice ran ahead of her, as it sometimes did when a thing was plain upon the page.

"— and of him it is said that he did not die in the way of men, but went into the ordering he had made, and is in it —"

She stopped.

There was a hole in the paper. Cael could see it from where he stood: a place the width of a thumb, a little way in from the edge, where the leaf was simply gone. Its rim was brown and soft. A worm had done it, or damp; the line of ink ran up to that rim, and went over it, and did not come out upon the other side.

Karis looked at the hole, and did not put her pencil near it.

"No," she said. "That is another day's work, and it is torn. I am not going to read into a hole."

She took a slip of card from her notebook, and laid it across the leaf.

"Marked."

Cleon had opened his eyes to see where the slip lay.

"Marked."

He shut them again. Neither of them said one word more about that line, then or afterward, and Cael did not go over it. He looked at the rim of the hole, and at a slip of card; and he put them where he put a hair.

It was the stick that broke the quiet.

It came down upon the rock, once, six strides away. The old man had sat through a reading and an account with his hands shut upon it, and his face turned to a bay of bundles; and whatever he had been holding off had got past him. He spoke to the shelves, low, and with care, as he had once begun to draft an order at a spring.

"The apparatus," he said. "The measure, the seat, the tier, the mark. He made it and he set it over the land. And the mark that is given at the kindling — "

"Two pages," said Karis.

She had turned round; it was the first time since the tape was untied that she had looked at anybody, and she looked at him as she had looked across a table at Lowmarch, when a courier's flat lay between them.

"There is what that leaf says. It says it in its own words, of its own time, and I shall copy it on one page, and nothing else shall go upon that page." She took a clean leaf from her notebook, and laid it on the desk, bare. "And there is this. What you were about to say is not on the skin. It may be so. I do not know, and nor do you. If it is ever written, it will be written here, when somebody can show it; and until then this page has nothing on it, and nobody is to rule a line from the one to the other because a word stands in both."

"I had not finished the sentence."

"No. I should be obliged if you did not."

The old mouth had set. Cael watched the right hand leave the stick, and hang, and go back.

"A measurement is a fact about a ruler, not about the hand that held it."

She had said it at a spring, over six years of an office's sheets, to stop a sentence of her own. She said it now with her bound hand on a bare leaf.

"I was drafting," said Vastin. His voice had very little breath behind it. "I was told at that same spring, by another person, what it is when I do so. I was drawing a conclusion, in a room where I have been at some pains for nine days to draw nothing." He looked down at the ash. "I leave it where it stopped."

"Thank you."

"It is not a courtesy. I find that I do not wish to hear the end of it myself."

---

She made the copy before she did anything else.

She would not be hurried over it, and nobody tried. She sat down at last, in the second chair, on the cushion that was not hers, with a board across her knees. She wrote the first face of that skin out fair, in our tongue, in her small upright hand, with the leaf before her and her eyes going from one to the other at every word.

Cael kept his place at the desk's side, and read it as it came, from the wrong side, with its head away from him, as he had read every paper that had ever concerned him.

He did not say it over; he had been asked not to, and he found that it was no hardship. He only watched the pencil.

At the line below the middle it stopped.

She looked at the pale place on the skin for some time. Then she took up the brass strip from Ternhall, which she had not had out of its cloth since the hide was rolled, and ruled a small box in the line that she was writing. It was four letters wide, by her own letters; she ruled it on all four sides, and left it empty, and went on.

*and the* — there the box stood, with nothing in it — *set the apparatus over all the land, that the un-bound should not come again, who had near unmade the world before the ordering of it.*

"I am not going to put a name where a knife has been," she said, without lifting her head. "I am not going to put a title there either. There was a word, and it is gone; and that it is gone is a part of what the leaf says."

"There's a stroke left," said Cael.

It was the first thing that he had said since she began to read.

"There is. I have drawn it." She turned the board a very little, so that he might see. At the left side of her box, inside the rule, there stood one upright line as tall as a letter. "I do not know what it began."

She finished the face. She laid the pencil down across the board, and flexed her fingers, and looked at what she had done as a mason looks along a course.

"That is one page," she said. "The other is in my notebook, and it is clean."

Boots were coming up the gallery, and a small brown light with them.

It was Brom's tread, and beside it a quicker one. Cael turned his head, and saw the lamp first, low, at a big man's knee, and above it Lira's face with dust on it to the eyebrows; and he saw the face take in that end of the gallery, in the time that it takes to draw a breath, and go still.

There was not much to take in. A tape lay untied. A woman sat in a chair with a board on her knees. A man stood with his eyes shut. And on a box by the nearest bay there was an old one who had gone the colour of the rock behind him, with both hands on a stick.

"Twelve," said Brom. "As they stood. She'd have me move the light for every one." He set the ring down on the table's end, a long way from any paper. His eyes had gone to the desk, and the two beech boards. "It's open, then."

"It's open," said Cael.

"And read?"

"The first of it. Once."

Lira had come to his shoulder. She did not look at the skin; she looked at him, as she had looked on a stair by a lamp, going over his face a line at a time.

"What does it say?"

"Tonight. She's making it fair, and you'll have it on paper, in its own words. She's asked that nobody say it back."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's what I've leave to give you."

"That's twice in three days that you've had leave and I've had none." She said it quite low. Her chin was up, and he knew that she was angry, and that she would not be angry for long. "Is it bad?"

He thought about that honestly, since it was she who asked.

"It's old," he said.

"That wasn't what I asked you."

"I know it wasn't. I've not got further than old. I've been counting the room."

She looked at him a moment longer; and then, for the first time, at the leaf of horn on its board, from six feet off.

"Tonight, then," said Lira. "I'll hold you to the page."

# Chapter 37 — How Far

The bridge was a made thing that morning, and it had never been one before.

It was the two hundred and ninety-second morning past the Line, and Cael saw it from the kerb of the carts' street, where the hill dropped away and all the roofs of the district lay below. It stood out on the brown water as it had stood the day before, small and dark, on five piers. He had looked at it then as a person looks at a road that he came in by. Now it was the oldest thing that he could see from that hill which anybody had built. He had that on the word of a small pale woman, who had laid her hand where the masons' pale stone was cut to fit it. And the new thing behind his eyes came round to it, across half a mile of air, like a head lifted from a book.

He did not know whether it could read so far. He did not wish to learn it on a street, with three hours' sleep in him and a lamp to hold.

He put his eyes on the tail-board of the handcart. That was no better. The tail-board had a hinge, and the hinge leaned.

It was the same wherever he turned. A river is not made, and a man is not; but a town is nothing else, from the ditch to the weathercock, and he had been given a thing that inclined toward every peg and course of it as a dog's nose goes to every post.

He had gone through this once already, with another matter. For eleven days he had not looked at a light on a crown, and he had not done it by shutting his eyes. He had done it by having somewhere else to put them, and a reason, twice a day, until his neck no longer set at an alley. He did the same now. He looked at a raw hand on a tarred case, which nobody had made, and at the back of a girl's neck, and pushed.

"You're walking like a horse in blinkers," said Lira.

"I'm learning not to look at things."

"You'd that already. You've had it since the rise."

"This is a different not-looking. It's the same road."

She thought about that for the length of ten strides.

"You're going to run out of places to put your eyes," she said.

The clerk wrote *the same*, and *four*. Vastin went down twenty-seven steps on his seat, with his heel in Cael's palm, and said at the foot that he believed he had improved upon his time.

There was tar on the lamp's ring before the first sheet had been looked at.

Cael saw it when he changed his grip: a black thumb's breadth on the brass, off a palm that a pump had not been able to clean. The man in the dusty coat saw it a moment after. He had stopped, with the reed lifted.

"There's tar on my lamp."

He said it to the ring. His red eyes had come to the brass, and to the smear upon it, and they stayed there as though a line had been drawn at the edge of the metal; they did not go on by the width of a finger to the hand that held it. For seven days they had gone over that hand as over a hook. This was not the same. Cael watched a man take care, and he knew what the care was for.

"It'll come off with a rag," said the rough voice. "After. Left."

The flame was there.

It was a long day, and Karis made it longer by being right four times.

The man gave her his joins from the second bell. He went along the Ring's eleven with his finger, and the Stair's twenty-nine, and at every place where a run came down flat he said *red*, and she laid a square of tile. By noon each of the fifty-two sheets was parted, in broken tile, into pieces of the length of words.

"Eighteen," she said, in the forenoon; and at noon, with her bread untouched, "Nineteen."

The first thing that she read which was not a number came a little after.

Cael saw her find it. It was a group of three signs, and it stood so often on the Stair's long table that she had passed it over as packing; and she went back to it, and looked from the pencil to the brown book, and sat quite still.

"It is *and*," she said. "It is only *and*. It is the commonest word that the old clerk has, and it is the commonest on these floors; there are nine of them on this one sheet." She laughed, which she seldom did, and put the back of her bound hand to her mouth. "I have waited a year to read a word of this, and the first word is one that a child has before it can walk."

"That's a good word," said Brom. "Nothing's built without it."

She had twenty by the time the flame was trimmed, and it was then that she took the glove off.

There was a group of six signs on the Stair's long table which stood four times in those twenty-nine, and always after a white. She had not let herself look at it for four days; she had laid a glove across the place whenever her work took her near. She lifted the glove now, by one finger. She had every letter of the six, and she did not read them at once. She looked first at the man with the reed.

"I had a word that I wished to find here," she said. "I had it when I knew four of these, and I covered the place so that I should not see it. I am going to say now that I wished for a name. I thought that a man who cut his orders into three floors might have signed them."

"And?"

"It is not a name." Her eyes had gone along the six. "It is *set*. As a stone is set, or a stake: set this, and what, and how many. It is a better word than mine. It is the one that is there."

"Twenty-one," she said, at the bell, and her voice was nearly gone.

The man had written his card. He stood by the desk, and his hand went up to the shelf, to the thick bundle tied both ways with tape gone brown; and two fingers rested there, as they had rested on the day when he named what it was made of.

"Not tonight. I'll not have that tape cut by anybody who's listening for a bell." He took his fingers away. "In the morning. It's the seventh. Come down with your eyes rested, if you know how that's done."

---

There was a mule in the inn yard when they brought the handcart in under the arch, and a man with his hat cut away on one side.

Cael knew the hat before he knew the man. He had seen it across a river with ice in it, on the far bank, on the day that the last ferry ran; and there had been a bag under that arm then, as there was now. It was the carrier who took whatever went between the towns of the Line and one plank under an eave, some days east.

The man was looking at Brom. More exactly, he was looking at Brom's back, between the shafts.

"I've seen that go up a bank," he said. "A fortnight since, at the western landing, with a box on it; and a tall girl, and a little one with a roll of hide. You're off the one-armed man's cloth. The eleventh."

"We were," said Brom. "We're here now."

"So I see. I'd have walked past you." The carrier had put his hand into the bag, without looking, as a man does who knows what lies where. "I lie here tonight, and I'm for the landing at first light. There's a thing in this for his plank. He'll say it out twice, the way he does, and there'll be no boy standing there to take it; and it'll come back to me by the next boat with a cross on it." He brought it out. "*For the boy with the book.* I'm not to break a bag for a stranger. I'll break it for a thumb that the old man knows."

It was small, and thick, and wrapped in oilcloth. There was a knot on it that Cael could have undone in the dark.

Brom set his thumb to the carrier's way-slip, in the grease of the cart's own hub, beside the line that named the parcel; it was a mark that a one-armed man had seen on a good many slips, and would know. Cael found a copper of his own for the carrying. No name was asked for, and none was put down.

He pulled the one end of cord that would loose that knot, sitting on the mounting block, with the others round him.

They were of brass. There were two legs, each as long as his hand, flat on the inner side so that they lay close. At the foot of each was a point of blued steel, as fine as a thorn, the pair of them thrust into a round of cork. The legs met at the head in a joint with a broad flat rivet, and above the rivet stood a little turned stalk for the fingers. They had been rubbed with something, so that the last of the light ran down them like water.

A leaf from an order-pad was folded round the legs. It was in a square upright hand.

*A rule says a line is true. A divider says how far, and carries it. You've the first. Here's the second, and mind the points; they'll go through a thumb as easy as a sheet. Lines are for finding out how far a thing is from another thing. Everything is. — H.*

He read it twice over, and the yard was very quiet.

An old man in Denvash had cut that leaf from his pad, and had rolled a pair of dividers in it, and tied his knot. He had sent a ruler before the snow, to a boy who was going where there were no instruments. He could not have known that there was a hide with four rings on it, or a line through the rings which ran off the edge of the skin, or that somebody would shortly need to know how far along that line the next thing lay. He had only thought, at his bench, that a person who has a straight edge will want a pair of legs to step it off with.

"He's sent you a thing to stab yourself on," said Lira. "He does know you."

"He says to mind the points."

"He said to mind the shape, with the last one. He's working his way down to your fingers."

Cael drew the cork off, and opened them.

He ought not to have looked at the joint. He had been not looking at things since the second bell; but this lay in his own two hands, and it was a gift, and for the space of a breath he was only a boy with a new tool.

*Bears*, said the twelfth, of the rivet.

It had come round before he could stop it. Both legs hung from that one pin; he saw the whole of the thing lean inward to it, the two brass arms and the steel at their feet, as everything on a gate leans to its hinge. It was true, and it was no more than his eyes could have told him. The sand came into them all the same.

He shut the legs, and put the cork back upon the points.

"What?" said Lira.

"It looked. I didn't tell it to."

"At a pair of compasses?"

"It'd look at a spoon."

Karis had put out her right hand. She did not take them; she held the hand a little way off, as she had once held it above a page.

"I should like the loan of those," she said. "Not now. When I have a hide in front of me again, and a point upon it to carry from. I have been stepping that line off with a strip of brass and my thumbnail." Her eyes had not left the points. "I am going up. I have twenty-one, and I have found the twenty-first only once."

"There's a dinner," said Brom.

"Then keep me what will keep."

---

It was Brom's ruling, and he made it at the half-door with the purse in his fist.

"A parcel's come the breadth of the country to a hayloft, and found it. That's eaten on. We've had the pot for ten nights and said nothing against it. Tonight we'll have what she'd give a carter with money."

"Four," said the woman, when he had told her what he wanted. She looked at him as she had looked on the first day, when he asked for a seventh bowl. "In front."

He counted four marks onto the ledge, and she swept them off it with the side of her hand.

"A hundred and thirty," he said, to the purse.

They ate in the lower room, off the yard, since it was level with the cobbles and one of them could be brought there on a shoulder. It had a single long table, and a hatch, and a candle in a dish. What came through the hatch was a goose. There was a pie after it with a lid as broad as a shield, and a white loaf, and a dish of apples baked in something sweet; and there was a jug of cider, cold, from which Brom poured seven cups.

"There's five of you sitting," said the woman, at the hatch.

"There's seven cups."

She had learned by now that it was his pot. She put two saucers on the board, and went away.

He set a saucer over each of the two cups, to keep the dust out, and stood them at the table's end. One was a small pale woman's, who was above their heads with a lantern and twenty-one letters. The other had been carried under the lashing of his box, as an empty bowl, for eleven days; and the person whom it was kept for was a long way north along a hill-foot, with a mule.

He made his second mistake of the day over the pie.

He had his elbows on the board, and his eyes had gone down to the wood, idly, as a person's do who is full. It was an old table. It had been mended. *Bears*, the thing said, of the two trestles in the middle; and of the leg at the far end, by the old man's stick, it said *nothing*, as flatly as it had said so of a red square of tile.

He looked away, at the candle, and that was worse. He looked at his own hands.

"That leg's doing no work," said Brom.

He had spoken with his mouth full. He leaned back, and knocked the far leg with the side of his boot, twice, and it gave a little dry rattle in its socket.

"It's been let in since. For the look of it, I'd say, after somebody sat on the end and it tipped. The weight's all on the two middle ones; you can see where the top's bowed to them." He took up his cup. "I'd have pegged it through. It'd carry, then."

Cael began to laugh. He could not have stopped it. He put the heels of his hands to his eyes, which were running, and laughed into them.

"What have I said?"

"Nothing. You've said what I got told, a breath before, and it cost me. You did it with your boot."

"You go and look at what's sitting on it," said Brom, mildly. "I told that fellow so. He's a good eye, for a man who never lifted anything."

The dividers went round the table once, shut, with the cork on. Seln turned them over in his fingers and gave them back without a word, which was praise.

It was near the end, when the apples had gone, that Cael smelled paper burning.

He looked along the board. There was a curl of black in the candle's dish, under a small grey man's fingers, with one corner of it still white; he saw close writing on that corner, a line and a half, before the flame had it as well. Seln turned the last of it over with his nail, and watched until it was ash.

"Closed."

"What is?" said Lira.

"It is in the dish."

She looked into the dish, and at him, and let it go. Cael went back over the minute before, since he went over anything. He had not seen a slip come out of a sleeve, nor seen it read; he had been watching a pair of brass legs go round a table. He put the word beside three others that the same man had given him at a pump. He understood this one no better, and found that he had not expected to.

Vastin had been looking at the two covered cups.

He had looked at them a good deal, through the goose and the pie, as he looked at an entry that he could not at once place. He had sat at more boards than anybody in that room. At every one of them a cover had been laid for whoever was present, in the order of the roll, and a person who was not in the room had no place in it.

"One of those is for a lady who is in a hayloft with a pencil," he said. "The other is for a woman who went north eleven days ago, and does not know that there is a table. I have kept the count at a great many boards. I should have struck both."

"Out here you count who's owed a seat," Cael said. "It's a different ledger."

He had said it across the table, to the one person at it who had audited seats for a registry; and he had not known that he knew it until it was out. A big man had been laying a bowl too many for a long while, and nobody had ever asked him to show the sum.

"I see that it is," said Vastin. He turned his own cup a quarter round upon the board, and looked into it. "I was never shown the book."

Brom reached over with the jug, since he could reach, and filled it.

---

Karis had her twenty-first twice when they came up.

She said so from the boxes, without lifting her head, in what was left of her voice. Brom set the covered cup down by her knee. She drank it cold, in three swallows, with her eyes on a leaf, and Cael did not think that she had tasted it.

He sat down against the third box with Hesk's book.

He had not meant to make the inventory that night. He made one at the start of a thing; and he could not have said, climbing the ladder, what was starting. But he had a twelfth, which was not yet in any count. He had a pair of brass legs in his coat from a man who had taught him to write the count first. And there was a bundle on a shelf, two hundred yards over his head, which a pair of square pale fingers would untie in the morning.

He had kept his day's lines in that town, as he kept them anywhere. This was the other thing, the long one; and it was the first of its kind that he had ever set down inside a wall.

He wrote the head.

*Treswick; the loft over the stable; one in the stall. The two hundred and ninety-second day. Eight days inside. A hundred and thirty: four to a dinner, on Brom's ruling, and worth it. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He ruled a line under that, and wrote the second entry slowly, and left nothing out.

*Nineteen. Twelve confirmed fragments.*

*Wind — Lira. Pressure — the Bronze from Ardenmere. Iron — Brom. Compression — Reydan. Ember — Karis. Shadow — Seln; source protected; sealed; twice in all; it asked for three hours last night and was told no. Storm — Daeva. Anchor — unnamed. Blade — Ephram. Tide — Oryn; ask first, every time; used on nobody this side of the snow, and five times on myself, the last two round the place and not in. Iron Wall — Vastin; held; entered, not told; deployment none. Rune — the reader; a made thing's lean; given; told; deployment none; it looks before I've said that it may, and I am learning to give it nothing to look at.*

*One anomaly, as before. The still place. Not the Quiet. Not a fragment. Not touched since the waypost, and not to be until a healer has been asked.*

*Held, with no ruling: a cord, two edges, a hair. A row that counted a sheet short. I have not laid a hand to an instrument since the hundred and sixty-eighth day.*

That was two. He took the dividers out of his coat and laid them, shut, on the open leaf, and wrote the third round them.

*Hesk sent dividers. Lines are for finding out how far a thing is from another thing. Everything is. I'll enter that as a rule and see what it's worth.*

He did not open the brass upon any distance. He had a ruling against that, a hundred and twenty-four days old, and a hair in a small pale woman's notebook to remind him why.

---

The line moved while the brass still lay upon the leaf.

There was a blanket on the gable window, hung on two nails by a girl on their first night, and it had hung there since. It did not quite meet the frame on the left side. Each night a thread of the crown's light came in by that gap, and lay across the boards a yard from his knee, pale and thin and as straight as a chalk line. He had never looked at it as a light. It was a mark on a floor, as the bars in the lane were marks on the cobbles, and he stepped over it going to his pallet.

It lay tonight where it always lay. He had the tail of his eye on it, and no more.

For a count of one, it was not steady.

It did not go out; it thickened, and thinned, as a lamp's flame does when a hand is put near the wick; and it was as it had been. He had counted it before he knew that he was counting. *One.*

He sat quite still, with the pencil off the leaf.

There was a weight on him. He knew it, and he would have given a good deal not to. It was the particular weight of an instrument turning toward him.

He made himself go on from there, since that was the place where a person stops thinking and begins to be afraid.

A needle swings to iron. Nobody says that the needle has seen the iron. A cord had read a stride short in his hand, and nobody had said that the cord knew him; a strip of brass had gone a hair past its mark, and it was brass. He had been a thing that instruments went wrong on since a morning in a frozen yard, and for a good while before it. A draught will move a blanket. A lamp-trimmer will put his hand to a lamp. And his own eyes had been paying for another matter since the afternoon.

It was three strides to that window.

He looked at the three strides. Vastin slept in a stall now. The pallet by the gable had been rolled, and there was nothing between his knee and the blanket but boards. He could cross them. He could lift one corner of a horse-blanket, and see for himself whether the thing on the crown burned as it had always burned.

He had written a sentence at a fork-stone, in this book, at a square woman's asking, and had read it back to her. It did not say *the steps* and *the square* because those were the only two ways of going near. It said them because she had seen what he did with a question.

He did not cross.

He did not look again at the line, either; he turned upon the boards, so that the third box was between his shoulder and it, and drew the lantern nearer. He wrote the fourth entry on that page in the form that he had used once before, on the hundred and sixty-eighth day, for a thing that he could neither prove nor put down.

*Claim: the light on the crown was not steady, for a count of one. Evidence: a pale line on the boards of a loft, through the edge of a blanket; one witness, with tired eyes. No cause found. Ruling: hold.*

Under it he put the last, since it was owed in that book and to nobody else.

*The promise stands. Not the steps. Not the square.*

He shut the dividers into the page, and the book on them.

Lira was awake. She was lying on her right side, with the slow arm uppermost, and she had watched him turn his back to the gable.

"What was it?"

"It's in there. Fourth down."

She read it by the lantern, with her lips going. She looked once at the blanket, and at the gap beside it, and then she got up without a word, in her shirt, and crossed the three strides that he had not. She did not lift the corner. She drew the blanket a hand's breadth along its nails, until it met the frame, and pressed the edge flat with her thumb.

There was no line on the boards.

"Twenty-seven days out of the wool," she said, of the arm that had done it. "It's good for something."

On the evening of that same day, the two hundred and ninety-second, and four gates north of a fork-stone, Oryn was asked to stand by for a child in her fourteenth year.

She had come in at dusk, two days behind her own reckoning and no longer troubling to count them. It was a larger hold than the others, walled in stone to a man's head with split oak above, and there were nine on the bench under its gate-house eave. She went along them at her stride and a half, with two fingers for each wrist: a herdsman's foot that a beast had trodden; a baby with a rash, and a mother who had not slept; a grandfather who wished only to be told that the thing in his chest was the same thing as last year, and it was. She asked each one, and had each one's yes, and it was full dark before she had her hands in the butt.

An old woman had waited for that. She was the one who kept that hold's days, as some woman did in each of them: when the rams went in, and when the beans; who was owed what, and whose turn it was at the gate.

"There's a thing at first light," she said. "I'd take it kindly if you were on that bench for it. Not to do anything. Only to be there."

"What thing?"

"My daughter's girl is in her year. Fourteen since the hay. We ring them on the threshing floor, at the first light after the moon's full, and it was full last night." She had her arms folded in her apron. "It's never gone amiss here. But her mother's mother went down like a felled calf, at hers, and lay till noon. It's in the family to take it hard."

"I'll not lay a hand on her unless she's hurt, and unless she says. She'll have enough that's new without a stranger's fingers."

"I never asked for your hand. I asked for the bench."

"Then you've the bench," said Oryn.

She ate what was brought out, and lay down beside the mule.

She had seen it done forty times on that road, at one gate or another, and had never got used to how little there was to see. Inside the Line it was a matter of offices. She had stood in one herself: a room with a desk, and a ring chalked on the boards, and a clerk who looked at a plate of brass and not at her. The district had named her day a year beforehand. Out here there was no desk, and no plate, and nobody to write. An old woman scratched a ring on a threshing floor with a hazel stick, on a morning that she chose by the moon; and the child stood in it; and it came.

Whether it needed the ring, Oryn had never learned. Nobody at any of those gates had been willing to try it without.

---

The floor was of beaten clay, in the open, with a low kerb of stones about it.

They were all there by the first grey: the whole hold, thirty or more, standing off beyond the kerb with their breath smoking, and not one of them speaking above a murmur. Oryn sat on the bench by the gate. It was forty yards. She had her hands in her sleeves.

The old woman drew the ring.

She walked it backward, bent, with the stick's point in the clay; and when the two ends met she stepped over the line, and held her hand out, and a girl came across the floor to her.

She was thin, and tall for her age, with her hair in one plait and a sheepskin on her shoulders that was too big. She was frightened, and she was trying to walk as though she were not. Oryn had a square face that showed little, and she kept it so; but she had looked for that walk, and it was there, as it always was. Every one of them came across the clay like a person carrying a full pail.

The girl stepped into the ring, and the old woman stepped out of it.

Nothing happened that a watcher could have named.

There was no light, and no sound, unless it was a ewe somewhere behind the wall. A thin child stood in a scratched circle with her hands shut at her sides, and a hold stood round her and held its breath.

Then her head turned.

It was a small movement, a little to the left, as a person turns toward a voice. Her eyes went with it. They went to a place in the air that was just beyond the corner of her sight, and when they came there the place had moved, and they followed it. Oryn watched a girl of fourteen look at something a hand's breadth from her own cheek that not one of thirty people could see.

She had seen that forty times as well. It was always the eyes. A child found, on such a morning, that there was a thing in the world which belonged to nobody else, and which nobody else would ever be shown; and the first that she did was to try to look straight at it, and fail, and try again.

The girl's lips had parted. She was listening now. Whatever it was had said its one word, down at the bottom of the breastbone, where nobody could overhear.

Her face came open like a door.

The old woman was over the line before the child's knees had begun to go. They did go, a little. She was caught, and held, with a grey head bent to hers. Oryn, on a bench forty yards off, saw the girl's mouth move against her grandmother's ear, three or four words, quick, and saw the old arms tighten.

That was the whole of it, and the hold let its breath go. Somebody laughed, and somebody was crying, and a small boy broke away from his mother's skirt and ran across the kerb to see what his sister had got.

She would not be told what the word had been, and she did not wish to be; it was theirs.

Oryn sat with her hands in her sleeves, and found that she was not thinking of the girl.

She was thinking of a boy on a table, under her two hands, a long while since, who had told her a thing with his breath going slow. He had stood in a ring too, and it had been chalk, and there had been a clerk. A small light had come up from behind his breastbone to the edge of his eye, as it had come just now to that child; and it had looked at him, for a count that he still knew; and then it had gone out. She had asked him whether it hurt. He had said no; he had said that it was like a wick being pinched.

This girl had looked up, when it was over, and found a face. Every one of the forty had. It was the second thing that they did.

She did not know whose face he had found. There had been a clerk in that room, and a plate of brass; she did not suppose that either had been looking at him.

She had put her hands on that boy more than once. She had found in him what she had found in nobody, and had told him to write it down and leave it be; and then she had walked north from a fork, owing him the one reading that she had not given.

She was two days behind, and there was a fifth gate, and a ford, and a mill.

She made her reckoning there, on the bench, as she would have made it over a wrist. The fifth gate should have its day, since it had waited a cold term. The ford could have an hour. What she would not do was come late to that mill; six people would be there on the day that she had named, and one of them had a thing for her that she had told him to bring. If the road cost her a night's walking, then it cost that. And when she had him on the plank, she would not go round him again as she had gone round before. She would go as deep as she went. She would ask him first, and have his yes; and then she would read what was there, and say what she found, whether she had a word for it or not.

The old woman had come across the clay. She stood by the bench with her arms folded, and her eyes were wet, and she was not troubling to hide it.

"You never stirred," she said.

"She never needed me. That's the best morning I've had on this road since the thaw."

"She kept her feet. Near enough."

"She did. Tell her that somebody saw."

Oryn got up, and went to see to the mule.

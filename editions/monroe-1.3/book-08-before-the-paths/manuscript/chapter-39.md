# Chapter 39 — Aimed

Vastin had been given three words in his life for the thing that he had served, and he set them out in their order, since he had never been able to think about a matter until it was set out.

He did it on a box, by a bay of bundles, in the afternoon of the two hundred and ninety-third day. Nobody wanted him. The lamp had come back to the long table, and the boy knelt beside it, a hand to the left, as he had knelt on every other afternoon. The small pale woman was making a second copy. The man in the dusty coat sat in his chair with his eyes shut. It was a room with one office in it, and Vastin had not once that day felt his boot begin to move.

The first word was *imperfect*.

He had held that one for very nearly the whole of his service, and he had held it comfortably. A scale has an error. A careful officer learns the error, and allows for it, and does not throw away the scale. He had sat through eleven fields and nine thousand readings on that footing. Where a plate gave a wrong answer he had called it a wrong answer, and had written so, and had been thought rather too scrupulous by people who were not obliged to sign.

The second had been given him by a file.

He had read it by the candles of four inns, a long while ago, and had carried it up a bluff to a house above Ostrand; and there five young people had shown him what the office that he served did with honest readings. He had said one sentence afterward, to a boy, in a room that was not his own. He had it by heart. He had never been sure that the boy did.

*I knew the system was imperfect. I didn't know it was designed to be.*

That had cost him a seal, left in a tray on a morning of rain, and three hundred miles on a hedge-stick. He had thought it the end of the matter. A man finds that the scale was made to read false; he lays down his office; he goes to dig up the one buried thing that he has the knowledge to find. He had supposed, on the road, that the thing would turn out to be a fraud. He had been ready for a fraud. He had dealt with a great many.

It was not a fraud. A fraud wants something. It leans the scale so that a house may keep its place, or a seat its revenue, and when the leaning is found there is at least a person behind it who has got richer.

He had heard a leaf of skin read out that morning, once, by a woman who would not say it twice. He did not say it over. He had been asked not to, and he was a man who kept to what he was asked; he had learned that lately, and at some cost.

But he could not help knowing what he had heard. A scale that reads false is still a scale, and whoever stands on it is at least weighed. This was another matter.

That was not *imperfect*, and it was not *designed*. He turned over what it was, on a box, with his hands on an ash stick; and the word came up of its own accord, as a third figure comes when two have been written.

*Aimed.*

He looked at that for some time.

He had put his hand to the foot of a great many sheets. He went over them now, since he owed it. Before the fields, and before the readings, he had been a junior officer of a district seat, and it had been his work on certain mornings to stand beside a desk while a child of fourteen was told what it was to be. A clerk wrote. He signed. It had been given to him as a duty of no weight, such as is given to the young, and he had thought it one.

He remembered very few of them. That was a finding in itself. He remembered a boy who had laughed aloud, and been hushed. He remembered a girl in a red cap, who had looked up afterward, as they all did, to find a face; and he had been reading the sheet.

The sentence rose in him then.

It was the one that he had begun that morning, to a bay of bundles, six strides from a desk. He knew its first half as he knew his own name. It came up exactly as a hand had once come up toward a lamp-ring, half-way there before he had seen it start; and he gave it the look that he had given the hand.

It was a draft. A small grey man had told him at a spring what he was when he drafted, and a small pale woman had laid a bare leaf on a desk and asked him to put nothing on it. He did not finish it. He did not let himself hear how it would have ended, even in the place behind his eyes where a man hears his own clauses before he speaks them.

It rose twice more before the light was trimmed. He put it down each time, and each time it was a little easier, like the leg.

There was one thing that he permitted himself, since it was not a conclusion. It was only something that he had done.

He looked along the tables at the boy.

Three nights ago, in a stair, six people had come to see what that boy was; and he had stood upon a step with his stick, on the eleventh day of thirty, and put a wall in front of him. He had not known then what stood behind it. He had said so, on a pallet, when he could not hold a bowl. He knew a little more this afternoon. He found that he would do it again, knowing; and that in a day which had taken the floor out from under forty years, it was the single act of his that he could look at without wishing to revise.

---

He asked for the stair a quarter of an hour before the bell.

"I shall want the start," he said to the boy, who had come to see what he needed. "And I should prefer to take it by myself. I have had your hand under my heel, or at the other end of a strap, on every flight for three days. I am obliged for each of them, and I should like to learn whether I can manage one stair without."

"If it goes, there's nobody below you."

"If it goes, I shall sit down, which I shall be doing in any case."

The eyes that looked back at him were tired. They were nineteen years old, and they had heard what he had heard, and they had gone back to holding a lamp.

"I'll be a flight behind," said the boy. "I'll not come nearer unless you call."

It was a hundred and ten strides to the cross-way, on the stick and the uprights. He put the strap of the tarred case over his head, so that it hung at his back. Then he turned about at the foot of the first flight, and let himself down, and began.

It was dark from the second tread. He had no need of light; he had learned those steps in three days as a blind man learns a room. He set his two hands on the stone behind him, and thrust with the sound leg, and came up by one. The bound leg went before him, stiff, like a thing that he had been asked to mind for somebody else.

A man who goes up a stair backward looks at where he has been.

There was nothing to see but the dark at the foot, and a little grey in it where a lamp burned a long way off down a gallery; but he faced it, tread after tread, and it grew smaller.

He thought about the walls.

He had been a wall. He did not say that with any pity for himself; it was the plainest account of Iron Wall, and of his office, and of what a seat had used him for. A crowd came to a gate, or a hearing drew too many, and he was sent for. He struck his stick upon the ground, and whatever stood on the far side of the line stayed there. He had done it eleven times for a seat, and twice since for no seat at all. He had believed, each of the eleven times, that the thing behind him was the law, and that it was imperfect, and that it was a great deal better than what would come through if he lifted his heel.

He would have to go over the eleven, one at a time, when he had a table and a month. It was the kind of work that he was good at.

Nine. The first landing. He rested with his hands open on his thighs.

What he could not get past was smaller than the eleven, and it had no file.

He had met the boy across a desk, with a plate between them, on a bluff. He had read him for three days, with every instrument that the seat owned, and had written that the instruments could not reach him. He had declined to read him again. He had told the boy why, on a bank, at dusk: the sound reason, and the poor one beside it.

He had thought, through all of that, that he was dealing with an error. A plate had failed upon one child, as a scale fails upon a weight that is out of its range. It had been his whole understanding of the case, and he had built a resignation and a road upon it.

He stopped there, on the dark stone, with one hand lifted to the next tread. He could feel that the thought had a second half; and the second half was the sentence again, come round by another door. He saw it for what it was. *Draft nothing. Just look.* He put the hand down upon the stone, which was cold and dry and laid without mortar, and looked at that instead until the thing had gone by.

He went on up.

There was a matter that he could settle, and he gave the second flight to it. They would sit in a yard tonight. The big young man had said so at noon: there would be a fire by the stall door, since one of them could not climb a ladder; and he knew who the one was. He would be at that fire. The leaf would be read there by those who had not heard it. And the boy would be across the flames from him, with all of it to carry.

He owed that boy a sentence.

He began, from forty years' habit, to set it in order. There would be three heads. The first would deal with what he had believed; the second with what he had been shown above Ostrand; the third —

He caught it at the third head, and very nearly laughed, alone, on a stair, in the dark.

He would not draft it. He would say what was so, when the time came, in whatever words were in his mouth; and if it came out badly, it would at least not have been composed. He thought that the girl with the long knife would ask him something afterward. She generally did. He thought that he knew what, and that he would have to answer it truly, and that the true answer was not one that a man would choose to give across a fire.

He did not prepare that either.

Eighteen. He rested.

The bell went while he sat there: one small flat stroke, a long way down, like a spoon on a cup. He heard boxes taken up. He heard the boy's step come as far as the stair's foot, and stop, a flight and a half below, exactly where it had been promised.

He had nine more. He had never yet got so high before the bell.

He set his hands behind him, and thrust, and came up by one. It hurt a good deal. He found that he did not much mind; it was only a knee, and he had been told to the day how long it would take. A healer had been exact with him. He had come to value that above most things.

At the twenty-fourth step he could go no farther without a rest, and he sat with the case against his shoulder and listened to four people climb.

They did not hurry him. A brown light came up over the second landing, and a big man's shadow with it. Nobody said that he had done well. He was glad of that. A pair of hands that he knew took the foot of the tarred case, without a word, and bore it up for the last three.

---

"They lie tonight," the man had said, at the bell, of the fifty-two sheets on their three tables.

He had stood by his desk to say it, with the card for the morning in his fingers, and his eyes on the floor between two boxes.

"I said a week. I'll be a day over it. There's the back of that skin to do, and I'll not have it done against a clerk's bell." He pushed the card along the slope. "He'll write *the same*. He'll have to find something else to write the day after."

Karis had risen from the second chair. She had two copies, and she folded one of them in three, and held it out across the desk's corner to Cael.

"That is the fair one. It is to be read by whoever was not here, and I do not mind in what order." She kept her eyes on the paper. "I should like it to be you who carries it. I think that it would be wrong in anybody else's coat."

He took it. It weighed nothing. He put it inside his coat, with an old man's book and a pair of brass legs, and it lay there against his ribs like a thing a good deal heavier than either.

She had turned to the man with the card.

"We lie at the house with two doors, a street back from the trough. There will be a fire in its yard this evening, since one of us cannot go up to the loft. I say it only so that you should know where your leaf's copy has gone." She took the card from the slope. "I do not ask you to do anything about it."

"I don't come up," said the man.

"I know that you do not."

He had not looked at her, or at the boy beside her with a hand inside his coat. He looked at the leaf of horn on its beech board. Then he laid the upper board over it, as a lid is laid, and set the jar upon the board.

Cael went up the stair a flight behind a sound that scraped, as he had said that he would. He did not go nearer. On the second landing he found that the sound had got above him, higher than it had ever been at a bell; and something in his chest that had been shut all day came a little way open at that, and he let it.

He took the foot of the case for the last three steps. Nobody spoke of it.

At the head they waited, pressed along the wall, while quick flat feet came up behind and a bar was drawn. Lira had the lamp. She stood a step above him, holding it low, at her knee.

He put his hand into his coat, to be sure of what was there, and brought the fold of paper out into the brown light.

He did not mean to read it. He meant only to see that it was whole. But it had opened a little in his coat, and the first line lay under his thumb in a small upright hand; and his eyes went along that line before he had given them leave.

It was the right way up.

He stood on the top step of twenty-seven and looked at that. He had read every sheet that had ever been written about him from the far side of somebody's desk. There had been a file in a district office, and a thicker one on a bluff; there had been a finding under two hands, and a paper with an hour in it. Each had lain with its head toward the person who owned it, and he had learned to take them so, as a matter of course, as a person learns to read a shop's sign from behind the glass.

There was no desk on the other side of this. There was nobody holding it. A woman had written out, as nearly as she could, what had been set down about a kind of people before there was a registry to file anything in; and she had folded it, and put it into his hand.

He read the one line through, the right way up, and it was no easier to read. He folded the paper, and put it back.

"Is that it?" said Lira, at his ear.

"That's it."

"You looked."

"I looked at how it lay."

The iron swung inward, and a lantern was waiting in the vault.

---

Seln was at the bar.

He stood beyond the painted pole, on the kerb side of the carts' street, with a carrying-frame at his feet that he had hired of somebody for a copper; and when the handcart came through he took the frame up, and fell in at its tail, like a porter who has waited all afternoon for a load and has decided to walk home behind another man's.

He looked at five faces as the cart went by him.

Cael watched him do it. It took no longer than the count of five. The mild eyes went to Brom, between the shafts, who was looking at his own boots; and to Karis, at the wheel, with her notebook held in both arms; and to an old man on the boxes, facing backward, as grey as the wall. They went to Lira, who shook her head by the width of a finger, since she did not yet know. They came last to Cael, and rested.

He asked nothing. He never did, in a street. He walked at the cart's tail with his frame on one shoulder, and waited to be told as much as could be said between a bar and a lane.

Cael had been asked not to say it over, and he did not. He gave it as a carter gives a load to the man who is to take the other shaft: who had put it up, and whom it was meant for.

"It's one man's work. All of it; the tiers, and the rest. He says so himself."

"Made," said Seln.

"And he says who it was built against. It wasn't a house. It was a sort of people."

"For a kind."

He said nothing after it. He walked ten paces on those two words, and did not turn his head. Cael, beside him, saw only what there was to see: that the left hand had come out of its sleeve, and had gone to a strap across the chest of a grey coat, and rested there for the length of those ten paces as it had rested once in a loft.

"On paper?" he said at last.

"On skin. She's made it fair. It's in my coat, and it's to be read; she's asked that nobody say it."

"Then I shall read it. I have had two sentences, which is as much as I trust from any mouth. I have never yet heard a telling that was the same size as the thing." The hand went back into its sleeve. "The boots were not on the kerb today, nor in any doorway that I could see. I do not take comfort from it. I only tell you."

"He's time. You said."

"I did. I had rather be wrong."

They came down the street with the brake on, which was Brom, walking backward into the hill. The roofs of the district went by on the left hand, grey, with their smoke going up straight. Cael looked at them. He had looked at them on every journey by that road, to keep his eyes off a wall; and he found, this evening, that he had no eyes to spare for a wall in any case.

He was carrying a thing, and it took the whole of him.

It was not the paper. The paper weighed nothing. It was what he had not let himself pick up in a gallery, at a desk, with a woman's back turned to him and a man's eyes shut. He had counted a room there. He had held a lamp afterward, for five hours, a hand to the left, and counted pebbles. He had the thing now as a person has a bowl that is full to the brim: level, in both hands, with all his care gone into not spilling it before there was a place to set it down.

He knew that he would have to. He did not think that a cart's tail was the place.

In the lane the four alleys had lost their white. He pushed through four bars of a paler light, with a shoulder against his own at each; and he did not look at them, and he did not think about them. He looked at a pair of raw hands on a tarred case, a yard from his face.

They were shut very tight. The old man had not said one word since the bell.

"He's been like that since noon," said Lira, low. "Brom tried him with a joke, in the yard. He said, *I beg your pardon?* As if Brom had asked him the hour."

"He was there."

"I know that he was. I can count who was." She pushed for a few strides. "It's the three of you. You, and her, and him. You've all got the same face on, and it's not a face that I've seen on any of you. I'd give my good arm to know what's on that paper, and I've only the one."

"You'll have it in an hour."

"I'd better."

The old keeper at the round arch counted one, and four, and said that their lame one looked no better for his ride. Nobody answered him. He looked a second time at the cart, and at the faces round it, and made his stroke without another word.

---

The stable kept an iron basket on three legs, for the carters, and Brom had it out by the stall door before the cart was swept.

He laid the fire himself, with ends of split log from the kitchen's heap, and struck it with his own flint. A small pale woman stood by and watched him do it, with her bound hand in her coat, and did not offer. She had not struck a point since a fork on a moor. There was a wall over that yard with a book at its gate.

"It'll want a quarter of an hour to be worth sitting at," he said. "I'm going for the pot."

They brought the old man out of his stall between them, and set him on the mounting block, with the leg along it and a blanket folded under the heel. There were buckets in the stable, of oak, with rope handles. Lira turned five of them upside down in a round about the basket. It was nearly dark under the arch, and the lamp over the painted door had been lit; and Seln sat down on the bucket that faced it.

Cael did not sit. He stood with his back to the stable wall and his hand inside his coat.

It was the first fire that they had had in thirteen days. He marked that as the wood caught. They had lain in a loft for eleven nights on another person's hearth, in a town where a struck point was a line in a book. He had forgotten how a yard changes when there is a flame in the middle of it: how the dark draws back to the walls, and people's faces come up out of it, one at a time, each lit from below.

Brom came across the cobbles with the pot on a board.

There were eight bowls on it.

"She gave me a look," he said. "I told her that there's seven of us, with the one that's owed. She asked what the eighth was for." He set the board down on an upturned bucket. "I said that I'd laid one for a mule before now."

"He said that he does not come up," said Karis.

"I heard what he said. I've heard a deal of people say what they don't do." He was ladling. "It costs me nothing to lay it. If nobody comes, there's the more for him on the block; he's eaten nothing since the bread."

"I have had no occasion for it," said Vastin.

It was the first thing that he had said since noon, and they all turned to it. He was looking into the basket.

"I should be obliged if nobody took that as a complaint against the cook. I find that I am not hungry. I expect to be so again." He put out one raw hand, and Brom set a bowl into it. "I shall hold this, in the meantime, since I am now able to."

The fire had begun to give heat. Karis came and sat on the bucket nearest it, very upright, with her notebook on her knee; and her eyes were shutting, and opening, and shutting.

Seln lifted his head.

He had heard something under the arch. Cael had not; he heard it a breath later, when he had been shown where to listen. It was a step on the stones of the carriage-way. It was quick, and flat, and perfectly even, and it did not put out a hand to any wall.

It stopped.

A man stood in the mouth of the arch, a full stride inside it, where the lamp of the painted door did not reach and the fire scarcely did. He was not tall. His coat was the colour of dust. He stood for the space of three breaths with his chin down, as he had once stood on a paving outside a cook-house, a step later than a person stops who is used to streets.

He had not come by the door with the book. He had come by the yard.

"I said that I don't come up," said the voice like a hinge.

Nobody answered. Brom had straightened, with the ladle in his fist.

"I've not been below the second wall after dark since my hair was another colour. I had to ask a keeper which street." The man had put up one square hand between his face and the basket, against the light. "I sat an hour under that skin, when you'd gone. I've sat under it ten years. I found tonight that I didn't care to do it by myself."

He came forward into the yard.

He did not look at the fire, which hurt him. He did not look at the boy by the stable wall; he took some trouble about that, and Cael watched him take it. He looked at the cobbles, and at five buckets set in a round, and at the sixth, which a big man had just turned over with his boot.

"Sit," said Brom. "There's a bowl."

"I didn't come to be fed."

"Nobody does. Sit down."

The man sat. He took the bowl in both hands, and held it without eating, with his eyes shut and the steam going up past his face. That made seven in a stable yard, and eight bowls, round an iron basket, with a pump behind them and a hill over their heads.

Cael took the paper out of his coat.

He did not open it. He stepped round the edge of the fire, to the bucket where a girl sat with her left arm across her knees and her face turned up to him, waiting, as she had waited since a gallery.

"You first," he said, and put it into her hand.

# Chapter 33 — Entered

They let the old man down onto the stair between them, and for a while nobody did anything but breathe.

He sat on the seventh step with his back against the wall and the bound leg thrust out across the slot. He had not fainted. His eyes were open, but they were looking at a strip of sky, and the hands that lay in his lap had fallen open like a pair of gloves. The stick had gone down two flights by itself. Lira fetched it, and laid it across his knees, and he did not take hold of it.

Cael had seen that man at the floor once, on a moor, where a healer had said so with two fingers on his wrist and had likened it to a well. He had bent over his stick then, and stood. He could not have stood now if the stair had been on fire.

"Who's hurt?" said Cael. "Say it. All of you."

"Shoulder," said Lira. "I hit the wall with it. It wasn't the arm, and I want that written; I never put the arm to anything."

"The left," said Brom. "It's worse than the cart left it. And I've had the right round the pair of you, so that's said something to me as well." He lowered himself onto his box. "I'll be hearing the rest of it from two healers at once."

Karis did not answer. She was still on the sixth step, where she had sat down, with the flat across her knees and her left hand inside her coat.

"Karis."

"It is my hand," she said, and brought it out.

She had not had her gloves on. They were in her pocket, where they had been since the forenoon, since a person does not turn the leaves of a tally-book in kid. The stone's edge had gone across the heel of her palm, from the root of the thumb to the wrist's other side, as straight as a line ruled with her own brass; and she had it shut upon a fold of her skirt, and there was a good deal of red in the fold.

"It is not deep. I looked. It is only long."

Brom had his neckcloth off already. He wound it round the hand, twice, and tied it, and she let him.

So that was the count, and Cael went over it as he would have gone over a cut on a moor. There had been six of them, with staves and a bow, in a place with no way out of it; and nobody was dead, and nobody had been carried off. One box had been dragged down two steps, and sat upon. Not one of the six had shown so much as a chin.

They had left a stave. It lay where Lira had dropped it, with her knife standing up out of the middle; and she put her boot on the ash and worked the blade free with both hands, the right and then, slowly, the left.

"That's good steel," she said. "It was dull for half a year. I'd not have got a finger's depth with it before the smith had it."

"Leave the stave," said a mild voice above them. "I shall want to look at it."

Seln was at the head of the cut. He came down the flights as he came down a ladder, winding a thin cord round his fingers as he came, with a small pear of lead swinging from its end. It was a thing off a desk: a copying clerk hangs such a weight over the edge of a ledger, to keep his page open.

"The four went by the lane," he said, "two east and two west, at a walk, and they had their hoods back before the first corner. I did not see a face. The two below went out by the carts' gate, which had been left upon the latch for them." He stopped a step above Vastin, and looked at him for some time. "I could take one of the four, and I took the one who was there to be believed."

"They cut your cord," said Lira.

"They did. I have the better half of it."

He put it into his sleeve. Then he went down on his heels, which Cael had seldom seen him do for any person, and got his shoulder under a brown coat's left arm.

"He cannot stay in a stair. They will count you out at two gates, and it is past the bell already. I can bring him to the head of this, and no farther; I am on no card."

They took him up between them, Seln and Cael, a step at a time. The sound leg did what it could, and the other hung. At each of the thirteen steps to the lane he let his breath out through his nose, and said nothing at all.

At the top Seln slid out from under the arm, and Brom came into his place with the wrong shoulder.

"I shall be at the arch before you," said Seln; and he went along the parapet, and was not there.

It was full dusk in the lane. The four alleys had no white in them. Each had, at its far end, the paleness that did not flicker, and it lay out along the cobbles in a bar. Cael went through all four with an old man's arm across his neck and a box dragging at his back, and he did not turn his head. It was not hard, tonight. He had too much on his shoulders to look at anything but the next sett; and a girl went by on his left each time, under two loads, with a tarred case knocking at her hip.

"You're past the bell," said the old keeper at the round arch. He had his board on his knee, and a lantern beside the stool, and the point of his pen went along them. "One, and four. Your lame one is a good deal lamer."

"He fell on your steps," said Karis. She had her left hand inside her coat. It was true, and she said it to the wall.

"I told you to mind him on them; they are older than he is." He made his stroke. "Go on. I shall not write the lateness against the card, this once, since I should not care to explain that card to anybody."

---

There was a house inside the second wall with a board at its door, and Lira spoke of it before the lantern was lit.

"It's two streets from the blue gate. We've gone by it a dozen times. *Second bell: blue.* She's Iron, the third rank, and she's got it on paper." She had Karis's wrist in her right hand, and was holding the whole arm up above the pallet, as a healer had once told her to hold her own. "They'd have that shut in a quarter of an hour, with a Path. It'd not even leave a mark."

"They would," said Seln. He was on the ladder's head, where he had been when they came in under the arch. "It would be the best-mended hand in the district. And before any healer in that house laid a finger on it, a clerk would write a line on a sheet at a bed's foot: her name, and where the name is registered, and the tier; and the sheet goes to the counter at the first bell."

"It is the ninth article of the schedule," said a voice from the gable, very faintly. "The entry precedes the treatment. I have signed for the keeping of it."

They had laid Vastin on the pallet under the blanketed window. He had not spoken since the cut, and Cael had thought him asleep.

"After the fourth bell there's the side door," said Lira. "With a card. It said so."

"*As the house's means allow.* A card is a figure, and a figure is seen when those with names have been seen. She would sit on that bench until the morning; and in the morning they would still ask her what she was."

"I am not going to be written down for a hand," said Karis.

She said it quite calmly. She was as white as the wall, and she sat very upright, with her hurt hand held up for her and the other flat on a courier's flat.

"I have three days, and I have fourteen letters. If a paper is served on me tomorrow, at a registry's own yard door, then four people have carried fifty-two sheets up that hill for a week to no purpose, and a man below will never be told why I did not come down." She drew a breath. "It is my left. I write with the other."

There was no healer on that side of a gate who would put hands on her for less than her name. The one who would have done it for a bowl of barley was five gates north, with a mule; and she would have asked first, and been told yes, and been grey for an hour afterward.

"Then it's me," said Brom.

He had got up off his box. He felt under its lashing with his right hand, and brought out a twist of cloth; and in the cloth there was a needle, as long as a finger and a little curved, and a hank of thread that had been drawn through wax. He had roped three boxes with those, at a ferry's shed and in an inn yard.

Karis looked at the needle.

"You have sewn a person before."

"A dog. And a boot. And the lining of her coat, on a mountain."

"I am to take comfort from the coat?"

"The dog lived," said Brom. "It lived to bite the carter."

It was the last thing that anybody said to make another smile. He held the needle in the lantern's flame, turning it, until the steel had gone dull, as he had seen a smith do for a horse; and then he sat down on the boards, since he could not lift either arm to a table's height, and laid her hand palm upward upon his own knee.

"Lira. The light. Lower than that; I want it where I'm looking, and not in my eyes."

"A hand to the left," said Lira, and knelt.

She held the lantern in her right hand, with her elbow on her thigh, and it did not move at all.

Brom took the neckcloth off. He looked for a long moment, with his head on one side, as he had looked at a pier and at a heap of slate; and then he began at the thumb's end.

He was slow. He had hands like shovels, and no arm behind either of them, and he worked from the wrists. He drew each stitch down flat and looked at it before he made the next. Karis did not watch. She had turned her face to the slates, and her lips were moving; and it was a little while before Cael understood what she was saying over.

It was the letters. She was telling them off, the hooked upright and the gate and the fork, each with its sound, one to a stitch.

There were seven stitches. She had come to the seventh letter by then, and she went on through the other seven with her eyes shut while he tied off.

He had no hand free for a knife, and so he bent his head over her palm, and bit the thread through.

"That's my whole stock of sewing," he said. "I've put it all in the one place."

He tore a strip from the tail of his shirt, the width of three fingers, and bound the hand from the knuckles to the wrist; and he tucked the end in, and pressed it flat with his thumb, as he pressed a knot that he meant to trust.

"I looked which hand it was, before I started," he said. "If it had been the one that draws, I'd not have touched it. I'd have carried you up to that board myself, and given them my own name to write beside yours."

Karis opened her eyes. She turned the bound hand over, and looked at the back of it, and then at him.

"Thank you," she said. "I know what that would have cost you, and I am very glad that it was the left."

---

Vastin could not hold a bowl.

Brom had brought the pot up, late, with seven on the board, and had set the seventh at the end by itself. The old man had been got up against the gable's foot, with a rolled coat behind him; and when he put out his right hand for the thing, the fingers would not shut upon its rim. The left, which had held a ferry's strap and a mule's head, lay open on the straw and did not stir.

"I am aware," he said, "that I am about to be fed like a horse."

"You're about to be fed like him," said Lira, with a jerk of her chin at Cael. "He couldn't lift a spoon for a day, after a stair. I did that as well."

She held the bowl to the old man's mouth, and he drank from its edge. He did it with his eyes on the far wall, and he thanked her after every mouthful, until she told him that she would stop if he did it again.

Brom had the linen off the knee. One of the two laths had cracked along its length. He took a slat from the lid of his own box, and split it with Lira's knife, and bound the joint again as he had seen a healer do it, turn upon turn.

"It's twice the size that it was," he said. "I can't tell you what's in it. I can only make it so that it won't bend."

"I was told what it would cost," said Vastin. "She was exact, and I should like it remembered that she was."

Cael had heard those words given, on a frozen road, by a square woman with her sleeves pushed up: that a man who stood a wall on that leg inside the month would keep the stick for the rest of his days. It was the eleventh day of thirty.

He went and sat down on his own pallet, at last, with his back against the third box.

It had been waiting for him since the stair. He had felt it there through all that came after, through a lane and two gates and seven stitches, standing in the place behind his eyes that belonged to nothing else, as each of the ten had stood, as patient as a clerk. He had not looked. He had had an old man's arm across his neck.

He looked at it now, in a loft that smelled of hay and horse and a pot of barley.

The lines were where they had always been, and nobody under those slates could see them but himself. He took them as he had taken the last, on a healer's plank: slowly, from the top, putting each into the plain words in which he would have to write it.

*Iron-Wall-adjacent. Vastin. Partial. Iron family. Held. A plane, not a body: the ground told not to give. Distinct from the third — Iron-adjacent, the bed; this is not the bed hardened, this is a line drawn on it and kept. Engagement field: defensive; rooted. Tier equivalent unknown. Deployment: none. Eleventh.*

Eleven.

He went over what was new in it, since he could not have helped doing so.

It was of a family that he had already. The third had come to him from a big man, a long while ago, and it read the ground under his feet; and he had half expected this to be the same thing made harder. It was not. The millstone told him what a floor weighed. This told a floor where it was to stop.

It had a word in it that he had not met, and the word was *held*. The ten had each been named for what they did, since they read, and burst, and burned, and bound; this one was named for what it did not do.

Its field was a new one as well. He had nine that had come out of a fight, and their fields said so. He had one that a healer had given him, lying down, and that said *clinical*. Here was a thing that had come out of a fight in which the person who owned it had struck nobody. A man had put his heels on a line and been hit, and the lines had found a name for that, and it was two words long.

And it had no tier. Every one of the others had been weighed, Iron or Bronze or heavier; but an old man had told a factor on a moor that this Path had a rank beside it on a roll which would mean nothing on that road, and it seemed that it meant nothing here.

He had it, then. It sat under his breastbone with the rest, as real as any of them, and as quiet as a gatepost in a field at night.

*This far?* it said, of the ladder's head; and of the boards between his boots; and of the door of the stable underneath.

He told it no, and thought that he would be telling it so for a long while.

There was one thing more, and he had kept it for the last. He knew whose it was. He had not had to grieve for a name that he would never learn, as he had once on a chalk bank; the name was three yards from him, against a gable, being given water by a girl, and it could not close its hand upon a bowl.

---

"I should be obliged for a word," said Vastin. "I regret that I cannot come to you for it."

The lantern had been turned low. Brom was down, on his back, and Karis lay by the boxes with her bound hand on her chest. Lira had gone to her own pallet; and Seln sat on the ladder's head, where he could hear the yard, and might have heard anything else besides.

Cael went and sat on the boards beside the old man's straw.

"I have been setting it in order," said Vastin. He spoke to the slates, and his voice had very little breath behind it. "It is a habit, and I am told that it is the tell; I shall give it up when I am stronger. There are three heads."

"Say them."

"The first is the knee. I was forbidden a wall on it, by the one person on this side of the Line who had the right to forbid me, and I stood one. I did not forget her; I had her in my mind as the stick came down, word for word." He shut his eyes. "I do not ask that it be excused. I ask only that she be told that I heard her."

"I'll tell her. She'll have a good deal to say about it."

"I should think the less of her if she had not."

"The second is what they came for. I have stood at the foot of eleven fields, and I know when a crowd wants a door and when it wants a man, and those six wanted neither. They had a light, and they kept it dark, and they looked past me. I stood uncovered, a yard from one of them, with a face that forty years of clerks have been obliged to know; and he did not once look at it." Something moved at the corner of the old mouth. "I find that I was a little affronted. They were looking at you."

Cael did not answer.

"I do not know what they hoped to see, and I do not ask. I told you on a bank that I had measured a person and could not read him, and it is as true tonight. I sat four feet from you for three days, with a plate, and I have walked a shoulder off you since a ferry. I know that six people shut a stair to watch you do something, and that you went up a step into a crossbow's eye and did nothing whatever." He drew what breath he had. "I know no more of what you are than the brass did. That is the third head, and it is the one that I wished to say."

He opened his eyes, and turned them, for the first time, to Cael's face.

"I put the wall in front of it anyway. I'd like that entered somewhere, since I'm not the one who keeps the ledger any more."

"It's entered," Cael said.

"Thank you."

He had said it so on a bank, with a hand on a boy's arm; he had no hand to put there tonight.

Cael sat on.

It was entered. He had not lied to the man by the width of a hair, since it was written already in the only place where such a thing was ever written first; and there was more in the entry than the man had asked for, or could have guessed.

He had taken something from him. He had not reached. It had come as nine of the ten had come, out of standing within a stride of a person who was using what he had in earnest. It had come while that person poured himself into a line on a step, to keep a bolt off a face that he could not read. Cael had stood there and been given it.

He could tell him. He looked at that squarely, as he had looked at a worse thing on a healer's plank.

He had told the healer within the minute, with her palm still on him. But she had known already what he was; she had found the ten with her own two hands, and it had been hers to be told.

This man knew nothing of it. He had not asked on a bank what read a still place, and he had not asked tonight what six people came to see. To say *I have your wall in me* was to say how it had got there, and how it got there was the one thing that Cael did not own alone. Five others held it. They had kept it out of every book for longer than he could say, and they had kept it above all from the office that lay on that straw with its hands open.

He believed that Vastin would have done nothing with it. That was not the question, since it was not his to spend upon a belief.

So it was a debt, and of a kind that he had not carried before. He had owed a healer a telling, and had paid it. He owed this one, and could not pay; and the man had thanked him.

He got up, and went back to his pallet, and took Hesk's book out of his coat.

Lira was not asleep. She had turned on her right side, with the slow arm uppermost, and her eyes were open in the lantern's last light.

"It's in there, then," she said, hardly above a breath. "Whatever it is that you've had on your face since the stair."

"It will be. Give me a quarter of an hour."

"Does he know?"

"No."

She looked across the loft, at a long shape under a blanketed window.

"That's a lot to owe a man who can't hold a cup," said Lira.

---

Seln came down off the ladder's head before the pencil was wet.

"I have a finding," he said. "I have looked at it by a lantern, and I shall look again by day; but I do not think that it will alter."

He had brought the stave up under his coat, and he laid it now on the boards in the round of the light, with the cut that a long knife had made in its middle.

"It is a watchman's, shortened, and the ferrule has been filed where a maker's mark would stand. The bolts are the same: a plain shaft, and no fletcher's thread." He put his hands into his sleeves. "Six people came into a stair in the oldest town on the Line, with their faces tied up, and not one of them opened a Path. They did not lack for them. I know how the one on the landing stood."

"Why not, then?" said Lira. "There were six. They'd have gone through Brom like a door."

"Because a Path that is opened under that wall is entered by whoever sees it; and they took the same care that we did to have nothing of theirs entered. They wished to have something of ours." His eyes went to Cael, and away. "The bow was to make one of you open, and the light was to tell somebody below that it had been done. When the light could not be shown, they had no reason to stay, and they went. That is their limit: they cannot be written down in this town any more than we can."

Cael had the pencil, and he wrote it as it was said, under the day.

"And the box?" said Brom, from the dark. "One of them had his fist through my becket. He never looked at me."

"They were told to bring away what was carried. I do not think that they were told what it would look like." The mild voice did not alter; but the left hand had come out of its sleeve, and it rested for a moment on a strap. "What they would have been glad to find was on a roof above their heads, for the whole of that minute. I had not thought to be grateful to a parapet."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Five of them knew; and whether the sixth, against the gable, was awake to hear it, Cael could not have told.

"There is one thing more," said Seln. "I came down by my own way, and I was at this arch before you. There was a man on the step of the painted door, under its lamp, beside the book."

"The one in the doorway," said Cael. "With the boots."

"They were the same boots. He was not in a doorway tonight. He stood in the light, where a person stands who wishes to be seen, with his hood up; and he waited there until the five of you had come in under the arch, and then he walked away along the ring road without hurrying."

"So they know where we sleep," said Lira.

"They have known it for four nights. Tonight they have told us that they know. It is a civility, in that trade, and it is meant to be thought about."

Cael thought about it. He had six people in a loft, of whom one could not stand, and another had seven stitches in her hand; and there was not a second house under that wall which would take them without a name. There were three days of a week, and fifty-two sheets on three tables, and a small pale woman who would go down to them tomorrow if she had to be carried.

"We don't go up that stair at dusk again," he said. "Whatever else we do. I'll say the rest in the morning, when I can see it."

"That is sensible," said Seln; and he went back to the ladder's head, and sat, with his face to the yard.

Cael wrote the count first, as an old man in Denvash had taught him.

*Two hundred and ninetieth day; Treswick; the loft; a hundred and thirty-four. The cut, at dusk: six, hooded, two from the yard and four from the lane, with staves, one bow and one dark lantern, and no Path opened by any of them. Nobody dead, none taken, no faces. Karis: the left palm, on a step's edge; seven stitches, Brom's; no healer, since a healer here wants a name. Brom: both shoulders. Lira: the right shoulder, on a wall, and the arm did not take it. V.: a wall, on the eleventh day of thirty; the knee has gone again, and he cannot hold a bowl.*

*Finding, S.'s: they cannot be written here either. They wanted one of us to open, and a light shown for it, and a box; they got a wall and a rock, and they left when the light failed.*

*They know the loft, and they've said so.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He ruled a line under that, and sat with the pencil over the leaf for some time. Then he wrote the rest, and it came all at once, as the largest things did.

*Eleventh. Iron Wall, from Vastin, in an alley, while he held a line for us with nothing behind him but the decision to. It's not Brom's — Brom gets harder, this holds. Since my Kindling I've learned to give ground and disappear. Now I've got the opposite in me, from the man who spent forty years being the wall they put in front of people like me. He held it for us instead. I don't have a word for what that is. I wrote it down anyway.*

Under it he put two lines more, since they were owed.

*Not told. I can't tell the taking without telling the rest, and the rest is six people's. He asked for the wall to be entered, and I said that it was.*

He turned the book round, and held it out across the gap between two pallets. Lira read it with her chin down, and she read the last two lines twice. She did not say anything at all. She shut the book over her thumb, and gave it back, and lay looking at the slates.

The lantern was nearly out. By its last brown light he could see Karis, on her back, with a strip of a big man's shirt lying white upon her chest; and she was not asleep, for her lips were moving still.

She had fourteen letters, and three days left of a week that a man below had called his own. There was a plank of black oak propped against a table in the dark, two hundred yards under her head, and a bundle on a shelf above it that nobody had untied.

Cael lay down. He put the book under the box's becket, where an awl had mended it.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the dark.

*No*, he said. He had a lamp to hold in the morning, and he knew already which way it would be wanted.

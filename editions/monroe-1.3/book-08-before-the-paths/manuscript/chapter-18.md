# Chapter 18 — A Year and a Day

Seln had taken the sheet an hour before, from the foot of the second row, while a small pale woman was reading a dead man's four words aloud; and it had troubled him a little that it was still so easy.

He had lifted papers out of heaps under their owners' eyes for the better part of his working life. One did not snatch. One was seen to be handling the heap for an honest reason, as he had been, reading six rows for what they left out. One's left hand squared the sheets while the right went on turning them. The sheet was in his sleeve before the pebble was back on its corner. Nobody at the slab had looked up. They were the only six people living from whom he had never before taken anything.

He stood at the basin now, with his back to the rock, and held the thing low against his body, where the wind could not get at it.

He had read it once already, on the night of the two hundred and seventy-seventh, over a shoulder, by one lamp. He had not meant to read the date at all. Karis's pencil had come to it, and written a year and a day in a clerk's figures, exactly as they stood; and a man who has copied the seat's paper for fifteen years does not see a station's date without turning it, any more than a carter sees a load without knowing what it weighs. The turning had been done before he knew that he was doing it. A day had come out.

He had known that day. He had written it.

It stood at the head of a file. It was the first line of the first sheet, after a name and before a word in square brackets, where the registry sets down the day on which a person was classed. The file had lain open on a good many desks at a house above Ostrand, in the years when he was employed there to carry such things from one desk to another, and to be overlooked. He had copied that line twice with his own hand, for two different officers. A clerk forgets a face in a week. He does not forget a line that he has copied twice.

So he had stopped. He remembered it without any pride: his hands, and his breath, and the eyes that he had not been quick enough to take off the pencil. The boy had seen. He saw most things, though he was slow to say so. Seln had said that he had read it, which was true, and had gone to a window and looked at nothing for the rest of a lamp.

He had not been certain. That was the reason that he had given himself, and it had been a good one for two days.

A sum done in the head, by lamplight, over another person's shoulder, at the end of such a day, by a man who would have paid a year's wages for it to come out otherwise, was not a finding. He might have taken one figure for another. He might have had the difference wrong; it was a long while since he had needed it. He might, after fifteen years of files, have laid the wrong day at the head of the right one. There were three places where he could have slipped, and he had spent two days on a road going over each of the three.

This morning a man on a stick had given the difference aloud, to a notebook, in front of seven people, and it was the difference that Seln had used.

He did the sum again now, properly.

He did it first as he had been taught to, taking off. Then he did it the other way about, from the day that he remembered, adding, to see whether he came back to the figure on the sheet. He checked the year. He turned the sheet over, and looked at the brush's work on its back, and turned it again. He read the technician's date a figure at a time, with his thumb moving under it, as he had not read anything since he was a junior. He tried it a third time by a different road, through the day column of the abstract that had lain next to it in the row.

It came out as it had by the lamp. It came out so all three times.

He stood for a while and watched the water run over the basin's lip.

He did not ask himself what it meant. He had a rule for that. It was older than the one that Karis kept, and a good deal less handsome. A clerk who guesses at a file in front of its subject has written the subject's finding for him. He had seen it done to this boy since his Kindling, by men who were better paid than himself and no wiser, each of whom had looked at an empty column and found it unbearable, and had put a word in it. He did not know what a sequence was. He could not have said what it was for one to be initiated, or what lay under a plate, and neither could the man who had carried the sheet three hundred miles. He knew that two papers agreed about a day. He had no more than that, and he did not intend to have more.

There remained the question of what a person did with it.

---

His training allowed three things to be done with a fact, and he went through them in their order, since it was the only order that he had.

A fact could be filed. One kept it, and said nothing, and let it lie against the day when it should be useful; and he had kept a great many so. It was what a desk did. He had cut a hundred and forty-seven notches in two hazel wands to count what a desk would do with a clerk's packet, and had been wrong about the manner of it; but he had not been wrong about desks. There was an office somewhere that had held six years of sheets where nobody would receive them. He had been reading its work all morning. He did not care to find, at his age, that he had become a small copy of it, with a locked case for a drawer.

He did not look down at the strap across his chest. He had carried that case for twelve years, shut, and it was shut now. He had found before today that he could not hold two closed things up to the light in one morning. He made no ruling upon it.

A fact could be reported. That was the second way, and it was the one that an honest man would be expected to take. He had only to walk back to the rock and say eleven words.

He considered it with care, since he distrusted his own distaste. If he said it at the slab, seven people would have it in the same breath. Karis would be obliged to enter it; she entered everything. She would then be obliged to refuse the sentence that followed from it, aloud, with the boy standing there, and he had watched what that refusal cost her once already today. A man who had certified readings for forty years would hear a day named, and his mouth would open on an order before he could prevent it. A healer would hear it from the basin. And the boy would be told one more thing about himself, in public, by a clerk.

He could take the boy aside. That was better. But it would still be Seln's mouth, and Seln's choice of words, and Seln's face to be read while he chose them. Afterward the boy would have to decide what to tell the others with somebody standing by who already knew. He had been handled so since he was a child. People had found things out about him and then come to tell him what they had found.

There was the third way. Seln had used it oftener than the other two together, and never once on a friend.

One placed a thing. One did not say it. A paper was left where the right person would come upon it, at a time when he could read it unobserved. He was let to make of it what he would, and was never afterward asked. It was how a man was turned, in the old trade, or warned off, or ruined. It was done so that the subject should believe that the knowledge was his own. Seln had been excellent at it.

He looked at that squarely, standing over a spring on a moor with a sheet in his sleeve.

The kindest thing that he knew how to do for the boy was, in every particular that could be seen from outside, the same act as the cruellest thing that he had been trained to do to a stranger. He could find no way round it. The difference lay wholly in what he wanted. He had been taught, by people who knew their business, that what a man wanted could not be seen and ought never to be trusted.

He wanted the boy to have it, and to be free of him in the having.

That was all. The boy could read a registry date; he had read them upside down on other people's desks for as long as Seln had known him. He had heard the difference given an hour ago, and he forgot nothing that had a number in it. If the sheet lay before him he would turn it, or he would not. If he did not, it was not yet his to carry. If he did, then he might write it or burn it, and tell five people or none. Nobody alive would have the right to ask him what he had made of it. Seln would least of all, having said nothing.

It had a cost, and he made himself price it, as that large young man would have.

A thing given so cannot be shared. He would never know for certain that it had been read. He could not stand beside the boy in it, or be asked what he thought, or say that he was sorry. He could not be thanked, which he did not mind; he could not be blamed either, and he found, with some surprise, that he minded that. He would be handing over the heaviest paper that he had ever carried, and he would have to do it as a man slides a bowl along a board, and look away.

He thought that it was probably what the boy would have done for him.

"Cael," said Karis's voice, behind him.

He heard the boy give a figure. He heard a pencil stop. *Again*, said Karis, quite gently; and then the boy's voice, a little too level, saying that he had done it twice, from the front and from the back.

"Then you are one short of me."

Seln turned round.

He had not thought of it. He had taken one sheet from the foot of one row, to keep a thing from being said before it was sure. He had forgotten that she never trusted a count until a second person had made it. She had put that row into the one pair of hands in which a cord had read short. He saw those hands now. They lay flat on the rock, on either side of the heap, and they had gone the colour of the paper. The boy was looking at them as a man looks at a tool that has failed him twice.

Seln had been wrong about a desk, after a hundred and forty-seven days of counting it. He had been wrong about this by the width of a slab of rock, and it had taken him less than an hour.

He crossed the turf, and made no more noise than he ever did. He set the sheet down between those two hands, with its head away from the boy, as every paper had lain before him in his life. He squared it with one finger. Then he went back to the water, and stood with his face to the mule. He counted the sound of the spring, since a man who has placed a thing does not watch it found.

He minded that a great deal more than he had allowed for.

---

It lay the wrong way up.

Cael saw that before he saw anything else, and for a breath it steadied him. A clerk who set a paper before a person turned it to face him. This one had been turned the other way, with its printed head toward the far side of the rock, as papers had lain on a registrar's counter at Denvash, and on a magister's desk, and on a table on a bluff. It was how he had learned to read. Somebody had noticed that, and had laid the sheet so on purpose.

He was not one short. That came next, like the blood coming back into a hand. There had been nothing the matter with his counting. A sheet had been out of the row, in a quiet man's sleeve, and here it was.

Then he understood that it had not been given back to the row.

He looked along the slab. Karis had sat down again on her stone. She had seen the sleeve, and she was writing something very small beside a figure, with her head bent; she did not look at him. Seln was at the basin with his back turned. Nobody else was nearer than Brom, who was frowning at the third row.

Cael read the sheet.

He had heard it read, by a lamp, in Lira's voice, from the far end of a table. There was the printed head. There were four short lines in a quick hand that leaned to the right, as a man writes on his knee. He knew what the first of them said, and the third, and where the fourth broke off. They had gone round in him for three days, with the stripe that lay across the rest.

The second line he had never seen. It was a date. There was a year of the registry's in it, and a day in that year, in a clerk's figures.

It told him nothing, at first. It was near a date that he knew, and it was not that date. He had read a great many, upside down, on a great many desks, and they were all of them near to one another. He would have left it there. But a man on a stick had said this morning that a return which came up from a station was dated in the stations' count, and had to be turned. He had said by how much.

It was not a hard sum. A child could have done it, the man had said.

He took off the difference.

He did it without moving his lips, with his two hands flat on the rock. He did not believe what he had, and he did it the other way about, from the far end, adding. He came back to the figures that lay before him in a stranger's ink.

He knew the day.

It was written in his own file, on the first line of the first sheet. It came after his name and before a word in square brackets, where the registry puts the day on which a person was classed. He had first read it at fourteen, the wrong way up, across a counter, while a woman with a pen made a small mark beside it. Every paper that the Compact had ever served on him had begun with it: a summons, and a challenge under a seal, and an order for a room and an hour. It was the day on which he had stood in a windowless building and put his hand where he was shown. A plate of brass had taken eleven seconds over him.

He had been waiting for it, he found, without knowing that he waited. A quiet man had stopped like a clock over that date, by a lamp. There was only one day in the registry's whole count that could have stopped him so on Cael's account.

*Deep layer*, the sheet said, in the first of its lines; and then two words; and then that day.

He stood very still.

He had two things. One was a line in a technician's hand, about something under a plate that nobody could open. The other was a date, and the date was his. He could feel the place where a verb would go between them. He could feel a dozen that would have fitted, each of them ready, as the ten were always ready, leaning toward the first stride.

He had learned from a small pale woman what a verb cost. She had sat up over one in a store behind a chimney, with a cord that read true and a hair in a fold of paper, and had refused to let it finish itself in her own head. He had not understood until now how hard that was.

He told it no.

A great many people were classed in a year. On that same morning, in other districts, other children had stood on other plates; a station's man who went below one, somewhere, might have found a thing beginning and written down the day. He did not know that it was his plate. He did not know what a sequence was, or what it meant for one to be running. The sheet did not say what it went with. It had begun to say, and somebody had laid a brush across the rest.

It was true, every word of it. It did not help at all, and he held to it regardless, as he would have held a rope.

His left hand had gone into his coat.

It had Hesk's book half out before he knew what it was about. He had written down everything that had ever happened to him. It was how an old man had taught him to keep from being told what he was: one noted the day, and the count, and the trend. He had the pencil. He had the place, under yesterday's line.

He looked at the blank beneath it for as long as he could have held his breath.

If he wrote the two days side by side, he would have drawn the line himself. It would not matter that he added no verb. A sum set down with its answer showing is a sentence. Anybody who opened that book would finish it for him: Lira, who had read it, or a healer, who had made him swear by it once. And it was not his alone. A man at a basin had carried it for three days, and had put it into his hands without a word, so that it need never be said.

He put the book back.

It was the first thing since he had learned to hold a pencil that he had known and had not entered. He was a little afraid of how easy it was.

He lifted the second row with both hands, and laid the technician's sheet in at its foot, where an office had filed it, and squared the heap upon it.

"It's whole," he said. "The row. It was only out for a while."

"I saw where it was." Karis had not looked up; and then she did, and her pale eyes went over his face once, carefully, as they went over a seal. "You read it."

"I've read it."

She had been given those words before, in a store, by another man, about the same sheet. He saw her hear them. She did not ask a second time. She wrote nothing, and tied the tape across the second row with a knot that she pulled very tight.

Lira was looking at him across the rock, with her chin up. She had a look for when he had put a thing away where she could not get at it, and he had seen it perhaps four times in his life. She gave it to him now, and held it, and let it go.

---

"That is all six," said Karis. "Counted twice, by two people, and agreed."

She had the rows back in the leather in their order, and the flap down. Nobody had spoken for some time. The sun had gone a little past its height, and the shadow of the outcrop had begun to creep out over the slab's western end.

Vastin had stood through the whole of the morning. He was standing now, a yard from the rock, with both hands on the head of his stick and his eyes on that flat of waxed leather. Cael, who had had reason to watch faces that day, saw something gather in his.

"There are five places," the old man said slowly. He was not speaking to anybody. "They have been measured for six years by an office that told no other office. They are classed, by a mark that the survey's own people carry on their iron. A surveyor who formed a view of them had the space for his view struck off the form, and is dead. And a return upon the deepest thing that the Compact owns has been laid among their sheets, for want of a shelf, with a brush drawn through it." His voice had quickened, and gone level. "This has to go up."

Nobody answered him.

He heard it then. Cael saw him hear it: the four words, in a voice that had said them from a desk every working day for forty years. He stood with his mouth a little open. His right hand had left the stick's head, and it hung in the air a foot from his coat, on its way to a pen.

"Up," he said, in another tone altogether. "To whom?"

It was Seln who spoke. He had come back from the basin when the tapes were tied, and had taken his place at the end of the slab, hands in his sleeves.

"You keep drafting orders."

"Habit."

"Out here the habit's the tell."

He let that lie for the length of a breath. He was not looking at the man. He was looking at the hand that hung in the air, as he had looked at it three times that morning and said nothing.

"Draft nothing. Just look."

Cael had known that voice for a long while. He had heard it give an inventory, and a warning, and a count of days; he had heard it refuse a good many things. He went back over all of that, standing by a rock on a moor, and he could not find one occasion on which it had told a person how a thing was done.

Seln kept doors. He had seen two posts a hand out of true, and had told one man, in eight words, and let him mend them. He had stood with his back to a shed through a whole cold term so that he could never be asked what was in it. He knew more of how to be overlooked than any of them; and when an Archmarshal had answered a hand aloud on a road, and stood like a gatepost at a ditch, it had been a girl who walked backward in front of him and beat it out. Seln had watched. He had not said a word.

He had said seven now. It was the first thing that he had ever taught anybody, in Cael's hearing. He had chosen for it the one man there who had once had the power to file him.

"The tell," said Vastin.

"A man who reaches for a pen that is not there has told the room what he was. So has one who classes a paper before he has read it, or says *it should be directed*." Seln's mild voice did not alter. "I knew you in a doorway for what you were before you had opened your mouth, and I was not the only person on this side of the Line who could have. There is a town at the end of that road. There is a gate in it, and a man in the gate who has been reading people for his living since before I entered the service. You will not need to tell him your name. You will only need to stand at his counter and begin to arrange it."

"And if I look?"

"Then you will see what is in front of you. It is all that the rest of us have ever had."

Vastin was silent. Cael watched him do the thing that he had been told, and it was a strange sight; the man had read rooms for forty years, in an order, giving each object its due, and he now had to look at one without doing anything to it. His right hand came down, and found the stick. His eyes went to the slab.

"What's there?" said Lira. She said it without any mischief.

"A courier's flat, strapped. A calfskin, with four stones on it." He was a long time over each. "A book that belonged to a dead man. An iron stake that has never been driven. Some pebbles, which that young woman has laid out by size." He stopped. "And a wind. I had not noticed until now that there was a wind."

"Good," said Seln.

He went to see to the mule's girth, which did not want seeing to. It was Brom who had the last of it, from the basin's rim, with his boot half on.

"He told you that for nothing," he said. "I'd keep it. He's not given any of us so much in a year."

---

Karis had not got up from her stone.

She sat with the flat across her knees and her two gloved hands laid on it. Cael had seen her so on the hundred and thirty-third night, with a strip of brass and a calfskin, going past the edge of what she had. She did not look tired. She looked like a person who has added a long column and is afraid to read the foot of it.

"Six years," Karis said. "They measured all of it. They graded it — the sites are ranked by something, and it's the same something in the kit, and I can't read either one."

She turned her head, and looked westward, where the road went over the next swell of the moor.

"Everything I can't read points at the same place."

"Treswick," Vastin said.

Karis said, "The archive under it. Cleon."

"Who's Cleon?" Cael said.

"The only historical-Path practitioner registered west of the Line. If the language is anywhere, it's under his hands. A day and a half."

She took one glove off, a finger at a time, and looked at the hand inside it.

"And then I find out whether I've been right for five years or wrong for five years, and I don't know which I'm more afraid of."

Nobody answered her at once.

It was the first time that the town had been named aloud since a page was opened on a table. It lay on the moor now among the seven of them, like a thing set down. Cael turned the other name over. It was a short one, and he had never heard it. A man sat under that town with his hands on a language, and had a name, and had been waiting there, it seemed, for somebody to bring him three floors.

At the basin a square woman had stopped wringing linen. She stood with a wet cloth in her two fists and her head a little turned. She had heard a station spoken of twice at a board, and had shut her mouth on something each time; she had heard an archive spoken of today. He saw her put the cloth down on the stones, very deliberately, as she put down a thing that she meant to come back to.

"If you're wrong," said Brom, "we've had a walk."

"If I am wrong, I have asked a person to go into the place that he fears most on the strength of fifty-two sheets and a feeling."

"He's not been asked," said Lira. "He took a slip." She had come round the slab, and she stood over the small pale woman with her left hand out of her pocket. "You said *ours* at that plank before any of us. I never heard you want a thing before. I'd sooner walk a day and a half behind somebody who wants something than sit at Lowmarch with somebody who's being careful."

"It is not a reason."

"No. It's a boot. Put the glove on; your hand's cold."

Karis put the glove on.

He went and got the mule himself. Nobody had asked for hands, and nobody did. There was a road, and it went one way. A job had put them on it, and what lay at its end had been named now by the person with the best right to name it. He found that he had no ruling left to give. He had only to pick up a box.

"The fork by dark," he said. "Oryn, how far is it?"

"Six miles," said the healer. She was drying her hands a finger at a time, and she did not look at him. "There's a fold there with a hearth in its corner, where a fire has walls round it. I'll have something to say when we're sat at it. I've put it off since a board at Lowmarch, and I've run out of road to put it off along."

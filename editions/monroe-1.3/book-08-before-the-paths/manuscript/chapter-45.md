# Chapter 45 — Oldest Last

She had the store behind the chimney, where five people had lain out a cold term, and there was not a hand's breadth of the deal that was not covered.

Cael stopped in the door. It was a little past sunrise on the two hundred and ninety-eighth day, and the one small window laid a square of thin light across the whole of it. The courier's flat stood open at the table's head, with its six rows out and a pebble on each. The calfskin lay unrolled beneath them, under the Ternhall brass, and a dead man's book was open at its first page. Three of the Stair's sheets had been taken from their box and set in a row; and in the middle of everything, side by side, lay a leaf of her notebook and a square of stiff card with a turn of brown tape beside it.

Karis stood over the two with her gloves off.

"Shut that," she said, without turning. "And come round to this side. I will not have it read wrong way up."

He came round. Lira had the door, and Brom filled it a moment after, with a quiet man at his elbow who had not been seen to cross the yard.

"I have it," said Karis. "I had it at first light, by the candle, and I have spent an hour and a half since trying to break it. I have not been able to, and I should like six people to try."

She put the butt of her pencil on the card.

"This is his drawing of the fourth line. He drew what was there, and where he could tell a stroke of the writer's from a scratch of the knife's, he has ticked it. This is mine, made the same afternoon with a sewn hand." The pencil crossed to the leaf. "I was sure of three signs in the last six, and I told him so. In that gallery, by one lamp, with the skin before me, it was the truth."

"And by a window?" said Cael.

"By a window I have two copies made by two people who see in two different ways, and I can lay the one upon the other. Where his tick stands and my stroke stands, in the same place, that is a stroke. It gives me the second sign." She drew breath. "Four, of six. And a word of six signs of which I have the first, the second, the fourth and the last is a word that I have met. I kept it under a glove for three days."

She had turned to the Stair's sheets. Her finger went to a group of signs on the nearest; and to the same group on the next, lower down; and to the third.

"It stands four times on that floor, always behind a sign that bears. I uncovered it on my ninth day under the town, and it was not the word that I had wished for. It was only *set*, as a stone is set, or a stake."

Vastin had come to the door by then, on the stick, with the tarred case under his arm; he had stopped on the sill as a man stops who is not sure of a room.

"So the fourth line says this," said Karis. "It says: the mark. And hung from the mark, level, with neither above the other, two things that take it. The one is those who are kindled, and the other is what is set. And after both, a number, which is three."

"What's set?" said Lira. "Set where?"

"I do not know where; the list does not say, and I shall not say it for the list." She held out her bound hand toward the door, palm upward, without looking. "I should like the case."

Vastin brought it to the table himself, and undid the strap.

She drew the iron out. It was a stake as long as her forearm, numbered thirty-two, with its point still bright, since nobody had ever driven it; and she laid its head under the window, beside the first page of a dead man's book and the corner of an office's sheet.

A short stroke had been struck into the iron, and beneath the stroke a bar. The same stood in pencil in the book, and in a clerk's ink in the sheet's ruled square.

"That is a thing which was made to be set in the ground," said Karis. "It has a mark on it, which I turned over for a hundred and eighty-three days, and called two copies of one ignorance. I had the word for it all the time. I had read it aloud, once, in another line, and it had not occurred to me that a hole in a moor and a child of fourteen could be given a thing out of the same drawer."

She laid her bound hand flat beside the three of them.

"A mark is a grade. The one they give a boy at fourteen says what he's *for*."

Cael looked at the three marks, in iron and lead and ink.

He had been stood on a plate once; and something had been written about him at the head of a sheet, in a place that a printer had ruled for it, and he had carried it ever since. He had known at a spring that a hole in the ground could be filed in the same fashion. He had not known until this morning that the two filings were one word, in one line, on the back of a skin.

He put it where he put the rest. It had its verb already, and the verb was hers.

---

"It says three," said Karis. "I have only ever seen one."

She had a clean sheet before her already; she laid the brass along it, held the strip down with the heel of her bound hand, which she was not to lean on, and ruled three boxes side by side.

"There is a stroke with a bar. That is on the stake, and in the book, and on every sheet of the four places that I matched to the hide." She drew it in the middle box, small. "I told you at the spring that there were other sheets, under other signs, and that I would not call them places. I turned their corners down, and I did not tell you everything that I had seen on them."

"You said nothing of their squares," said Seln, from the sill. "I remarked it."

"I thought that you had; I saw the square, and I shut my mouth on it. A second mark, on sheets that I would not so much as call a place, was the first step of a theory; and I had six people round a rock who would have taken the second step for me." She had drawn the sheets out while she spoke, from under six turned corners, one from each row. "They are one group: the same four signs on all six. And this is what their square holds."

It was a stroke, and beneath the stroke two bars.

She drew that in the right-hand box; and then she laid her pencil down on the left-hand one, which was empty.

"I have been through all six rows this morning, by the squares alone. There is no sheet with a stroke and nothing under it. I have ruled a box for it because the list says three, and because a thing with one bar and a thing with two want something to stand before them. Nothing goes in it, and I shall not guess what would."

"So there's a kind that there aren't any of," said Brom.

"There is a box that the office has not used in six years. That is all that I will sign."

"It's tiers," said Lira. She had come to the table's corner, and she was looking at the three ruled squares as she had looked at three walls from a rise. "First, second, third. They've a ladder for holes in the ground."

"It is three marks. Call them what you like." Karis took the pencil up. "But a grade grades something. The one that is given at a kindling grades a person, and there is a man in this room who signed for a good many. This one is not for size, as I showed you at the spring; a place of twice the measure has the same bar. It is not for which is fenced. So I have gone through everything else in which one of these sheets differs from another."

She turned the Stair's newest sheet so that its foot lay toward them, and set her finger in the right-hand corner.

There was a number there, small, after a clerk's flourish; Cael had seen it at the spring, on every sheet, and had taken no more notice of it than of the printer's name.

"Ninety-four," said Karis. "On the row beneath it, the same place has ninety-three, and then ninety-two; it goes down by one with every row, to eighty-nine. I took it for a drawer's number, and so it is. I never asked what the drawer was counting."

"It is a series number," said Vastin.

He had not moved from the table's end; he spoke to the sheet, low, and with great care, as a man walks across ice that has let him through once.

"I can tell you what the form is. A return of that kind carries the year of its series: the count of years from the first sheet that the office holds upon the place. The figure is carried forward by whoever draws the next, and nobody is obliged to ask where it began." He stopped. "It does not say that anybody walked there in each of those years. It says how long ago the first sheet was drawn, and no more. I say what the form is; I do not say who drew the first."

"Thank you. That is exactly as much as I wanted." Her finger had gone to the hide, and it touched each ring in turn, going east. "The Fallow's newest sheet stands in its sixty-first year, and the Stair's in its ninety-fourth; the hall's is a hundred and thirty-eight. And the place behind the iron—"

She laid the sheet down upon the fourth ring.

"Two hundred and six."

Nobody at that table said anything at all.

Cael did the sum, since it was there to be done. The paper did not say who had gone to that fence, or how often. It said only that a first sheet of the place behind it had been drawn two hundred and six years before the one that lay under her hand.

"I had supposed that it was six years," said Seln, very quietly.

"And the two bars?" said Lira.

Karis turned up the corner of the sixth sheet.

"It has no figure; there are two signs there, out of the index, which I cannot read." She looked down the table. "Is that the form?"

"A series that was opened before the present manner of numbering is carried under its old style," said Vastin. "I should expect it, and I do not know when the manner changed."

"Then I can say that it is older than two hundred and six, and I can say nothing more. I shall not put a figure to it until I have one."

She laid her pencil down across the three ruled boxes.

"The one on a sheet says how *old* the thing is."

The old man had taken the iron up from under the window while she spoke. He laid it back in the tarred case, beside the cord and the mallet, and drew the strap; it had still never been driven.

---

There was a man in the door.

Cael had not heard him come, and did not know how long he had stood there. He had his thumbs in his belt, and his right arm hung a little away from his side, in its leather. He had not come in, and he had not so much as looked at three ruled boxes; his eyes were on the calfskin, and on a strip of brass that lay along its pencil line and ran off the edge of the skin.

"I'm not here for the marks," said Teague. "I'd not know one from another; I want to know how far."

"I was coming to that," said Karis. She did not seem put out. "The sheets with two bars have one line that the others lack, above their eight stations. It gives the four signs of the place behind the iron, and then a point of the compass, and then a measure in lengths. I saw that at the spring as well, and took it for a fence's survey tied to its own floor." Her pencil tapped the line. "It is a run, from the fourth place to this one. An office has written down how far it is from the one to the other, and which way."

"Which way?"

"By a point of the compass, which I shall lay against the hide before I say it aloud." She held out her right hand, across the table, toward Cael. "You have a pair of brass legs in your coat. I asked for the loan of them when I should have a hide in front of me again."

He took out Hesk's book, and the dividers slid from between its leaves into his palm, shut, with the cork upon their points. He had carried them for six days, under a ruling of his own that was a hundred and thirty days old: he did not put his hand to a thing that measured, since a cord had run short in it and two edges had disagreed. An old man in Denvash had sent them to step off a line with.

Cael laid them on the deal, and pushed them across with one finger.

"They're lent," he said. "I'll not open them."

"I had not asked you to."

She drew off the cork, and opened the legs, with her right hand, upon the hide; it was the first time that they had been opened on a distance by anybody. One point went down in the middle of the hall's ring, and the other in the middle of the fourth.

"The carter who drew this skin drew it in days. Brom. The hall to the iron."

"Three," said Brom. "With a mule, and a girl in a sling, and a healer gone north."

"A surveyor's length I know to the stride. I walked behind that cord for a whole cold term, and I know what a day of ours comes to in it." Her lips moved over the office's figure. "This run is three such days, less a little." She closed the legs by the width of a hair; then she lifted one point, and swung it round, along the brass.

It came down off the skin.

It stood on the bare deal, a hand's breadth beyond the calfskin's edge, hard against the Ternhall strip; and the low sun from the window laid the brass's shadow over it, so that the point stood in a narrow dark line.

"There," said Karis. "I have laid the office's point against the carter's line, and they do not differ by the width of this strip. It is under the edge, and I did not put it there. I laid a figure on a hide, and that is where it fell."

"Three days past the iron," said Teague.

"As we walk. A little less."

Cael looked along the skin, from the left hand to the right. There were four rings, and a point that was not yet drawn, and a number against each: sixty-one, and ninety-four, and a hundred and thirty-eight, and two hundred and six. They rose as they went. Beyond the last of them, in a strip of shadow, stood something that the office had stopped counting.

"Somebody laid them down in order," said Karis. She said it to the hide, as she would have read out a measure.

"Who?" said Lira.

It was the question, which had been asked on a road once, of a pattern, and had not been answered. The old man at the table's end had drawn breath, and Cael saw the right hand leave the stick, and begin to rise.

Karis had not turned her head; but her bound hand had come down flat upon the sheet of three boxes.

"It's observation. I'm telling you what the paper says. Not what it means."

The hand came down upon the ash.

"Noted," said Vastin.

It was one word. He had not begged anybody's pardon, or said that he was drafting. He had made a stroke at the foot of the thing, as a person does who has heard a term and means to keep it; and then he looked up the table at the boy whose word it was, with something in the grey face that was very nearly surprise at himself.

Lira had turned her head. So had Brom.

"That's ours," she said, under her breath.

"He's had it by him long enough," said Brom.

The man in the door had not followed any of that; he had been looking at the hide the whole while, as he looked at a load.

"Oldest last," said Teague.

It was not put as a question. Karis answered it all the same, with one small nod, as she would have passed a figure that had been read back to her correctly; and he took his thumbs out of his belt.

"Then that's where we're going."

---

They were on the south road by the middle of the forenoon, in the file that a captain had set under an ash.

It held. Cael had not been sure that it would, outside a meadow. Seln went ahead, small against the pale grass, and after him seven feet of ash with a leaf of steel on it. Then came a pair of shoulders that did not look round; and Cael walked a stride behind the left of those, where he had been put. He had never in his life walked at anybody's shoulder to be asked what he saw.

Behind him he could hear the rest by their feet. There was Karis's, with the flat and the hide, and beside it the tap of a lean old woman's staff. A big man came next with a box; and last of all there was a slow uneven step, the bad hip of a man with a crossbow on his back.

There was no stick in it.

That had been settled in the yard, and it was the first thing that the field had cost anybody. The salt cart that had been put out of the mud was going down to the mill for meal, light, behind its two mules; and its carter had said to Teague, cap in hand, that a man's arm was owed something. Teague had looked at an old man with a slat on his leg, and at the length of a ridge.

"It's owed a seat," he had said. "For him. I'll not walk ten at the pace of one."

The old man had opened his mouth. Then he had looked at the nine who would have had to wait for him, and had shut it, and gone to the tail-board. He had offered his copper, and the carter would not have it; he said that it was the captain's fare, and paid.

So a cart went on a furlong ahead, with a brown coat on its tail, and for the fourth day together Cael walked toward a pair of boots that hung.

They passed the well at the fork, which had a house over it now, with a door hung and a new rope on the windlass; a crew had taken that from the plank in the cold. Then the road climbed, and they were on the back of the ridge.

It was the same pale grass, combed all one way, and the same wind. He had come along it once in the dark before a frost, behind a mule, to ask a healer whether she would come; and he had gone back along it with the answer.

Nobody spoke. Twice a flat hand went up, far ahead, for nothing that Cael ever saw, and the file stood, and went on at two fingers. The second time the boy stood exactly when Lira did.

They came to the fork a little after noon, where one pair of ruts kept the ridge. The other went down the western face through a notch that wheels had worn, and the cart had stopped at its head, where the carter was locking a wheel. The old man had got down there, as he got down for every hill.

There was a flat rock there. Teague lifted his chin at it, and the file broke, and sat, and the old woman undid a strap of leather and looked at a wrist.

Cael took out the book.

He had sat on this rock with it once, and written two lines about a healer. The mill's wheel was to be heard below, very small, like a tired man splitting wood. He set the day at the head, and the count.

*Two hundred and ninety-eighth day. The fork above Millrace; the flat rock. A hundred and twenty-eight. Vastin: nineteen of thirty. He rides, on the field's ruling. Karis opened the dividers. I lent them.*

Then he wrote the rest as it came, and it came all in one piece.

*Karis cracked the grade. The sites are ranked by age and the bearing runs oldest-last — somebody laid them out in order, and the order runs into the deep edge, toward the first one they ever made. Second-tier's the fourth site, the one the Compact fenced. Third-tier's out past it, on the line, quiet for centuries. Teague's crew came after the thaw like he said — ten of us now, and he's still better at this than I am, and the ledger still says so. Two crews, one bearing, and a straightedge my guardian sent me because my grandmother said a man who catalogues everything eventually needs to draw a line through it. Turns out she was right about the line. Turns out the line was already drawn four hundred years ago. We're just the first to follow it to the end.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He read it through. Only the ranking was the paper's, with a place past the fourth that was older than two hundred and six. The centuries, and the figure near its foot, he had from a gallery, where a thing was read aloud once; he had laid them beside her morning's work himself, and it was more than she would have let him say at a table. He had not said it at a table. He had put it where he put what he thought, with his own name to it, and he let it stand.

A hand came over his shoulder; he gave Lira the book, and she read it with her lips.

"Your grandmother," she said.

"Hesk's wife, and I never knew her. She's only ever been a sentence on an order-leaf."

"It's a good one," she said. She shut the book and handed it back, and looked down the notch, where a wheel was knocking.

---

The valley was green, and the water came down it brown and fast over a weir of stakes and stone.

Three of the stakes were gone from the weir, and Cael saw a captain see the gap. The tall grey building stood beyond, with its wheel going round in a white fall. There was the fence of split posts, and the dozen roofs, and the ford below with a flat stone on either bank.

A woman sat on the nearer stone with a net across her knees.

She raised her head at the sound of boots, and counted them down the track with her chin, one at a time, and did not hurry over it. Then she turned and called toward the mill, without getting up, in a voice that had been pitched to carry over a wheel.

"Eleven off the ridge."

The answer came from a lean-to against the mill's southern wall, low and hoarse, from somebody who had not troubled to come to the door.

"I know."

There was a brown mule tied to a fence-post, with a white foot. She had her head up already, and both ears pricked at a big man with a box; and Brom, who could not lift an arm to her, stopped in the track and said something under his breath that was not meant for anybody else.

The bench along the mill's wall was empty, at the end of a day. Vastin had come down the notch last, a tread at a time, behind the locked wheel; he reached the bench as the others reached the fence, and looked at it, and plainly thought it wiser to be found on his feet.

Oryn came out of the lean-to, wiping her hands a finger at a time.

She was thinner. That was the first thing, and Cael had not expected it; three weeks of five gates had come off her as a cold term comes off a horse. She stopped at her stride and a half and looked the eleven of them over, and it took her no longer than it had in a cut.

Her eyes passed over him as they passed over a gatepost. They went to a girl who stood with both hands on her hips; to a small pale woman with one of hers inside her coat; to a big man whose arms hung. They went to four people whom she had seen across a meadow, and never had her hands in. And they stopped on a leg that was twice the size it had been when she bound it.

Her mouth went as it went at the smell of a wound left too long.

The lean old woman had come up along the wall, with her staff and her board. She stood a pace from the healer's elbow, and neither of them said a word. They looked at one another once, for the length of a breath, as two carpenters look who have each been shown the other's joint; and then both of them, together, turned and looked at Brom's right shoulder.

"I'll come to that," said Oryn. "It's had a box on it; I can see the lean from here." She had not taken her eyes from the big man. "And I'll come to you," she said, in the same voice, to the old one by the bench. "Stand there."

It was a stride to his left, by the lean-to's post, where the light was.

Cael watched him. He had heard that man answer every instruction that was ever put to him, for three hundred miles: with *I cannot undertake*, with *as to the last*, with a clause hung on the end like a seal on a ribbon. He saw the mouth open.

It shut again. The stick moved, and the leg after it, and the old man stood where he had been told. He hung no clause upon it. He gave her a figure instead, as he would have handed up a return.

"Nineteen days," Vastin said.

She did not so much as glance at the slat.

"Then it's eleven more."

"There's a count," said Teague.

He had come down to the fence. It was the first time that he had spoken to her.

"Ten on it, for a line that runs east. It's contract, and it's mine in the field, and neither of you is on it." His chin went to the old woman. "She's said that she'll not be counted. I'd sooner hear yours from you than be told it by a boy."

Oryn looked at him as she looked at a patient who has walked in on a broken foot and wishes to discuss the weather.

"I've three holds owed yet on this round," she said. "They lie the way that you're walking, for a day and some; and after that my road turns, and yours doesn't. You'll not count me, and you'll not send me. A crew doesn't write a well on its sheet because the road happens to pass it."

She threw the cloth over her shoulder.

"I'm a stop on my own route."

Nobody argued it. Brom had gone into the mill by then, and he came out with the miller's wife behind him and a sack on the bound shoulder; it was a mark's worth of meal and a flitch, for five days of country with no gate in it. He said the figure as he came: a hundred and twenty-seven.

Then he set down what he had carried under his box's lashing for seventeen days, which was a wooden bowl, on the end of the bench; and he turned a keg up on its end before it.

Lira looked at the keg, and then at the healer.

"Sixth chair," she said.

Oryn considered it for the space of a breath, as she had once considered a milking stool.

"On my terms. Which are the only kind."

She sat.

It was a keg, and not a chair. She lowered herself onto it as she did everything, squarely, and took up the bowl; and Cael, who had watched that woman eat standing for as long as he had known her, a stride and a half from the nearest of them, found that he had to look at the weir for a while.

---

"Shoulder," said Oryn. "The left. May I?"

She had lit a lantern in the lean-to, and hung it from the turf roof's pole. The wheel was still going on the other side of the stone, and Brom sat on the plank across two kegs, with his shirt off, like a boulder that somebody had brought indoors.

"Aye," he said.

The old woman had already gone to the right, and she did not ask; it was her own knot, and nobody else had been let to touch it. She picked at the drover's turn with her nails, and it came, and four yards of webbing went slack across his chest. Oryn watched her do it, and did not put out a hand.

Then her own thick fingers went round the left shoulder, before and behind.

"That's a wrench," she said, with her eyes shut. "It's a fortnight old, near enough, and you've used it since. You took a weight on it that was coming down, and you took it as a man. There's nothing of yours in it."

"I'd a clerk's yard all round me; I'd have been a line in a book."

"I'm not blaming you; I'm reading it." Her lips moved. "I can shut that, since it's torn, and a torn thing I can do. Hold still."

It took her a quarter of an hour. When her hands came away she was grey about the mouth, and Brom lifted his left arm slowly, as a man lifts a lid that he expects to be nailed. It went to the height of his chin.

She had moved to the right, where the old woman's hands were.

"And this I said I'd not touch. Twice mended, and not a third. It's had a box on it for twelve days, against what I told you at a fork." Two fingers pressed. "It's no worse. I can't think why."

The old woman said nothing. She took the webbing up again, and laid it shorter this time, in two yards and not four, with the turn behind the arm, and drew it down with a jerk that made the big man grunt. Then she looked at the healer, and Oryn looked at the knot.

"That'll do," said Oryn.

It was as much as either had said to the other, and Cael thought that it was a good deal.

"Arm," she said next. "The left. May I?"

"Yes," said Lira.

She had it out already, bare to the elbow, with the eight marks pale along it. She sat on the plank with her chin up, as she had sat on a block at Thornwater when a knot was undone; and the healer took the forearm in her two hands and went along it from the wrist, with her eyes shut.

"You listened when it told you the second time."

"Once," said Lira.

Cael thought of a girl stepping off a stair, under a town, with that arm folded against her ribs.

"Once is once more than I priced." The healer opened her eyes. "It's an arm," she said, and laid it down in the girl's lap.

Lira looked at it there, and shut the hand, and opened it. She did not say anything; but she drew her sleeve down over the eight marks herself, which Cael had not seen her do since the Fallow.

Karis had to be asked twice, since she had brought her notebook to the lean-to's post and was writing in it by the lantern.

"Hand. May I?"

"Yes. I beg your pardon. Yes."

The strip of shirt came off, turn by turn. Under it the palm was laid open to the light: a long straight seam across the heel, drawn together with seven stitches of black waxed thread, each as thick as a bootlace's end.

"Who sewed this."

"I did," said Brom. He had gone red. "With a roping needle. There wasn't a healer inside that gate who'd touch her without a name."

"It's the ugliest piece of sewing that I ever saw hold," she said. "It's held; it's shut from end to end, and it's clean." She had a small knife out. "They've done what they were put in for. I'll have them out, and I'll spend a little where the thumb pulls. You've been writing with it."

"It was required."

"I don't doubt it. Write. Don't lean."

The stitches came out one at a time, and Karis counted them, under her breath, in a tongue that nobody else there had.

That left the post.

He was still standing at it; he had stood there for the better part of an hour, on one leg and a stick, in the lantern's edge; and when she turned to him at last, wiping the knife, she stopped.

"You've not moved."

"I was told to stand there," said Vastin. "I was not told that I might leave off."

"Sit on the plank. Leg out. I want my hands on that knee, and on your wrist. May I?"

"Yes." He let himself down. "There is a thing which you must have first. I asked that you be told it, eight days ago, and I find that I had rather tell you. I stood a wall upon that leg on the eleventh day, and I had your words in my head as the stick came down. I heard them, and I did it."

"I can see what you did. I've been looking at it since you came down that notch."

Her hands went round the joint, above the slat and below.

"It held in a cut," said Oryn. Her thumbs moved. "And then you asked it for a second, with eleven days on it. I can feel the pair."

She did not spend on it. She took the slat off, and bound the joint again in her own linen with two new laths; and what she said, when she had counted his wrist, was that the month stood as it had been counted, and that he was not to mistake that for a kindness.

"Noted," Vastin said.

Her hands stopped on the linen.

"That's new."

"It's a useful word."

"It's his." She did not look round at the lean-to's post, where a boy stood. She tied off the last turn, and her mouth went down at one corner, which with her was most of the way to a smile.

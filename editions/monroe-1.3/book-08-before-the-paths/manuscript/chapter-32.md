# Chapter 32 — No Round

The fourteenth letter came out of the Hall on the two hundred and ninetieth day, an hour before the bell, and Karis did not say so until she had found it twice.

Cael had the lamp. He had put it where the reed was going, three times that afternoon, before the rough voice had finished the word; and the third time the man had not troubled to begin it. He only moved, and the light was there. Neither of them remarked on it. A lamp is not thanked for burning.

The old man had stood by the nearest bay since the morning, with both hands on his stick. He had not gone toward the ring once. Cael had watched for it, at the trimming and at the noon hour and when the light was wanted high; and each time the grey eyes had gone to the thing, and rested, and come away. He thought that it was a good deal harder than the four had been. It is one matter to stop a foot that has started. It is another to stand in a room with one office in it for six hours, and look at the office, and leave it where it is.

"Fourteen," said Karis. She said it to her notebook. "It closes the number after five. I had the head of that word two days since, and I could not have the tail."

"Of twenty-three," said the man.

"Yes."

"Three days."

"I can count as far as three," said Karis. "I have the figures for it."

He made a sound in his chest that was nearly a laugh; it was the first that Cael had heard out of him, and it seemed to surprise him as much as anybody. He wrote the card for the morning with his nose an inch from the pen.

The clerk's bell went, small and flat.

They roped the boxes. Brom took his on the right shoulder, as he had for four days, and Lira got her arms through her beckets, the right and then the left. Cael felt the third box settle between his shoulder-blades, with a purse in it under five thumbs. The old man hooked the tarred case with the head of his stick, and brought it up.

At the iron the man in the dusty coat drew the bar and stood back. His red eyes were on their boots.

"Second bell," he said, as he said every evening.

The clerk walked behind them through the vault with his lantern, and turned his key in the low door, and Cael did not look round at him. He had not needed to for five days. He was a quarter of *the same*.

The yard was empty.

He had never seen it so. They had used the whole of the clerk's quarter-hour that evening, since a small pale woman would not leave a tally-book lying open; and the wagons were gone from the dock, and the pan had been hooked up on the weigh-beam, and the carts' gate at the bottom was shut and barred. The light had begun to go. It lay grey on the long worn trough of the paving, and there was not a soul on it.

"It's quiet," said Brom.

"It's late," said Lira. "They'll count us out at that arch with a face on."

Cael looked up, at the roof of the last building, where a parapet ran along above the slot in the wall. There was nothing on it. There had been nothing on it for two days, morning and evening, and he had learned to read that as a small grey man doing his work.

The gate at the cut's foot stood open, as it always stood.

They went into it in the order that the stair had taught them. Vastin went first, since the slowest must set the pace, with the stick going up a tread ahead of him and the bound leg swung after; and Cael came two steps below, where a man would fall. Lira followed, with her arms through her beckets. Karis was behind her, with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other; and Brom came last, and his box all but brushed both walls.

The slot took the light from them at once. The walls went up three times the height of a man on either hand, blind and dark and damp, and the sky was a strip of dull violet a long way over their heads.

On the third tread of the bottom flight a stone rocked under Brom. He had told it for six evenings what it wanted. He only looked at it tonight, and came on.

"Five," said Lira, at the first landing. She counted them every time.

The second flight was the short one, of four; and it had the wet tread in it, the one worn into a dish, where a stick had slipped on the first morning. Cael saw the old man find it with the ferrule, and go round. He saw the sound leg bend and take the whole of him up onto the ninth step, where the next landing began.

Behind them all, at the foot, the gate shut.

---

It did not swing to, as a gate does in a wind. It was put to, hard, and a bar went down across it; and the sound came up the slot like a blow on a cask.

Every one of them turned. Cael got his head round over the box's corner, and looked down past a girl and a small pale woman and a big man, to the bottom of twenty-two steps.

There were two people inside the gate.

They stood with their backs to it. They wore long coats of no colour, and hoods, and each had a cloth tied across the lower half of his face, so that there was nothing under the hood at all. Each had a stave. They were not porters' poles. They were as long as a man and as thick as a wrist, and shod.

Nobody spoke. One of the two shifted his grip, and they began to come up the steps, side by side, without any haste.

Cael's breath had stopped. He had ten things in him, and every one had come awake at that bar.

He felt the stick turn.

It was the smallest sound in the slot: a ferrule grating round on stone, a step and a half above his head. He was looking downhill, as they all were. He did not at once understand why the old man was not.

Vastin had stood on eleven fields. He had given that figure beside a hold's hearth, and again on a bank, and Cael had taken it for a count of honours. It was a count of something else. A man does not hold a line for forty years at gates and hearings without learning what is done at the other end of a street to make him look away from his own; and when a thing was shown him at the bottom of a stair, with a great noise, it was to the top that his head went.

"Above," he said. It was said quite low.

Cael looked up.

There were four at the head of the cut, against the strip of sky.

One knelt on the highest landing, with a thing laid along his forearm that Cael knew before he could see it plainly. It was short, and it had a bow across its end. A second stood over him with a stave. A third was already coming down the flight below them, in the middle of the steps, as a man walks who knows that the ground is his. And at the very top, where the slot opened beside the parapet, the fourth stood by himself. He had one hand on a bar of iron that was let into the wall there for people coming up under loads. In the other he held a lantern, low, against his coat, with its shutter closed.

They were hooded, all four, and there was a cloth across each face.

It was a stair a stride and a half wide. It had no door in it, and no window, and nothing to stand behind. There were ten yards of it between a crossbow and an old man's chest.

Cael saw the bow come up.

The stick came down.

It struck the edge of the ninth step, once, with the whole of a tall man's weight upon it. The sound was not the sound of ash on stone. He had heard it on a moor. A line went out from the ferrule to either wall, along the lip of the tread, as a crack runs across ice. Then the air stood up from it.

There was no frost here to show where it was. For the space of a breath he saw it all the same: a bloom went over the dusk, from wall to wall and from the step to the sky, like a breath on a pane of glass. It was gone, and there was only a place in front of the old man's coat that the eye slid off.

It roared. He could not have said how a thing roars that makes no noise. He stood two steps beneath it, with an Arbiter in his breast that had been dark since the day it woke, and it came over him all the same, as the heat comes out of a forge when the door is thrown back. It was going out of the man steadily, as it had gone out of him on a road, with nothing kept in hand.

The bolt was already in the air.

It stopped a yard in front of a brown coat. It stopped with a crack like a slate struck by a hammer, at the height of a man's breast, on nothing. It dropped to the landing and rolled against the wall.

Vastin made a sound, shut behind his teeth. Cael saw the bound leg take it. Whatever struck that plane came down through the man who was its foot, and it went into the knee.

His boots had not moved. They stood on the lip of the step, on the line, and they would stand there until he let the thing go, or until it left him. He could not lift a heel. He could not turn round. He had shut a stair with his own body, facing uphill, and whatever was done at his back was to be done without him.

Cael had seen that wall go up once before, across a cart's width of road, and had seen what it was worth there. Eight hounds had gone round it. A bank had been enough.

He looked at the two walls of the slot, blind and sheer, going up into the dusk.

A wall on a road may be gone round. In that place there is no round.

---

It was Brom who met the two below.

He had turned at the bar, with the rest, and he had not turned back. Cael did not see the beginning of it. He had it afterward from three people, and from the stair. He saw the end, over his shoulder, between one breath and the next.

The big man let his box go. He did not set it down; he had no arm that would have set it. He dropped his right shoulder, and the thing slid off the webbing and fell across the fourth step with its long side to the gate, and lay there. It shut one half of the slot. He stood in the other.

He had nothing in his hands. He had not been able to lift the left above the elbow for four days, and the right was in another woman's knot.

"That's far enough," he said to the two with staves, exactly as he would have said it to a carter.

They came on. Neither had said a word. The nearer put the shod end of his stave at Brom's chest, like a man shoving a boat from a bank.

Brom stepped down.

It was not what a person does who is being pushed. He went a step toward them, onto the third tread, and trod on the back of it with his whole weight; and the stone that had rocked under him for six evenings tipped up at its front edge like the lid of a box.

He was off it, and had it before it could fall back. He could not raise his arms. He could bend, and he bent, and got both hands under a slab of the dark stone as long as his own forearm and a hand thick; and he took it up as far as his thighs, on his legs and his back, as he had taken a dray.

He had said on a moor that a rock only wants lifting and letting go.

He let it go. He did not throw it. He opened his hands, with a heave of the hips behind them, and it went down the last two steps edge foremost, turning over, at the height of a shin.

The first of the two was a quick man. He got one leg clear, and the slab took the other across the boot and the foot of his stave together, and he went back into the gate with a noise of breath and timber and sat down against it.

Brom had not straightened. Something had gone in his left shoulder as the stone left him, deeper than what the dray had done, and he was bent over it with his teeth showing.

The second came past his fellow in one stride. His stave was up in both hands, above his hood, and it was coming down on a bowed head.

Lira went off the landing.

She had been on the second flight, two steps under Cael's heels, with a box on her back and a small pale woman between her and the fight. She had got out of her beckets. Cael never learned how; the box was found afterward standing square on the seventh step, as if she had set it there for a seat. She went down past Karis, who had pressed herself to the wall, and took the two strides that the landing gave her; and she did not go on down the flight. She stepped off its head into the air.

It was not far, as she reckoned falls: the height of a table, and the length of a man outward. She came down over Brom's box, with her knees drawn up and the long knife in her right hand, and her left arm was folded against her ribs as tight as a wing.

She did not strike at the man. She struck at what he held. The knife went into the ash between his two fists, point first, with the whole drop behind it, and stood there to a third of its length.

Her boots hit the step in the same instant. Her knees went, and took it. She hung from the hilt with all that she weighed.

A stave cannot be swung with a girl upon it. It came down between them, slowly, like a gate-bar, and its shod end struck the tread and stayed. The man had a hand free. He let go with it, and reached for her hood.

Brom's boot met him at the hip.

It was a carter's shove, with a leg like a newel post, from a man who had got his breath; and the hooded one went backward down three steps and into the other, and the pair of them into the gate.

Lira had gone over to the left with the stave. The wall was there. She took it on the point of her shoulder, and Cael heard that. She slid down it, and sat, with a man's stave across her knees and her own knife standing up out of the middle of it.

"It didn't touch," she said, to nobody, through her teeth. She was holding the left arm against her with the right. "It didn't touch a thing."

There was a small sound higher up.

Karis had flattened herself to let the girl go by. She had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as she had carried them up and down that stair for seven days, and she had not a hand to spare for the wall. Her boot was on the sixth step. It was the wet one, worn into a dish.

It went from under her. She sat down hard on the stair, and the flat did not leave her arm. It was her left hand that went out, flat, to save the rest of her, and found the lip of the step above.

That stone had not been worn round. It had been cut to an edge by whoever laid it, before there were walls on that hill, and seven days of porters going by on the other side had done nothing to blunt it.

She did not cry out. She looked at her palm, and then she shut it, and put it inside her coat.

"The flat is dry," she said, in a voice that was not quite steady. "I should like that known."

The two at the gate had got up.

One had no stave now. He looked up the stair, past Brom, and it was not at any person that he looked. He looked at a box lying across a step. He went to it, low, with one arm up against a boot that did not come, and got his fist through its becket, and hauled.

Brom sat down on it.

He could do nothing else, and so he did that. He lowered himself onto the lid, with his two arms hanging and his heels dug in against the tread. The man below pulled at a rope with a great deal of a Copper from Velmere seated on the other end of it.

---

Cael had turned back uphill before the slab was out of Brom's hands.

It was the hardest thing that he had done on that stair. Everybody that he had was behind him: a girl going through the air, and a big man with no arms, and a small one with a courier's flat. He heard the stone go down, and the gate take a man's back. He heard a knife go into wood.

He had the whole ten, and they had come up in him like hounds to a gate.

Wind was first, as it had always been, the dry hiss in his feet. *Away?* it said; and there was nowhere on that stair that was away. Under it the millstone went round, and told him the weight of a slab that he could not see, and which way it was falling. Ember ticked in his right hand, and showed him a cord, ten yards off, drawn back along a stock; a single point would have gone through that cord as through a thread. Pressure had drawn itself as tight as a held breath. A slot between two walls was the very thing that it was made for; it would have gone up that stair like a cork up the neck of a bottle. *There?* asked the thin held note, of the place by the parapet where the slot began; and a point set there would have shut the head of the cut like a door.

It was dusk. Shadow had lifted while he was on the first flight, and it stood at his knees now and asked harder than all the rest together. He was in a place with no window, in failing light, with four people looking down at him. He could have been gone from every one of those eyes between two breaths.

He told each of them no. He did it one at a time, as a man goes along a row of dogs.

It was not the clerk that held him, though a clerk was at the end of every one of them, and behind the clerk an hour, and four names. It was a lantern.

He had been looking at it since he raised his eyes. A man stood at the top of that stair, with one hand on a rail, and a lantern held shut against his coat. He was not fighting. He had no stave. He was doing nothing whatever, and he was doing it with great attention, with his hood turned straight down the slot.

Nobody brings a light to a beating and keeps it dark. That man had been put there to show it when a certain thing happened. It had not happened yet. He was waiting for it, and so were the other three; the one with the bow had not laid a second bolt on his string.

Cael had been stared at on roads since he was fourteen, by people who had been told what to look for. He knew the look of it through a hood.

They had been told. Somebody had stood these six in a place with no way out of it, and had given them a bow to frighten with and staves to hurt with; and all that they wanted in return was to see what a boy with no tier did when he was hurt enough.

Then they should see him do nothing, from as near as he could bring it. A wall wanted a reason behind it; he would be the reason, and stand.

An old man had said a thing at a board, on a moor, with his bowl set down upon a sill, about a wall and what it wants behind it. Cael had been that once, by going ten strides off and landing like a post.

He went one step up.

It put him on the eighth tread, at a brown coat's left hip, in the open half of the slot, where there was nothing between the bow and himself but a place that the eye slid off. He stood square. He lifted his face, so that the last of the light was on it, and looked up the stair at four hoods.

The bow came round to him at once.

He had known that it would. He watched the man lay the bolt, and draw, and he did not shift his feet. He had put his skin behind this wall once already, on the word of having seen it hold. He had seen it hold a good deal since.

The bolt broke on the air a hand's breadth from his eyes.

He did not blink. It was not courage. He had only nowhere else to look. He heard the breath go out of the man beside him, and he saw the laths move under the linen.

"You need not have come up," said Vastin, without turning his head. His voice was thin, and quite steady. "It will hold as well for one as for two."

"It's not for the wall that I came."

"No," said the old man, after a moment. "I see what it is for. I had not thought to have it done for me twice."

The third hood had reached the landing.

He came across it without hurrying, and stopped where the air had bloomed. He put up a gloved hand. He laid the palm of it flat on nothing, a yard from Cael's face, as a man lays a hand on a window to learn whether there is glass in it.

He pushed. He set his shoulder to it, once, with his weight behind, and Cael heard the knee take that as well.

Then he stood back by half a pace, with his palm still upon the plane, and looked through it.

Cael looked back. There was a cloth, and the shadow of a hood, and inside the shadow no face that he could have sworn to.

After that nothing moved on the upper stair at all.

It was the quietest minute that he had ever stood through. There was noise below him, of stone and timber and people's breath, and he could do nothing about any of it. In front of him four people had gone as still as he had. They were waiting for a boy to be whatever they had been told that he was; and the boy stood on a step, with a box on his back, and gave them a porter.

He had never been so near that wall for so long. On the moor he had stood by it for a count of twenty, and gone.

He felt what it was, from two feet beneath it, with nothing to do but feel. Every one of his ten went somewhere. The hiss ran, and the millstone turned, and the point ticked, and the bellows drew; even the thing that read went along with whatever it read. This did not go. It had been told to stay, and it stayed, with an old man's whole strength poured down into it like water into sand. It was a heel set upon a line, and meant.

Everything that he owned was a way of going somewhere, or of getting out of the way. He had not known that a person could be given a thing which only stayed.

Something in him set.

It was not as the others had come. Tide had filled him like a cup, under a healer's hand. This did not fill. It was as a post is stood in its hole: not driven, but put there, and the earth trodden down about it a heel at a time, until a person could have hung a gate on it.

He had not reached for it. He had been telling ten things no, and it had not been there to tell.

*This far?* it said.

It asked of the step in front of his boots, in a voice like a bar going down into two sockets. He knew what it was before he knew that it was new.

*No*, he told it; and that was the eleventh time, in that stair.

---

The hand came away from the plane.

The hooded man had taken it off as a person takes his hand from a door that he has decided not to open. He turned his head, up the stair, and lifted two fingers.

At the top, the one by the rail moved for the first time.

He raised the lantern. He brought it up from his coat to the height of the parapet, where the whole town lay below to see it, and his thumb went to the shutter. A wall would serve, it seemed, if the other thing was not to be had. A wall was a Path opened in a district. He did not doubt that somebody down there in the dusk had been told to watch for a light, and had a pen.

A line came out of the sky.

Cael saw it only because he was looking at the lantern. It was no thicker than a bootlace. It came down from the roof's edge, above the slot's mouth, with something small and heavy at its end that swung once, round an arm and a bar of iron together, three times, quicker than he could follow, and bit.

The lantern jerked sideways. It struck the rail, and fell on the top step with its shutter still closed, and rolled, and went out inside itself with a small flat cough.

The man wrenched at his arm. It was bound to the iron from the wrist to the elbow, and it did not come.

Cael looked along the roof. There was a parapet, and a strip of violet sky, and for the space of half a breath a shape upon it no bigger than a chimney-pot; and then there was only the parapet. Seln had said once that if they saw him, he had done it badly. He had been seen by four people in hoods, and Cael did not think that it was badly done.

The one on the landing looked up at the roof for a good deal longer than Cael had.

Then he whistled. It was two low notes, such as a carter gives a horse.

It ended as quietly as it had begun. The man with the bow rose from his knee, and unstrung the thing, and put it under his coat. The one beside him went up to the rail, and drew a knife, and cut a thin cord in two places; and the fourth took up his dead lantern. None of them ran. They went into the lane, one after another, like people who have come to the wrong door, and the hood on the landing went last. He walked up the two flights backward, with his face to the plane, and at the top he stood for a moment and looked down it.

Then the head of the cut was empty. Nobody had spoken. Nobody had run.

Below, a bar was lifted. Cael heard the gate swing, and two pairs of boots on the paving of a yard, going away at a walk.

He counted. He had been counting since the stick came down, as he counted anything, and he was at eighty-one. It had been a minute and some.

"You can let it go," he said.

"You are certain."

"I counted. Four went up, and two went out by the gate. That's all of them."

The old man shut his eyes. His hands opened on the stick.

There was no frost to fall. The place in front of him that the eye had slid off was only a stair again, with two bolts lying on it; and the roar went out of the slot as a fire goes out when the door is shut on it.

He stood one moment longer on the lip of the step. Then the knee went.

It did not bend. It let go, sideways, all at once, as a prop is knocked from under a beam. The whole long weight of him came over backward, with the case and the stick, onto the step where Cael stood.

*Both heels*, said Compression. It had come up into them already, of its own accord, and his frame creaked like hemp.

He told it no. He took him as a porter would have. He got both arms round a brown coat, under the shoulders, and the box on his own back dragged him the way that they were both going. His left heel lost the tread, and found the next. They went down two steps so, the pair of them, and a tarred case came after and struck him on the ear.

Something stopped them. It was as hard as a gatepost, and it had four yards of webbing round it.

"I've got you," said Brom, from below, between his teeth. He had his right arm across Cael's back. It was the one that a healer had said was to carry nothing. "Stand still, the pair of you. I've only the one, and it's the wrong one."

# Chapter 21 — The Road's Edge

They came to the third post with the sun a hand above the town, and they were not going to do the last three miles.

Seln had known it for some time. He had read two boards at two lane-ends, and both had said the same thing in the same paint: the gate was shut at sunset and opened at first light, and whoever was outside it slept outside it. A stick could not have done three miles in what was left of the day. Nobody said so, since nobody needed to; and it was Vastin himself who looked at the sun, and at the figure on the stone, and stopped.

"Here," he said. "I shall not have five people locked out of a town on my account and told that they chose it."

There was a place made for it: a strip of trodden ground between the road and the river meadows, as long as four wagons, with a stone trough at one end and a ring of blackened stones at the other, and a thorn hedge along its weather side. A bank and a ditch ran the length of it at the road's edge, and carters who missed the gate had been pulling in there for longer than the post had stood.

Two of them had it tonight, with a wagon of hurdles under a sheet, and four horses on a line, and a fire at the trough's end. The elder lifted one hand from his knee when the boxes came in off the road; he looked at the loads, and at the old man on the stick, and went back to his pot.

Brom built the fire in the ring, from thorn that he bought of the carters for a copper.

Karis looked at the heap when it was laid, and then put both her hands under her arms and stood back from it. Brom felt in his coat. He brought out a steel and a flint and a twist of charred rag, which Cael had not seen in his fingers since before a small pale woman joined them, and he knelt and struck. It took him four tries.

"I'd lost the trick," he said, when it caught.

"You have been spoiled," said Karis. She was watching the flame as a person watches somebody else do her work badly. "So have I. I have not been cold at a fire-laying for years. I had not known that I minded."

"It's one point."

"It is one point, on a town's ground, three miles from a gate. Seln?"

"She is right," said Seln. He had not sat; he stood at the hedge's end, where he could see the road in both directions, with his hands in his sleeves. "Those two will be at that gate before us in the morning, and a carter talks while he waits. *There was a woman at the three-mile who lit a fire by looking at it.* It is not a crime; it is a line for somebody to write."

Seven bowls went round. Brom set the seventh on a flat stone a little apart, empty, and looked at it; and then he put a heel of bread in it, and took the bread out again, and ate it himself.

"She'd say it was waste," he said.

Vastin had his bowl standing, in the gap at the hedge's end, across from Seln. He ate as he did everything, with attention, and he thanked Brom for it, once. His face was the grey of the stone that he leaned beside. He had come nine miles since noon on one leg and a stick; and when the bowl was empty he held it for a while, as though the setting of it down were a thing that would have to be planned.

Lira took it out of his hand as she went by with her own.

"I had it," said Vastin.

"I know you had. I was going that way."

The light went gold, and then it began to go. Cael felt Shadow stir under its seal, as it stirred at every dusk, and every other sound in him dropped as a yard goes quiet under snow. He told it no, and it lay down. He had been telling it so for longer than two years, and it had never once left off asking.

He took Hesk's book out of his coat, and then put it back. There was a thing that he wished to do before he wrote.

---

He went along the bank to the post.

It stood at the pull-in's western end, where the hedge stopped and the ditch ran on beside the road. He sat down on the bank with his back against the stone and his face to the east. The moor lay along the sky there, a long way off and very dark; they had been on the top of it at noon. The town was behind his shoulder, with whatever lights it had.

He had found a thing at a brook, and it had seemed to him a large one. But he had found it cold and empty, after a quarter of an hour on his back in a field, and he had learned from Karis what a first count was worth. She never trusted a figure until it had been made a second time, by a person in a different temper. He was in a different temper now. He had a bowl in him, and nine miles, and he was too tired to want anything in particular.

He put his palm on his breastbone and two fingers on his wrist, and counted.

It did not take a quarter of an hour, since he knew the road. At ten he had his own gait, slow now, as slow as he had ever felt it; and he went down the hiss of Wind without steering, and let it lean where it leaned. He did not go round a second time. He stopped a long way out, where the leaning began, and stood there, and asked the going-along the one thing that he had come to ask.

*What is it that you feel?*

It felt the Wind. It felt the Wind lean. It felt the millstone, going by on the other hand, and the slow bellows drawing close in beside. It felt everything in him that went from one place to another.

*And there?*

Nothing came. It was not a silence, as he had been used to call it, since a silence is a thing that is heard. The tenth simply had no way of going where nothing went. He had the same nothing that he had had at noon, and that he had had three times before on other people's floors; and he knew it at last for what it was. He had laid a foot-rule against a smell.

He took his fingers off his wrist, and the going-along came home.

So it would stand. He could write it. He sat a little longer against the stone, in the last of the light, and discovered that being right about it was no comfort at all. He had read himself five times now with the only thing he owned that read. He had used it up.

A stick came down on the verge.

He had heard it a good way off, since he could have told that step from any other on the continent by now. It came along the bank from the fire at its own pace. It stopped a yard short of the post.

"I said a quarter of an hour," said Vastin. "I did not say when. If this is an ill time, you have only to tell me so."

"It's as good as another."

The old man looked at the bank, and he looked at it for longer than a bank deserved: it was two feet of turf, sloping, with a ditch below it on the road's side. Then he looked at his right leg, which stuck out before him in its linen and its two laths like a thing that belonged to somebody else.

"I have a matter to put before you," he said. "I find that I will not do it standing over you, since I have said a great many things to people from that height, and you were one of the people. So I am going to sit down. I tell you beforehand, since I shall not be able to get up again without you, and I had rather that you knew it now than learned it afterward."

He did not wait for an answer.

He turned his back to the bank. He set the stick's foot in the turf, a long way out, and took its head in both his raw hands. Then he let himself down by the strength of his arms, an inch at a time, with the bound leg held off the ground in front of him and the other bending under the whole of his weight. Cael saw the hands shake on the ash. He saw the left one, which did not open flat, slip a little, and take hold again.

The old man was down. He sat on the bank with one boot braced in the turf and the other leg stuck straight out over the ditch, and he was breathing as he had breathed at the top of the rise.

It was the first time that Cael had seen him lower than his own two feet since a cut on a moor, where he had gone down because nothing was left in him to stand with. This was not that. He had chosen it. A man who had stood for forty years at the upper end of every room had put himself where he could not rise without the arm of a boy whom the registry had no column for; and he had done it before he said a word, so that it could not afterward be taken back.

He laid the stick across his knees.

---

"I read your file," said Vastin.

He said it to the moor. They sat a yard apart, with the post between them, facing the same way.

"I read it for the first time in the week before I came up your bluff, on a road, by the candles of four inns, and for the last time on the morning when I left a seal in a tray. Between those two mornings I read it oftener than I have read anything that I did not write myself. I should like you to hear what was in it, since you have only ever seen it upside down."

"I've seen most of it."

"You have seen the sheets. I am speaking of what it was."

"It began with a day, and a word in square brackets, and after those two lines it was instruments: a registrar at a market town, with a counter and a pen; wardens, who came and stood in a yard and wrote; a panel; the frames of a house on a hill, at the end of every term, and the officers who watched the frames. Each of them laid upon you whatever he had been issued, and wrote down what it showed him, and what it showed him went under the last sheet and was sent up." The left hand moved on the stick. "There was not a paper in that folder which was not somebody's reading of you. I have handled a great many files, and I had not before met one in which the subject had been asked nothing and measured so often."

"They asked. They asked the same six things."

"They put six things to you and wrote down what the needle did. It is not the same."

Cael did not answer. He had sat across a counter at fourteen while a woman with a pen made a small mark beside a line, and he had learned his letters over again that year, the wrong way up, so that he should know what was being said of him.

"When the folder had grown as thick as my hand," said Vastin, "the seat did what it does when its instruments have not served. It sent the one that it keeps for such cases. It sent me. I came up that road with a plate, and a set of questions, and three days."

"Four feet off. At a table."

"Four feet. I measured it, as I measure everything." His voice had not altered at all, and it was that which made it hard to listen to. "I brought to those three days whatever forty years had given me: I have stood on eleven fields, and made something over nine thousand evaluations, and I can tell a man's rank from the way that he takes a stair, before he has reached the head of it. At the end of the third day I went down your hill with nothing whatever. The plate had lain as flat as a dish; every question had been answered, civilly, and I believe that every answer was true; and I could not have built one finding upon the whole of them. I had never in my life come away from a person without a figure."

"You were the instrument," Cael said. "I was the error."

Nothing more was said for a while. A cart went by on the road at their backs, late for the gate and hurrying, and its lantern threw the shadow of the post out across the ditch and drew it in again.

"I declined to read you a second time," said Vastin. "You know that. What you do not know is why."

"You told us. In a room at Ostrand, with five of us at a table." Cael kept his eyes on the moor. "You said there were three things you'd not do. That was the third. You said it was the only sentence you had left that they still needed."

"I did say so, and it was true. It was the reason, and you are not to think otherwise for anything that I say next. Five young people had laid a case on a table that hour, and shown me what the office I served did with honest readings; I would not lend it my name for one more. I have let that stand for the whole of the matter ever since. It is not the whole."

He lifted the stick an inch from his knees, and laid it down.

"The order would have stood you on a plate again, in a room, at an hour, under the standard protocol. My certificate was wanted upon the protocol, and I had the request on my desk for longer than any paper has lain there. I did with it what I do with anything: I set out what a second reading could come to, and there were two things."

"Say them."

"It could be silent, as the first was; and then there would be two silences upon one file, and both would stand under my name. An office does not file two silences. I have sat in the rooms where that is settled, and a second blank is no longer the instrument's failure: it is entered as a quality of the subject; a word is found for it; and the word is set at the head of every sheet that follows. I had lately learned what that particular office did with words."

"And the other?"

"The other was that it would light." He said it very evenly. "That the plate would wake under your hand as it wakes under any child's, and give you a tier, and a Path, and a paper to carry. I should like to be able to tell you that I hoped for that."

Cael turned his head.

The old man was not looking at him. He was looking at his own right leg, laid out before him like a plank.

"I did not hope for it. If that plate lit, then I had sat four feet from you for three days, with everything that I have, and had seen nothing of a thing that a sheet of brass could find in eleven seconds. I should have had to ask what my eyes were worth, and to ask it of nine thousand readings, each of which I had sworn to." His breath went out. "So I could afford neither answer: the one would have been turned upon you, and the other upon me. I weighed the pair of them for the better part of a night; and I found that it was the second which kept me from sleeping, and that a better man would have been kept awake by the first."

"You wrote *declined*."

"I wrote *declined*, on a form which has no line for why; and I should have written it if there had been no such night. The reason that I gave you at Ostrand was the reason. I do not take it back." He shut his eyes. "But when the pen was down I found that I was glad, and it was not on your account that I was glad. I was relieved that I need never learn what the plate would say. I had kept my hand off a door for a sound reason, and I was thankful of it for a poor one. I have been thought the better of since, by six people on a moor, for the half that I let them see. That is what I have carried from the seat to this bank, and it is not in the flat."

---

Cael sat with it.

He had wanted that answer for four days, since a board at Lowmarch, where he had kept the question behind his teeth and let a small pale woman ask hers. He saw now that he had wanted it for a great deal longer. He had turned it over at an academy, and on a bluff, and across two hundred and eighty days of country; and he had tried mercy in the place where the reason should go, and then policy, and then some long game of the seat's, and every one of them had been a thing done to him or for him. He had been given the true one in a borrowed room, and had never quite believed that it was all. It had not once occurred to him that what lay folded inside it might be a man's relief on his own account.

It ought to have made the man smaller, and he waited for it to do so.

It did not. There had been people in his life who had filed him because they were afraid, and each of them had called it procedure to the end. This one had done a right thing, for the reason that he gave. He had then walked three hundred miles on a bad knee and got down on a bank, where he could not rise, to say that he had also been glad of it for a wrong one. Nobody had asked him. Nobody living could have found it out.

"It kept me off the plate," said Cael.

"The refusal did, and I shall answer for the refusal, since it was honestly made. I shall not take credit for the gladness."

"I'm not offering any. I'm saying what happened."

"Then it is entered," said Vastin, "as what happened."

The light was nearly gone. Cael looked along the bank at a brown coat and a pair of bound hands, and found that there was a thing in his own keeping which he was going to give this man. It surprised him. He had meant it for the book, and after the book for Lira, who read the book. But he had been paid tonight in a coin that he had not handled before, and he owned exactly one piece of the same mint.

"You heard her at the fork," he said. "The healer. She told the fire that she'd had her hands on me a long while back, and found a thing, and made me write it down and leave it alone."

"I heard it. I did not understand it, and I did not ask."

"It was read again today. Twice. Once behind a hedge at a brook, and once here, against this stone, just before you came along."

The old head came round a little, and stopped. Cael watched the question arrive. He had seen a great many arrive in that face across a table, each in its order, and he knew the look of this one: it was the obvious next thing, and any officer living would have put it, and put it at once. *By whom?* The healer was two days north, with a mule. There was a boy on a bank who had lately had a hand on his own chest.

Vastin did not put it. His eyes went back to the moor.

He had done the same once before, on a bluff, with a notebook lying shut on a table between them and one more question plainly in reach. Cael had taken it then for a thing that the man had missed, and he knew better now: it had been looked at and left where it lay, and tonight it was being left again.

"I'll not tell you what read it, or how," said Cael. "That part's not only mine."

"I have not asked."

"No. I saw you not ask." He drew a breath. "It was read with the one thing I've got that reads, and it came back with nothing. I don't mean that it's empty in there; I mean nothing came back. What I read with can feel a thing that moves, and whatever's in that place doesn't move, so the reading's got no way to say a word about it. I've been writing that nothing down for the better part of a year as if it were an answer. It wasn't one. It was only the thing I read with, telling me where it stops."

Vastin had gone very still.

"The instrument is not at fault," he said slowly. "It has told you where its reach ends."

"That's it. That's near enough what I'd have written."

"It is a sentence of mine. I have said it at inquiries for forty years, about frames." The left hand opened as far as it could, and shut. "I wrote it once of a person, in an assessment which a certain seat declined to receive, as lying outside its schedule; and I did not suppose that I should hear it said back to me on a roadside by the person."

"I never saw that paper."

"No. Nobody did. It was the one honest reading of you that the Compact ever held, and it said that I could not make one."

"So you'd have had the first of your two," said Cael. "I can't swear it; a plate's not what I've got. But there's nothing in that place that moves, and I think a plate's the same sort of tool as mine. It would have lain flat, and I'd have been two blanks on one file, and they'd have found their word." "Nobody has that but you. It's not in the book yet. I've not told her, and she's read every line I ever wrote."

"Why is it given to me?"

"Because you gave me the other. I don't know any different way to pay for a thing like that." He stopped. "I'm not making it out to be more than it is."

"It is not more than it is," said Vastin. "I measured a person and could not read him, and tonight he has told me that he cannot read himself. I have had few friendships, and I know the look of them. This has not that look."

"No."

"It is a place where two people can stand. I have not had many of those, either."

It was not friendship, and Cael did not mistake it for that. A friend was a girl who sat on a bank with her back to a hedge and pulled the heads off grass. This was something flatter and harder, and he thought that it would bear more weight than it looked able to. Two people had each come to the end of an instrument, from opposite sides, and had met there. It was ground.

"I shall want your arm now," said Vastin. "I told you that I should."

Cael got up. He went round the post and stood on the slope below the old man, where the footing was, and put out his right arm, bent. A raw hand closed on it above the elbow. It was a good deal stronger than it looked. The stick went into the turf, and the whole long weight of the man came up through that grip in one pull, with a breath shut hard behind the teeth, and stood.

He did not let go at once.

"Thank you," he said. He had said it to Brom for a bowl, a dozen times, in the same two words. It did not sound the same.

Then he took his hand away, and found his balance on the ash, and went back along the bank toward the fire. He went slowly, with the bound leg swinging; and he carried himself, Cael thought, like somebody who had set down the heavier of two things.

---

He wrote it by the fire, with the book on his knee and Lira's shoulder a foot from his own.

He set the day at the head, since he could not write anything without it, and the place: the three-mile post. Then he put down what he owed, before what he had found, as an old man in Denvash had taught him.

*Lira asked whether it was the thing I was told to leave alone. I said beside it. I read a healer's order for the leave I wanted in it. I'd do it again, and she's to be told so at the mill, by me, before anything else is said. Round, both times. Not in. Nothing taken up to it.*

He looked at that, and it was true, and he let it stand.

The rest came more slowly. He had it in order now. He had said most of it aloud on a bank within the hour, to the last person living whom he would have expected to say it to, and he found that it had been easier to say than it was to write. A thing said to one man in the dusk could be walked away from. He knew what Hesk's book did with a sentence.

*Ran the reading on myself again. Same nothing at the center. I think the reading can't do it — you can't measure a stillness with a thing that only feels movement. I need an instrument for what doesn't move. Karis says there's a man in Treswick who reads how things are built. Maybe that's the instrument. Maybe I'm about to hand a stranger the one question I've never let anyone hold. Tomorrow. The walls are close enough now to have a color.*

He stopped there, with the pencil's point on the leaf, and read the second line from the end.

He had not known that he was afraid of it until the pencil said so. He had kept that question since he was fourteen, on a great many desks, the wrong way up. Registrars had reached for it, and wardens, and an Archmarshal with a plate; and a healer had found the edge of it with her two hands, on a mountain, and had been made to wait a long while for the rest. Every one of them had come to him, and he had never in his life carried it to anybody. And he was walking to a town, three miles off, to lay it down in front of a man whom he had not seen, on the word of a catalogue.

He wrote two lines more, since they were owed.

*V. sat. He told me the rest of why. I told him what the reading can't do, and nothing of what reads. What he said is his, and it isn't entered.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two. Myself, twice: not counted, as before.*

He turned the book round on his knee, and held it out.

Lira took it. She read as she always read, with her lips moving and her chin down, and she went through it twice. Then she laid her thumb on the leaf, beside one line, and did not say the line.

"You've let me hold your boots," she said. "And your ribs, on a mountain. And a rope with you on the wrong end of it."

"Not that."

"No. I know that." She shut the book over her thumb. "I never asked for it. I want that in there as well. I could have, and I'd have got it, and you'd have hated me for a month."

"A week."

"A month. I know you." She gave the book back. "Is he going to get it? This man in a hole?"

"I don't know. I don't know what he reads. I've a line off a catalogue."

"If he does, I'll not mind. I'd sooner somebody had it who could do something with it. I'd only have sat on it." She pulled her knees up, and looked at the fire and not at him. "And the old man had it before the book did."

"Not the question. What the reading can't do."

"I saw him go down on that bank. I've not seen him sit since a hound had his knee. I thought he'd fallen, and then I saw that you weren't moving, and I sat down again." She was quiet for a moment. "Did you pick up what he put down?"

"No. I put one down beside it."

"That's two things on a bank."

"It's what there was."

Across the fire Brom was showing Karis how a flint was held. She was attending as she attended to a stratum, and striking her own knuckle. Seln stood at the hedge's end. Vastin was on his feet in the gap, with both hands on his stick, looking at nothing; but he was looking at it, Cael thought, as a man looks who has for once no order that he is in the middle of drafting.

The town lay behind them. He could tell that it was there by the two carters, who sat on the other side of their own fire and faced it, as people face a hearth. There would be lights along the lowest wall by now, and in the gate towers. Higher up there would be one that was pale, and did not flicker.

He did not turn round. Neither did she. They sat with their backs to it, and their faces to a moor that the six of them had spent the whole day coming down, until the thorn had burned to a red heap and Brom said that somebody ought to sleep.

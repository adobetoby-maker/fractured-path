# Chapter 24 — Noon

Karis did not choose.

The day, when it came, was the two hundred and eighty-third. She had the second box unroped before there was any sun, and its lid off, and she knelt on the boards of the loft with fifty-two sheets in three heaps before her: eleven, and twenty-nine, and twelve. They were her own first copies, the ones that she had drawn on her knees under a ring of stones, and on a stair, and in a drowned hall, with a lantern held low. Cael had carried them a long way. He had not seen them out of their wrapper since a cold term.

"I had meant to take him the best," she said. "I lay awake over which they were: there are three from the Stair that came out clean, where the cutting was deep, and one from the Hall that I am proud of." She squared the third heap with the side of her hand. "And then I saw what I was doing, which was choosing what a man was to think before he had looked. If I show him my four best, I have shown him four things that a careful person can draw; so I shall take him all of it, with the bad sheets in their places."

"To a cook-house counter," said Brom.

"One, to the counter, since I am not a fool; and the rest under my arm, in their order, where he can see how thick the roll is."

She had a square of oilcloth for them, and a tape; and Seln watched her tie it, from the ladder's head, with a carrying-frame on his back that he had hired for the day and a keg lashed to the frame. He had been down to the boards before the lamp was out and had come back with a card in his cap, and the card's edge was buff.

"Lamp oil," he said, when Lira looked at it. "It is for a chandler inside the third wall: one hand, counted in at both gates and out again before the fourth bell. I shall have the better part of a forenoon up there, with a reason to be seen in every lane; and by tonight I shall know how a load goes to that yard."

"And us?"

"You will rope three boxes for a stair, as though you knew that there was one, and Brom will keep off that foot. At noon there will be two of you at the cook-house, and no more." He turned his eyes on Karis, and then on Cael. "One at the counter, who has something to show; and one in the wood-yard, with a bowl in his hand, who is nobody. The yard is where porters are fed, through a hatch, and its door stands open to the room for the heat, so that a person may hear from there and need not be seen."

"Why is it him?" said Lira. There was no complaint in it; she wished to have it right.

"Since he has asked," said Seln. "He asked me last night, on the ladder, when the rest of you were asleep."

Cael had. He had lain with a strange short word going round in him until the lantern was out, and then he had got up, and found a grey man sitting on a rung in the dark, as he had known that he would.

"I want to see him," he said now. "That's all it is. I've a line in the book about him and I've never seen his back, and I'd like to see how he looks at a thing before I'm in a hole with him."

Karis considered that, with her roll held against her chest, in both arms, like a child that she had been told not to drop.

"You may see his back," she said. "I should be obliged if he did not see your face. He is said not to care for being spoken to, and I do not know what he would make of being looked at."

"I'll look at the logs."

"You will look at him. I know you. Do it from behind something."

Vastin had been standing at the gable all this while, under the blanket, with a strap of the tarred case in his hands. He had found a place where the stitching had begun to go, and he was working at it with an awl that he had borrowed of the stable.

"A practitioner of the Bronze, in a class which the registry ranks beneath its notice, who has gone down by one door for longer than a porter can remember," he said, to the strap. "I certified this town on four occasions, and his name was on a roll that was laid before me each time, and I did not once ask who he was."

Nobody answered that, and he did not appear to want it answered.

---

The cook-house was built against the second wall, hard beside the gate, and its back was the wall itself.

Cael saw that from the wood-yard. It was a single long room, low, with a counter running down the far side of it; and behind the counter the pale stone went up bare into the smoke, with pots hung on iron pins that had been driven into its joints. Three keepers in blue coats were eating at a table by the street door. Along the counter stood clerks, and a groom or two, and a woman in a good cloak with a child.

The yard lay at the room's lower end, and it was only a space between the cook-house and the next building, roofed over with boards and stacked to the height of a man with split logs. There was a hatch in the wall, and a boy at the hatch, to whom Cael gave a copper, and from whom he had a bowl and half the copper back. A dozen porters stood about among the logs, eating on their feet; and he found a place by the doorpost, with a stack at his shoulder, where he could see the whole length of the counter end on.

The nearest man to him at that counter was not five strides off.

He stood at the very end of it, by the yard door, where the draught was, with his back to the room and his face to the stone. He was not tall. He had grey hair, cut short and badly, as a person cuts his own; and a coat that had been brown and was now the colour of dust, with dust worked into every seam of it, paler than any that the street could have put there. His shoulders were round; and there was a bowl in front of him, from which he was eating steadily, with his eyes shut.

Cael looked at that for some time. The man did not blink them shut and open them, as someone does who is tired. He kept them closed, and found the bowl with his spoon by habit, and chewed, and rested them as a carter rests a horse.

His hands were square, with the nails pared to the quick, and they were as pale as roots.

So this was Cleon. The name had been said at a spring, by Karis with one glove off, and asked after once; nobody had said it since. It sat on a dusty coat and a pair of shut eyes no better and no worse than a name ever sits on a stranger.

Nobody stood next to him. There was room for two at that end of the counter, and the clerks had left it, as the carters on a bench had made room for a keeper; but this was the other kind of room, and Cael knew it well. It was the space that people leave about a person whom they have long since stopped trying to talk to.

Then Karis came in by the street door.

She was very small in that doorway, with the light behind her, and a roll of oilcloth held against her chest. He saw her look down the room and find the dusty coat. She did not hurry. She came along behind the clerks with her chin level, as she had once walked up to a broker's plank with a hide; and she stopped at the counter's end, in the place that nobody took.

She did not speak. She undid the tape, and drew out the uppermost sheet, and laid it on the counter beside his bowl, and squared it to the counter's edge with one finger.

The spoon went on to his mouth, and came back.

It was the paper that he heard, Cael thought, or the breath of it settling. The grey head turned a little, and the eyes opened; and they were not as Cael had expected, since they were red at the rims, and wet, like the eyes of a man who has been all night over a small flame. They went to the sheet.

He did not read it.

Cael had watched a great many people read. He had watched registrars do it, and magisters, and a small pale woman who went along a line as a hen goes along a row of seed; and this man's eyes did not go along anything. They went to the foot of the sheet first, and stayed there, and then they climbed, as Brom's had climbed a pier on a river bank, from the footing upward, stopping at whatever seemed to be holding weight. He might have been looking at a wall.

It took him no longer than it takes to swallow, and then he laid the spoon down.

"Off floors," Cleon said.

"Fused. Grey. No join. Three of them. I copied every mark on all three. Fifty-two sheets," Karis said.

"Fifty-two sheets," Cleon said.

The eyes had gone back to the sheet. One square pale finger came out, and did not touch it; it moved along above a row of her drawing, an inch off the paper, and stopped.

"Rubbed, or drawn?" His voice was rough, as a hinge is that is seldom used.

"Drawn: by eye, with a rule, squared to a hand's breadth. I had no paper wide enough to rub, and in two of the three places the cutting was too shallow to take wax."

"What light?"

"A lantern, held a foot from the stone, low, and from the left."

"Always the left?"

"On every sheet, so that every shadow in all of them should fall the same way, and a person who came after me might subtract it."

He grunted. It was a short sound, down in the chest; and Cael, who had spent three days across a table from an Archmarshal, knew approval when it was trying not to be heard.

"Which edge is north?"

"The top. I took it from a needle each time, and it is written in the corner."

"I saw it was written. I asked if it was so." The finger moved. "Three, you said. Far apart?"

"Many days. The same forty-one kinds of sign are on all three, and they stand in one order." Her voice had gone thin, and she steadied it; he heard her do it. "I cannot date stone, but I can date what lies upon it. One of those floors is beneath a ring that nobody remembers raising, and one under a stair, and one under a hall that has a marsh grown over its roof; and I have laid them against every stratum of paper that I know, and they are underneath all of it."

"Under the Compact's."

"Under what the Compact found when it came. Under that, again."

He was silent, and he had not looked at her yet; he had looked at nothing in that room but a sheet of paper, since his eyes opened.

"And you want me to say what's written," he said at last. There was a great weariness in it. "They come every few years, with a rubbing off a lintel. They want it read to them."

"No," said Karis.

She had both hands on the roll, and they were white.

"I shall find out what it says, since that is my work, and I have never asked anybody to do it for me. I have come to ask you how it stands. I have forty-one kinds of sign, and I do not know which of them is carrying the others; and I am told that there is one person west of the Line who could look at a thing and tell me."

The grey head came round.

He looked at her then, for the first time, as he had looked at the sheet: from the boots up.

---

"Not here," he said.

He had picked up his spoon again, and he spoke between mouthfuls, to the wall, in short pieces; and Cael, by the doorpost, with a bowl going cold in his hand, understood that he was hearing a list that had been ready for a very long time.

"I don't read on a counter. There's gravy on it. That's the first."

"I had not supposed that you would."

"All of them. Not the ones you like. I'll not be shown a choice."

"You will not be. The bad ones are in their places."

The spoon stopped for the space of a breath, and went on.

"They come down to me. I don't come up. I've come up as far as this bowl at noon since before those hinges were hung, and it's as far as I come." He jerked his head, without looking, toward the gate. "What is there to carry?"

"Three boxes. A surveyor's case. What I have under my arms."

"Then it's a stair's worth." He felt in the dusty coat, and brought out a square of stiff card, the size of a man's palm, with a buff edge and a seal pressed into one corner, old, and rubbed nearly flat. He reached across the counter without asking, and took the pen that the cook kept for his tallies, and wrote three short lines with his face close to the card, and blew on them.

"That's tomorrow's. It's got the day on it. It's no good the day after, and no good this afternoon. Second bell. You go where loads go, and you give that to the man at the yard door, and you say nothing to him that he doesn't ask for."

"And if he asks?"

"He's not asked me anything in a long while. I can't speak for what he'll do with somebody new." He pushed the card along the counter with one finger. "No names. I don't want yours. If I've got it, I can be asked for it, and I've been asked for things before. You'll not have mine off my own tongue, either. It's on the card. That's as near as it comes to being said."

Karis took the card, and held it by its edges.

"You'll not tell me what you think they are."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Not a word. Not what they're for, and not who you reckon made them, and not how old. Not till I've looked." For the first time there was something in the rough voice besides weariness. "I've had half my life of people telling me what a thing is before I've seen how it's put together. And then I can't see it. I see what I was told. You'll keep your mouth shut on it till I say."

"That," said Karis, "I can do. I have had a good deal of practice."

"One lamp goes down with you. Mine. Whoever holds it, holds it where I tell them. No other carried light, and no other fire of anybody's, however it's made." He looked at her hands on the roll, briefly, and Cael could not tell what he saw there. "There's paper under that town that's waited longer than the town has for somebody to drop a spark on it. It's not going to be one of yours."

"No."

"That's the lot."

"I agree to all of it."

"I didn't ask if you agreed. I told you what they were."

He put a coin on the counter. With the same finger he pushed her sheet back toward her, a hand's breadth, without having once laid his skin on it.

"Bring that with the rest," he said. "In its place."

He turned, and went up the room toward the street door.

He passed within two strides of the yard, and of a boy of nineteen who stood against a doorpost with a bowl; and his red eyes did not come that way at all. They were on the floor, and they went along the flags, a joint at a time, as though he were reading how those had been laid as well.

Cael had wished to be nobody in that yard, and he had been, and he ought to have been the easier for it. Some part of him had wanted the head to turn. He set it by, to be gone over later.

The man went out by the street door. He went a full stride beyond it, onto the paving, into the noon, with one hand coming up already to shade his eyes.

There he stopped.

It was not the stop of someone who has left his change behind, which comes at the door, with a hand going to a pocket. This came a step late, as a cart stops after the horse has; and he stood with his back to the room, quite still, for as long as it takes to draw three breaths, while a porter with a frame went round him.

He did not turn; his shoulders came up a little, and went down.

Then he went on toward the blue coats, a good deal faster than he had come.

Cael knew what he had seen. He had watched Karis do the same thing at a bridge the morning before, so short that he had nearly walked a box into her. It was what a body did when its legs had gone on with the day, and the rest of it had only then taken in what had been set before it.

---

She did not say a word the whole way down the hill.

She went up the ladder with the roll in one arm and the card in her teeth. She sat down on the nearest pallet, and took the card out of her mouth, and looked at it; and Cael saw that she had begun to shake, a little, and all over, as a person does who has come in out of the cold.

"He did not ask my name," she said.

Brom had got up off the boxes.

"He did not ask what I thought. I have been waiting for somebody to ask me that for as long as I can remember, and the people who asked did not believe it, and I had grown used to that. I had it ready, all of it, in order, with the evidence under each head." She laid the card on her knee. "And he would not have it. He did not disbelieve me; he simply would not hear it yet, and I do not know what a person does with that."

"Eats," said Brom.

He had been across the yard already. He had a bowl in his hand, from the hatch, with a spoon standing in it, and he set it on the boards between her boots. Then he stood there, as he had told a healer at a fork that he would.

Karis looked at the bowl. She looked up at a great deal of Brom.

"You are going to remain there."

"Till it's empty."

"I see." She picked up the spoon. "It appears that I am under several people's terms today."

Lira had the card, since she had lifted it off Karis's knee by its edges, as it had been held, and carried it to where the light came round the blanket.

"*Matter*," she read, with her lips. "*Its bearer, one. Hands—*" She stopped. "There's a figure. And his name, at the top, and a rank, and a number as long as my arm. And a day." She turned it over. The back was blank. "That's all that there is. That'd take five people through the buff gate and up to the top of everything. And it's not got one of them on it."

He had read the card's head across her thumb, the wrong way up, as he read anything. It was five letters, in a blunt square hand, above a Path and a tier; he had heard that name once, and had thought it at a counter, and he had not before seen it written. She had not read it out, and he found that he did not care to either.

"It's got how many," said Brom.

"It's got how many." She was quiet for a moment, holding it. "I've a paper in this coat that would walk me through the blue on my own two feet, with a keeper reading my name out like a herald. I worked for that paper harder than I've worked for anything." She did not touch the pocket. "And this one's a scrap of card with a number, and it goes higher."

"Which would you sooner have?" said Cael.

"I'm thinking about it. Don't push."

He did not push. He had seen her look at a blue coat from a smith's yard, the day before, with both her hands held still; and he thought that whatever she was working at had not finished with her yet.

Seln came up the ladder a little before dusk, with lamp oil on his sleeve and no frame.

He did not sit; he took a straw from the nearest pallet, and went down on his heels in the middle of the loft, and drew in the dust of the boards while they stood round him.

"The third gate," he said, and made a stroke. "From it, the street goes on, straight, and opens into the square within eighty yards, and that is the way which a person is told." He drew it, and then he laid the straw across it. "We do not take that. Under the wall, on the right hand as you come through, there is a lane, which loads use; and a keeper will point you to it if he is asked where loads go, and I should let him."

"And then a road round," said Brom.

"There are two. Carts go to the left, by a street that runs the whole way about the crown, half a mile, to the yard's lower gate; and half way along it there is a bar, and a beam, where every load is weighed, and the weight is written against an owner." His eyes came up. "It is a book."

"And the right?"

"The lane runs under the wall for two hundred paces, and there is no bar on it, since no cart could use what is at its end." He drew five short strokes, one below another, like the rungs of a ladder. "It is a cut, between two buildings, that goes down the back of the bluff to the same yard: a stair of five flights, which I made twenty-two steps. It is a stride and a half wide, and the walls on either hand are three men high and have no door in them, nor any window. Nobody stands in it. I went down it with a keg, and up again empty, and I did not meet a soul."

"Stairs," said Brom. He was looking at a bound leg.

"I have been down stairs before," said Vastin.

"Twenty-two. Down. With a knee that doesn't bend."

"I shall take them as they come. I should prefer that to being weighed."

Nobody said who was to carry. There was a figure on that card, and three boxes had three backs under them; and there was a tarred case, and a man with a straw in his fingers who went ahead of every load and had never yet been counted with one. Cael marked it, as he marked anything that people took care to step round. It could be left until morning.

---

He wrote the day by the lantern, when the pot had gone round.

*Two hundred and eighty-third day. Treswick, the district under the first wall. A hundred and thirty-six. Saw the man, from a wood-yard, at five strides. He looked at one sheet for as long as it takes to swallow, and he did not read it as anything of mine has ever been read: he asked it nothing, and he started at the bottom. He wouldn't hear what she thinks they are, and he wouldn't have a name, and he never looked at the yard. I'd wanted that, and I find I'd wanted the other as well. Entered.*

*S. has a way to the yard that crosses nothing I've written about. A stair. Tomorrow, the second bell, on a card with a figure on it.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

Shadow had lifted while he wrote, and he had told it no without raising his pencil.

He read the entry over, and it was thinner than what he had seen. Every reading that had ever been made of him had begun at the top, with a name, and a day, and a word, and had gone on down the sheet looking for whatever would bear the word out. At a cook-house counter he had watched a pair of sore eyes begin at the foot of a drawing, where the weight came down, and climb, and ask the paper for nothing that it had not offered. He did not know whether a person could be looked at so. He did not know whether he wished to be.

The loft was quiet. Brom slept as he always slept, like something felled, with seven bowls stacked by his head, and Lira lay on her side with her back to the gable. Vastin stood at it, since he would not lie down while anybody else was awake, and the awl was still going in his fingers, in and out of a strap, by feel.

Karis had not lain down either. She sat against the boxes, with the roll across her knees and both her hands flat on it.

Cael shut the book, and went and sat on the boards beside her.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I should. I have been told so by a healer, in front of witnesses." She did not look round. "I walked into a cook-house today with fifty-two sheets, and a man looked at one of them and stopped eating; and I have not been so happy since I was a child, and I am not able to tell it from terror. I did not know that they could be the same."

"He stopped on the paving, after. A step outside the door. You'd gone to pick up your sheet."

Her head came round.

"He stopped?"

"As you did at the bridge. He stood for three breaths, and then he went up that hill like somebody who's left a pot on."

She took that in, and he watched it go into her, slowly, like water into dry ground; and her hands closed on the oilcloth.

"Thank you," she said. "I should not have known that. He would never have told me."

"No. I don't think he tells."

They sat, and below them a horse shifted its weight from one hip to the other, and sighed.

"I asked you into this," said Karis, "and I have not forgotten it for an hour. If he looks at all three floors tomorrow and tells me that I have fifty-two sheets of a mason's pattern-book, then I have walked you under a station for it."

"You asked. I took a slip. It was my hand on the paper."

"That is generous, and it is not quite true."

"It's true enough to sleep on."

"You are frightened of him," she said.

It was not a question. She had said it as she would have read a date off a seal.

"Yes."

"So am I. I do not think that it is of the same thing."

"No. You're afraid that he'll look at them and say you're wrong."

"And you?"

He had not meant to say it. But he had told one thing on a bank, two nights since, to a man who had earned it, and he had found that the telling had not killed him.

"I'm afraid that he'll look up," said Cael.

She did not ask him what he meant. She was the one person he knew who never asked for the sentence after the one that she had been given. She only moved the roll, a little, so that the end of it lay across his knee as well as her own; and they sat so, under a slate roof, with fifty-two sheets between them, until the lantern guttered and Vastin at last put down the awl.

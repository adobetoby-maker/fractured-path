# Chapter 43 — Here

There were sheep on the high moor, and that was the first thing which told Cael that the country had changed while they were under a town.

He saw them at the third mile on the two hundred and ninety-sixth morning: a scatter of grey backs on the swell ahead, with their heads down in the bents and the east wind laying their wool over. There had been none on that ground sixteen days ago. A flock is not turned out where eight things lie up round a hole in the hill.

At the fourth mile the ruts began to draw in toward a black place in the rock, and a spare grey person stood beside them with a crook.

It was the factor. There was the leather apron, and the slate on its cord, and the pair of pale eyes that had gone along six people and a healer at a gate and counted them. They counted now. They went from a quiet man, who had come up level with the cobs, to four with loads; and they rested last upon the tail-board, where a pair of raw hands lay on an ash stick.

"Five," said the factor. "And the one that walked behind. He's not behind today."

"He's paid for where he's sitting," said Brom.

"I'd not doubt it. He paid my woman a copper to be told where the straw was." The crook went out toward the moor. "Those are mine. I've not had a ewe on this top since the first frost, and I'd three hundred lambed in the yard for want of it. I've had thirty-one carts up that cut in a fortnight, where I told you thirty. The salting-shed's full to the door."

Nobody answered, since it was a road. But they were at the lip of a hold's own ground, with the hold's keeper speaking, and Cael judged that the rule would bear a low voice there, as it had borne one in that yard.

"We're glad of it," he said.

"I didn't come up onto this top to make you glad. I've a thing to put to you, and I'll put it once, since I was told beforehand what I'd hear." The pale eyes had come to Brom, who had the purse. "That seep'll have its glass back at the first frost. Another den'll find it, in a year, or in five. I said so on that bank with your wet one standing by. I'd as soon not sit in my yard and wait to learn which."

"What's the offer?" said Brom.

"Twenty, at each first frost, on the same bar, to the five that walk it before I let a wheel on. The long house's fire for the week that it takes, and the pot." The crook's foot came round, without haste, and pointed at the tail-board. "And for him, a seat at that fire for the whole of a cold term, every year that he cares to come, with nothing asked of him but to be within a shout of my road. I'll not say what for. I was on the lip."

The old man had not stirred. He looked at the crook's foot as he had once looked at a milking stool, with grave attention, and it was plain that he did not mean to answer for a thing which had been put to another person's purse.

Brom priced it. Cael watched him do it, with his head a little on one side and his weight on both feet, as he priced any wall.

"It's a fair wage," he said. "It's a better one than the first, though it's the same twenty; you didn't know the first time that it could be done." He rubbed his jaw with the back of his wrist. "We're walking east. There's a line that we've undertaken to follow, and it goes clean away from your frost, and I can't tell you which side of a cold term it ends on. I'll not take a hold's silver against a week that I can't swear to be standing in."

The factor nodded, once, like a person who has heard a figure read back correctly.

"He said you'd say that."

"Who did?" said Lira.

"Him that's first on the one-armed man's cloth. He's leather on his wrist." The slate was turned over, where a column of strokes stood. "He brought my salt up himself, behind the first of the carts, with three of his own. He asked me what I'd seen from that lip; and when I'd told him, he asked it again, which nobody ever did before. Then I said that I meant to keep the five that had done it, if silver would keep them. He said that they were for the east. He said I might offer, and welcome, and that I'd have it back in very nearly those words."

Cael heard that with a small cold shock, as a person hears his own step counted by somebody behind him.

"What did he ask you twice?"

"How long the thing stood, that the eight ran into. And whether the old man lifted a foot while it did." The pale eyes went to the tail-board, and stayed. "I told him what I saw. It stood for as long as he never moved; and he never moved. I said that the man who made it called himself a fool for the place he'd made it in. He didn't seem to take the fool's word for that."

"I had hoped that he would," said Vastin.

It was low, and exact, and it was the only thing that he said.

---

The dray went into the cut first, with its wheels in the water.

There was no glass. A fortnight and more of thaw had come up that hill since a stick cracked it, and the seep ran down the middle of the road now as a seep ought, a stride wide and a finger deep, talking over the stones. The lean man had his near cob by the cheek-strap. He walked it down on the wet with its head held, and the other followed, as beasts had been walked down that place all through a cold term by people who had the nerve.

The six went after on the dry edge, in file.

Cael had gone up this strip of gravel with a twist of tow in his fist and his mouth as dry as the tow. He had come down it afterward at a walk, a little bent, with eight things pouring over a bank behind him. He knew every yard of it. The black rock stood at the head, with the water coming out under it in a skin; the scree hung on his right hand like a steep roof, where a girl had lain on plates; and on his left the bank was no higher than his belt, with three worn places in its upper half.

It was the same cut. It was not the place that he had been afraid in, and he could not at first have said what was different. Then he had it. The cold was gone out of it. On that day the air had come up to meet him like the breath of a cellar, and now it was only the shade of a hill.

Lira went down it with her face turned up to the scree. She had lain on those plates with a knife, on a day when the left arm could do no more than hold. He saw her mark the place, and shut that hand once at her side, and look away. Karis did not look at the banks at all. She was watching the water go under the dray's wheels, with her lips moving, like a person who has written six findings about a thing and suspects that there is a seventh.

Behind him the stick's sound had stopped.

Vastin had got down at the head, as he had undertaken to do for every slope. He was coming down the dry edge last of all, a tread at a time, with the bound leg swung out stiff; and forty strides from the top he was standing still.

There was nothing there to stop for. Wet stones lay in the road, and gravel beside them, with a tuft of dead bent growing out of it. No crack ran from bank to bank. That had been made in ice, and it had gone where the ice went.

It was the place. Cael knew it by Brom's stone, three strides below, and by the lie of the two banks; there was no other sign of it in the world. A plane had stood on that spot from one bank to the other, as high as the scree, and a man had been the foot of it.

The old man looked at the wet stones for the space of several breaths.

Then he lifted the stick.

He did not lift it far. The ash came up off the gravel by the breadth of a hand, and was held there, level, in his right fist; and the whole of him came over onto the bound leg, as a load is let down onto a prop that nobody is yet sure of. Cael saw the slat take it. He saw the grey mouth shut hard. It was one breath, and the half of another.

The stick came down.

The knee had held. It was not mended; anybody could have seen that, by the sweat that had sprung on the old forehead in that cold shade. But it had borne a man once, with nothing beside it, on the very ground where eight hounds had been stopped by it and where four holes had been torn in it afterward. He had not been told to try it. He had not asked leave of anybody; and Cael noticed that for once he did not turn to see who would enter the thing.

Brom had turned on his stone, below. He had put out one flat hand, without knowing that he did it, as a man steadies the foot of a ladder that somebody else is on.

"Noted," Cael said.

He had given that word to a captain three times, at a hearth and on a landing. He had never before given it to this man. It was a clerk's word, and he had kept it from him as a person keeps his purse from somebody who once had the counting of it.

It did not feel like a clerk's word on that road. It felt like a stroke made at the foot of a slip.

Lira's two fingers had gone up, below, where the dray's tail was going out into the light. They went on down.

---

He stopped once more in that cut, and it was not for the old man.

It was a stride farther down, level with the middle of the road, where the water ran now. Brom had gone by his own flat stone without a look, since he was not a person who visited places. But Cael had stood in that road on the two hundred and seventy-ninth day, behind the middle of an invisible thing, with his face two feet from it. He could not have lifted a hand there, while eight broke on what was in front of him.

*This far?* said the eleventh.

It had asked that of every sill and kerb and milestone for six days. It had never asked as it asked now. It had the voice of the place in it. There were two banks, and the gap between them, and one line where a line had been proved; and the thing under his breastbone knew that ground as a key knows its own ward.

He had stood two feet from that wall, and nothing had come of it. He had written so in a byre loft. *Ten.* He had not understood then why not, and he thought that he did now. A thing came to him when somebody meant it, with the whole of himself, at something that could mean it back. On this ground the man had stood a wall before hounds, on his own judgment, for a rule that he did not yet believe in. In a stair he had stood one before six people with hoods, for nobody's rule, with nothing behind him but the choosing; and it was there that the thing had come.

*No*, he told it.

It lay down. It did not sulk. But he felt the weight of it as he went on, as he would have felt a coin in his boot; and he knew whose coin it was. He had six people's leave to find before he could hand it back.

The three carts were gone from the foot.

Somebody had broken them up for their timber. A single wheel leaned against the bank's end, with two spokes out of it, and there was a black ring on the turf where the staves of a barrel had been burned. A cairn stood beyond, where the foot-track left the ruts and went up the spur to a wall with smoke behind it. The factor had gone back along the lip by the sheep-walk. Nobody had been asked up to the pot, and nobody had expected to be; a hold that has been told no does its civilities afterward by leaving a person alone.

Seln's fist came up at the cairn.

It did not go out to the side, where a hound would have been. It went back, over his shoulder, toward the hill that they had come down from. Cael looked where it went.

The cut was a black notch in the spur behind them, with the road coming out of it. Above the notch the shoulder of the moor stood against a grey sky; and on that line, a long way off and quite small, there was a man on a horse.

He was not moving. He sat where the ruts came over the skyline, and it was not possible at that distance to tell which way he faced. He wore something dark, and the horse was a bay or a brown; a mile of grey air took the rest. Then the horse put its head down to the bents.

The fist opened, and lay flat. They stood. A carter stood at his cob's head as a carter does, and an old man on a tail-board looked at his own knees.

Nothing came of it. It was a rider on a road, on a morning when carts were using that road again, behind six people who had no quarrel with any horse. After the space of forty breaths Seln lifted two fingers, and the file went on round the foot of the spur. But the small grey man had let it come up to him, which he never did; and as Cael went by he saw the right hand go out at the hip and turn over, palm upward, not empty but asking.

*Nothing?*

Cael did not know. He made a fist of his own, low, where it could be seen: *seen*. It was all that either of them had.

They did nine miles that afternoon, down off the shoulder, with the wind at last a little on the quarter. The moor fell away in long steps toward the east. There was a beck across the ruts in a spread of wet stones, where a grey thing had once walked beside them for half a mile and stopped as if at a wall; the cobs drank there, and nothing paced them. Twice Cael turned his head by the width of a hand. The first time there was only heather. The second time the horseman was on the last rise but one, a mile behind, coming on at exactly the pace of a dray.

---

The halting-ground lay in its fold beside the beck, with a dozen rings of stones on it, and there was ash in three of the rings that had not been there before.

Carters had lain on it since the cut was opened. They had not lit fires; the black was old, and the white was only what a wind had turned over. But somebody had left a bundle of dry heather under a flat stone for the next comer's bed, and a crust for the birds.

They ate by the same dead ring as before. Cael marked who sat where, since it was the kind of thing that he marked.

The old man sat.

He did it without a word, on the stone that he had stood beside on the two hundred and seventy-eighth night with his bread in his hand. He let himself down by the stick and one palm, with the bound leg out before him across the ring; and when he was down he looked round the six faces, in the order that he kept, as though he expected the thing to be remarked.

"You sat," said Lira, under her breath.

"I did. I have sat on a board in front of six people for two days, going backward, at a copper a day. I find that the ground is no farther off, and that it is free."

"You'll have to get up again. In front of us. With that."

"I am aware of it. I have been got up by one or another of you a good many times since a stair, and I have not yet been charged for it." He laid the stick across his thighs. "I am told that a road has no keeper."

Brom had been ladling cold barley into the seventh bowl. He stopped with the ladle lifted, and a slow look went over his face.

"It hasn't," he said, and put the bowl into the raw hands.

No copper was laid upon a stone that night.

Seln had not sat. He stood at the fold's upper end, where he could see the ruts in both directions, and he spoke when the bowls were empty, at the pitch that the dusk allowed.

"There is a horse and a man. He kept a mile from us for the whole of the afternoon, as nearly as I could pace it against a dray. He did not come into the cut while we were in it. He came through afterward, at a walk." The hands were in the sleeves. "I did not see his boots. I should not have known them at a mile."

"A hold's man," said Brom. "Or a carter's lad sent on for the boat."

"He may be either. A hold's man does not ride a mile behind a dray for nine miles when he could be past it in a quarter of an hour. A lad sent for a boat does not stop when a file stops." Seln looked down the fold. "I do not say that it is the same man. I say that I told you a road would be watched, and that somebody is on it who behaves as a watcher would. I can put it no higher, and I take no comfort from how low I must put it."

"What does he learn?" said Cael.

"How many. Which way. That one of you rides, and five carry, and that there is rope on the cart. That you stop at a cairn and do not go up to a gate." He was silent a moment. "It is very little, and it is exact. I have sold less."

Lira had been watching the old man across the ring with her chin on her knee.

Her right hand came out, flat, palm to the ground.

It was done without warning, as she had done it to him on this same road, walking backward down the ruts. Every person at that ring went still, since that was what the hand meant; a big man stopped with his thumb in a bowl. But she was not looking at any of them.

Vastin had not risen. He could not have, in the time. He had let his shoulders come forward, and his head hang, and both hands go loose on the ash across his knees. He looked like an old carter who has nodded off on a stone after his supper. There was nothing upright in him anywhere.

She turned the hand over, empty.

"That's bent," she said. "You said you didn't know that you could do it bent."

"I had it from a girl on this road. She told me that a post is a thing which nobody looks at, and that she had looked at me first of everything on the moor." He straightened by degrees. "I have since been a hand on a card, and matter on a cart, and I was not looked at for eleven days by a town that used to send a horse to its bridge for me. I find that I have the way of it."

"You've the way of sitting," said Lira. "It's a start."

Cael wrote by feel, on his knee, when the light was gone.

*Two hundred and ninety-sixth day. The halting-ground in the fold; thirteen miles. The cut walked east. Water in it, and no glass, and sheep on the top. Vastin walked it: seventeen of thirty. He stood on the leg once, with the stick off the ground, where the line had been. The factor offered twenty at each first frost, and a seat for the old man at the hold's fire. Brom said no. A hundred and twenty-eight.*

*Known, twice. The factor had been told what we should answer, by Teague, who took the salt up; he asked how long the wall stood and whether its maker moved. It was noon of the two hundred and ninety-seventh day when the road came down through the alders, and the river lay beyond them, wide and brown, with the cable swung across it.

It had fallen two feet since they left it. The lowest plank of the landing was dry. Up the bank the long stack lay under its tarred sheets, and a brisk man with ink on his cuffs was walking along it with his clerk; and when he saw two brown cobs come out of the trees, with forty coils behind them, he began to walk a good deal faster.

Then he saw who walked beside the near wheel, and he stopped.

"That's him," said the lean man, quite loud, to nobody. "That's the wrong kind."

Brom had gone a dull red again. He kept his eyes on the sheet. Cael and Lira had the lashings off before the wheels had stopped, and they drew out the one coil that was not the broker's, and ran a stave through its eye. It came off between them like a dead calf. It was the weight of half a man, as it had been called.

"I've four," said the broker. He had come up, in spite of himself. "Round a heart. Forty coils, and more laying."

"I see that you have. It's good four. I looked at it on the peg." Brom said it gravely, and he meant it. "I'd have had mine of you a fortnight since, and been glad."

"You'll not want any now."

"I've what I was asked for. I'd not have said a word against the other, to anybody, if I'd known that it would be carried about."

The broker opened his mouth, and found nothing in it. Somewhere down the landing a lad laughed. The lean man took the straw out of his own mouth and held up one open hand to the six, as carters did; and it was all the leave that he took of them, after three days and forty-odd miles.

Vastin got down off the tail-board for the last time, and paid his third copper.

The ferry was at the planks. She had come over for the afternoon's carts, set a little aslant on her two straps, and the ferryman stood at her rail with a long sour face and watched a coil of rope carried down a bank toward him on a stave.

They laid it on the planks at his feet.

He did not speak. He went down on one knee, which Cael had not seen him do for anything; and he took the rope's end between finger and thumb, and bent it back on itself, and opened the lay with his nail. He looked in for some time. Then he drew a fathom through his two hands, slowly, with his eyes shut, as a woman feels a cloth.

"That's four," he said.

"Round a heart," said Brom. "Sixty yards. I paced it off against her wall."

"Where's it from?"

"Treswick. The walk under the town wall."

"I know the walk. She lays tight." The ferryman got up. He looked along his own hauling rope, where it came in over the sheave from the oak post, grey and fretted; and Cael remembered that two yarns had been gone from it since a flood. "I've crossed on that since the thaw, forty times, with my heart in my mouth and a turn of old line for a stern strap. I lent you a rope before the snow. I said what I wanted back, and I never looked to have it."

"It's what you asked for."

"It is." He stooped, and took the coil by its eye in one hand, and dragged it up the planks toward his rope-house without help. He said the rest over his shoulder. "She's a copper a head. Not today. Today she's paid."

It was the nearest that Cael had ever heard that man come to thanking anybody, and it was a good deal nearer than he had come for a strap held with a back.

They went over with the carts. Seln stood at the stern and watched the western bank grow small.

"He has not come down to the landing," he said. "There is a horse tied in the alders, above the last bend. It has no rider on it. I do not know where the rider is."

Nobody answered, since there was nothing to be done about a horse in a wood.

Lowmarch came up out of the dusk on the eastern side as it had come up on the first evening that Cael ever saw it. But the meadow was not as he had left it. There had been nineteen fires along that water on the night before the first boat. There were more than forty now; the holds had opened, and every crew that had lain up for the cold was down to hear what the plank would say. Carts stood wheel to wheel by the smith's rail. There was a light in every window of the lower inn.

He counted the meadow, since he counted anything.

Then his eyes went up the bank, to the house on the rise. It had one window on the river side. There was a lamp in it; and against the lamp, for a moment, before the scow's bow touched, he saw the shape of a man who was standing quite still, with his thumbs in his belt.

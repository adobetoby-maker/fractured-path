# Chapter 27 — The Middle Gallery

The lamp was put into Lira's hand before she had thought to ask for it.

It was done as a thing is passed over a counter. The man in the dusty coat had shot the bar home behind them, a slide of black iron as long as his arm, with the flat of his palm; and he turned from it, and found a free hand at the height of his own, and hung the lamp's ring upon its fingers. He did not look to see whose hand it was.

"Twenty-seven," he said. "Three nines. I'll thank you not to count them aloud."

Then he went down the stair ahead of them, into the dark, with no light at all.

Cael stood on the first tread and listened to him go. The steps were quick and flat and perfectly even, with never a hand put out to the wall, and they grew small as a dropped stone grows small in a well. A man who has gone up and down one stair for the better part of his life does not need to see it.

"He's left us," said Brom.

"He has left us his lamp," said Karis. Her voice was not quite her own. "It was in his terms. I think that we are meant to bring it."

They went down in the order that the stair allowed. Lira went first, with the lamp held low at her knee, as she had seen it held; and the light went before her boots in a small yellow round, three treads at a time, and showed nothing beyond them. Karis followed with her bare hand on the wall. Brom came after her, bent under his box, since the roof of that stair had been cut for shorter people.

The stick came last but one. Cael went below it, backward, as he had gone down a slot in the open air an hour ago, with his heels feeling for each tread and his palms on the stone at either side.

"You need not," said Vastin. "These are dry."

"I know they are. I'm here all the same."

"It was an observation, and not a complaint."

The treads were of the dark stone, and so were the walls; each block lay against its neighbour without mortar, so close that his fingers could not find the joint until they had passed it. Nothing had worn them. A sill above had been rubbed bright by more feet than he could reckon, and here, a dozen steps beneath it, the edges were as sharp as on the day they were cut. Few of those feet had come farther than the door.

At the ninth step there was a landing, and at the eighteenth another. Lira did not count aloud. He saw her lips go, in the lamp's edge.

The cold deepened with every flight. He had felt cold in a good many places since the Line: on a mountain, and on a river that had turned into a road, and in a shed with the hurdles tied up against the wind. All of those had been moving. This one stood. It lay on the stair like water in a cistern, and when he went down into it he felt it close over his knees, and his belt, and his collar, a tread at a time. The flame in the lamp did not lean. It stood up as straight as a painted one.

*There?* he waited to hear, from the thin held note; and he did not hear it.

He marked that. Anchor had asked at every sill and gate since Ostrand, and at an iron door it had asked very low. It asked nothing here. There was nothing in that place to set a bar across, and the fragment seemed to know so before he did.

"Twenty-seven," said Vastin, on the floor. He was breathing through his nose, and he stood for a moment with both hands on the ash and the bound leg thrust out. "He can count as well. I had begun to think it a rare accomplishment in this town."

They were in a passage that ran across the stair's foot, a dozen strides from end to end. The dark stone lined it. Where the lining stopped, on the far side, there were three openings, side by side, each as broad as a cart and half as high again as Brom; and each was a square of perfect black.

Karis had gone straight to the nearest. She laid her palm on the place where the dark blocks ended, and then on what came after them, and Cael saw her go quite still.

"It is the bluff," she said. "Look. It is pale, and it lies in beds, and it has been cut with a pick; there are the strokes." Her fingers moved. "And the dark has been built up against it afterward, block by block, to make a doorway of a hole. So the hole was here first."

"Later," said Brom. He said it as he had said it in the yard, gently, and he was looking at the three mouths. "Which one did he go down?"

Nobody could have told by listening, for there was nothing to hear. It was Lira who found it. She had lifted the lamp, and lowered it again at once, as though the man could have seen her from wherever he was; and then she put it behind her back altogether, so that her own body shut the light off.

In the middle opening, a very long way down, there was a spark.

"That's a lamp," she said. "He's got a second one. He said one."

"He said one that is carried," said Vastin. "I heard the term given, and it was drawn with some care. What he keeps burning on his own ground is another article."

"You'd have liked him," said Lira. "In a room with a seal."

"I should have found him exceedingly difficult. It is not the same thing."

She brought the lamp round again and held it low, and they went into the middle gallery.

---

It was shelved from the floor to the roof.

The shelves began a stride inside the mouth and ran on down both walls, in bays, each bay as wide as a man's two arms and divided by an upright of oak that the years had turned black. The lamp showed four strides of them at a time. Cael saw bundles tied in tape, laid flat, and rolls stood on end in racks like spears; he saw boxes of thin wood with no mark on them, and things in sacking, and a whole bay of boards, each as long as a forearm, stacked like slates. Nothing had a ticket. He looked for one in bay after bay, as he would have looked on any shelf in any office that he had ever stood in, and did not find so much as a number.

He counted his strides instead, since it was something to count.

The floor was the bluff's own rock, cut level, and it was clean. No dust lay on it as dust lies in a loft; there was only a fine pale bloom along the foot of each bay, where nobody walked, and it was the colour of a certain coat. The air smelled of dry paper, and of old glue, and of cold stone, and under those of nothing whatever. It was the smell of a book that has stood shut on a shelf since before its reader was born.

He listened for the feet. There had been five storeys of clerks over his head in the vault outside, going to and fro, and he had carried the sound of them down to an iron sill. It was gone. He could hear the lamp burn. He could hear the old man's stick, and Brom's breath, and the small dry rub of a calfskin against a sleeve.

"There is no register of this," said Vastin.

He had spoken very low, as people do in such places. The stick had slowed. He was looking at a bay of rolls as he had looked at a bridge with five piers.

"I certified the keeping of this town's books on four occasions. There is a line in the return, near the foot, which reads *lower archive: undisturbed; sound*. I put my hand to it each time." He drew a breath. "I had supposed it to mean a cellar, with a shelf. I did not ask to be shown it. I see now that the line was exact, and that I had no notion of what it was exact about."

"It's sound, though," said Brom. "He wasn't wrong. There's not a damp patch in it."

"That is very nearly what troubles me."

At ninety strides the shelves on the right hand gave place to tables. They stood end to end down the middle of the gallery, long and dark and as heavy as church doors, and Cael knew what they were from a hall above Ostrand: somebody had once sorted paper here by the cartload. They were heaped. Bundles lay on them a foot deep, squared, with slips of card thrust between.

The last three were bare.

He saw that before he saw the man, and it stopped his counting at a hundred and four. Three tables had been cleared to the wood, and wiped; and along the floor beside each, in rows as straight as a clerk's ruling, stood what had been taken off them. There were a great many bundles in those rows. Each lay under a slip, and the slips ran in an order. Somebody had lifted the work of years from where it lay, a piece at a time, and set it down on cold rock so that it might be put back exactly; and he had done it between yesterday's noon and this morning's second bell.

Karis had seen it too. He heard her breath go in.

Beyond the bare tables the gallery ended, in a wall of the pale rock with the pick's strokes still on it. A high desk stood against that wall, sloped, of the same black oak as the shelves; and over the desk, on an iron arm let into the stone, a lamp burned inside a chimney of glass. It was a far better light than the one that Lira carried. It lay on the desk's slope like a cloth, and on a leaf that lay there, and on the bent grey head of the man who was reading it.

He had his back to them. One square pale finger went along above a line, an inch off the page.

Lira stopped at the edge of his light, and so they all stopped.

He did not turn round. He raised his left hand a little from the desk, with the palm toward them, as a carter checks a horse; and then he laid it down again, and went on reading.

Brom looked at Cael. Cael lifted one shoulder, under the strap. The box on it held a third part of fifty-two sheets, and a purse sealed under five thumbs, and it had been on his back since a frost in an inn yard; he found that he could carry it for one page more.

It was a long page. Lira counted the lines by the finger, since she could not help it, and he saw her reach nineteen. Brom let his own box down to the floor by inches, with his knees, so that it should make no noise, and stood beside it with his arms hanging. Karis did not put anything down. She stood with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, at the very rim of that good light, and her face had gone as still as water in a butt. She had waited the whole of her life for this room. It appeared that she could wait for a stranger to finish his paragraph.

It was Vastin whom Cael watched.

He did not think that anybody had kept that man standing at the bottom of a room in forty years. Rooms had risen when he came into them. Books had been held out to him, open, at the gate of this very town. He stood now with a case on his shoulder, behind a girl with a lamp, while a Bronze of the least-regarded class on the roll read to the foot of a leaf; and Cael saw the right hand leave the stick, and hang, and go back. The old mouth had set. Then, slowly, as a thing is done that has been practised, the grey eyes came away from the reader's back and went to the shelves, and stayed there, on a bay of boards.

The finger reached the page's foot. The man turned the leaf face down, and laid a slip across it, and turned round.

"Tables," said Cleon. "Which place is which?"

"The first lies under a ring of standing stones, and it has eleven sheets. The second is under a stair: twenty-nine. The third is a hall. Twelve."

"Then eleven there. Twenty-nine on the long one; it'll take them in three rows. Twelve there." The finger went from table to table. "As they lay on the floors. North to the stair. If two sheets met on the stone, they meet on my table, and if there was a gap, I want the gap."

"I have the gaps measured."

"I'd thought you might." His red eyes went to the lamp then, and to nothing that was near it. "That comes with me. Low. As low as you had it on the stair. A hand to the left of whatever I'm at; not on it, and not behind me, and not where I've been. Is that understood?"

"It's understood," said Lira.

He had already turned back to the tables. He had not heard a girl's voice, Cael thought; he had heard the lamp agree.

---

It took Karis the best part of an hour to lay them out, and she would let nobody help her.

She had the second box unroped on the floor, and the oilcloth open, and she carried the sheets to their tables one at a time, flat on her two forearms, as a person carries a dish that is full to the brim. She set each down by a measure that she had in her notebook. Twice she took one up again and moved it the width of a finger. The man in the dusty coat stood at the end of the first table through all of it, with his eyes shut and his hands behind him, and waited as she had waited for his page.

Then he opened them, and began.

He did not read. Cael had seen that at a cook-house counter, and he saw it now for two hours together, from four strides off, with nothing to do but watch. The man bent over the first of the Ring's eleven, at its southern edge, where the foot of the sheet lay nearest him. His eyes went to the lowest row of her drawing and rested there. Then they climbed. They went up the sheet as a mason's eyes go up a wall that he has been asked to buy, stopping wherever a thing seemed to bear weight, and going back down from it to see what it stood on; and when they came to the top they did not go on to the next line, since there was none. They went to the next sheet's foot.

He did not blink. That was the second thing. Cael counted to sixty, once, and to sixty again, and the lids had not come down; and the red rims had begun to run. Water went down beside the man's nose and dripped from his chin onto the breast of his coat. He paid it no more heed than a carter pays to rain.

"It moved," he said.

Lira was on one knee at his left side, with the lamp's foot resting on the floor and the ring in her right hand. She had not stirred. Cael would have sworn to it.

"It didn't."

"It moved the breadth of a straw. You changed hands."

She had. The left had come up to steady the ring, slowly, as it came to everything; and the flame had not so much as shivered.

"Give it to the big one," said the man, to the sheet.

So Brom knelt with it. He got down as he did everything, without haste, and held the ring in his left fist as he would have held a plumb-line; and for the length of three sheets nothing was said.

"I've half a shoulder on my paper."

"That's mine," said Brom. "I can't take it off."

"Then take it somewhere else. You're a wall. I asked for a lamp."

Brom considered that. He looked at the breadth of his own shadow, which lay across the Ring's seventh sheet like a hill at evening, and Cael saw the corner of his mouth go.

"He's not wrong," he said, and gave the ring back to Lira.

She held it in her right hand alone after that, and she did not change. Cael watched the hand begin to shake a little, at the wrist, somewhere among the Stair's twenty-nine. She put her elbow on her knee, and it stopped.

The long table took the man the greater part of the time. He went down its three rows from the south, and came back, and went down them again; and there was a place in the second row where he stood so long that Karis took a step toward him, and checked herself. He said nothing. He went on.

A bell would have rung by now in the town, for noon. Cael knew it by his stomach. A porter had told Seln that this man went up for his bowl on every day that anybody could remember, and stood at one end of a counter, and came down again within the hour.

"He's missed his dinner," said Brom, under his breath.

Karis heard that. She did not turn her head; but her gloved hand shut on the edge of the flat.

At the third table he was quicker, until he came to the fourth of the Hall's twelve.

He stopped over that one as a dog stops at a gate. He looked at it from its foot. Then he went round the table's corner, and looked at it from the side, with his head laid over; and he came back, and bent until his face was a hand's breadth from her pencil, and stayed so for longer than he had given to any six of the others. Lira brought the light round to his left side, a hand off, without being told. He did not seem to know that she had done it.

When he straightened, he had to feel for the table's edge. He stood holding it, with his eyes shut and the water drying on his face.

"Now," he said. "I've looked. How old?"

"Older than [UNBOUND]," Karis said.

Cael had heard her say that word once before, at a trestle under a marsh, and shut her mouth upon it afterward as upon a box. She had said then that she would not say it twice. She had kept it for this table.

The man knew it. Cael saw that he did, though his eyes did not open: the square hands tightened upon the wood, and were still. There could not be many living people to whom that word was a date, and there were two of them now in one gallery.

He asked nothing more. He let go of the table, and walked to his desk with one hand before him, by habit, as a man crosses his own bedroom in the dark. There was a shelf above the desk, under the lamp's iron arm, and on it a cup, and a jar, and a thick bundle tied with tape; and at its end a long flat thing wrapped in sacking. He took that down. He stood with his back to all of them, and turned the sacking open, and looked at whatever lay in it. He looked from its foot.

It did not take him long. He folded the sacking over, and put the thing back upon its shelf, and came down the gallery again to the third table. Cleon stood over the fourth sheet.

"I know that hand."

"Whose is it?"

"I didn't say whose. I said that I know it."

He felt in his coat, and brought out a square of card with a buff edge, and went back to the slope of his desk to write it. It took him three lines, with his face close to the pen.

"Tomorrow. Second bell, as before." He pushed the card along the desk with one finger and did not look to see who took it. "Your boxes go up when the bell goes, and they come down with you in the morning; I'll not keep what I'm not reading. What's on those tables stays on those tables. Nobody squares it. Nobody leans on it. Nobody breathes over it with his mouth open."

"And today?" said Karis. "There are some hours left."

"Today I'm no use to you. I'll be an hour getting these back." He had sat down in the chair that stood at his desk, heavily, with his hands flat on his knees and his eyes shut. "Put the lamp on the table's end, where I can find it. Then do what you like, so long as it's quiet, and it's nowhere near my paper."

---

Karis did not go away from the third table.

She stood at its corner, where a square pale hand had rested, and looked down at the fourth of the Hall's twelve. Cael came and stood beside her. He had left the box against a bay. The carried lamp burned at the table's end, a yard from them, where Lira had set it down as carefully as an egg; and the other, on its iron arm, lit a grey head that did not move.

He looked at the sheet, since she was looking at it. He saw what he had seen under a marsh, by a lantern, with water to his knees: rows of small cut signs, drawn square, in her hard clean pencil. He could not have said why a man should stop at that one. He was reading it the wrong way up, from the far side of the table, as he read everything; and it came to him that he stood at the sheet's foot, where the man himself had begun.

"He knows the hand," he said, very low. "He's got something on that shelf that's cut the same. He looked at it, and he came back, and he said so. That's not a mason's pattern-book."

"No."

"He cleared three tables for you. He went without his dinner."

"I saw that he did."

"Then what is it?"

She was shaking again. It was not as she had shaken in a hayloft, with a card on her knee. He saw her take hold of it, a breath at a time, and put it down.

He had no answer for her. He had lain awake over this day, as she had, and he had been afraid on her account of one thing only: that a man would look at three floors and shake his head. It had not come into his mind to be afraid of the other.

Brom had come up on her far side. He had a loaf out of his box, and Lira's knife, and he cut it against his chest into five, and laid a piece on the bare wood at the table's very corner, a long way from any paper.

"Eat that," he said. "Then say what you want of us. He'll not say. He doesn't say things."

"How long's it going to take him?" said Lira. She had sat down on the floor with her back against a table's leg and her right hand open on her knee. She was working the fingers. "I only want to know how many days I'm a lamp."

"I do not know. I do not believe that he knows." Karis took up the bread, and looked at it, and set it down. "I have one man who can see how a thing stands, and he has seen it for two hours. I do not yet know what he saw. Whatever it was, I shall have to lay it against all that I have, sheet by sheet; and I shall not be hurried over it, by him or by myself."

She turned round to them. She was the smallest person in that gallery, and she stood with her back to fifty-two sheets, as she had once stood at a broker's plank with a hide.

"Give me a week."

Brom had the figures before she had shut her mouth.

"The loft's paid to the two hundred and eighty-eighth. A week from today wants two marks more, and I've got them. It's seven days of the hill, both ways." He looked at a bound leg. "And of that stair."

"I shall take them as they come," said Vastin. "I believe that I have said so."

He stood a little apart, by the nearest bay. He had been looking at it, Cael saw, for most of two hours, and he had not put out a hand to it. There were tags on some of those bundles after all, on the under side, where a string went through; and the old man's fingers had gone toward one, and stopped a hand's breadth off, and gone back to the ash.

"You'd want me to say it," said Cael. They were looking at him; they had been doing so since a plank at Lowmarch. "It's not a slip. There's nothing to take or leave. She's asked for a week, and it's hers."

"A week," said Brom. "It's priced."

Karis picked up the bread again. She ate it standing, with her eyes on the dark end of the passage that they had come down, where the black stone was.

"I can give you one thing today," she said, when it was gone. "It is small. The rock was cut first. The dark stone was laid against the cut, which makes it second. And what the town has built on the top of both, in mortar, is third." She brushed a crumb from her glove. "That is three kinds of work, one upon another, and I have had my hand on all three since the second bell. I cannot put a year to any of them."

"It's your tree," said Brom. "With the rings."

"It is the tree. I am standing inside it."

---

Cael went down the gallery by himself, away from both lamps.

Nobody had forbidden it. There was a term against a carried light, and he carried none; and a man with shut eyes had said that they might do as they liked, so long as it was quiet. He walked on the rock between the black uprights, counting, and the two flames drew together behind him into one. At forty strides the bundles on either hand had lost their edges. At sixty he could tell the shelves only as a person tells the walls of his own room at night, and he stopped there, and stood.

He did not at once know what it was that he was listening for.

He had been in a great many buildings since he was fourteen, and in every one of them there had been a drawer. There was a counter at Denvash, with a pen on it. There was a house on a bluff, where a file had grown as thick as a man's hand; there was a district seat, and a borrowed office, and a counter in every town between them. He had learned to feel for the drawer on any threshold, as a dog feels for the one board in a floor that gives. Even on the kind side of the Line there had been a book, kept by a man with one arm; that one had been honest, and he had been glad to stand in it, and it had put him eleventh all the same.

He stood among those shelves, and felt for it, and there was nothing to find.

Paper rose on both sides of him to the roof, more of it than a hall above Ostrand had held, and not a leaf of the whole had a line for what he was. It could not have had. Whoever had tied those tapes had done it before there was a plate to stand a child on, or a word in square brackets to put after his name; he had that on the word of a small pale woman, who did not give her word lightly, and of a man who had gone without his dinner.

He drew a breath of the dry cold, and let it go.

It was a strange thing to find in himself. He would have expected to feel light; and he did, a little, as he had felt at a hedge when a rule was lifted off a road. But he did not know how to stand. He had braced against that drawer for so long that his shoulders had forgotten any other way to hang, and he stood between two walls of other people's records with his hands open and nothing to set them against.

It was a hole under a town. He made himself say so. Five storeys of the oldest registry on the Line sat upon its roof. A station stood above that, and a man who kept a green book would let him up at the bell, to be a quarter of a figure again. He was not free of anything. He was only, for the length of a breath, in a place that had no means of knowing him.

He stayed there for a good deal longer than a breath.

---

It was a sound that brought him round. It was small and flat, like a spoon struck once on a cup, and it came from the stair.

"That's him," said the voice like a hinge, from the far end, without stirring. "He pulls a wire. You've a quarter of an hour before he shuts his yard."

They roped the boxes and went up. The second was lighter by fifty-two sheets, and Lira said that she could tell. The man came behind them as far as the iron, and drew the bar, and stood back against the wall with his eyes on their boots; and the clerk was waiting in the vault with his lantern, exactly where he had been left, as though he had not moved since the morning.

The cut was harder going up. Vastin went first, since the slowest must set the pace, and Cael went two steps below him, where a man would fall. On the bottom flight a tread rocked under Brom's weight, and he stopped, and trod on it again to be sure.

"That wants bedding," he said. Nobody answered him, since there was nobody whose business it was.

It was dusk in the lane under the wall. The four alleys had lost their white. There was a paleness at the far end of each now which did not come from any sky, and it lay along the cobbles in a bar that did not flicker. Cael walked through all four with an old man's breath labouring at his shoulder; and a box went by on his left hand each time, between him and it, at the height of a girl's head.

Seln was at the ladder's foot when they came in under the arch. He looked the five of them over as a man counts his change.

"Three people came to look at that low door in the yard, when it had shut on you," he said. "Two were yard-men, and they looked as people look at a thing which they have never seen used. The third was the clerk. He came out upon his own sill, with his book under his arm, and stood there for as long as it takes to read a page. Then he went in again to his hatch and sat down."

"And did what?" said Cael.

"Nothing whatever. I believe that he was pleased."

# Chapter 28 — Be Nobody

The second card took them through two gates, on the two hundred and eighty-fifth day, a good deal more easily than the first.

The keepers in blue had seen it now. One of them said *lower archive* to the other, as a man says the name of a bird that he saw once and has now seen twice; then the feather of his pen went along five caps without stopping at any. The old keeper at the round arch read the seal from its stamp inward, exactly as before, and said *one, and four*, and told them again to mind their lame one on the steps.

Cael went down the lane with his eyes on Brom's heels. The four alleys were white again, in the forenoon, and a box went by between him and each of them.

In the cut the third tread of the bottom flight rocked under Brom, as it had rocked the evening before.

"Still wants bedding," he said.

"You could bed it," said Lira.

"It's not my stair."

# Chapter 29 — The Cart and the Board

The dray came into the yard behind them, on the two hundred and eighty-sixth day, by the carts' gate at the bottom.

Cael marked it only because it was the heaviest thing there. It had two wheels as tall as Lira, and one mule between the shafts, and it was loaded to the rails with roofing slate, stacked on edge in straw. A lad of twelve or so walked at the mule's head. A man in a good grey cloak and a broad hat walked beside the tail-board, with a tally in his hand; and there was a square of slate painted on the dray's side, which was some broker's sign.

Loads came into that yard all day, and Cael had learned in two mornings how it took them. Whatever had wheels came up from the bottom, past the beam, where a man with a slate wrote a weight against an owner; whatever had legs came down the slot in the wall, as they had, and was written nowhere until it reached a hatch. The yard sloped between the two a good deal more steeply than a carter would have chosen, and its paving had been worn into one long shallow trough by more iron tyres than anybody had counted.

The four of them had come out of the cut a moment before, and Karis had gone to the hatch with the card. Brom had set his box down by the bench. He was looking at the dray as he looked at any load, to see how it had been put together.

"That's too much for one axle," he said. "On a slope."

It was the near wheel that went. Cael saw the pin jump from its hole and ring on the stones; and after that the wheel walked off its arm, quite slowly, as a drunken man leaves a doorway, and rolled away down the yard by itself.

The dray dropped on that corner; its bed tipped, and the whole stack of slate slid to the low side in one long clattering rush, like a dresser of plates going over. The mule screamed and plunged. The lad had let go of its head and jumped back toward the dock, as anybody would have; and the bed's rail came down across his boot and held it against the kerb.

He did not cry out; he stood bent over, with both hands on the rail, and said the same thing three times in a small surprised voice.

"It's on my foot. It's on my foot."

Two yard-men were running already, with a pole. The man in the cloak was shouting for a lever, and pointing. They went where he pointed, to the high side, where the other wheel stood a foot clear of the ground, and got the pole's end under the axle.

"Not there," said Brom.

He had not raised his voice, and it carried all the same, as a bell carries. He was walking, not running, with his eyes on the heap of slate; and Cael, who had watched him read a wall a hundred times, saw him read this.

"You'll roll it. It's sitting on that corner like a box on one becket. Lift the high side and the whole of it goes over onto him." He had come to the low corner. He crouched, and looked along the under side of the bed, and at the stub of the axle, and at a boot. "Leave that pole. Put it down. All that's holding it off his leg is the slate that slid, and the slate's not done sliding."

It was not. A single plate shifted in the heap while he spoke, and the rail came down by the width of a thumb, and the lad's breath went in through his teeth.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read.

It asked under the soles of Cael's feet, low and even, as it had asked of every rut for nine days; and it asked also which way the weight was going, since that was the half of its trade. It could have told him to a stone. It could have told him which slate would move next.

He told it no. He stood in a registry's yard, twenty feet from a hatch with its shutter up. A girl with eggs had been turned from a gate in that town for the wrong hour on a card; he did not care to learn what was written against a porter who was seen to know a thing that he had no way of knowing.

"And you?" said Lira.

# Chapter 30 — What Seln Looked At

Brom lay on his back that night, since there was no side left to him that he could lie upon.

Lira had begged a pot of something from the stable, which was meant for horses, and smelled of it. She rubbed it into the left shoulder by the lantern, with the heel of her right hand, while he looked at the slates and said that it was a waste of good grease. He did not let her near the right. That one was another woman's knot, he said, and nobody was to lay a finger on it until the woman had.

"You lifted a cart," said Lira.

"I lifted a corner of one."

"Then lie still for a corner."

The old man stood at the gable, under the blanket, as he stood every night until the last of them was down. He had an awl in his fingers again. There was nothing left on the tarred case that wanted stitching, and he had begun on the becket of Cael's box, without asking, where the rope had started to fray.

Shadow had lifted at dusk, in the lane, between the second alley and the third. Cael had told it no while a bar of pale light went over his boots. It lay shut now, and the other nine had come back, each to its own place.

He could not sleep. He had written three words about a wish, and the writing had not put it down.

The lantern went out. He lay with his hands behind his head and listened to the loft, as he had listened to every room that he had ever slept in.

A room full of sleepers has a shape, and he had known this one's for five nights. Brom made the most of it, in the middle, breathing like something felled. Lira lay on her right side, a yard from Cael's elbow, and Karis by the boxes, small and quick, with a catch at the top of every breath that had been there since a cook-house. There was the old man's, upright, by the gable. And there was a place near the ladder's head where nothing was. He had learned to know it as a person knows where a rug lies in a dark room, because the floor does not ring there.

The room changed shape.

He heard nothing. The ladder did not creak; it never had, under that one. There was only a difference in the dark by the ladder's head, as when a coat that has hung on a door for a week is taken down, and the door is seen to be a door.

Seln had gone out.

Cael lay and looked at the slates that he could not see.

He had got up once before, in that loft, and found a grey man sitting on a rung, and asked him for something. He could have gone to the ladder now. He could have looked down into the yard, and seen which way a shadow went; he had Shadow of his own, under a seal, which would have told him more than his eyes.

He did not move.

There was a case. It hung on a strap across that man's chest, and it had hung there on every road since Ostrand, shut. Cael knew what was in it. Five of them had gone through it on a table, by a shaded lamp, on a night above Ostrand; and he had written the next morning that it did not go into a book. It had not gone into this one either.

And they were inside a wall now. They were two hundred yards beneath the oldest registry on the Line; and that case had not been inside a Compact wall since five people went down a bluff with a cart. He did not know what a man did who brought such a thing back within reach of the people that it concerned. He did not think that Seln knew.

A sheet had been laid before him once, at a spring, with its head turned away; and the person who laid it had gone to a basin and stood with his back turned, so that it need never be asked what was made of it. Cael had understood only afterward what that had cost.

He could pay some of it back, by lying where he was.

It was harder than holding a lamp. He stopped wanting to be at the ladder, as he had stopped wanting to be anywhere else on a floor of cold rock, and after a time it held.

---

Seln went down the ladder on the outer ends of the rungs, where the wood does not speak, and crossed the yard in the shadow of the pump.

He did not look up at the loft. He knew that the boy was awake. He had known it by the breathing, which was a little too even, and he had found that he did not much mind. There had been a time when he would have waited an hour for it to change.

The half-door was shut and barred at that hour, and the woman who leaned on it was asleep. He went out by the carriage-arch, past the painted door. Its lamp burned, and the book lay open on its stand within, with a pen on a chain, for whoever came late and had a name to give.

It was a quiet district at night. He had expected that. A town which lets its labour in by the piece does not keep it up after dark; the benches were empty, and the slates had been wiped, and the only wheels that he heard were the watch's handcart, two streets off, going its round with a lantern. He let it go by. Then he went up to the ring road, and along under the wall, as far as the chandler's.

He had marked the roof three days since, with a keg on his back. It was the tallest below the blue gate: a warehouse of four floors, with a hoist-beam at its gable, and an outside stair of wood for the men who worked the hoist. Nobody had thought to put a door on the stair. Nobody ever did.

He went up it, and out along the leads, and sat down with his back against a chimney that was still warm.

The whole hill stood in front of him.

He had not seen it so before. He had gone up it once as a counted hand, with his eyes where a porter's eyes are, on the setts; and he had watched a yard from a bench, and a door from a parapet. From here it was laid out as a plan is. The second wall went round below his feet, with the blue gate in it, shut, and a brazier burning in the arch where two keepers stood their night. Above that the slate roofs climbed, close and dark. Above the slates ran the third wall, the old one, low and pale; and over the wall, like the back of a great book stood on a shelf, the registry.

It was five storeys. He could see three of them. There was a light in one window, high up, where somebody was working late or had forgotten a lamp.

To the left of it, and a little higher, there was the other light.

He looked at that for some time. It was small, at such a distance, and pale, and it did not flicker. Every station that he had ever served under had burned so; a man who works in a registry leaves off seeing it within a month, as he leaves off hearing the clock. He had not been inside a wall with one for longer than he cared to reckon. He found that he saw it again.

The boy had not looked at it once. Seln had watched him not look, from a roof, down the whole length of a lane with four alleys in it, and had thought it very well done.

He put his hand up to the strap.

It weighed something under three pounds. He had known that weight for twelve years, to the ounce, as a man knows the weight of his own arm; which is to say that he had not felt it since the first month. It had gone through three postings on him, and across a Line. It had stood for some months between a researcher's grey boxes, on a shelf, with its key in his waistcoat, and had gone back onto the strap on the night that five people left a hill.

He felt it tonight. It pulled a little on the left side, as a debt does.

He did not go over what was in it. He could have named every sheet, and the order in which they lay, and that was exactly the reason. He had told a boy once that a thing told three times is a thing on its way to being known, and he had kept that case for twelve years by never once describing it to anybody. He had found, a long while ago, that the rule held best if he did not except himself.

It was shut. That was all that he needed to have about it tonight.

He had stood over a spring six days since, with a stolen sheet in his sleeve, and had gone through the three things that his training let him do with a fact. He had not been able to look at the case that morning. He had said to himself that a man cannot hold two closed things up to the light at once; and it had been true, and it had also been convenient.

He had opened the one. He had set it between two hands on a rock, and gone to stand by the water.

This was a night, and he had nothing in his sleeve. He looked down at the dark leather against his coat, squarely, as he would have looked at another man's file.

---

There were three doors to that building, as there are to every registry on the Line, and he had used the third for twelve years.

The front was for names. It faced a flight of steps across a paved square, and whoever went in at it was written. The yard was for loads; he had sat by that one on a bench, and it had cost him his face. And at the side, down a passage off the square's eastern corner, there would be a hatch with a ledge and a lamp over it, where a courier might lay a sealed thing at any hour of the night.

He had passed it three days ago, under a keg. He had not needed to turn his head. He knew how it would be: a slot of light, and a night clerk behind it, half asleep, with a book. The courier lays his packet on the ledge. The clerk enters it by its seal and its weight, and tears off a slip. Neither is permitted to ask the other a question. It is the oldest rule in the service, and the only one that he had ever wholly respected. A registry enters what is laid on its ledge. It may bury the thing afterward, or burn the copy, or hold it for six years in a drawer where nobody will receive it; but for the length of one line, in one book, on one night, the thing has been set down as having come.

He could be at that hatch in a quarter of an hour, if he chose to show a keeper what he was.

He considered it with care, since he distrusted himself on the subject.

A line would be written. *One case, locked. By hand.* It would go up in the morning to a counter. Somebody would open it there, with a key or without one. He could not see past that. He had been able to see past it once; he had known the names of the men who read that sort of return, and what each of them wanted, and which would carry it up a stair and which would carry it down. An old officer had stood in a hayloft four days ago and said that he no longer knew who read this town's books at night. If that man did not know, a copying clerk could hardly pretend to.

There were four names, besides, which must not be written inside a wall. He had counted an hour for them, on a ladder. What was in the case had those names nowhere in it, and it led to them all the same, as a road leads to a house.

So it was not that door. He had known it before he sat down. He had come up here to learn whether he still wished that it were, and he found that he could not tell.

There was a fourth thing which he had not thought of at the spring, since it had not then existed.

Under that registry there were a hundred and ten strides of shelving with not a ticket on it. He had the measure from the boy. A paper might be put there, behind a heap of a dead town's ledgers, and never be entered, and never be found; and it would not be lost either, since six people would know the bay. It was the one place on the continent where a thing could be neither kept nor given up.

He looked at that for longer than at the hatch.

It was filing. He saw it at last for what it was, and was a little ashamed that it had taken him so long. It was what an office had done with six years of sheets: put them where nobody would receive them, and called the matter held. He would have made a drawer of a quarry. He did not care to be a small copy of that office, at his age, with a better cellar.

That left him where he had sat down, with the weight of the thing on his left side.

He found, when he had been still for a while, that one matter had changed, and it was not the case.

A clerk had looked at him yesterday. He had been seen at a registry's door, plainly, by daylight, by a man who would know him in ten years; and he had told that man the truth about a form. For fifteen years he had supposed that the day on which that happened would be the day on which something fell. He had built a trade on the supposition. Nothing had fallen. The clerk had wiped his pen upon his cuff and written two words.

It did not rule on anything. He set it beside the case, as a fact about doors which he had not possessed before.

The watch's cart went by beneath him, on its second round. He let it pass before he moved; and it was then that he saw the other.

There was a man in the smith's doorway, across the open place, where the trough was. He stood well back, out of the light of the lamp at the corner. He had been there while the cart went by, and he was there after it, and he had not shifted his feet. He was not looking at the gate. He was looking at the carriage-arch of an inn.

He stood as Seln would have stood.

It was one man, in one doorway, seen once. A district is full of people who have reasons to stand in doorways at night, and nine in ten of the reasons are a girl or a debt. Seln did not make a finding of it. He put it where he put a date read once by lamplight, over another person's shoulder: to be looked at again by day, before anything was done.

He sat until the brazier in the blue gate had burned low. He had looked at the registry, and at its three doors, and at a pale light that did not flicker; and he had looked at the thing on his chest for the first time in twelve years without his hand going to the key.

He had ruled nothing. He came down the hoist-stair as he had gone up, and the man in the doorway was gone.

Cael was at the pump when the light came, with his head under it.

"Yes."

"No."

The mild eyes rested on him for the length of a breath. It was the nearest thing to thanks that Cael had ever had from that quarter, and he did not suppose that he would get nearer. Then Seln spoke again.

"I looked at it."

He said nothing more. Cael waited, with his hair dripping, since it was plain that something was meant to follow; and nothing did. The man turned, and went to see whether the half-door was open, as though he had given a full report and been dismissed.

*Brom was offered the life. He picked up the box.*

*Seln looked at it. Decided nothing.*

"Hang from nothing I can see. They're by themselves."

She thanked him, and he grunted, and she went down on her heels in front of the board.

The man had gone back to the plank, to look at the head of it, where the first row began; and for that he wanted the light high.

He drew the boot back.

Cael stood, and put his arm up, and held the flame above the plank's head for as long as it was wanted. It was a good while. His shoulder was shaking before the end, and he let it shake, and kept the wrist still.

# Chapter 31 — One at a Time

Karis sat up that night against the boxes, with the lantern turned low between her boots and her notebook open on her knee.

She had not struck a point for it; Brom had lit the thing at the kitchen fire, with a spill, as he did every evening now, and it gave a brown uneven light that she would once have been ashamed to work by. She had got used to it in six nights, and it appeared that a person could get used to a great deal.

A man had spoken to her at the bell.

She did not go over what he had said. She had it by heart, every word, with the place where he had stopped to rub his eyes; and she had put the whole of it into the box where she kept things that might be so and could not yet be shown. It was a large thing for that box, and the lid would not quite shut on it.

He had believed her, and that was what she could not get used to. She had walked into a cook-house with fifty-two sheets and a lifetime of being smiled at by better-placed people, and a stranger had cleared three tables and given her his cushion and told her that he knew the hand. She had not understood until now how light a pencil is in the fingers of a person whom nobody attends to. She might draw a line wrong on one evening and rub it out on the next, and no harm came of it to anybody.

The pencil was not light tonight. A man had waited ten years with a board, and thirty with a lamp in one hand, and he was going to build upon whatever she said next.

So she would say nothing next. She would read a number.

She turned back a leaf, to where she had copied the plank's four rows that forenoon, squared to a hand's breadth, with the lamp held low upon the left.

Twelve signs, and after them two rings and two pecks; nine signs, and a ring and four; twelve again; seven, and a ring and two. She had that much, and it was sound; a child could have had it. The cutter had counted his own work in figures, and his figures were of the plainest kind that there is.

It was what stood after the figures that had kept her awake.

There were six signs after the first twelve, and four after the nine. After the second twelve there were six again; and they were the same six, sign for sign, in the same order, as had followed the first; and after the seven there were five.

She knew what she thought it was. She had known it on her heels in front of the board, with a girl asking, and had shut her notebook on it.

A careful man writes a sum twice, once in figures and once in words, so that the one may be checked against the other, and a clerk at any counter did it still. If this cutter had done it, then those little groups were the names of his numbers; and if they were, each sign in them was not a thing that bore or hung. It was a sound.

It was a handsome idea, and it explained why two twelves were followed by the same six signs. It did not explain it any better than three others that she could think of. The six might be a maker's mark, set on every full row. They might say *checked*.

She had a sentence for occasions of this kind, and had said it over a cord in a frozen yard.

A person who knows what she is hoping to find will find it. She was hoping, tonight, harder than she had hoped for anything since she was a girl. That was the very reason why she could not be the one to say what those signs were. Something else would have to say it, which did not care.

She took a clean leaf, and headed it in her small upright hand.

*What would have to be so. And how it could fail.*

If the groups were number-words, they were words in some tongue, and she did not know the tongue; but there were not many that it could be. The stone of that archive was older than the town's first wall, and the oldest paper in it would be the nearest in age to whoever had cut the board. She had been told that the old town's first tally-books stood in the eastern gallery, on shelves that she had not yet seen. A tally-book wrote its numbers out in full, as every clerk's did.

She would not trust her memory of how such a hand spelled. She had read perhaps a dozen leaves of that age in her life, at Ternhall, in copies, and a copy carries the copier's wishes. She would have the book itself.

And she would not look at the board again until she had written down, from the book alone, what the board would have to show.

That was the whole of the method, and she set it out in three lines. From the old hand's own spelling of *twelve*, and of *nine*, and of *seven*, she would write which letters in each were alike, and which letter of one word stood also in another; a pattern of same and different, and nothing more. She would fold that leaf; then she would carry it to the plank, and lay it beside the cutting, and see whether the signs repeated where the letters did.

If they did not, in a single place, the idea was dead, and she would have killed it herself before it could be built on.

Under the three lines she wrote the ways in which it could fail. There were four. She found a fifth, and wrote that as well; and somewhere behind the fifth, with the lantern guttering and Brom breathing like a felled tree, she understood that she had not been so nearly content since a store behind a chimney.

She was not happy. That was another thing, and it had terror in it. This was only the old plain pleasure of having found a way to be proved wrong.

---

The book came down from the eleventh shelf of the eastern gallery, on the two hundred and eighty-eighth day, in a girl's right hand.

Karis had asked for it at the desk, before the man had turned his first page. He had told her the bay without looking round: the third on the left, the tall books, as high as the roof. He had said that the lamp might go there and come back, and that it was not to be set down on any shelf.

So the boy carried the lamp, and held it low, and Lira went up a ladder that had not borne anybody's weight in a lifetime.

She went up it like a cat going up a curtain. Karis stood at the foot with her neck bent back, and saw the great boot find each rung, and the left hand come to the rail a beat behind the right. It did come; it closed, and held.

"Which?" said the girl, from the dark under the roof.

"The oldest. It will be the one whose back has gone."

It was as broad as a paving stone. It came down the ladder against a thin chest, a rung at a time, and it was put into Karis's arms with a cloud of that pale dust.

She did not take it to the board; she took it to the bare end of the long table, a good way from any sheet of her own, and opened it at the middle.

It was a tally of loads, as she had known that it would be. A cramped brown hand had written, line under line, what had come in at some gate and what had been paid on it; and the hand wrote every figure out, in full, in the old spelling, with the strokes that she had learned at Ternhall to call the long *s* and the open *e*.

She found *twelve* within the first leaf, and *nine*, twice, spelled alike both times. *Seven* took her longer; this town had brought in a great many things by the dozen and the score, and few by sevens.

She did not look toward the plank at all; she kept her shoulder to it.

On her folded leaf she wrote the three words as that clerk had written them, one under another. Then she wrote the pattern, as she had sworn to, and nothing else. In the first word, six letters; the third and the sixth alike. In the second, four; the first and the third alike, and its last the same as that pair in the word above. In the third, five; its second and fourth alike, and the same again as that letter; its third the same as the fifth of the first word; its last the same as the first of the second.

She read it over. It was a dull little table of sameness, such as a child makes of a counting-rhyme, and it had no sign in it, and no wish.

"Lamp," she said.

She had not meant to say it so. The boy came, without a word, and went down on one knee a hand to the left of the plank; and she saw the corner of his mouth, and was too frightened to be ashamed.

She laid the leaf on the rock at the board's foot. She put the point of her pencil in the air above the first group, the six signs that followed two rings and two pecks.

The third sign was a small upright with a hook. She went along. The sixth was an upright with a hook.

Her hand had begun to shake, and she stopped, and held it with the other until it was still.

The four signs after the nine. The first, a thing like a gate; the third, a gate. The last was an upright with a hook.

She did not let herself breathe out. The seven. Five signs. The second was the hooked upright, and so was the fourth. The third was a sign like a fork, and she went back up the board to the fifth of the six, and it was a fork. The last was a gate.

There was not one place where it failed.

She sat back on her heels, and the rock was very cold through her skirt, and the lamp stood up beside her like a painted one, and she could hear the man at the desk turn a leaf.

It was a tongue. It was the old town's, or that tongue's grandmother; and a man who had cut orders into the floors of three buried places had counted his rows in it, as a carter counts sacks.

"Well?" said the rough voice, from the desk.

"They are the figures over again. In words."

"Whose words?"

"This town's. From before its walls; or kin to that, and near."

He grunted, and it was the sound that he had made at a counter when she told him that her light was always on the left, and she had learned by now what it was worth.

She wrote them in a column on a fresh leaf, each sign and the sound that it stood for. The hooked upright. The gate. The fork. There were eight when she had done, from three small words.

She found then that she was sitting on the cushion, and she did not remember crossing to the chair. The man had turned his head, by a very little, and seen where she sat; and he said nothing about it, and went back to his page.

"Read the rest, then," said Lira, at noon.

She was on the floor against the table's leg, with her bread, and she said it as she would have told somebody with a key to open a door.

"I have eight letters."

"Of how many?"

"Twenty-three." Karis had that figure now. The last of the Ring's eleven had been laid that morning, and she had gone down all fifty-two with her pencil upright. "Seven kinds bear. Eleven are nothing at all. And there are twenty-three which are carried; and those, it appears, are what a word is spelled in."

"That's a third of them. You could have a go at it, with a third."

"I could. I should be very good at it." She brushed dust from her glove. "I should look at a word with two letters in it out of six, and I should know at once what I wished it to say, and I should fill the other four from my own head. And then it would say that for ever. I should never see it plain again."

"So what d'you do?"

"I look for more numbers."

It was slower than anything that she had ever done, and she did it through the whole of that afternoon, with the lamp moving beside her and the man's reed going over her shoulder from time to time, to tell her where a run began.

She went along the carried signs only, looking for a group of the right length, in which every sign was one of her eight; and on the fifth sheet of the Ring she found one. It had three signs, and she did not say it aloud. She wrote down what the three letters were, in their order, and then she turned to the tally-book, and it was *ten*.

A carving under a ring of stones had told somebody to do a thing ten times, or to ten of something. She did not know which. She knew ten.

On the fourteenth sheet of the Stair there was a longer one, of six signs, and all six were hers. She spelled it out with her lips shut. It was the number after ten; and the hooked upright stood in it three times over, exactly where the old clerk's letter did.

That gave her nothing new; it only made the eight more certain, and she was glad of it as a mason is glad of a second plumb.

The new ones were harder, and she would not be hurried.

There was a group on the Ring's ninth sheet, of five signs, which began with one of hers and ended with her hooked upright, twice. Two in the middle were strangers. She knew what number, in that old spelling, was shaped so. She could have written the two strangers into her column then and there.

She covered the place with her glove.

"That's one that you know," said the boy.

It was on the Hall's eighth sheet, near the foot. It took her until the reed had gone twice more round the long table to find it: eight signs, with the two strangers standing second and fourth, and everything else in it hers.

She wrote them into the column, and that made ten.

They had come to her from three floors, one at a time, like guests to a house in bad weather. She thought of that, and thought it fanciful, and let it stand.

There were other groups, which were not numbers.

She saw them now on every sheet, and had no means of not seeing them. One stood four times in the Stair's twenty-nine: six signs, always the same six, always after a white. She had four of its letters. She had turned the old tongue over in her head, before she could stop herself, and found a word that would lie in those four as a hand lies in a glove.

It was a word that she very much wished to find there.

She did not write it, and she did not say it, even in the place behind her eyes where she said things first; she turned the notebook's leaf over upon it and went back to the tally-book.

There were two other things in that gallery which she did not look at.

One lay on a shelf above the desk, beside a jar, tied across both ways in brown tape. He had put two fingers on it and told her what it was made of. She had wanted it from that moment as she had once wanted a page with four signs upon it; and she had not asked. She would not open that tape with ten letters. She would only read into it what she had been told to expect.

The other was in her own coat: it was a single sheet, folded twice, in oilcloth, in an old man's square hand, and it had one line on it with a word that she had never met in any stratum. The man at the desk had made it a term that nobody should tell him what a thing was before he had looked. She found that she had made the same term for herself. If she showed him that word, he would see it in everything. If she let herself think of it now, so would she.

It could wait until she could read; she had waited a good deal longer for less.

"How many?" said the rough voice, when the bell was near.

"Ten."

"Of twenty-three."

"Yes."

"You're slow."

"I am."

"Good," said Cleon; and he bent to his page.

Seln was at the ladder's foot that evening before they had the boxes off.

He did not wait for the pot; he stood in the round of the lantern, with his hands in his sleeves, and he gave it as he would have given a count of days.

"We are looked at."

Brom set his box down very slowly, with his knees.

"Two nights ago there was a man in the smith's doorway, across from this arch, at an hour when nobody has business in a doorway. I saw him once. I did not speak of it, since one is not a finding." The mild voice did not alter. "Today there was a porter on the kerb opposite the cut's head, in the lane, with a frame at his feet. He sat there from the second bell until the third, and nothing was put on the frame. I do not know his face. I know his boots. They were a horseman's, with the spur-rests cut away, and so were the boots in the doorway."

"By who?" said Lira.

"I cannot tell you whose he is. I can tell you how he stands, and it is how I was taught to."

Nobody spoke; Karis had her notebook shut against her chest in both arms.

"It's the card," said Brom. "Those two in blue had never seen one. They'll have said so at a table by the first night."

"I told you then that I could not tell the one case from the other," said Vastin. He was standing under the blanket, with the stick. "I cannot now. I looked for the hand that sent it, and I did not find one."

"I do not say that they are yours. I say that a man may be followed without being told."

The old mouth had opened, and Cael saw it shut again, and saw what it had shut on.

"I was about to post a watch," said Vastin. "By turns, with a relief at the half-night, and a word."

"That's one," said Lira.

"It is. I am obliged to you for the count."

They were all looking at Cael by then, as he had known that they would be.

He went over what he had, and it took no time at all, since it was so little. He had ten things in him, and not one of them could be opened on that hill; a declaration under those walls drew a clerk, and a clerk was the end of four people. He had a lamp to hold. He had a girl with a slow arm, and a man with two bad shoulders, and another with one leg; and a researcher who had this afternoon learned the tenth letter of a tongue that nobody had read in the life of the town.

"We don't move," he said. "The loft's ours for tonight, and I'd pay again tomorrow. There aren't three houses under this wall that'd take seven bowls and no names. If we shift, we've told him that we saw him."

"And the way up?" said Brom.

"It's the cut. It's that or the square, or a beam with a book on it. We knew that at the start." He looked at Seln. "You'll be on that roof."

"From first light. I shall see both ends of it."

"Then nobody walks it alone, and no box gets set down where a hand's not on it. And nobody opens anything. Whatever comes." He heard how it sounded, and did not soften it. "If we're pushed, we're porters. We've hands and backs. That's all that anybody in this town has seen of us, and it's all that they're going to see."

"I've a knife," said Lira.

"You've a knife. That's a hand."

Karis let her breath go.

"I have five days of his week," she said. "I should like to be alive for them."

"So'd I," said Brom, and went down for the pot.

It was a quiet meal; and when the bowls had gone round, Lira leaned over from her pallet.

"He went for that lamp twice today," she said, in Cael's ear. "The old one. I'd been counting. It was four yesterday."

"I saw the second."

"He's getting better at it. Or he's only getting tired."

"I don't think that he knows which."

"No," said Lira. "Nor did you."

---

Brom paid at the half-door in the morning, on the two hundred and eighty-ninth day, and the woman swept two marks off her ledge with the side of her hand.

"A hundred and thirty-four," he said, to the purse.

He had his right boot off before he went up the hill; he sat on the mounting block, and unwound a strip of linen from two toes, and bent them one at a time.

"Ten days," he said. "She said ten. That's her to the hour."

"You've two shoulders owing," said Lira.

"That's shoulders. I'm speaking of a foot."

Nobody sat on the kerb at the cut's head when they came down the lane. Cael looked at every doorway. Then he looked at the roof of the last building, where a parapet ran along above the slot, and saw nothing whatever, and took what comfort he could from that.

Karis wanted three more of the tall books, and so the lamp went into the eastern gallery again, and Lira went up.

She handed them down this time, since they were the size of hearthstones, and there was nobody at the ladder's foot who could take them. Brom could not lift either arm above the elbow, and Cael had the light.

"You," she said, from the seventh rung, to a brown coat. "You've a hand. Hold these."

Cael heard the stick shift.

"I can take them against my chest, with the one arm," said Vastin, "and I can stand under them. I cannot undertake to carry them to a table. I should require the other hand for that, and it is spoken for."

"Did I say carry?"

"You did not. I wished it to be clear."

He stood at the foot, with the stick's head in his right hand and his left arm crooked, and she put the books into it one upon another, as wood is stacked. He held them as he would have held a seal on a cushion, with his chin lifted and his eyes on nothing; and when the third was laid on, and the dust of it went into his face, he did not sneeze. Cael thought that he had seldom seen a person so pleased to be a shelf.

Lira came down.

She did not come down the last of it. At the fifth rung she let go with both hands, and stepped off backward into the air.

He had the lamp, and could do nothing but watch. She dropped the height of a man, and her knees went, and she took the whole of it on her two feet and her haunches, with the left arm folded tight against her ribs; and she came up out of the crouch without having put a finger to the rock.

"Don't," said Cael.

"I didn't. Did you see that arm touch anything?"

"She told you not to fall on it."

"She told me not to fall *on* it. I've not. I've done that eleven times since the first day, down the dark end, when nobody had the light." She was breathing a little quickly, and her chin was up. "I can't use it to catch me. So I'm learning to come down without it. If I'm ever to go off something in a hurry, I'd sooner not find out then where it goes."

"And if you're wrong, it's the arm."

"If I'm wrong, it's the arm whether I've practised or not."

He had nothing to set against that; he was afraid for her, and he had said so once, which was all that she had ever allowed him.

"It didn't touch," he said.

"No," said Lira. "It didn't." She took the top book off an old man's arm. "You can put those down now. Thank you."

"It was no trouble," said Vastin; and it was plain that he meant it.

Cael knelt at the plank through the afternoon.

Karis had the tall books open along the table's edge, and the man with the reed was going back and forth between her sheets and the board, for a row that she wanted. It was dull work for a lamp. He let his breath go long, and watched the grey head and the square pale hands, as he had watched them for four days, with nothing else to do.

"Lef—"

The flame was there.

He had moved it before the word. He knew that he had, since the word had been cut off in the man's mouth; and he had not decided to. He went back over it: the eyes had been at the foot of the third row, and they would go up from there, and across, as they always went. He had put the light where they were about to be.

It happened twice more before the bell.

He turned it over on the cold rock, with his forearm along his thigh. He had thought that he was learning one man's habits, as a groom learns a horse. That was not quite it. All the looking in that gallery ran one way. It began at the low left corner of whatever was looked at, and it climbed to the right; and it did so on the plank, and on every sheet of fifty-two, and it had done so at a cook-house counter. The board stood propped against the table's edge, with its head a little back; and he found that he knew, without the grey head's help, which end of each row the reed would come to first.

He could not have said how he knew, since he had not been told anything; and he did not make a finding of it. He put it with a cord that read short, and a hair, and a wish; and the heap was getting large.

"Thirteen," said Karis, at the bell. She had dust to the elbows. "Three more today. They came out of the Stair together, in one word, and I had to wait for the Hall to spell it again."

"Of twenty-three," said the man.

"Yes."

"You've four days."

"I know what I have," said Karis; and she shut the tall book very gently, as a thing is shut that will be wanted in the morning.

# Chapter 32 — No Round

The fourteenth letter came out of the Hall on the two hundred and ninetieth day, an hour before the bell, and Karis did not say so until she had found it twice.

Cael had the lamp. He had put it where the reed was going, three times that afternoon, before the rough voice had finished the word; and the third time the man had not troubled to begin it. He only moved, and the light was there. Neither of them remarked on it. A lamp is not thanked for burning.

The old man had stood by the nearest bay since the morning, with both hands on his stick. He had not gone toward the ring once. Cael had watched for it, at the trimming and at the noon hour and when the light was wanted high; and each time the grey eyes had gone to the thing, and rested, and come away. He thought that it was a good deal harder than the four had been. It is one matter to stop a foot that has started. It is another to stand in a room with one office in it for six hours, and look at the office, and leave it where it is.

"Fourteen," said Karis. She said it to her notebook. "It closes the number after five. I had the head of that word two days since, and I could not have the tail."

"Of twenty-three," said the man.

"Yes."

"Three days."

"I can count as far as three," said Karis. "I have the figures for it."

He made a sound in his chest that was nearly a laugh; it was the first that Cael had heard out of him, and it seemed to surprise him as much as anybody. He wrote the card for the morning with his nose an inch from the pen.

The clerk's bell went, small and flat.

They roped the boxes. Brom took his on the right shoulder, as he had for four days, and Lira got her arms through her beckets, the right and then the left. Cael felt the third box settle between his shoulder-blades, with a purse in it under five thumbs. The old man hooked the tarred case with the head of his stick, and brought it up.

At the iron the man in the dusty coat drew the bar and stood back. His red eyes were on their boots.

"Second bell," he said, as he said every evening.

The clerk walked behind them through the vault with his lantern, and turned his key in the low door, and Cael did not look round at him. He had not needed to for five days. He was a quarter of *the same*.

The yard was empty.

He had never seen it so. They had used the whole of the clerk's quarter-hour that evening, since a small pale woman would not leave a tally-book lying open; and the wagons were gone from the dock, and the pan had been hooked up on the weigh-beam, and the carts' gate at the bottom was shut and barred. The light had begun to go. It lay grey on the long worn trough of the paving, and there was not a soul on it.

"It's quiet," said Brom.

"It's late," said Lira. "They'll count us out at that arch with a face on."

Cael looked up, at the roof of the last building, where a parapet ran along above the slot in the wall. There was nothing on it. There had been nothing on it for two days, morning and evening, and he had learned to read that as a small grey man doing his work.

The gate at the cut's foot stood open, as it always stood.

They went into it in the order that the stair had taught them. Vastin went first, since the slowest must set the pace, with the stick going up a tread ahead of him and the bound leg swung after; and Cael came two steps below, where a man would fall. Lira followed, with her arms through her beckets. Karis was behind her, with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other; and Brom came last, and his box all but brushed both walls.

The slot took the light from them at once. The walls went up three times the height of a man on either hand, blind and dark and damp, and the sky was a strip of dull violet a long way over their heads.

On the third tread of the bottom flight a stone rocked under Brom. He had told it for six evenings what it wanted. He only looked at it tonight, and came on.

"Five," said Lira, at the first landing. She counted them every time.

The second flight was the short one, of four; and it had the wet tread in it, the one worn into a dish, where a stick had slipped on the first morning. Cael saw the old man find it with the ferrule, and go round. He saw the sound leg bend and take the whole of him up onto the ninth step, where the next landing began.

Behind them all, at the foot, the gate shut.

---

It did not swing to, as a gate does in a wind. It was put to, hard, and a bar went down across it; and the sound came up the slot like a blow on a cask.

Every one of them turned. Cael got his head round over the box's corner, and looked down past a girl and a small pale woman and a big man, to the bottom of twenty-two steps.

There were two people inside the gate.

They stood with their backs to it. They wore long coats of no colour, and hoods, and each had a cloth tied across the lower half of his face, so that there was nothing under the hood at all. Each had a stave. They were not porters' poles. They were as long as a man and as thick as a wrist, and shod.

Nobody spoke. One of the two shifted his grip, and they began to come up the steps, side by side, without any haste.

Cael's breath had stopped. He had ten things in him, and every one had come awake at that bar.

He felt the stick turn.

It was the smallest sound in the slot: a ferrule grating round on stone, a step and a half above his head. He was looking downhill, as they all were. He did not at once understand why the old man was not.

Vastin had stood on eleven fields. He had given that figure beside a hold's hearth, and again on a bank, and Cael had taken it for a count of honours. It was a count of something else. A man does not hold a line for forty years at gates and hearings without learning what is done at the other end of a street to make him look away from his own; and when a thing was shown him at the bottom of a stair, with a great noise, it was to the top that his head went.

"Above," he said. It was said quite low.

Cael looked up.

There were four at the head of the cut, against the strip of sky.

One knelt on the highest landing, with a thing laid along his forearm that Cael knew before he could see it plainly. It was short, and it had a bow across its end. A second stood over him with a stave. A third was already coming down the flight below them, in the middle of the steps, as a man walks who knows that the ground is his. And at the very top, where the slot opened beside the parapet, the fourth stood by himself. He had one hand on a bar of iron that was let into the wall there for people coming up under loads. In the other he held a lantern, low, against his coat, with its shutter closed.

They were hooded, all four, and there was a cloth across each face.

It was a stair a stride and a half wide. It had no door in it, and no window, and nothing to stand behind. There were ten yards of it between a crossbow and an old man's chest.

Cael saw the bow come up.

The stick came down.

It struck the edge of the ninth step, once, with the whole of a tall man's weight upon it. The sound was not the sound of ash on stone. He had heard it on a moor. A line went out from the ferrule to either wall, along the lip of the tread, as a crack runs across ice. Then the air stood up from it.

There was no frost here to show where it was. For the space of a breath he saw it all the same: a bloom went over the dusk, from wall to wall and from the step to the sky, like a breath on a pane of glass. It was gone, and there was only a place in front of the old man's coat that the eye slid off.

It roared. He could not have said how a thing roars that makes no noise. He stood two steps beneath it, with an Arbiter in his breast that had been dark since the day it woke, and it came over him all the same, as the heat comes out of a forge when the door is thrown back. It was going out of the man steadily, as it had gone out of him on a road, with nothing kept in hand.

The bolt was already in the air.

It stopped a yard in front of a brown coat. It stopped with a crack like a slate struck by a hammer, at the height of a man's breast, on nothing. It dropped to the landing and rolled against the wall.

Vastin made a sound, shut behind his teeth. Cael saw the bound leg take it. Whatever struck that plane came down through the man who was its foot, and it went into the knee.

His boots had not moved. They stood on the lip of the step, on the line, and they would stand there until he let the thing go, or until it left him. He could not lift a heel. He could not turn round. He had shut a stair with his own body, facing uphill, and whatever was done at his back was to be done without him.

Cael had seen that wall go up once before, across a cart's width of road, and had seen what it was worth there. Eight hounds had gone round it. A bank had been enough.

He looked at the two walls of the slot, blind and sheer, going up into the dusk.

A wall on a road may be gone round. In that place there is no round.

---

It was Brom who met the two below.

He had turned at the bar, with the rest, and he had not turned back. Cael did not see the beginning of it. He had it afterward from three people, and from the stair. He saw the end, over his shoulder, between one breath and the next.

The big man let his box go. He did not set it down; he had no arm that would have set it. He dropped his right shoulder, and the thing slid off the webbing and fell across the fourth step with its long side to the gate, and lay there. It shut one half of the slot. He stood in the other.

He had nothing in his hands. He had not been able to lift the left above the elbow for four days, and the right was in another woman's knot.

"That's far enough," he said to the two with staves, exactly as he would have said it to a carter.

They came on. Neither had said a word. The nearer put the shod end of his stave at Brom's chest, like a man shoving a boat from a bank.

Brom stepped down.

It was not what a person does who is being pushed. He went a step toward them, onto the third tread, and trod on the back of it with his whole weight; and the stone that had rocked under him for six evenings tipped up at its front edge like the lid of a box.

He was off it, and had it before it could fall back. He could not raise his arms. He could bend, and he bent, and got both hands under a slab of the dark stone as long as his own forearm and a hand thick; and he took it up as far as his thighs, on his legs and his back, as he had taken a dray.

He had said on a moor that a rock only wants lifting and letting go.

He let it go. He did not throw it. He opened his hands, with a heave of the hips behind them, and it went down the last two steps edge foremost, turning over, at the height of a shin.

The first of the two was a quick man. He got one leg clear, and the slab took the other across the boot and the foot of his stave together, and he went back into the gate with a noise of breath and timber and sat down against it.

Brom had not straightened. Something had gone in his left shoulder as the stone left him, deeper than what the dray had done, and he was bent over it with his teeth showing.

The second came past his fellow in one stride. His stave was up in both hands, above his hood, and it was coming down on a bowed head.

Lira went off the landing.

She had been on the second flight, two steps under Cael's heels, with a box on her back and a small pale woman between her and the fight. She had got out of her beckets. Cael never learned how; the box was found afterward standing square on the seventh step, as if she had set it there for a seat. She went down past Karis, who had pressed herself to the wall, and took the two strides that the landing gave her; and she did not go on down the flight. She stepped off its head into the air.

It was not far, as she reckoned falls: the height of a table, and the length of a man outward. She came down over Brom's box, with her knees drawn up and the long knife in her right hand, and her left arm was folded against her ribs as tight as a wing.

She did not strike at the man. She struck at what he held. The knife went into the ash between his two fists, point first, with the whole drop behind it, and stood there to a third of its length.

Her boots hit the step in the same instant. Her knees went, and took it. She hung from the hilt with all that she weighed.

A stave cannot be swung with a girl upon it. It came down between them, slowly, like a gate-bar, and its shod end struck the tread and stayed. The man had a hand free. He let go with it, and reached for her hood.

Brom's boot met him at the hip.

It was a carter's shove, with a leg like a newel post, from a man who had got his breath; and the hooded one went backward down three steps and into the other, and the pair of them into the gate.

Lira had gone over to the left with the stave. The wall was there. She took it on the point of her shoulder, and Cael heard that. She slid down it, and sat, with a man's stave across her knees and her own knife standing up out of the middle of it.

"It didn't touch," she said, to nobody, through her teeth. She was holding the left arm against her with the right. "It didn't touch a thing."

There was a small sound higher up.

Karis had flattened herself to let the girl go by. She had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as she had carried them up and down that stair for seven days, and she had not a hand to spare for the wall. Her boot was on the sixth step. It was the wet one, worn into a dish.

It went from under her. She sat down hard on the stair, and the flat did not leave her arm. It was her left hand that went out, flat, to save the rest of her, and found the lip of the step above.

That stone had not been worn round. It had been cut to an edge by whoever laid it, before there were walls on that hill, and seven days of porters going by on the other side had done nothing to blunt it.

She did not cry out. She looked at her palm, and then she shut it, and put it inside her coat.

"The flat is dry," she said, in a voice that was not quite steady. "I should like that known."

The two at the gate had got up.

One had no stave now. He looked up the stair, past Brom, and it was not at any person that he looked. He looked at a box lying across a step. He went to it, low, with one arm up against a boot that did not come, and got his fist through its becket, and hauled.

Brom sat down on it.

He could do nothing else, and so he did that. He lowered himself onto the lid, with his two arms hanging and his heels dug in against the tread. The man below pulled at a rope with a great deal of a Copper from Velmere seated on the other end of it.

---

Cael had turned back uphill before the slab was out of Brom's hands.

It was the hardest thing that he had done on that stair. Everybody that he had was behind him: a girl going through the air, and a big man with no arms, and a small one with a courier's flat. He heard the stone go down, and the gate take a man's back. He heard a knife go into wood.

He had the whole ten, and they had come up in him like hounds to a gate.

Wind was first, as it had always been, the dry hiss in his feet. *Away?* it said; and there was nowhere on that stair that was away. Under it the millstone went round, and told him the weight of a slab that he could not see, and which way it was falling. Ember ticked in his right hand, and showed him a cord, ten yards off, drawn back along a stock; a single point would have gone through that cord as through a thread. Pressure had drawn itself as tight as a held breath. A slot between two walls was the very thing that it was made for; it would have gone up that stair like a cork up the neck of a bottle. *There?* asked the thin held note, of the place by the parapet where the slot began; and a point set there would have shut the head of the cut like a door.

It was dusk. Shadow had lifted while he was on the first flight, and it stood at his knees now and asked harder than all the rest together. He was in a place with no window, in failing light, with four people looking down at him. He could have been gone from every one of those eyes between two breaths.

He told each of them no. He did it one at a time, as a man goes along a row of dogs.

It was not the clerk that held him, though a clerk was at the end of every one of them, and behind the clerk an hour, and four names. It was a lantern.

He had been looking at it since he raised his eyes. A man stood at the top of that stair, with one hand on a rail, and a lantern held shut against his coat. He was not fighting. He had no stave. He was doing nothing whatever, and he was doing it with great attention, with his hood turned straight down the slot.

Nobody brings a light to a beating and keeps it dark. That man had been put there to show it when a certain thing happened. It had not happened yet. He was waiting for it, and so were the other three; the one with the bow had not laid a second bolt on his string.

Cael had been stared at on roads since he was fourteen, by people who had been told what to look for. He knew the look of it through a hood.

They had been told. Somebody had stood these six in a place with no way out of it, and had given them a bow to frighten with and staves to hurt with; and all that they wanted in return was to see what a boy with no tier did when he was hurt enough.

Then they should see him do nothing, from as near as he could bring it. A wall wanted a reason behind it; he would be the reason, and stand.

An old man had said a thing at a board, on a moor, with his bowl set down upon a sill, about a wall and what it wants behind it. Cael had been that once, by going ten strides off and landing like a post.

He went one step up.

It put him on the eighth tread, at a brown coat's left hip, in the open half of the slot, where there was nothing between the bow and himself but a place that the eye slid off. He stood square. He lifted his face, so that the last of the light was on it, and looked up the stair at four hoods.

The bow came round to him at once.

He had known that it would. He watched the man lay the bolt, and draw, and he did not shift his feet. He had put his skin behind this wall once already, on the word of having seen it hold. He had seen it hold a good deal since.

The bolt broke on the air a hand's breadth from his eyes.

He did not blink. It was not courage. He had only nowhere else to look. He heard the breath go out of the man beside him, and he saw the laths move under the linen.

"You need not have come up," said Vastin, without turning his head. His voice was thin, and quite steady. "It will hold as well for one as for two."

"It's not for the wall that I came."

"No," said the old man, after a moment. "I see what it is for. I had not thought to have it done for me twice."

The third hood had reached the landing.

He came across it without hurrying, and stopped where the air had bloomed. He put up a gloved hand. He laid the palm of it flat on nothing, a yard from Cael's face, as a man lays a hand on a window to learn whether there is glass in it.

He pushed. He set his shoulder to it, once, with his weight behind, and Cael heard the knee take that as well.

Then he stood back by half a pace, with his palm still upon the plane, and looked through it.

Cael looked back. There was a cloth, and the shadow of a hood, and inside the shadow no face that he could have sworn to.

After that nothing moved on the upper stair at all.

It was the quietest minute that he had ever stood through. There was noise below him, of stone and timber and people's breath, and he could do nothing about any of it. In front of him four people had gone as still as he had. They were waiting for a boy to be whatever they had been told that he was; and the boy stood on a step, with a box on his back, and gave them a porter.

He had never been so near that wall for so long. On the moor he had stood by it for a count of twenty, and gone.

He felt what it was, from two feet beneath it, with nothing to do but feel. Every one of his ten went somewhere. The hiss ran, and the millstone turned, and the point ticked, and the bellows drew; even the thing that read went along with whatever it read. This did not go. It had been told to stay, and it stayed, with an old man's whole strength poured down into it like water into sand. It was a heel set upon a line, and meant.

Everything that he owned was a way of going somewhere, or of getting out of the way. He had not known that a person could be given a thing which only stayed.

Something in him set.

It was not as the others had come. Tide had filled him like a cup, under a healer's hand. This did not fill. It was as a post is stood in its hole: not driven, but put there, and the earth trodden down about it a heel at a time, until a person could have hung a gate on it.

He had not reached for it. He had been telling ten things no, and it had not been there to tell.

*This far?* it said.

It asked of the step in front of his boots, in a voice like a bar going down into two sockets. He knew what it was before he knew that it was new.

*No*, he told it; and that was the eleventh time, in that stair.

---

The hand came away from the plane.

The hooded man had taken it off as a person takes his hand from a door that he has decided not to open. He turned his head, up the stair, and lifted two fingers.

At the top, the one by the rail moved for the first time.

He raised the lantern. He brought it up from his coat to the height of the parapet, where the whole town lay below to see it, and his thumb went to the shutter. A wall would serve, it seemed, if the other thing was not to be had. A wall was a Path opened in a district. He did not doubt that somebody down there in the dusk had been told to watch for a light, and had a pen.

A line came out of the sky.

Cael saw it only because he was looking at the lantern. It was no thicker than a bootlace. It came down from the roof's edge, above the slot's mouth, with something small and heavy at its end that swung once, round an arm and a bar of iron together, three times, quicker than he could follow, and bit.

The lantern jerked sideways. It struck the rail, and fell on the top step with its shutter still closed, and rolled, and went out inside itself with a small flat cough.

The man wrenched at his arm. It was bound to the iron from the wrist to the elbow, and it did not come.

Cael looked along the roof. There was a parapet, and a strip of violet sky, and for the space of half a breath a shape upon it no bigger than a chimney-pot; and then there was only the parapet. Seln had said once that if they saw him, he had done it badly. He had been seen by four people in hoods, and Cael did not think that it was badly done.

The one on the landing looked up at the roof for a good deal longer than Cael had.

Then he whistled. It was two low notes, such as a carter gives a horse.

It ended as quietly as it had begun. The man with the bow rose from his knee, and unstrung the thing, and put it under his coat. The one beside him went up to the rail, and drew a knife, and cut a thin cord in two places; and the fourth took up his dead lantern. None of them ran. They went into the lane, one after another, like people who have come to the wrong door, and the hood on the landing went last. He walked up the two flights backward, with his face to the plane, and at the top he stood for a moment and looked down it.

Then the head of the cut was empty. Nobody had spoken. Nobody had run.

He went over what was new in it, since he could not have helped doing so.

It was of a family that he had already. The third had come to him from a big man, a long while ago, and it read the ground under his feet; and he had half expected this to be the same thing made harder. It was not. It had a word in it that he had not met, and the word was *held*. The ten had each been named for what they did, since they read, and burst, and burned, and bound; this one was named for what it did not do.

Its field was a new one as well. He had nine that had come out of a fight, and their fields said so. He had one that a healer had given him, lying down, and that said *clinical*.

# Chapter 34 — The Carts' Street

They let the old man down out of the loft at first light, on a box-rope, like a sack of meal that nobody wished to bruise.

It was Brom's rope, and Brom's knot, a loop under the arms that would not draw tight. He had taken a turn of the line round the post at the ladder's head and sat down against it, with his heels on the boards, since he could not lift either arm and did not need to. Lira went down first, to mind the bound leg, and Cael stood on the rungs beneath a brown coat, as he had stood beneath it on a good many stairs.

"I am able to hold a rung," said Vastin. "I held a bowl this morning. I should like both facts noted."

"They're noted. Hold the rung."

He came down on one leg and two hands, with the other leg swung out stiff, twice the thickness that a knee should be. He did not make a sound until his boot was on the cobbles. Then he let his breath go, and stood on the sound foot with the ash under him, grey to the lips.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the ladder's head, where a line might have been set that nobody could have climbed past.

Cael told it no. It had asked seven times since he woke, of a trapdoor and a stall and the lip of the trough, and it took the answer each time like a post taking rain.

They put the old man on the mounting block, with the leg out along it. He looked at the ladder for some time.

"I shall not go up that again," he said. "I say it now, so that it need not be argued at dusk by people who are tired. The stall at the end has nothing in it. I have looked at it through the boards for nine nights. I will not say that I looked forward to it; but it has straw, and its floor is level with the yard."

"It's a horse's," said Lira.

"I was fed like one last night, and came to no harm."

Nobody laughed. He had not said it to be laughed at.

Cael gave the rest of his ruling then, with his hair still wet from the pump. He had promised it for the morning, when he could see it; and he had lain awake a good part of the night finding how little there was to see. He had six people, and a hill with two gates in it, and a card that was good for one day; and there was a slot in a wall at the top of that hill, twenty-two steps long, which had been shut on them at both ends like a box.

"We're done with the cut," he said. "At dusk, and at noon as well. It's two ends and no middle, and they know its hours as well as we do."

"That leaves the carts' street," said Seln. He stood by the pump, with his hands in his sleeves. "I walked it once, under a keg of oil. It is half a mile, round the back of the crown, and there is not a yard of it that is ever empty by day."

"That's why. Nobody bars a road with a dray coming up it every minute." Cael looked at the three boxes, in their row beside the block. "And we go on wheels. A man had his fist through a becket last evening, and it took the whole of Brom to stop him. He'd not have got far with a cart."

"I'd as soon not sit on them a second time," said Brom. "There's a handcart in that stable, with shafts for one. I've asked. She says we may have the use of it while the loft's ours, if it comes back swept."

"What's she want for it?"

"She wants it swept."

Karis had come down the ladder last, with the flat held against her by one arm. The other hand lay in the breast of her coat, in a strip of a big man's shirt. She had not complained of it, and she had not slept.

"The card says four," she said.

They all looked at the mounting block.

It was a square of card with a buff edge, and it had been written the evening before, a hundred and ten strides under a registry, by a man who knew nothing of what had waited for them in a stair. It gave a door, and a purpose, and one bearer, and under her a figure; and that figure would be counted against caps at two gates and against boots at a hatch.

"Three, with a four on the card, is a question," said Seln. "It would be put civilly. It would be put three times."

"I am aware of what I am on that card," said Vastin. "I do not propose to be the reason that it is read twice." He looked at the handcart, which Brom had run out under the arch. It was a low thing on two wheels, with a rail, and it smelled of hay. "I had not expected to go up to that building as matter. I find that I mind it less than I ought."

"You'll ride on the paper," said Brom. "It's the softest load we've got."

Seln had already turned toward the arch.

"I cannot keep a roof over half a mile of street. I shall be on the ground, in front of you, and if you see me I have done it poorly." He stopped. "Nobody walks alone. No box is set down where a hand is not upon it. And nobody opens anything. I only say again what was ruled; it has been tried once since then, and it held."

He was gone before the sentence had quite ended, as he generally was.

The old man put out one raw hand, when the boxes were going onto the cart, and touched Cael's sleeve.

"There is a thing which I should rather say here, where it costs nothing. At the foot of that street there is a bar, and a beam, and a man who writes a weight against whoever owns the load. He will ask whose it is." The grey eyes were level. "A load that travels on a practitioner's card is entered to the head of the card. That is the rule, and it has an article, which I do not give you. I have told you now, in a yard. I shall not tell him."

"You'll want to."

"I shall want to very much," said Vastin. "I have learned what it costs when I do."

---

A handcart went up a hill a good deal worse than a box did, and it went through a gate a good deal better.

Brom had put himself between the shafts. He could not lift, and he could not haul with his arms; but he could lean, and there was nobody in that town who leaned as he did. He took the shafts low, at his hips, with the webbing of his right shoulder showing at his collar, and walked into the weight as into a wind. Cael and Lira pushed at the tail. Karis went beside the wheel, with the flat. On the three boxes, facing backward, with the tarred case across his thighs and the stick laid along it, sat a person who had come through that gate four times on horseback, to be met.

He did not look at anybody whom they passed; he looked at the cobbles going away under the tail-board, and now and then at the two who pushed.

"I am sensible that I am the greater part of the load," he said, on the steep of the street.

"You're not," said Lira, through her teeth. "The paper is. I've carried it. You're only the awkward part."

"That is a distinction which I shall treasure."

The keepers in blue had counted that card on seven mornings, and they knew it as they knew the baker's boy. One of them had the feather of his pen going along the caps before the wheels were under the arch. It stopped at the cart.

"That's four," he said. "One of them's sitting."

"He fell on your steps," said Karis. "Above. He cannot walk the hill."

It was true, and she had said it once already, to an old man at another gate; and she said it now to the pen and not to the face.

The keeper looked at a leg like a bolster, and at a slat of box-wood bound along it with strips of linen. He did not ask on what steps. A porter who was hurt in that town was no affair of a gate, so long as the figure came right; and the figure came right.

"One, and four," he said, and tore his corner. "Mind the groove with those wheels. It's taken a rim off better carts."

The groove lay across the roadway under the arch, a slot in the paving as wide as a hand, lined with iron, where a grating came down at night. Brom took the shafts over it a wheel at a time. Cael felt the cart drop and rise under his palms, and he saw the old man's jaw set, and heard nothing.

The eleventh had asked at that slot. *This far?* It had asked in the voice of a bar going home into two sockets; and it had been quite right, since that was exactly what the place was for.

*No*, he said. He thought that he had never carried a thing with so little to do. Wind had a road to wish itself along, and the millstone had weight under it at every stride. This one wanted a line, and somebody to stop at it, and he had spent his life on the wrong side of lines that other people drew.

The old keeper at the round arch read the seal from its stamp inward, as he read it every day.

"One, and four. Your lame one is riding. I told you that those steps were older than he is."

"You did," said Vastin, to the cobbles. It was a civil answer, and no more than any porter would have given; and Cael saw him hear how easily it had come.

Then they were in the lane.

It was two hundred paces, under the wall, and he had walked it fourteen times with his eyes on Brom's heels. He had no heels to look at this morning. Brom was in front of a cart, and the cart was as high as Cael's chest with an old man on it; so he looked at the tail-board, and at a pair of raw hands laid on a tarred case, and pushed.

The four alleys were white. Each laid its bar across the setts in front of the wheels, and the wheels went through it, and then his own boots. At every one a girl was on his left, bent to the rail, between him and whatever stood at the alley's far end. She had not said that she would be. She never had.

At the third there was no broom going. There was only the white, and the quiet of a paved place a hundred yards off, with nobody on it so early.

He found that his neck had not set, as it had on the first day. He did not make much of that. A person can learn to walk past anything, if it is put in his road twice a day; it does not mean that the thing has grown smaller.

At the lane's end the cobbles tipped downhill, toward a parapet and a slot in a wall. Two plain bolts would be lying in that slot yet, unless somebody had swept. Brom did not so much as turn his head. He swung the shafts left, where a broader way opened between the last house and the wall, and the handcart rattled out onto the carts' street.

---

It was a road that a town had made for its own back.

It ran round the crown on the outer side, under the top wall, cut into the bluff like a shelf; and it was paved with great setts, grooved by iron, and wide enough for two drays to pass with a hand between their hubs. On the right the hill went up in pale stone, with the backs of tall buildings on it and not a window lower than a ladder could reach. On the left there was a kerb, and a drop, and all the roofs of the district.

Everything that the crown burned or ate or wrote upon came up that road. They met a wagon of split logs in the first hundred yards, and a string of four mules under meal, and a cart with nothing in it but stoppered jars packed in straw. Things on wheels kept the wall, coming up, and things going down kept the kerb. Nobody had painted a line there either.

"Kerb side," said Brom. "We're down."

He had turned round between the shafts, and now he walked backward into the hill with the cart leaning on him, since half a mile of downhill wants a brake and not a horse. Cael and Lira hung on the tail and let it draw them.

Cael kept his eyes on the roofs.

He had to put them somewhere. The wall was on his right hand, and above the wall was the crown; and he knew, without having once looked, where on that crown a pale thing stood. He had felt it go round him all that way, like a lamp carried round a room behind a person's back. So he looked left, and down, at a town laid out in three steps to a brown river. There was the bridge. It was small and dark and it stood on five piers; and a square woman had gone north from a fork with a mule, ten days ago, and would be at a mill before he was.

*Bring me whatever walks out of that archive with you.* He had brought fourteen letters, as yet, in another person's notebook.

The street curled in under an archway and came out at a bar.

It was a pole, painted in bands, let down across the way between two posts; and beyond it the road dropped into the long sloping yard that he had crossed fourteen times from the other end. He could see the dock, and the sheds, and the back of five storeys. Beside the bar stood the beam on its post, with a pan as broad as a cartwheel hung from one arm by three chains. A man in a leather cap sat under it on a stool. He had a slate on his knee, as big as a tray, ruled in columns.

"Whose?" he said, before the wheels had stopped.

Nobody answered him at once.

It was the question that had been asked of Cael in every building that he had entered since a plate lit; and it had never been asked of a box. He felt, at his back, on the cart's rail, a pair of raw hands shut upon a tarred case. He did not turn round. He heard the old man breathe in, as a man breathes who has the whole answer by heart, with its number; and breathe out again, slowly, through his nose.

Karis laid the card on the ledge of the post.

"It is matter for the lower archive," she said. "It travels on its practitioner's card, as you see, and it has gone down to him on eight mornings. The clerk at your hatch has it in his book."

The man took up the card, and held it at arm's length. He read the head of it with his lips going, as Lira read; and Cael watched a name that nobody had spoken go silently across a stranger's mouth.

"Him," said the man. "Down the hole. I've had this beam eleven years, and I never chalked a thing to him yet." He looked at the boxes. "Came by the slot, the other days, I suppose. On backs."

"Yes."

"What goes by the slot's the hatch's. What comes on wheels is mine. Get them in the pan."

They went into the pan one at a time, since Lira could lift and Cael could, and the beam's arm came down to each with a long iron groan. The man slid his weight along the bar with one finger, and chalked three figures, and a fourth for the case; and beside the four of them he wrote a name in a round slow hand.

Then he looked at what was left on the cart.

"And that?"

"That's a hand," said Lira. "He's on the card. He's not walking, this week."

"He's on my side of the bar, on wheels."

The old man's chin had come up. Cael knew that look. He had seen it in a registry's yard, six days ago, a breath before a name and a rank and an office were laid on a ledge.

"I should not care to be thought to evade a beam," Vastin said. He said it slowly, and he said nothing after it.

The man in the cap considered him: the brown coat, and the leg, and the ash stick.

"I don't weigh men," he said. "There's no column." He rubbed his thumb along the slate's edge. "A hand's the hatch's. Have him off those wheels before you're at the dock, and I never saw him ride."

He put up the bar.

"That was well done," said Cael, low, when the cart was moving.

"It was not done at all. I said one sentence, and it was a foolish one." The old man was looking back at the beam, and at a slate on a knee. "He had the whole of it in him, with no article. They generally have. I begin to think that I was sent for, all those years, chiefly by people who wished to be spared the trouble of deciding."

---

The clerk had the green book open before they were at the bench.

They had got the old man off the wheels at the dock's corner, as they were bidden. He stood between Cael and the wall, on one leg, with his left arm across a pair of shoulders and the stick in his right hand; and so they came along the paving to the hatch, three boots abreast and a fourth that did not touch.

The pen was already going. *The same.* It went to the narrow column. Then the clerk's eyes came along the stones at the height of a boot, as they came every morning, to find his figure.

They stopped.

Cael watched him count it. There were Brom's two, and Lira's two, and his own; and beside his own there was one boot standing and one that hung a hand's breadth above the ground, at the end of a leg like a bolster. The eyes went on to the bearer, as the form required. They found a small pale hand, the left, held flat against a coat, in a strip of linen that had not been there the day before.

The pen had risen from the leaf.

There were three things by now that the man had not asked. Cael had them counted, since he counted anything. Six days ago an old porter had laid a name and a rank and an office on that ledge, and nothing had been said of it since. This morning the same matter had come in by the bottom gate, on wheels, with another man's chalk against it, after seven days on backs. And somewhere in the yard there would be a yard-man who had gone up the cut at first light with a broom, and found a tread lying out of its bed, and two plain bolts, and had brought the bolts to somebody.

The clerk looked at a hand and a leg. His mouth opened a very little.

Then he looked at his own form. It had a line for the matter, and a line for its bearer, who was one; and it had a column for hands, by count, and no line at all for what had happened to them in the night.

He wrote *four*, and blotted it.

"He's kind," said Lira, under her breath, when the key was turning.

"He's exact," said Cael. "I think that it comes to the same thing, with him."

The man in the dusty coat drew the iron from within at the third knock, and stood back against the wall with his eyes on the floor.

"Three," he said. "And the case." The red eyes had stopped at a boot that hung. They went up the slat, and no higher. "That's slower than it was."

"It is," said Vastin.

"There's twenty-seven. They'll not have got fewer."

He hung the lamp's ring on Lira's fingers, and went down into the dark ahead of them on his quick flat feet. He had asked nothing. He had seen a leg, as he would have seen a cracked shelf, and had made his reckoning of it, which was that the morning would begin late.

It began a good deal later.

The old man looked down the first flight for a moment, as he had once looked into a cut. Then he gave Cael the stick to carry, and turned about, and lowered himself by his hands until he sat upon the top step with his back to the dark.

"I have gone down into a number of archives," he said. "I had not hitherto done it in the manner of a child of two."

He went down on his seat. It was done a tread at a time: the two hands were set on the stone at either side, and the sound leg bent, and the whole of him let down to the next step, with the bound leg borne out stiff before him like a plank carried through a doorway. Cael went backward below it. He took the heel of that boot in his palm at each step, so that it should come to the stone and not strike; and nobody spoke of that, either.

Lira held the light at the first landing, low, and counted under her breath, and Brom had gone on with his box. Karis stood a step above the old man's head, with the flat, and waited as she would have waited behind a slow cart in a lane.

It was twenty-seven, and it took as long as the whole of the hill.

*Both heels*, said Compression, once, on the second flight, when a raw hand slipped upon the stone; and Cael's frame creaked, and the hand found its hold, and he told the thing to lie down.

At the foot the old man sat for the space of ten breaths. Then he held up his right hand, without a word, for the ash.

---

The eleventh had nothing to do in that gallery, and it did it all day.

Cael knelt at the Stair's table from the third hour, a hand to the left, with his forearm along his thigh and the flame standing up. He had been a lamp for six days; he could have done it asleep. That was the trouble. It left the rest of him free, and there was a new thing in the rest of him that had not yet learned the house.

*This far?* it said, of the round of the light upon the rock.

*This far?* of the table's edge, where nobody was to lean.

It asked of the cross-way, and of each of the black uprights, one after another, as a new dog goes along a fence. He told it no at every post. It did not sulk; it only stood where it had been stood, under his breastbone, with the patience of a thing that had been made for waiting.

And two yards from his right shoulder, on Brom's box, with the bound leg out along a plank that Lira had found for it, sat the person whom it had come from.

Cael did not look at him, since he had the flame to mind; but he knew where the man was, as he knew where the desk was, and the stair; and each time that the thing in him asked its question, he heard under it the other, which he had lain awake upon. *He does not know.* A man had asked that a wall be entered, and had been told that it was. He sat now with his two raw hands on an ash stick, watching a reed go along a row, and did not know that what he had stood on a step and poured himself into was kneeling at his elbow, in a boy, asking how far.

It was a debt, and it had not grown lighter for a night's sleep.

"White," said the rough voice. "Three black. Red."

Karis laid them with her right hand. The left lay in her lap, palm upward, and she did not use it even to steady the bowl.

The man had seen that, an hour since; he did not look at faces, but he looked at hands all day, since hands were what laid his counters.

"What's that?" he had said.

"I cut it on a stair."

"Which lays?"

"The other."

"Hm," he had said, and pointed with the reed.

She had told him one thing more, before she sat. She said that his name stood on a slate at the bottom gate, in chalk, against two hundredweight of paper, since there had been no other name to give a man who wanted one.

"It's been on worse," he said. He did not ask why they had come by the bottom gate.

It was dull work for a lamp, and slow for a researcher. She had fourteen letters, and she wanted nine, and they would not be hurried. Twice in the forenoon Cael saw her glove go down over a group of signs, and stay there, and come away again with nothing written.

"You're stuck," said the man, at the second.

"I am not stuck. I have a group of nine signs, and I do not know whether it is one word, or two, or the tail of one and the head of the next. If I knew where his words ended, I could lay them against the old books by their length. I have been guessing at the joins for two days, and I do not care for it."

"He doesn't leave a space."

"He leaves nothing. He packs a row to its edge like a wall."

The man grunted. He stood a moment with the reed lifted, as a man stands who has been asked for a tool that he owns and has never lent. Then he went on along the row.

He went up for his bowl at noon. When he came down he sat in his chair for the hour that his eyes wanted, with his hands flat upon his knees; and the five of them ate their bread by the nearest bay, a long way from any paper.

"He's turning something over," said Brom, with his mouth full. "Look at his thumbs."

The square thumbs were going, one upon the other, slowly, in the good light.

"He has turned it over since she said *joins*," said Vastin. "I should know the look. It is a man deciding whether to show a subordinate how the thing is really done."

"She's not his subordinate."

"No. I think that is why it has taken him an hour."

The afternoon went as the morning had. It was late, and the flame had been trimmed twice, when the chair at the desk scraped upon the rock.

The man got up, and did not take the reed. He went by the tables to the eastern gallery, with one hand before him, and came back out of the dark carrying one of the tall books against his chest. He laid it on the long table's bare end and opened it at the middle, where a cramped brown hand had set down loads.

"Bring that," he said, to the chair by the wall; and one square finger went toward the ninth of the Stair's twenty-nine. "Lay it by this. Foot to foot."

Karis brought it, flat on her forearms. Her face had gone very still.

"Stand here. Where I stand. No; here." He had taken a pace to his right, and he pointed at the rock where his own boots had been. "I've given you bones for a week, from outside, with pebbles. That's all I've ever given anybody. There's another way that I see it, and I've not found the words for it in thirty years, since nobody wanted them." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "You want his joins. I can't hand them to you. I can only show you where I'm standing when I see them."

He put out his hand, behind him, to nothing.

"Lamp," he said.

Cael was there already, on one knee, a hand to the left of two things laid foot to foot.

# Chapter 35 — Lean

The tall book lay open on the table's bare end, and the ninth sheet of the Stair lay beside it, foot to foot, in the round of the carried lamp.

Cael had the best place in the gallery for both, as he had for everything, and the worst way up. From his knee he saw a page of the cramped brown hand, line under line, with a figure at the end of each: some clerk of the old town had set down what came in at a gate on one day, and what was paid on it. A hand's breadth off lay Karis's hard clean pencil, in rows, with the white pebbles and the black still lying where she had laid them that forenoon.

The man had not begun at once. He stood with his hands behind him and his eyes shut, as he had stood on the first morning while she laid out fifty-two sheets. Karis stood on the rock where he had put her. She was a head shorter than he was, and she had come up a little on her toes without knowing it, to have her eyes where his would be.

Then he opened them, and looked at the book.

He looked from its foot. Cael had moved the flame before the grey head moved, and it was there. The eyes went up the brown page slowly, without a blink, and the water came into them, and they went up the sheet beside it. Then, which they had never done in eight days, they went back and forth between the two.

He spoke while he looked. That was new as well. He had always looked first, and said afterward what the looking had found, in as few words as would carry it.

Nobody in the gallery moved.

Karis was not looking at the sheet any longer. She had turned, on the spot where she had been put, and her eyes were going down the gallery: over the Hall's twelve, and the long table with its three rows, and the Ring's eleven beyond. She looked at them as she had once looked at a bluff from six miles off, counting.

"The strata lean too."

She had said it very low, and to herself.

The man made a sound in his chest. It was not the grunt that he gave for a thing well answered, and it was not the other, which had been nearly a laugh. He had heard a thing that he had not thought of; Cael could tell so much from the set of his shoulders, and no more. He did not take it up. He bent again to the sheet, and one square pale finger came out over the paper, an inch above her pencil, as it went above the lines of his own page in the mornings.

The finger began at the low left corner, and went up, a row at a time, and across.

"There is the other matter. The boots are at the painted door."

Nobody moved.

"He came a quarter of an hour behind your cart. He stands under the lamp, as he stood last night, with his hood up. Last night he stayed until you were in, and walked away by the ring road, and I let him go, since I had a man to carry." The voice did not alter. "Tonight I have not. When he leaves, I mean to learn where it is that he goes."

"Not alone," said Cael. "That was ruled this morning. You said it back to me."

"I did. I had hoped that I might be excepted from that one as well. I find that I am not." There was the very least movement at the corner of his mouth. "One man walking behind another is a thing that is seen. Two are a street. I want a second pair of feet, and I had thought to ask for hers."

"I can walk," said Lira.

"You can run, which is what I am afraid of; and you have a shoulder the colour of a plum."

"Then it's me," said Cael.

He had not known that he would say it until it was out. He had been looked at for nine days, from a doorway and a kerb and the head of a stair, by people who had been told what to look for; and he found that he wished, once, to do the looking.

"It is you whom they came to see," said Seln. "I have weighed that. If he turns and finds you behind him, he will have seen a porter walking in a street at night, which is no more than he has seen already." He got up off the rung. "You will open nothing."

"Nobody opens anything."

"I say it because it will be dark, and I know what you carry that likes the dark."

---

They went out by the door where the muck went.

It was at the stable's far end, a low thing on leather hinges, and it gave upon a lane no wider than a barrow. They passed the end stall to reach it. There was a long shape in the straw there, under two blankets, with a bound leg laid out along a plank; and its eyes were open.

The old man did not speak; he looked at the two of them, and at the door, and then one raw hand came out from under the blanket and shut, slowly, into a fist.

It was the second of the four hands that a road had taught him. *Seen.*

"He learns," said Seln, in the lane, very low. "I had not been sure that he would."

They stood at the lane's mouth, a house's length from the carriage-arch, and Cael was taught the trade in three sentences.

"You will wish to look at him. Do not. Look at the place where he will be in twenty strides, and let him walk into it." The small grey man did not turn his head while he talked. "Never be the nearest thing that moves. And when he stops, you do not stop. A man who halts when another halts has signed his name; you go by, and give him to me, and when I go by I give him back."

"And if he looks round?"

"Then you are going home. You have been going home the whole time."

That made two people whom Seln had ever taught, and Cael did not say so. He stood in the mouth of a muck-lane and looked along the house-front, at the round of yellow light under a painted door's lamp.

There was a man in it.

He stood on the step, where a person stands who wishes to be seen, beside a book on a stand that he had not written in. His hood was up, and he wore a long coat of no colour. Cael looked at the boots, since he had been told of them four times: a horseman's, cut high, and at each heel a square of paler leather where a spur's rest had been taken off with a knife.

*Away?* said the hiss in his own feet. It was not a thing that Wind asked often, standing still.

He told it no. And at his knees, where it had stood since the lane under the wall, the other thing lifted its head.

Shadow had come up at dusk, as it came every dusk, and he had told it no in a lane while his eyes ran. It had not lain down; it was full night now, in a town of doorways, and he was going to follow a man through it. There was a seal on that thing, and a reason for the seal which had not altered since Ostrand; and he had never felt it lean so against a shut door.

The man under the lamp stepped down into the street.

He did not look to left or right; he went away up the house-fronts at a stroll, toward the open place, with his hands behind him, like somebody who has dined.

"Count thirty," said Seln. "Then follow me, and not him."

The open place was empty. There were forty feet of trough, with the pipe running into its upper end, and not a beast at it, and the smith's two fires were banked. The man in the hood went to the pipe, and bent, and drank from his hand. He drank for a long time, and stood afterward with the water dripping from his fingers and looked at nothing, in the middle of a paved space with no cover on it for sixty yards.

Cael stood in a doorway and looked at a place twenty strides beyond him.

It was a great deal harder than not looking at a crown. The crown did not move.

A light came round the corner by the chandler's, low, jogging, with a rumble of small wheels. It was the watch: two men in leather caps, and a handcart between them, and a lantern slung from a pole at its front.

Seln's hand came up beside his coat, flat, palm down. *Stand.* Then two fingers.

They fell in behind the cart. It was done as a thing is done on a road, at a walk, without any word; and Cael understood it only when he was there. A watch looks where its own lantern falls; and anybody who turned to see that light would see the light, and two caps, and behind them a darkness in which a pair of porters might have been going home.

The watchmen did not turn round at all. One of them was telling the other about a goose.

They crossed the open place so, twenty strides behind a hood, to the mouth of the brokers' street. There the cart swung right, for the ring road; and the man with the cut boots went straight on.

It was as wide as a field, and there was nothing in it. Every slate had been wiped, and every bench stood empty against its wall.

"In an empty street nobody hides," said Seln, into his collar. "He goes home. You have the right-hand houses; I have the left. Sixty yards. Do not match his step."

Cael went down the right-hand side of the brokers' street, in the open, as a porter goes who has been kept late and has a bed at the bottom of the hill. His boots made a noise, and he let them. He looked at a place twenty strides ahead of a long coat, and the coat walked into it, and he chose another.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read, of a tread sixty yards in front of him that he could have had through the soles of his feet, heel and toe, for as long as he liked.

*No.*

*There?* asked the thin held note, of the lane's mouth half-way down, where the man was turning.

He told that one no as well, and he had begun to count them.

---

The lane went down toward the river, and it smelled of tar and new hemp.

Cael knew where it led before he saw. He had stood in that place with a big man, nine days ago, by daylight, and watched him look into the cut end of a rope as a person looks into a flower. It had been full of people walking backward then.

It was a shed, open all down one side, and it ran away along the inside of the town wall for three hundred yards. There was a great wheel at its head, and beyond the wheel a floor of trodden boards as straight as a ruled line, under a roof on posts, with coils hanging the whole length of the wall like hats on a row of pegs. A lantern burned at the far end, very small, where the tar yard was.

The man in the hood walked in under the roof, past the wheel, and went on down the middle of the boards.

Seln's fist had shut; then the palm came down flat, and stayed.

They stood at the corner of the last house, and Cael could hear the boots on the planking, unhurried, going away.

"It is a pipe," said Seln. He scarcely moved his lips. "Whoever goes into it behind him stands against that wheel and the sky for three hundred yards. Whoever goes round it by the open side has lost him for as long; and there is a quay at the bottom, and a lane up from the quay, and a wicket in the town wall for the tar-men. He has three doors out of the far end, and we should not know which."

"He knows that."

"He chose it because of that. I should have." The grey eyes went along the roof. "I shall stay at the wheel, in case he has only gone in to come out again. You will go round by the open side, behind the drying-racks, to the barrels. Go softly, and be there before he is. And when you are there, you are a barrel."

"And if he comes out past me?"

"Then let him. Count where he goes. I shall find you."

Cael went.

There was a strip of beaten ground along the shed's open side, and a line of racks on it, taller than a man, hung with yarn. He kept the racks between himself and the boards, and ran on the outer edges of his feet, as he had been taught on a different road by a different person, and he did not run fast.

*Away?*

Ten strides at a burst; thirty bursts; and a crack at the end of each like a carter's whip, in a town asleep. He told it no without breaking step.

He made one mistake, and it was not with his feet.

He glanced along the shed, to judge how much of it was left; he only meant to measure. But it was three hundred yards of a thing that somebody had built to go in one direction, with every post and plank and peg of it laid toward the far end; and the new thing behind his eyes came round to that as a weathercock comes round, before he had told it anything.

*Goes*, it said. The whole roof leaned away from him, down the length of the wall, toward a lantern.

He shut it, and it had been open for the space of a blink. A grain of sand came into each eye and sat there, and he ran the last hundred yards with his lids half down, and was angry, and had not the time to be.

The tar yard was a square of cobbles above the river, with barrels stood on end in rows and a black kettle on a cold hearth. He went in among the barrels and found two that stood a little apart, and put himself between them with his back to the wall.

Then he waited for a man to come out of the end of the shed.

He counted, since there was nothing else to do with the time. A person at a stroll goes three hundred yards in a count of two hundred and forty, or thereabouts. He gave it three hundred, and then four.

Nobody came.

The lantern burned on its post at the head of the quay steps, and under it the river went by with a noise like cloth being drawn over a table. He could see the shed's mouth, a square of blacker dark, twelve yards off; and nothing came out of it, and there was no sound of boots on boards.

At five hundred he understood.

The man had stopped. He was standing somewhere in that long roofed dark, among the coils, with his hands behind him, and he was doing what Cael was doing: he was listening for a second pair of feet to come down the boards after him, or round by the racks; and he was prepared to stand there until he heard them, or until it was morning.

Cael had been told what to do when a man stops. He had not been told what to do when the man stopped out of sight, and stayed, and there was no walking by.

He knew one thing that might serve, and he let his breath go long. He counted it, to ten and to ten again, as a healer had taught him on a plank; and he gave over wanting to be anywhere.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the shed's mouth.

It was a good place for it, and he saw that plainly. A line stood from that post to the wall would have kept a man in a pipe like a cork in a bottle, until a grey porter came down from the wheel and they had him between them. It would have roared across half the district, and he told it no.

And Shadow asked.

It had not once left off, and it asked now as water asks at a sluice. He was standing between two tar barrels at night, in a black coat, making himself a part of the dark by holding his breath; and there was a thing under a seal that would have made him so in truth, to any eyes at all, between one heartbeat and the next.

*No*, he said. He had said it to that one twice a day since Ostrand. He said it now as a person leans on a door with somebody leaning on the other side.

At seven hundred and ten the boards spoke.

---

The boots came down the last of the shed without haste, and the man walked out of its mouth into the yard and stood still.

He was five yards off. Cael could have counted the buttons on the long coat, if there had been light to count by, and there was none on that side. The lantern was at the man's back, and it made of him a flat black shape with a hood on it and nothing under the hood at all.

The hood turned.

It went round the yard slowly, as a lamp is carried round a room: over the kettle, and the hearth, and the rows of barrels on end. It came to the two that stood apart, and rested there.

Cael did not breathe, and he did not want to be elsewhere. He had learned that in the corners of halls, and at the edges of yards, and it had never been asked of him by anybody who stood so near.

He counted ten. The hood went on round, as slowly as it had come, to the wall and the quay steps; and the man went down the steps.

At the foot, for the length of two strides, the lantern was on him from above. Cael saw a pair of horseman's boots go through its light, with a square of paler leather at each heel. He saw gloved hands, clasped behind, and the back of a hood.

He did not know whether he had been seen. He thought, afterward, that the man had looked at the place where a follower would be standing if there were one, and had neither found him nor needed to.

"You did not come through after him," said a voice at his shoulder. "That was well judged."

Cael had not heard a step, and he let his breath go.

"He stood in there. I counted seven hundred."

"He stood for eleven minutes. I heard him not walking, which is a sound that I know." Seln was looking down at the quay. "He is in no hurry at all. I begin to dislike that more than I should dislike a man who ran."

The quay was long and bare; six flat boats lay along it, nose to tail, with their sweeps shipped. Beyond them the great wheel of the hoist stood up against the water, and beyond that again a cable went out over the river into the dark.

Cael looked at the cable, and his stomach went cold.

"There's a ferry."

"There is. It has no crew before first light. But a man who had one waiting would be on the other bank in a quarter of an hour, and we should be standing here."

The man in the hood walked the whole length of that quay, and went by the six boats, and laid a hand on the mooring post of each as he passed, as a person touches the heads of children. He stopped at the cable, and looked along it at the far shore; and he stood so long that Cael began again to count.

Then he turned his back on the river, and went up a flight of steps between two warehouses, toward the hill.

"He has shown us the ferry," said Seln. "He wished us to think of it."

They followed by the steps. There was a lane above, and at its head the ring road, lying under the second wall like a ditch under a dyke; and there the man turned right, and went along it at his stroll, with the pale stone going up on his left hand higher than a house.

Cael knew that road. He had walked the whole round of it on his second day in the town, by himself, and had not once lifted his eyes above the top of that wall. He did not lift them now; he looked at a place twenty strides ahead of a long coat, on the cobbles, and went home.

It was a quarter of the way round the bluff, and it took the better part of an hour, and the man did not once look round. He stopped twice: once to do up a boot, with his back to them, for a long minute; and once for no reason, in the middle of the way, with his hands behind him. Each time one of the two walked on past him, on the far side, with his chin down; and each time the other had him.

The watch's cart came toward them, by the chandler's, with its lantern jogging. The man in the hood lifted two fingers to it as it passed, and one of the leather caps nodded.

"They know him," said Cael, in the dark of a warehouse door.

"They know that he may be out. It is a different thing, and it is the thing that matters." Seln's voice was as mild as ever. "You and I have gone round this town tonight like mice in a wall. He has gone round it like a man taking the air."

The road brought them out, at last, by the back of the smith's, into the open place where the trough was. They had come the whole way round to within eighty yards of their own beds.

The man crossed the open place, and went by the smith's two cold fires, and set his foot on the one street that climbed.

"No nearer," said Seln, and drew Cael into the smith's doorway. "Look where we are standing."

It was a deep doorway, well back from the lamp at the corner. A man had stood in it five nights ago, at an hour when nobody has business in a doorway, and looked at the carriage-arch of an inn.

At the head of the street, two hundred yards above them, a brazier burned red in the arch of the second wall. The grating was down; it filled the arch from the keystone to the paving, a net of black iron with the fire behind it, and in front of the fire stood two shapes in long coats.

The man in the hood walked up the middle of the street to them.

He did not show anything at once; he went to the brazier, and held out his gloved hands to it, and stood so, with his back to the whole district, for as long as it takes to say a rhyme. One of the keepers spoke. The man turned his head, and answered, and put a hand into his coat; whatever came out of it was held low, in the palm, and was looked at, and put away.

A small door opened in the foot of the grating.

He stooped to it. And in it, with one boot through, he turned round.

The brazier was beside him, and its light came up under the hood. There was something pale there, two hundred yards off, no larger than a thumbnail held at arm's length, and it was turned down the street toward a smith's doorway. It stayed for the space of one breath.

Then he went in, and the small door shut.

---

"I'm going up," said Cael.

"To do what?"

"To see where it stops."

Seln looked at him, and did not say that it was foolish. He said, "A person may walk up a street. Do not speak first, and do not stand as though you were waiting to be let in."

So Cael walked up it, in the middle, as the other had.

It was two hundred yards, and he had gone up them on eight mornings with a card in front of him and a box on his back; he had never done it with his hands empty. The brazier grew as he climbed, and the two long coats took on a colour, which was dark blue.

*This far?*

It had never asked so loud. Every other place where it had asked had been a doorway that he chose to see as a line. This was a line. Somebody had cut a groove across a road, a hand wide, and lined it with iron, and hung a net of bars above it to come down at dusk; and it had come down. The thing in him knew its own kind. It asked in the voice of a bar going home into two sockets, and it very nearly did not need an answer.

He stopped a hand short of the iron.

The keepers had watched him come. One of them had a pike, grounded, and the other a board on a cord round his neck, and they were not unfriendly. The one with the board looked him over, from the cap to the boots, and saw what there was to see.

"First bell," he said, quite kindly. "And you'll want your card. There's no kerb-work to be had up here before the light."

Cael nodded. He did not speak, since he had been told not to, and since there was nothing that he could have said to that man which would not have been a lie or a great deal too much.

He stood at the groove for as long as a porter might, who has come up early by mistake.

He could have crossed it; he looked at that squarely, with the fire on his face. There was a thing at his knees that would have had him through the small door on anybody's heels, and neither of those two would have known that the air had moved. There was a hiss in his feet which made nothing of walls. He had twelve things in him, and not a keeper in that arch could have named one.

He could not cross it lawfully, and that was another matter, and it was the matter that the groove was for.

By day he might go through as the fourth part of a figure on another person's card, for a named door and a stated purpose, and be counted out again by dusk; by right he might not go through at all. Iron walked under that arch, and showed a paper, and was written. There was a colour for Iron, and a paler one for the wall above. A plate had put a word after his name that had no colour. There was no card in the district with an edge for what he was; and a man with his spurs cut off had just put a hand into his coat and brought out the one thing that Cael would never own, which was leave.

He had been stopped by lines for as long as he could remember being anything. They had all been written ones, in files, in rooms that he was not let into. He had never before been able to stand at one, and see the iron, and measure with his eye how wide it was.

It was a hand wide, and he found that he was glad to know it.

*No*, he told the eleventh; and he turned his back on a brazier, and went down the street.

---

Seln had not stirred from the doorway.

"Well?"

"He's Iron, or he's got a paper that does as well. He showed it low. They opened the little door." Cael leaned on the smith's jamb. His legs had begun to tell him how far they had been. "That's all. We walked three hours to learn that a man can go through a gate."

"Eleven guards on one broker's doors can go through that gate at night, and every Iron in the district. I do not call it a finding. I call it a wall." Seln was looking up the street at the red point of the fire. "But I shall tell you what I have, since you have earned the half of it. He was not learning whether he was followed. He knew that from the trough. He took us round by the one shed where we must divide, and stood eleven minutes in it; he showed us a ferry; he showed us a watch that nods to him; and then he went to bed, through a thing that we cannot pass, and turned in the door so that we should be sure of having seen."

"Why?"

"So that we should know how much time they have. That is the whole of it. They need not take you in a stair. They may stand under a lamp for a month."

Cael thought about that, with the cold of the doorway at his back.

"He wanted me to do something," he said. "At the barrels. Or at that gate. He stood five yards off and looked at where I was, for a count of ten. It'd have been the easiest thing I've ever done, to be gone."

"Yes."

"I don't think it was the stair they shut, two nights ago. I think it was this. They want it shown, and they'll wait."

"Then they have been shown a porter, twice." The small grey man came out of the doorway. "It was worth one night to learn that it is not worth two. I shall not follow him again; and I should be obliged if you did not."

They went back by the muck-lane. The fist came out from under two blankets as they passed the end stall, and opened, palm upward, on the straw. *Nothing?*

Cael turned his own hand over, empty, and the old man shut his eyes.

There was a greyness over the roofs by then, and he washed at the pump, and the tar would not come off his palms; he had not known that he had leaned upon a barrel. He wrote three lines by the last of the lantern, with the book held at the length of his arm.

*Night of the 291st. Followed the boots, with S., round the whole of the lower town. All of them asked; Shadow asked for three hours. One look that I didn't mean, with the twelfth, at the rope-walk: a grain in each eye. Not counted. Nothing opened.*

*He may go through the blue gate at night. I may not go through it at all, except as a figure. It is a hand wide.*

*S.: they have time. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

The second bell was three hours off. He lay down in his coat, and slept like a thing dropped.

# Chapter 37 — How Far

The bridge was a made thing that morning, and it had never been one before.

It was the two hundred and ninety-second morning past the Line, and Cael saw it from the kerb of the carts' street, where the hill dropped away and all the roofs of the district lay below. It stood out on the brown water as it had stood the day before, small and dark, on five piers. He had looked at it then as a person looks at a road that he came in by. Now it was the oldest thing that he could see from that hill which anybody had built. He had that on the word of a small pale woman, who had laid her hand where the masons' pale stone was cut to fit it. And the new thing behind his eyes came round to it, across half a mile of air, like a head lifted from a book.

He did not know whether it could read so far. He found that he did not wish to learn it on a street, with three hours' sleep in him and a lamp to hold.

He put his eyes on the tail-board of the handcart. That was no better. The tail-board had a hinge, and the hinge leaned.

It was the same wherever he turned. A river is not made, and a man is not; but a town is nothing else, from the ditch to the weathercock, and he had been given a thing that inclined toward every peg and course of it as a dog's nose goes to every post.

He had gone through this once already, with another matter. For eleven days he had not looked at a light on a crown, and he had not done it by shutting his eyes. He had done it by having somewhere else to put them, and a reason, twice a day, until his neck no longer set at an alley. He did the same now. He looked at a raw hand on a tarred case, which nobody had made, and at the back of a girl's neck, and pushed.

"You're walking like a horse in blinkers," said Lira.

"I'm learning not to look at things."

"You'd that already. You've had it since the rise."

"This is a different not-looking. It's the same road."

She thought about that for the length of ten strides.

"You're going to run out of places to put your eyes," she said.

The clerk wrote *the same*, and *four*. The old man went down twenty-seven steps on his seat, with his heel in Cael's palm, and said at the foot that he believed he had improved upon his time.

There was tar on the lamp's ring before the first sheet had been looked at.

Cael saw it when he changed his grip: a black thumb's breadth on the brass, off a palm that a pump had not been able to clean. The man in the dusty coat saw it a moment after. He had stopped, with the reed lifted.

"There's tar on my lamp."

He said it to the ring. His red eyes had come to the brass, and to the smear upon it, and they stayed there as though a line had been drawn at the edge of the metal; they did not go on by the width of a finger to the hand that held it. For seven days they had gone over that hand as over a hook. This was not the same. Cael watched a man take care, and he knew what the care was for.

"It'll come off with a rag," said the rough voice. "After. Left."

The flame was there.

It was a long day, and Karis made it longer by being right four times.

The man gave her his joins from the second bell. He went along the Ring's eleven with his finger, and the Stair's twenty-nine, and at every place where a run came down flat he said *red*, and she laid a square of tile. By noon each of the fifty-two sheets was parted, in broken tile, into pieces of the length of words. She had the tall books open along the table's edge. She laid a word against a word.

"Eighteen," she said, in the forenoon; and at noon, with her bread untouched, "Nineteen."

The first thing that she read which was not a number came a little after.

Cael saw her find it. It was a group of three signs, and it stood so often on the Stair's long table that she had passed it over as packing; and she went back to it, and looked from the pencil to the brown book, and sat quite still.

"It is *and*," she said. "It is only *and*. It is the commonest word that the old clerk has, and it is the commonest on these floors; there are nine of them on this one sheet." She laughed, which she seldom did, and put the back of her bound hand to her mouth. "I have waited a year to read a word of this, and the first word is one that a child has before it can walk."

"That's a good word," said Brom. "Nothing's built without it."

She had twenty by the time the flame was trimmed, and it was then that she took the glove off.

There was a group of six signs on the Stair's long table which stood four times in those twenty-nine, and always after a white. She had not let herself look at it for four days; she had laid a glove across the place whenever her work took her near. She lifted the glove now, by one finger. She had every letter of the six, and she did not read them at once. She looked first at the man with the reed.

"I had a word that I wished to find here," she said. "I had it when I knew four of these, and I covered the place so that I should not see it. I am going to say now that I wished for a name. I thought that a man who cut his orders into three floors might have signed them."

"And?"

"It is not a name." Her eyes had gone along the six. "It is *set*. As a stone is set, or a stake: set this, and what, and how many. It is a better word than mine. It is the one that is there."

"Twenty-one," she said, at the bell, and her voice was nearly gone.

The man had written his card. He stood by the desk, and his hand went up to the shelf, to the thick bundle tied both ways with tape gone brown; and two fingers rested there, as they had rested on the day when he named what it was made of.

"Not tonight. I'll not have that tape cut by anybody who's listening for a bell." He took his fingers away. "In the morning. It's the seventh. Come down with your eyes rested, if you know how that's done."

---

There was a mule in the inn yard when they brought the handcart in under the arch, and a man with his hat cut away on one side.

Cael knew the hat before he knew the man. He had seen it across a river with ice in it, on the far bank, on the day that the last ferry ran; and there had been a bag under that arm then, as there was now. It was the carrier who took whatever went between the towns of the Line and one plank under an eave, some days east.

The man was looking at Brom. More exactly, he was looking at Brom's back, between the shafts.

She had seen that forty times as well. It was always the eyes. A child found, on such a morning, that there was a thing in the world which belonged to nobody else, and which nobody else would ever be shown; and the first that she did was to try to look straight at it, and fail, and try again.

Her face came open like a door.

The old woman was over the line before the child's knees had begun to go. They did go, a little. She was caught, and held, with a grey head bent to hers. She was thinking of a boy on a table, under her two hands, a long while since, who had told her a thing with his breath going slow. He had stood in a ring too, and it had been chalk, and there had been a clerk. A small light had come up from behind his breastbone to the edge of his eye, as it had come just now to that child; and it had looked at him, for a count that he still knew; and then it had gone out. She had put her hands on that boy more than once. She had found in him what she had found in nobody, and had told him to write it down and leave it be; and then she had walked north from a fork, owing him the one reading that she had not given.

"She did. Tell her that somebody saw."

# Chapter 39 — Aimed

Vastin had been given three words in his life for the thing that he had served, and he set them out in their order, since he had never been able to think about a matter until it was set out.

He did it on a box, by a bay of bundles, in the afternoon of the two hundred and ninety-third day. Nobody wanted him. The lamp had come back to the long table, and the boy knelt beside it, a hand to the left, as he had knelt on every other afternoon. The small pale woman was making a second copy. The man in the dusty coat sat in his chair with his eyes shut. It was a room with one office in it, and Vastin had not once that day felt his boot begin to move.

He noted that. He did not take much credit for it; he had something else to look at.

The first word was *imperfect*.

He had held that one for very nearly the whole of his service, and he had held it comfortably. A scale has an error. A careful officer learns the error, and allows for it, and does not throw away the scale. He had sat through eleven fields and nine thousand readings on that footing. Where a plate gave a wrong answer he had called it a wrong answer, and had written so, and had been thought rather too scrupulous by people who were not obliged to sign.

The second had been given him by a file.

He had read it by the candles of four inns, a long while ago, and had carried it up a bluff to a house above Ostrand; and there five young people had shown him what the office that he served did with honest readings. He had said one sentence afterward, to a boy, in a room that was not his own. He had it by heart. He had never been sure that the boy did.

*I knew the system was imperfect. I didn't know it was designed to be.*

That had cost him a seal, left in a tray on a morning of rain, and three hundred miles on a hedge-stick. He had thought it the end of the matter. A man finds that the scale was made to read false; he lays down his office; he goes to dig up the one buried thing that he has the knowledge to find. He had supposed, on the road, that the thing would turn out to be a fraud. He had been ready for a fraud. He had dealt with a great many.

It was not a fraud. A fraud wants something. It leans the scale so that a house may keep its place, or a seat its revenue, and when the leaning is found there is at least a person behind it who has got richer.

He had heard a leaf of skin read out that morning, once, by a woman who would not say it twice. He did not say it over. He had been asked not to, and he was a man who kept to what he was asked; he had learned that lately, and at some cost.

But he could not help knowing what he had heard. A scale that reads false is still a scale, and whoever stands on it is at least weighed. This was another matter.

That was not *imperfect*, and it was not *designed*. He turned over what it was, on a box, with his hands on an ash stick; and the word came up of its own accord, as a third figure comes when two have been written.

*Aimed.*

He looked at that for some time.

He had put his hand to the foot of a great many sheets. He went over them now, since he owed it. Before the fields, and before the readings, he had been a junior officer of a district seat, and it had been his work on certain mornings to stand beside a desk while a child of fourteen was told what it was to be. A clerk wrote. He signed. It had been given to him as a duty of no weight, such as is given to the young, and he had thought it one.

He remembered very few of them. That was a finding in itself. He remembered a boy who had laughed aloud, and been hushed. He remembered a girl in a red cap, who had looked up afterward, as they all did, to find a face; and he had been reading the sheet.

The sentence rose in him then.

It was the one that he had begun that morning, to a bay of bundles, six strides from a desk. He knew its first half as he knew his own name. It came up exactly as a hand had once come up toward a lamp-ring, half-way there before he had seen it start; and he gave it the look that he had given the hand.

It was a draft. A small grey man had told him at a spring what he was when he drafted, and a small pale woman had laid a bare leaf on a desk and asked him to put nothing on it. He did not finish it. He did not let himself hear how it would have ended, even in the place behind his eyes where a man hears his own clauses before he speaks them.

It rose twice more before the light was trimmed. He put it down each time, and each time it was a little easier, like the leg.

There was one thing that he permitted himself, since it was not a conclusion. It was only something that he had done.

He looked along the tables at the boy.

Three nights ago, in a stair, six people had come to see what that boy was; and he had stood upon a step with his stick, on the eleventh day of thirty, and put a wall in front of him. He had not known then what stood behind it. He had said so, on a pallet, when he could not hold a bowl. He knew a little more this afternoon. He found that he would do it again, knowing; and that in a day which had taken the floor out from under forty years, it was the single act of his that he could look at without wishing to revise.

"I shall want the start," he said to the boy, who had come to see what he needed. "And I should prefer to take it by myself. I have had your hand under my heel, or at the other end of a strap, on every flight for three days. I am obliged for each of them, and I should like to learn whether I can manage one stair without."

"If it goes, there's nobody below you."

"If it goes, I shall sit down, which I shall be doing in any case."

"I'll be a flight behind," said the boy. "I'll not come nearer unless you call."

A man who goes up a stair backward looks at where he has been.

He had not thought of that when he began to do it. There was nothing to see. There was only the dark at the foot, and a little grey in it where the lamp burned a long way off down a gallery; but he faced it, tread after tread, and it grew smaller.

He had been a wall. He did not say that with any pity for himself; it was the plainest account of Iron Wall, and of his office, and of what a seat had used him for. He was not sure that he had been wrong in every case. He would not let himself off so cheaply as to say that he had been wrong in all of them, either. He would have to go over the eleven, one at a time, when he had a table and a month. It was the kind of work that he was good at.

He went away under the arch, by the yard, as he had come. His step was quick and flat and even. It stopped once, a stride beyond the arch's mouth, for the space of three breaths; and then it went on up the street toward a brazier and a wall, and they did not hear it any longer.

"He never ate his dinner," said Brom. "Again."

"He came down a whole hill," said Lira. "To sit with his eyes shut, and say a dozen words to somebody who was asleep."

"He came so as not to be by himself with it. It's the same as the bowl." Brom was stacking the eight of them on the board. "You lay one. Sometimes a man sits down to it."

The fire had burned down to red, and nobody fed it.

They got Karis up the ladder between them, Lira above and Cael below, more asleep than awake, with her notebook held against her in both arms and the bound hand uppermost. She said one thing on the rungs, quite clearly: she said that there were twenty-three, and that she had them all.

Seln took the ladder's head; he had the folded page yet, and he gave it into Cael's hand as he went by, without a word.

They put the old man into his stall; he went on the stick, and one shoulder, and let himself down into the straw by his hands; and when he was down he lay looking at the boards over his head, where six people would sleep.

"Fourteen," he said.

"Of thirty."

"I had it as fourteen. I am glad to have it confirmed." He was silent a moment. "You stood up to say it."

"Yes."

"I marked that. I sat for mine. I do not think that I could have done otherwise, with this leg; but I should have liked to." He shut his eyes. "Good night."

Cael went up.

He sat down against the third box, and took out Hesk's book, and the dividers slid from it into his lap. He set the day at the head of a clean leaf.

*Two hundred and ninety-third day. Treswick; the yard, and then the loft; one in the stall. A hundred and thirty. A leaf read once, below, by Karis. A copy made. Read at a fire by the three who had not heard it. The reader came up, and ate half a bowl. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He held the pencil over the leaf for some time.

Then he ruled a line under what he had, and wrote nothing beneath it.

Lira had turned on her pallet. She put out her hand for the book, as she always did, and read the short lines by the last of the lantern; and she turned the leaf over, to see whether the rest was on its back.

"That's all?"

"That's all that's for the book."

"You said it better than that. In the yard."

"I said it to you. All of you. You've got it." He took the book from her, and shut the brass into it. "It doesn't want keeping. It's not going anywhere."

She thought about that, with the slow arm uppermost and her eyes on the slates.

"No," she said. "I don't suppose that it is."

There was a bearing on a calfskin, in the second box, with four rings on it and a line that ran off the edge. There was a set of marks in a ruled square which nobody could yet read. There was a bundle on a shelf with its tape untied, and the back of a leaf to be gone through in the morning; and after the morning there was a gate, and a bridge on five piers, and a road that went the way they had come and farther.

He blew out the lantern.

The blanket lay close to the frame, where a girl's thumb had pressed it. There was no line on the boards.

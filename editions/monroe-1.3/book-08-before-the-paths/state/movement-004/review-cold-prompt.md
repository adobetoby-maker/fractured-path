# Chapter 20 — Wayposts

The hold came off at the first hedge.

It was Seln who took it off. He had gone down the western side of the rise thirty yards ahead of the boxes, as he went ahead of everything; and at the foot, where the ruts ran in between two banks of thorn, he stopped and let the file come up to him. He had not done that on any road since the Line.

"We may talk," he said.

Nobody did. They had come two hundred and eighty-one days through a country where a road was a place for hands and not for mouths, and the habit had gone deeper into them than any of the six had known.

"Who says?" said Brom at last. His own voice seemed to startle him.

"The hedge does. A hound does not lie up where a plough goes, and a thing that hunts what a Path gives off wants a mile of nothing round its den." Seln nodded at the bank. It had been laid within the year, with the stems cut half through and bent over and woven between stakes. "Somebody tends this. That town works these fields to the foot of the moor, and it will keep a watch that rides as far as its fields go. There will be posts."

"So we're inside," said Lira.

"We are not. The river is the Line here, and the sill lies under the gate. This is ground that the town uses and does not own." He put his hands into his sleeves. "I had better say the rest now, since it will be wanted before night. The hold was one rule in two halves, and only one of the halves is lifted. You may speak. Nobody declares."

"There's nothing to hear it. You said so."

"There is nothing to hunt it. Out there a declaration draws a thing with teeth. In here it draws a clerk. A Path that is opened on a town's ground is opened in somebody's district, under somebody's eye, and he is paid to write down what he saw and whose it was." The mild voice did not alter. "I should take the teeth, for choice. A hound does not send a copy up."

Lira laughed. It was one short breath through the nose, and it was the first sound of the kind that Cael had heard on a road since a meadow at Lowmarch.

"So we can talk, and we can't do anything," said Brom.

"That is a town."

"I'd forgotten that I didn't like them."

They went on between the hedges, and for half a mile nobody used the leave that he had been given. Cael found that he had a great deal to say and no notion of how a person began. He had not been silent all those days; they had talked at every fire, behind every wall, and through a whole cold term in a back room. It was only on a road that the mouth was shut, with the sigils in their pockets and his ten lying closed, and he had left off noticing it as a carter leaves off noticing his own wheels. He noticed it now, as it lifted. It was like setting down the box.

The road found its tongue sooner than they did. A cart came out of a field gate ahead of them, piled with dung and steaming, and the man at the horse's head was whistling. He whistled the same four notes over and over, badly, and did not leave off when he saw six strangers under loads. He looked at the boxes, and at the beckets, and pulled his horse over to let the weight go by, as one carrier does for another. He had given Cael the same length of glance that he gave the hedge.

After him there was a boy with a switch and nine geese, and a woman under a yoke, and two men mending a hurdle; and not one of them gave the six more than a glance, which took in the loads and priced the distance. Cael counted them, since he counted anything. He had been stared at on roads since he was fourteen, by people who had been told what to look for, and for the better part of a year he had walked where there was nobody to stare. This was a third thing. He was a back with a box on it. He thought that he could have borne a great deal of that.

Vastin went beside him on the ditch side, a shoulder off. The stick came down a little after each of Cael's strides, and the tarred case knocked softly against a brown coat, and neither of them spoke; of the six they were the two who found the leave hardest to use. Cael had kept one question from that man since a board at Lowmarch. He thought that the man was keeping something too. He walked like somebody carrying a full bowl.

---

The first post stood where a lane came in from the north. It was a dressed stone as high as Cael's waist, squared, with a figure cut deep in its eastern face, and under the figure three short bars, one above another.

"Nine," said Lira. "They've counted it for us."

"A town of the second schedule is bound to post its approaches at intervals of—" Vastin stopped. Nobody had asked him. He looked at the stone for a moment. "It is a milestone," he said.

"There," said Seln, without turning his head.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read, civilly, of the post, as it had asked of every rut for three days. Cael told it that it need not know. Brom lowered his box to the verge, and rolled the left shoulder, and looked at his right boot as a man looks at a debtor.

Lira had her box off before him. She set it down on the grass and sat on it; and she sat with her back to the west.

Cael would not have marked it, had he not been doing the same thing himself for three miles. Since the foot of the rise the town had dropped behind the hedges, and come up again, and dropped; and each time that it rose he had put his eyes on the lowest wall and held them there, since there was a small pale light above the highest roofs that did not flicker. It was work. It was like carrying a second box.

She had never once looked up. He went back over the morning, and he was sure of it. On the rise she had looked at three walls and called them a ladder. After that she had come down two miles of open road with the whole bluff in front of her, and that quick chin had stayed level the whole way, on Brom's heels, and on a hedge, and on a boy's geese. Lira looked at the top of everything. She had looked at the tops of walls since a house on a bluff, to see what it would cost to stand there.

Nobody had asked it of her. A healer had set a term at a fire, for one person, and had made him write it; and a girl on a meal-sack had heard, and had taken a share of it without a word, as she would have taken one end of a load.

He did not thank her. She would have asked him what for, and made him say it, and then told him that it was a boot.

"What?" said Lira.

"Nothing. You're sitting on fifty-two sheets."

"Then they'll be flat."

---

He fell back beside Karis when the road went on.

She walked with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as she had since a landing, and her eyes had not left the bluff since it came up over the hedges. She was not looking at its walls.

"It is bedded," she said, before he could speak. "The rock. Do you see the lines in the face of it, under the lowest wall, where the river has cut? Each of those is a floor that was laid down and then covered. I have been trying to count them for a mile, and I lose my place at the seventh."

"At the spring you said what he was. The man under it." He shifted the box. "You said a historical-Path practitioner, and I let it go by, since I'd a mule to fetch. What does one do?"

"I shall tell you what a catalogue says, and you will remember that it is a catalogue." She did not look at him. "The registry keeps a class that it calls historical studies. It is a small one. The Paths in it are of no use on a floor or in a field, and so nobody has troubled to rank them high; I do not know which of them he has. What they have in common is that they are turned on made things. A wall. A seal. The binding of a book. Such a person can say how a thing was put together: what was laid first, and what rests on what, and whether two things came from one hand."

"And what it means?"

"No. Every account of them is careful to say so in its first line. He reads how a thing is built. He does not read what it says."

"That's half."

"It is the half that I have not got." Her voice had gone thin, as it did when she came near the edge of what she had. "I have dated three floors past anything that I can lay paper beside. I have forty-one kinds of sign in one order. And I cannot tell you which of them is holding the others up. I do not need a man to tell me what it says, Cael. I shall do that, if it can be done. I need one who can tell me how it stands."

She went on counting beds of rock, and he let her go ahead.

He turned the phrase over for a furlong, with the box riding on his shoulders. *How a thing is built.* He had not thought to ask before what kind of reader sat under that town. He had only known that there was one more of them.

Then he went over what he was carrying there, a stride at a time; and it was not the box.

There was a station on that crown. Oryn had told him what such a thing was, at a fire, in the plainest words that she owned: it was the one building in a town that was made to read whatever was brought near it. He had stood on the plate of one, once, and it had taken eleven seconds over him and written a word. He had a term in Hesk's book about this one, and he meant to keep it. But the term kept him off a square. It did not tell him what he was keeping off it.

He did not know. There was a place under his breastbone where nothing moved. It had been read three times, on the boards of a loft and at the edge of a marsh, and not once since a square woman stood over him in the grey of a morning and gave him seven words.

*Write it down and stop touching it.*

He had done both. It was a hundred and sixty-one days since he had laid two fingers on his own wrist.

He had kept that order as he kept few things, and he looked at it now as he had learned to look at a slip on a board: for what it said, and not for what he wished that it said. She had said *touching*. She had said what she meant by it, too, standing over him: that a person who has found a place in himself goes back to it every night with his fingers.

He knew what his fingers had been. Three times he had gone down the middle of himself and straight at that place, to learn whether it would let him in. Three times it had turned him; and each time he had fetched the ten up to it, one after another, like a man who tries every key on a ring in a door that has no keyhole. That was touching. He had been asking it something.

There was another way to go. He could let the reading take its own road, and follow where it bent, and ask the place nothing whatever. A person could walk the whole way round a pond and never put his hand in.

He was honest enough to see what he was about. He had an order from the one person whose orders he kept, and he was reading it for the leave that he wanted in it, with its author three weeks and a moor away from telling him that he had read it wrong. A registrar would have been proud of him. He would write that down as well.

But he was walking toward a thing that read, with a thing in him that had never been read; and the healer who owed him that reading had said herself that she did not know what the one would do to the other. He did not see how a person kept a promise about a place sensibly without once looking at what he was keeping away from it.

He lengthened his stride until the second box was beside him.

"At the next post I'm going to lie down behind a hedge for a quarter of an hour."

"That's a long time to lie behind a hedge," said Lira.

"I'm going to read. Myself. As she showed me."

She did not turn her head. Her pace broke by half a stride, and mended.

"Is it the thing that she told you to leave alone?"

"It's beside it. I'll go round, and I'll not go in, and I'll take nothing up to it."

"And she'd call that leaving it alone."

"I don't know what she'd call it. She's gone north with a mule."

"So you've settled what she'd say, and she's not here to say different." There was no unkindness in it. She hitched the beckets higher on her shoulders. "Write down that I asked. When you write it. I'd like it in there that somebody did."

"I'll write it."

"And I'll sit on the bank. If Brom comes round that hedge and finds you flat on your back with a hand on your chest, he'll have you over his shoulder before you can open your eyes, box and all."

---

The second post stood beside a brook, where a plank bridge had been thrown over for people on foot and the carts went through the water. It said six. They had done three miles in an hour and a half, and it was a stick that had set the pace.

Brom sat down on the plank's end and had his right boot off inside a breath. He held the bound foot out over the water without wetting it, and frowned at two toes, and counted on the fingers of one hand to eight.

Vastin did not sit. He put his back against the post and both hands on the head of his stick, and stood with the bound leg thrust out before him, as he had stood at every halt for two days. A man in that binding could get himself down to the ground. It was the getting up again that could not be done without another person's arm; and Cael had watched him measure that at a fold, and at a fork, and at the top of a rise, and choose his feet every time.

There was a gate into the field behind the hedge, hung on a post of the same dressed stone as the mile-mark. Cael lifted its latch. He set his box down inside, on a headland where the grass had not been ploughed, and lay flat in the lee of the bank with his boots toward the brook.

Lira's back came against the far side of the hedge. He heard her settle, and begin to pull the heads off grass.

He laid his right palm on his breastbone. He put the first two fingers of his left hand on the inside of his right wrist, where the healer put hers; and he let his breath go long, as a sleeper's does, and counted.

At ten the short reading gave him the moving. It was his own gait, quick and slow, and a good deal quicker than it ought to have been in a person who was lying down.

He went on beyond ten, into the deep reading. They came up out of it one at a time, as they always had, like the furniture of a room when the eyes have grown used to the dark.

Wind was first, since it was the oldest, and it had always leaned toward a girl. It was a dry hiss in his feet, like pale grass going over in a breath of air. It was good for ten strides, and it had to be paid for at the end of them with a locked beat in which he could not have lifted a heel. He had spent it two days ago in a cut, running away from the people whom he was guarding.

The read went round beneath it, under his soles, low and even: a millstone heard through a floor. That one was Brom's, and it worked for its keep. It would tell him the weight of anything within a pace and a half, and which way the weight was going; and if he leaned on it long it took his near sight for an hour.

Something ticked in his right hand, as a lamp-wick ticks when the oil is poor. That was Ember, a single point in a single place, and the heat of it came home to the hand that held the thing.

Pressure drew and filled under his breastbone, slowly, like a bellows under a tired arm. It went out of him narrow and deep, and it came back at him off anything curved.

Compression made no sound of its own. He found it, as he would have found a rope in a dark barn, by leaning on it: his whole frame creaked, very faintly, like hemp under a load. It wanted both heels down, and ground worth sending a load to.

There was the held note, thin and steady, like a nail that has been driven and has not quite done ringing. Anchor had asked at every sill and gate since Ostrand whether he would not like something set across. He had told it yes twice since the Line, and no more times than he could count.

Higher than that, in his right arm, a wet finger went round the rim of a glass. Blade would put an edge on steel for as long as a struck match burns, and it was the steel that paid.

The ninth he knew by what was missing. By day Shadow lay shut under its seal, and it made no sound whatever; he could tell where it was only as a person tells where a rug lies in a dark room, because the floor does not ring there. It would lift at dusk, as it lifted every dusk. He would tell it no.

Under everything lay Storm, the heaviest, a sea turning over a long way off, so low that he had it in his teeth before he had it in his ears. It was laid a lane at a time, and every lane came with its own bill.

And the tenth was the thing that he was going along with. It had no sound. He knew it as a walker knows his own stride. It had been a healer's, and it read whatever flowed in a living body under his hand, his own or another's. For another's the rule stood over it in two people's writing: *ask first, every time*.

That made ten. He had called them fragments since he was fourteen, and pieces, and entries in a list. Lying in the grass he had them as the going-along had them, which was none of those. They were ten currents, and not one of them had a bank. Each ran on into the next as water runs into water, and a healer had found that out before he did, with both her hands, and had told him so on a ridge.

---

He went down toward the middle.

He did not go at it. He had gone at it three times, and it had cost him an order. He chose one current, the hiss of Wind, and gave the going-along to it as a leaf is given to a stream. He did not steer.

A long way short of the place, the Wind began to lean.

It was farther out than he had ever marked, since on every other night he had been looking ahead, at the place itself. There was no check in the lean, and nothing piled. The Wind bore off as a road bears off round the shoulder of a hill, which has gone that way for as long as there has been a road. He leaned with it. He went by on the one hand, and did not look in; the lean eased, a long way beyond, and the hiss ran on toward his feet as though nothing had ever lain in front of it.

He came back and took the millstone, and it went by on the other hand. He took the wick. He took the bellows, which lived closest of the nine, and bent least far and most sharply. He took every one that had a sound, in its turn, and none of them touched the place or slowed for it. Then he let the going-along go by itself, with nothing to ride on, the whole way round, as a man walks the bounds of a field, until it stood again where it had set out.

It was the first time that he had been all the way round.

He lay and counted what he had, and it was a good deal. He knew where the place was. He knew how far out the leaning began, for each of the nine. He knew that the way round was worn smooth, with never a ripple in it; and he knew now how far it was about. He went down that column with something like pride, as a small pale woman went down a row that agreed.

Then he saw what kind of column it was.

Every line of it was about a thing that moved. It told him where the Wind leaned, and how the millstone went by. There was not one line in it about the place. There never had been. *Doesn't flow. Doesn't change.* He had written those on a leaf, and had taken them for findings about the place; and they were not. They were only the instrument's way of saying where it left off.

The going-along felt current, and it had never done anything else. It had been a healer's means of telling how the rivers ran in a body, quick or slow or choked, and it knew a river only by going along with it. Set it where nothing ran, and it did not come back and say *empty*. It did not come back and say *full*, either. It had nothing to go along with, and so it said nothing at all; and for the better part of a year he had been writing that nothing down in pencil, with the day at its head, as though it had been an answer.

It was a cord on a stone. A man might run a cord round a boulder in a field, and knot it, and carry the knot home with him, and know to the width of a finger how far it was about; and he would know no more of what lay inside the boulder than he had known at breakfast.

He took his fingers from his wrist, and put his right hand down to push himself up.

It came down on the foot of the gatepost. The going-along had not been called home yet. It went down his arm to the palm, as it had gone to a healer's wrist when he had her leave, and there it stood. The stone gave it nothing to travel on. He had always known that it would not; the tenth did nothing whatever on a dead thing, and he had never troubled to think about that.

What held him there, half risen, with his weight on that hand, was that he knew the feel of it.

It was the same nothing. To the tenth of his fragments, a gatepost and the middle of himself came back alike.

He did not care for that at all. He made himself hold it at arm's length until he could see what it was worth, and it was not worth much. It did not say that the place in him was stone, or dead, or of any sort. It said that this instrument could not tell the two apart. Karis kept a rule for exactly this, and had said it twice over a cord in a frozen yard, and he had not understood until now what it cost her to keep. He had learned something about the tool. He had learned nothing about what the tool had been laid on.

He called the going-along home, and it came, and lay down. He was cold through, as he always was after it, and as empty as if he had hauled on a rope all morning.

He needed an instrument for what did not move.

He knew of two. One stood on the crown of the town ahead of him. It had been built to read whatever was brought near it, and he had written in pencil that he would not go within a square's width of the thing. The other sat underneath the same town, with a short strange name, and read how things were built. Whether a man who could tell how a wall stood could tell how a boy did, Cael had no idea.

He lay a moment longer, looking up through the hedge at a sky with nothing in it, and found that he feared the second a good deal more than the first. He had only to keep away from the first. He would have to walk up to the second and ask.

---

Lira was on her feet when he came through the gate, with a stem of grass between her teeth.

"You were as long as Brom's boot," she said. "Did you go in?"

"No. Round."

"And?"

"It'll be in the book tonight. You can read it there."

"You'll not say it?"

"I've not got it in the right order yet. It'll be in order by tonight." He got the box up by one becket, and she steadied the other, without being asked, with her right hand. "I didn't touch it. That part I can say now."

She looked at him for the space of a stride. Then she took the stem out of her mouth and threw it at the brook, and went to get her own box.

The road ran straighter from the brook, between hedges that were older and thicker, and it began to be a town's road. There were carts in both directions. At five miles there was a farm that stood close on the verge, with a woman beating a rug in its yard; at four there was a lane with a signboard at its mouth, and a painted hand on the board, and Seln read whatever stood under the hand without breaking his stride.

The town came up with every furlong. He kept his eyes on its foot, and he learned the foot of it very well. The lowest wall ran clear round the bluff, as far as he could follow it, with square towers at even distances, and he counted eleven of those before the river hid the rest. There was a gate in it with a tower on either hand. A bridge went up to the gate, long and low, and there were things crawling on the bridge that he took for wagons.

He did not raise his eyes above the second wall. He knew where the third was, and what stood within it, as a person knows where a dog is lying in a room.

"That's a deal of wall," said Brom, from ahead. "Somebody's paid for that by the yard. Three times."

"The third is the oldest," said Karis. "It will be the smallest, and the best laid. Each of the others was built when the one inside it grew too full."

"Then it's a tree. With rings."

"It is very like a tree." She sounded pleased. "I had not thought of putting it so."

"I've split enough of them."

The stick had taken its place again on Cael's left, on the ditch side. It had not lost a stride on him in six miles. He could hear what that was costing, since the breath beside him came short at each planting of the bound leg, and the tarred case had been shifted twice to the other shoulder and twice back.

"I saw a man go through a gate, at that brook," said Vastin.

He said it to the road in front of him, at about the fourth mile, in a voice pitched for one hearer.

"He was gone for some time. He came back the colour of that road, and a young woman who had been set to keep the hedge asked him three questions and was content with half an answer." The stick came down. "I say so in order that you may know it was seen. I have found that people on this side of the Line prefer to be told. I do not ask what it was."

"No," said Cael.

"I should have asked, once. I should have asked it in writing, and required the answer under two hands by the week's end."

"I know. I was there."

"So you were."

They went another hundred yards. A cart went by them with a load of slates, and the carter touched his cap to the boxes.

"I shall want a quarter of an hour of you before that town," said Vastin. "Not on the road. Somewhere that a man can stop. I have carried a thing three hundred miles that is not in any flat, and I find that I should like to set it down before I go through a gate behind you."

Cael turned that over. He had kept a question for that man since a board at Lowmarch, and had thought himself the only one of the two who was carrying anything.

"Tonight," he said. "There'll be a halt. They'll shut those gates at dusk, and we'll not be at them by dusk, at this pace."

"At my pace. You may say it."

"At your pace."

"Thank you." The old man drew a breath, and let it out. "It is a curious thing, to be the slowest in a company. I had never had the opportunity."

The third post came up out of the afternoon, a long way ahead, where the hedges ended and the river meadows began. It was too far off to read; but there was a figure on it, and under the figure three short bars.

# Chapter 21 — The Road's Edge

They came to the third post with the sun a hand above the town, and they were not going to do the last three miles.

Seln had known it for some time. He had read two boards at two lane-ends, and both had said the same thing in the same paint: the gate was shut at sunset and opened at first light, and whoever was outside it slept outside it. A stick could not have done three miles in what was left of the day. Nobody said so, since nobody needed to; and it was Vastin himself who looked at the sun, and at the figure on the stone, and stopped.

"Here," he said. "I shall not have five people locked out of a town on my account and told that they chose it."

There was a place made for it: a strip of trodden ground between the road and the river meadows, as long as four wagons, with a stone trough at one end and a ring of blackened stones at the other, and a thorn hedge along its weather side. A bank and a ditch ran the length of it at the road's edge, and carters who missed the gate had been pulling in there for longer than the post had stood.

Two of them had it tonight, with a wagon of hurdles under a sheet, and four horses on a line, and a fire at the trough's end. The elder lifted one hand from his knee when the boxes came in off the road; he looked at the loads, and at the old man on the stick, and went back to his pot. He did not ask where they were from, and it came to Cael that inside a hedge a person was not asked.

Brom built the fire in the ring, from thorn that he bought of the carters for a copper.

Karis looked at the heap when it was laid, and then put both her hands under her arms and stood back from it. Brom felt in his coat. He brought out a steel and a flint and a twist of charred rag, which Cael had not seen in his fingers since before a small pale woman joined them, and he knelt and struck. It took him four tries.

"I'd lost the trick," he said, when it caught.

"You have been spoiled," said Karis. She was watching the flame as a person watches somebody else do her work badly. "So have I. I have not been cold at a fire-laying for years. I had not known that I minded."

"It's one point."

"It is one point, on a town's ground, three miles from a gate. Seln?"

# Chapter 22 — The Grey Bridge

The carters were gone before the two hundred and eighty-second day had any light in it, and they had left their fire raked out and a stack of thorn beside the ring for whoever came next.

The six did the last three miles through river meadows, in a white mist that lay as high as Brom's belt. The road ran on a causeway there, with a ditch on either hand, and the town stood up out of the mist ahead of them like a thing set on a table. Cael kept his eyes on the foot of it. He had done so for a day now, and it had begun to be a habit of his neck.

Then the causeway lifted, and they were at the river.

It was wider than he had judged from the rise, and it ran brown and quick with the thaw of a whole moor in it. A bridge went over, long and low, with five piers standing in the water. He counted them twice, since Karis would ask. The roadway was only wide enough for one cart, with a bay built out over each pier where a person on foot could stand aside; and beyond the last pier there were two square towers with an arch between them, and the grey wall going away on both hands along the bank.

Karis had stopped.

She had stopped so short that he all but walked the box into her. She stood on the bank at the bridge's end, with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, and she was not looking at the gate.

"That is not the same stone," she said.

Nobody answered, since she was plainly not speaking to any of them yet.

"The wall is the bluff's stone. It is pale, and it lies in beds, and it has been laid in mortar, in courses, by people who were paid by the yard. I have seen forty such walls." She took three quick steps down the bank, to where she could see the first pier side on, and bent, with the calfskin sticking out behind her like a wing. "This is dark. It has no beds in it at all. And there is no mortar. Brom. Come and look at this, and tell me whether there is mortar."

Brom set his box down. He went and stood beside her, with his head on one side, and studied the pier for some time, as he would have studied any pier.

"There's none," he said. "It's cut to fit. You'd not get a knife in. Whoever laid that never meant to come back and point it."

"And the tower. Look where the tower's foot comes down on the bridge's end." Her voice had gone up, and thin. "The courses do not meet. The mason has cut each of his pale stones to lie against the dark, one at a time, as a joiner cuts a shelf to fit a crooked wall. Nobody does that with a thing that he is building at the same time. He does it when the thing was standing there before he came, and he has been told to build up against it."

"So the bridge was first," said Lira.

"The bridge was first. The bridge was here when there was no wall for it to lead to." She straightened, and she had gone a little pink, which he had seen perhaps three times. "I can say that. I can say it on the evidence of my own eyes, on a river bank, without a catalogue. It is the first thing that I have been able to say about that town which I did not read on the spine of somebody else's book."

"How much older?" said Cael.

"I do not know. I should want to see the bed of the river, and I should want a week." She looked along the five piers, hungrily, as Brom looked at a pot. In his picture there had been a counter across the arch, and a man behind the counter with a pen, and a line of people giving their names one after another. He went on up the paved slope beyond the arch with his box on his shoulders, and his heart going as it had gone on the lip of a cut, and found that he could not have said whether he was relieved or insulted.

It was Vastin whom he turned to see.

The old man came over the sill on his stick, with the tarred case on his shoulder. The keeper's eyes were on the stick's foot. The thumb moved one bead, and stayed there, and the grey head did not lift.

Cael saw the right hand leave the stick.

It went a little way toward the breast of the brown coat, where a man carries his papers. It was the hand of somebody who had come to forty years of gates and had at each of them been met at the sill, by a person standing, with a book already open. It hung in the air for the space of one breath. Then it came back, and found the ash, and gripped it.

"Six," said the keeper, to his frame.

Vastin walked through the gate of Treswick as the sixth bead on a wire, and nobody stood up.

---

The street beyond the arch was as wide as a field.

It ran straight from the gate toward the bluff's foot, between tall houses of the pale stone, and it was full from wall to wall of things being moved. There were carts, and handcarts, and people bent under frames, and barrels rolled along by boys. There was a great noise of wheels on cobbles. After two hundred and eighty days of roads on which nobody spoke, it came against Cael's ears like a hand.

"Stand out of it," said Seln, and put them into a doorway. "Look for a little, before you walk in it. It is not a crowd. It is a mechanism, and it will tell you how it works if you are civil enough to watch."

There was a board at every door.

Cael saw them when he had been shown. Each was a slab of black slate as tall as a child, hung beside the step, and each was ruled with chalk into the same columns. He read the nearest upside down by old habit, and then turned his head and read it the right way. *Wanted*, said its head. Under that, a load, or a number of hands. Then a place: *the landing*, or *the second wall*, or once, *the third*. Then a rate. And at the row's end there was a daub of colour, which was blue against the second wall and a pale buff against the third, and against the landing nothing at all.

"Those are the brokers," said Seln. "Each house buys below and sells above. Nothing that is eaten or burned or worn inside the two upper walls is grown there. It comes in at that gate, and it stops in this street, and then it must be carried up a hill by somebody."

"And the colour?" said Lira.

"Is the pass that goes with the work. A person without the tier may not live above a wall. But the wall cannot do without him by day. So it lets him in by the piece. He has a slip, with a coloured edge, that says which door he is going to and how many he is; he is counted in at the gate above, with a pen, and he is counted out again before dusk."

There was a long bench against the house across the way, and a dozen people sat on it, men and women, with carrying-frames between their knees. While the six watched, a clerk in an apron came out of the next door and rubbed a line off his slate. He chalked another. Four of the dozen got up without a word and went to him, and each was given a slip of card; and Cael saw the blue on its edge as it went into a cap.

"It's day-labour," said Brom.

"It is counted labour. There is a difference, and you will have it by tonight."

Lira had read four boards while he spoke, with her lips going.

"Everything up there is carried up by somebody down here," she said. "And then they're let out at dusk like hens."

"That is very nearly how the code puts it," said Vastin.

They walked in the mechanism after that, close along the house-fronts, and it took no more notice of them than a mill takes of a mouse. It was plain that the street had its own roads within it. Loads went up on the right hand and came down empty on the left. Things on wheels kept the crown of the cobbles. Nobody had painted a line, and nobody crossed one.

Half way along, a lane went off toward the river, and there was a smell down it of tar and new hemp. Brom's head came round like a dog's.

"That's a walk," he said. "I'll be as long as it takes to look."

It was a shed, open all down one side, and Cael had never seen a building so long. It ran away along the inside of the town wall for three hundred yards. Down the middle of it, people were walking backward with bundles of hemp at their waists, paying out yarn between their fingers, while a wheel turned at the shed's head. Coils hung on pegs the whole length of the wall, tarred and white.

Brom went along the pegs. He passed three-strand without a glance. He stopped at a coil as thick as his thumb, and took a fathom of it off the peg, and bent it double, and looked at the cut end as a person looks into a flower.

"Four," he said. "Round a heart. That'd live in a block."

A woman with tarred hands had come up beside him. He asked her the price of sixty yards. She named it, and it was a fair one, since he nodded as he nodded at a wall that stood plumb.

"I'll take it going out," he said. "I'll not carry sixty yards of rope up a hill to carry it down again."

"It'll be the same going out."

"Then it'll keep. It's kept since the snow."

He hung the fathom back on its peg, exactly as he had found it. Cael had watched that debt ride in a big man's head since before the snow, through a whole cold term, and over a river that wanted the rope and could not be paid in the wrong kind. It was the first time that he had seen Brom look at it without frowning.

---

The street ended in an open place, where the ground began to climb.

There was a trough there, forty feet of hollowed stone with a pipe running into its upper end, and a row of beasts drinking at it with their carters beside them. One of the beasts was a mule. She was small, and brown, and she stood with her rump to the wind and her ears laid out sideways, entirely at her ease.

Brom had gone half a dozen strides toward her before he stopped.

He stood in the middle of that open place with a box on his shoulder. He looked at her feet, the near fore and the off, and then at the others.

"It's not her," said Lira.

"I know that it's not." He did not move. "This one's brown all the way down. Ours has the white foot."

"He's counted bowls for a day and a night," said Lira, to nobody. "Now he's counting mules by the leg."

"She's a day and a half north, with oats," said Cael.

"I know where she is." Brom turned away from the trough at last. "I only looked."

The smith's stood on the upper side of the open place, and it had two fires.

Cael marked nothing in that at first; a busy smith might well keep two. It was Brom who slowed, and then stood, and then set his box down on its end and folded his arms on the top of it, as a man does who means to be some time. He had spent a good many nights of his boyhood on a heap of sacks in a shed where his father kept a forge for the pleasure of hitting something, and he looked at a fire as Karis looked at a seal.

The left-hand forge was the kind that Cael knew. It had a hood, and a bed of charcoal, and a great leather bellows with a lad swinging on the pole. A smith in a scorched apron was fitting a shoe to a carter's cob, in a stink of burned hoof, and three more carters waited behind with their beasts on short ropes.

The right-hand forge had no bellows.

It had no lad, and no smoke. Its bed was white from edge to edge, with the quiet evenness of a thing that is not being fed; and beside it, well back, stood a woman in a long coat of dark blue with her hands in her apron pockets. She was not doing anything, as far as could be seen. She looked like somebody waiting for a ferry. A second smith worked at that fire, and the horse under his hand was a tall bay with a blue band braided into its forelock, held by a groom in a clean jerkin. Nothing else was waiting.

"That is an Ember," said Karis, very low. She was looking at the woman's hands, through the apron. "She is holding the whole bed. Nine points, I should think, or ten, and she has held them since the bell. It is Rank Two work, at the most." She was silent. "It is steady. It is a great deal steadier than charcoal."

"Is the shoe better?" said Lira.

Brom did not answer at once. He was watching the two smiths' hands.

"It's the same bar," he said. "Look. They're both drawing off the one rack, by the door. Same iron. Same nails; that's one keg between them. He's making the same shoe at both fires, and the white one costs him a woman's wages to keep." He scratched his jaw. "The horse can't tell. A hoof's a hoof."

"Then what's the second fire for?"

"For whoever owns the horse."

He looked a moment longer, at the blue band in a bay's forelock, and at a carter's cob with a rope round its neck.

"They shoe by the tier."

He picked up his box. Nobody added anything, and Cael did not think that anything could have been added. He had spent the whole of his life since his Kindling learning how the Compact sorted people. He had read it in codes, and argued it at hearings, and had it explained to him by a registrar with a mirror. Brom had found the whole of it in a smith's yard, in the time that it takes to drink a cup of water, and had said it in five words.

Beyond the smith's the ground went up steeply, and one street climbed it. At its head, two hundred yards above them, stood the second wall, with an arch in it; and in the arch were two figures in long coats of that same dark blue. Cael looked at the coats. He did not look higher.

Lira was looking at them too. Her right hand hung at her side, and her left was on the becket at her shoulder, and neither of them went near the breast of her coat.

Seln found the inn.

It was a long house one street back from the trough, with a carriage-arch through the middle of it. There were two ways in, and they were not ten strides apart. On the street side there was a painted door, with a step, and a lamp over it. It stood open; and just within, on a stand, lay a book with a pen beside it on a chain.

The other was under the arch. It was a half-door in the side wall, with a ledge on top, and a woman leaned on the ledge with her sleeves rolled. Behind her were the yard, and a pump, and a long stable with a loft above it.

"That one has a book," said Seln, of the first. "This one has a woman who wishes to be paid."

"The long loft," the woman said, when Brom asked. She had counted them as the keeper had, by the feet. "It's over the horses, so it's warm. A week, the six of you: two marks, in front. That's the pot at dusk as well, a bowl a head."

Brom counted two marks onto the ledge, and she swept them off it with the side of her hand.

"A hundred and thirty-six," he said, to the purse.

"And a seventh bowl," he added.

"There's six of you."

"There's seven bowls."

She looked at him as a person looks at a horse that may be going to kick, and then she shrugged with one shoulder.

"It's your pot," she said.

Vastin had stopped in the arch. He stood between the two doors, and his eyes were on the painted one, and on the step, and on the lamp. Cael saw the bound leg begin to turn toward it of its own accord, as a horse turns in at its own gate. Then the old man looked down at the leg, and brought it round, and came in by the yard.

---

The first of the five gates lay a day and a half north of the fork, under the hill-foot, and Oryn came to it at dusk on the second day with the mule's shadow going long in front of her.

It was a small hold. It had a wall of field-stone, and a gate of oak bars, and a gate-house built against the wall's outer face, with a bench along it under a roof of turf. She had sat on that bench on thirty rounds. She had never once been through the gate, and it was a long while since anybody there had asked her to come in.

There were five on the bench. Somebody had seen a mule on the hill road an hour ago, and they had come out and sat down.

She did not eat first. She tied the mule, and washed to the elbow at the gate-house butt, and went along the five at her stride and a half, with two fingers for each wrist and her lips moving to ten. A child with a cough that was nearly gone. An old man's hip, which she could ease and not mend. A woman who was only tired, and needed to be told that she was allowed to be. A boy with a boil.

The fifth was a carter, a big slow man with a red beard, and he was not of that hold. He sat at the bench's end, holding his right wrist in his left hand as a person holds a cup that is too full.

"You're Oxhollow's," she said. "I've seen you on the ford with a grey pair."

"I was. I'm here for the hay." He did not look at her. "You never came."

"No. I was seventeen days late to your gate before the snow, and I'd a day there and no more; and then the cold shut the top road, and it's only now let me up it from this end."

"I broke it a week after you'd gone by. Under a wheel, on the ice. The keeper's wife strapped it, at the gate, with a wet strap and two spoons. She did her best."

"I don't doubt that she did. Wrist. May I?"

He held it out to her, which was not an answer.

"I'll have it said."

"Yes, then."

She took it in both hands, and counted, and went in.

It was a river with a stone dropped in it. The arm's current came down to the joint, strong enough, a carter's; and at the joint it went round. It ought to have gone through. She followed it, as she would have followed a path round a fallen tree, and found the place: two ends of bone that had been laid a thumb's breadth off one another, and had done what bone does, and joined where they lay. It was good, sound, patient knitting. The body had made the best of what it was given, as the keeper's wife had.

She came out, and laid the wrist back in his other hand.

"It's joined," she said, "and it's joined wrong. It'll hold a spoon. It'll not hold a rein with a pair of mules on the end of it, and it'll ache worse every cold term till you're old."

"Can you mend it?"

"I can't mend a thing that's whole. It'd have to be parted again, where it's knit, and laid straight; and then I can knit it, and it'll be a wrist." She let him have that. She had never found a way of saying it that was easier to hear. "That's a hurt, and I'd be the one doing it, on purpose. I'll not do it tonight. I've walked a day and a half, and I don't put my hands on that sort of work tired. And I'll not do it at all unless you tell me to in the morning, when you've slept on it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you've a wrist that pours soup, and I've four more gates."

She saw to the other four, and ate what the gate-house woman brought out, and lay down in the lean-to beside the mule with her coat over her.

She did not sleep at once. There had been a Gold, in a bed by a window, with a wrenched wrist. It had not even been broken. He had had six healers' attention on the first morning, and had gone home before noon; and a woman from the river sheds had waited three nights at the bottom of a heap. Oryn had walked out of a gate to be quit of those sums.

A carter had waited a whole cold term, with a crooked wrist, for want of her.

Nobody had sorted him to the bottom. There was no column out here. There was a top road, and snow on it, and one healer with one pair of feet; and the snow had done exactly what a board at a bed's foot would have done, and had not needed a clerk. She could not fault those sums, either. She had only liked them better because nobody had written them down.

She turned over, and the mule shifted its feet.

And there was a thing that she had not got to at all. She had walked away from it at a fork two days south, with her sleeve half pushed up, and had told a boy that it would keep three weeks. She had read a hundred bodies since she first laid hands on his. She knew a wrist with a stone in it when she met one. She had met something in him a long while back that everything went round, as that carter's current went round a bad join; and she had not been able to say then whether it was a join, or a stone, or a thing that had always lain there. She had told him to leave it be. Then she had walked north, owing him the one reading that might have told her.

She did not like to owe. She had said so, and it had not made the road any shorter.

He said yes in the morning, with his chin down. She had the hold's smith stand behind him, and gave him a strap to bite, and asked him once more, and had his answer. It took no longer than a breath. He made a sound like a man lifting a cart.

Then she had her hands on it, and she was in, and she spent.

It took an hour. She spent as she had not spent since a cut on a moor, laying the two ends true and holding them so while the body was coaxed into beginning again; and when she sat back, the yard had gone grey at its edges and somebody was holding a cup against her teeth.

"Six weeks in that binding," she said, when she could. "Nothing lifted with it. And if you break yourself again where there's no healer, you tell whoever's by to bind it straight and wait. Straight, and wait. I can do a deal with straight."

"What's owed?"

"Oats for the mule. And tell that keeper's wife that she kept the hand alive. She'll have thought about it all the cold."

She had meant to be on the road by sunrise. It was past noon before her legs would have it. Three weeks had been five gates and a ford, with one day in hand; and she had spent the day at the first gate, on a carter. She untied the mule, and turned its head along the hill-foot, and began to walk it off.

# Chapter 23 — Two Doors

The long loft ran the whole length of the stable, under a roof of slates that a person could touch with his hand, and it smelled of hay and warm horse.

There were a dozen straw pallets along one side of it, and a rope to hang wet things on, and a single window in the gable at the far end. The window looked up the hill. Cael saw so much from the ladder's head, and no more, since he put his eyes on the floor; and by the time that he had his box off, Lira had crossed the boards, and taken a horse-blanket from a peg, and hung it over the glass on two nails.

"For the draught," she said.

"There's no draught," said Brom. "It's as tight as a cask in here."

"There is now."

Nobody argued with her. Brom looked at the blanket for a moment, and at Cael's back, and began to lay the three boxes in a row under the eaves with their beckets to the wall.

Karis had not sat down. She stood in the middle of the loft with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as though she had been set there by somebody who would be back for her directly.

"It is two hundred yards," she said. "I counted them from the trough. There is a gate, and a street, and another gate, and the thing that I have wanted for the whole of my life is under the second. I should like somebody to tell me why I am standing in a hayloft."

"Because of the blue coats," said Brom. "I'm Copper. That's this street and the one behind it. I read the boards."

"Iron walks through," said Lira. She was shaking out a pallet, and she spoke as she would have spoken of the weather. "That's what the blue is. It's not a pass at all, up there; it's what the place is for. You show the paper and you go by."

She did not touch her coat. Cael marked that, since he had been listening for it. At a board in Lowmarch she had laid her hand on the pocket, and said what was in it, and that she had almost forgotten which side; she had spoken then of a certificate, and she spoke now of a wall.

"I have Iron, of the third rank, on registry paper," said Karis. "She has it of the first. Either of us could be at that arch in the time that it takes to climb a street."

"And be written," said Seln.

He stood by the ladder's head, where he could hear the yard.

"The man below counts, since feet are all that he is asked for; the men above do not count those who come by right. They read. A certificate is a name, and the district that issued it, and a number, and it is entered at the gate, with the hour, and goes up to a counter before night to be laid against a list." He did not raise his voice. "There are four names in this loft which must not be written in any book inside that wall. I told you why on the ninth day, and a man who used to certify such papers told you again at a board. One hour is what the paper allows; you would not be at the third gate before it was served."

"And yours?" said Brom. "You said four. There's six of us."

"Mine is not on that paper. I do not propose to offer it to a gate for all that. I should be asked where I had been employed."

So that left one; and Cael felt the loft come round to it, as he had once felt a table take care not to look at himself.

An old man leaned at the foot of the gable, under the blanket, with both hands on his stick. He had come in by a yard, and climbed a ladder on one leg with a healer's laths on the other, and had not yet been heard to say what he thought of either. There was a flatness under the breast of the brown coat, on the left side. Cael had taken it for a receipt.

Vastin had seen them turn. He did not wait to be asked.

"I have papers I will not show. I want that said once, so that nobody asks twice."

It was said without heat. He looked at each of the five in turn, as he looked at a room, and gave every one of them the same length of time.

"They would open both of those gates, and a good deal more than the gates: a man in a pale coat would come down that hill to meet me, at a run, with a clerk behind him carrying a chair." His mouth moved very slightly. "And from that minute you would not be six people with boxes. You would be an officer's party; your number would be taken, and your business, as a courtesy to me, and the whole of it would be minuted by evening and read by somebody whom I cannot name to you. I do not know who reads this town's books at night. A cold term ago I should have said that I did."

"So there's three ways through that gate in this loft," said Lira. "And we're not using one of them."

"We shall use the fourth," said Seln. "I am going to find out what it is. I shall be some hours, and nobody is to go above the trough while I am gone."

"And I am to sit in a hayloft," said Karis, "two hundred yards beneath it."

"You are to sit in a hayloft and not be written down. It is harder than it sounds. I have done it for most of my life."

He went down the ladder without a sound. Karis looked after him; and then she lowered herself onto the nearest pallet, very carefully, with a courier's flat held in both arms against her chest.

---

Cael went out an hour after noon.

He had not meant to. He had lain on a pallet with his hands behind his head and listened to the street, and he had found that he could not lie under a town which he had not looked at. He had looked at the foot of it for a day. He wished to see how it stood.

"Which way?" said Lira. She had sat up as soon as his boots moved.

"Round. Along under the second wall, with the wall on my left hand."

She saw it at once. With the wall on his left, the hill would be on his left as well; and whatever stood on the crown of the hill would be behind his shoulder for the whole of the walk, where he need do nothing about it.

"Don't go up anything," she said, and lay down again.

A road ran round the foot of the bluff, under the second wall, and he found it by going uphill until he could go no farther. It was cobbled, and it was narrow, and the houses on its upper side had been built straight against the wall's foot, so that their back rooms were the town's own masonry. He walked with one hand almost on their doors. The wall went up above the roofs on his left, pale and sheer, as high as the scree in a cut that he remembered.

He came to the gate from the side.

The road opened there into a small paved place, where the street from the trough came up to meet the arch. There was a cook-house built against the wall on the near hand, with smoke going up from it, and a water-butt at its corner. Cael put his shoulder against the butt and stood. Nobody looks twice at a person who is waiting beside water.

*There?* said Anchor, of the arch. It was a tall one, with a groove cut down each jamb where something heavy could be let fall. He told it no.

Two keepers stood in the arch, in long coats of dark blue. One had a board hung from his neck by a strap, with a sheet on it, and a pen. The other had nothing in his hands at all, and did the looking.

He watched for a quarter of an hour, by the shadow of the butt, and it was as plain as a mill.

There were people whom the keepers knew. They were the greater number: a woman with a basket and a good cloak, and a pair of clerks, and an old man with a dog. They went by with a nod, and the pen did not move. They lived on the other side of that arch, and they had gone through it twice a day since they were children.

There were strangers who came by right. He saw only one, a young man in travelling boots, who had the look of a courier. He took a folded paper from his coat without being asked, and the keeper with empty hands opened it and read it all, and read out a name and a district and a rank to his fellow; and the pen wrote for as long as it takes to say a line. The young man had his paper back and went up. He had been perfectly easy. He had been written in a book as a person is who has never had cause to mind it.

And there were the slips.

They came up the street in knots, with frames on their backs, and each knot had one card among it with a blue edge. The keeper took the card. He did not look at a single face. He looked at the figure on the card, and then he pointed with the feather of his pen at each cap as it went under the arch, and his lips moved. When the caps and the figure agreed, he made one stroke on his sheet, tore the corner off the card, and gave the card back.

"Three," Cael heard him say, to a load of firewood. "Three. Go on."

A girl came up with a basket of eggs. She had a card. The keeper read it, and shook his head, and tapped the card with his pen.

"That's the forenoon's. It's past the bell."

She said something that Cael could not hear, quickly, with her chin up.

"I know they're fresh. They'll be fresh in the morning." He was not unkind about it. He gave her the card, and pointed down the hill with the pen; and she went and sat on the kerb with the basket on her knees, and looked at the arch as a person looks at a shop that has shut.

Cael took his shoulder off the butt.

He had been afraid of that gate since a back room, and he saw now that he had been afraid of the wrong thing. He had pictured a machine that would catch him and grind. This one had not teeth of that kind. It sorted whatever it could see, by a colour, and what it could not sort it did not see at all. A girl with eggs was a card with the wrong hour on it, and a carter was a number of caps.

There was a colour for Copper, even. It was the street below, and the one behind.

He had no tier of any sort. He stood by a water-butt, four strides from two men who read people for their living, with a box-strap's mark still red on his neck; and neither of them had so much as wondered what he was. There was no sieve in that town fine enough to hold him. He had fallen clean through the lowest.

---

He went on round, and the road began to go down.

It came out above the river on the bluff's far side, where the town wall ran along a quay, and there was a landing. He had not seen a flat boat since Lowmarch. There were six of them here, tied nose to tail, and a great wooden wheel on the quay with a man walking inside it, round and round, so that a sack went up on a rope at every dozen steps. A ferry lay at the landing's lower end, on a cable. He looked at the cable's blocks for Brom's sake, and could not have said what lay the rope was.

The rope-walk came down to the quay there, and ended, in a yard full of tar barrels.

There were children in the yard. They had a stick, which two of them held across the mouth of an alley at the height of a belt, and the others were coming up to it one at a time.

"What are you?"

"Iron!"

"Show."

A leaf was shown, with great ceremony.

"Go by. What are you?"

"Copper."

"Round by the wall. You know that. You always try it."

The smallest came last. It was a child of perhaps four, of no kind that Cael could determine, in a smock to its ankles, and it stood before the stick with its hands behind it.

"What are you?"

"Nothing."

"You can't be nothing. You've to be something, or you can't play."

The child thought about that. Cael found that he had stopped walking, by a barrel, and that he very much wished to hear the answer.

"Then I'm the gate," it said, and took hold of the middle of the stick with both fists.

There was an uproar. He went on before it was settled, since a stranger who stands and watches children is marked in any town; but he carried it with him up the quay, and it was a little while before he could have said whether it had made him want to laugh.

The road brought him round at last, by the back of the smith's, into the open place where the trough was. He had gone the whole circuit of the bluff, and he had not once lifted his eyes above a line of pale stone.

He stopped at the foot of the street that climbed.

It began there, between the smith's and a chandler's, at a kerb where the cobbles of the open place gave way to setts that had been laid aslant for the grip of hooves. It went straight up. He could follow it with his eyes on the ground: two hundred yards to the blue coats, and through, and on under slate roofs, growing narrower, to a second arch where the coats were a paler colour. Beyond that he did not follow it.

An old woman sat on the kerb at his elbow, with a carrying-frame beside her and her boots off.

"Boards are down the street," she said. She had looked at his neck, where the strap had been. "There's nothing going up before the second bell."

"I'm not after work. I was looking for where the registry is."

"That's the top." She pointed with a bare heel, without troubling to get up. "Straight up. Through the blue, and through the buff, and then you're in the square. You go across it. It's the door that faces the steps." She spat, neatly. "I've carried paper to it for thirty years. You'll not get that far in those boots without a card."

"Across the square."

"There's no other way to a front door, is there? It's a square. It's got the one thing on one side of it and the other thing on the other, and you walk between."

He thanked her. She had shut her eyes already.

So that was how it lay. He stood with the toes of his boots against the kerb, and looked at the first of the slanted setts, a hand's breadth in front of them.

He had written two sentences in a book, at a fire, for a healer who would not take his word. They had been easy to write. The square had been two days off, in a town that he had never seen, and a thing which is two days off costs nothing to forgo. He had not known that it would stand at the head of the only street; that the record he had walked here for would be kept behind a door on its far side; or that the two of them would face one another across forty yards of paving, like a pair of people who have been set to keep each other honest.

He could break it by walking. That was all that the promise was made of. There were four hundred yards of hill, and two gates that had not seen him, and a paved place.

He did not put his foot on the sett.

It was not a hard thing to refrain from, today. He had no card, and a blanket hung over a window for him in a hayloft. He thought that it would be harder on the day when he had a card. He turned his back on the street, and went down past the trough, where a brown mule that was not theirs lifted her head and looked through him.

---

Vastin had sat down.

He had done it in the lower room of the inn, which opened off the yard: a long low place with one table down the middle of it and a bench on either side, a hearth at the far end, and a hatch through which bowls came. He had sat on the bench's end with the bound leg laid along it. There was a post behind him, and a strap hung from the post, and he had marked both before he let himself down. He would be able to get up by them. He had sat twice now in two days, and he noted, with the interest that he gave to any finding, that the second time had cost him less than the first.

He had come in by a yard.

He turned that over. In forty years he had entered some hundreds of houses on the seat's business, and he could not call to mind one in which he had seen the back of the kitchen door. He saw this one now. It had been mended with a board of a different wood, and somebody had cut a tally into the frame with a knife, in fives.

Every district that he had ever inspected had been told that he was coming.

He had known it, of course. An officer of his grade did not arrive; he was announced, by a form, four days before, and the form went to the very people whom he came to look at. So he had ridden for forty years through streets that had been swept the day before, past whitewashed kerbs, to offices where every book lay squared on its desk and a man stood at the sill with his cap in his hand. He had allowed for it, as a careful officer allows for the known error of a scale, and had supposed that he could subtract the whitewash and arrive at the street.

He looked through the open door, and across the yard, and out under the carriage-arch, where a strip of the street went by in the sun.

It was filthy. It was loud, and exact, and nobody in it had been told anything. A boy went past with a yoke of slops. Two porters were quarrelling over a card, with their foreheads almost touching. A woman had been turned away from the painted door ten minutes since; he had heard the whole of it, *that side's for names, mistress, you want the arch*, and had heard her come in by the arch, grumbling, and pay.

It was the first honest district that he had ever been in.

He had certified this town on four occasions. He had put his name to the soundness of its station, and the order of its gates, and the keeping of its books. He had ridden over a bridge without knowing that it had piers. It had taken a stick, and a brown coat, and a place in the count of a keeper's thumb, to show him what any carter saw from his seat.

There was a keeper at the hearth.

He was off his watch, by the unbuttoned collar; but he had kept the blue coat on, and he sat in the one chair that had a back. A bowl had come to him through the hatch before he asked for it. He had not paid. The carters along the bench had moved up for him as water moves for a boat, without looking round, and the bench had closed again behind him.

Vastin's right hand left his knee.

*Irregular. Subsistence taken in a licensed house by an officer of the gate, without payment; to be drawn to the notice of—*

It was half way to his breast before he saw it go. He looked at it. It was an old man's hand, with a strip of linen round the palm, and it had been about to write a district's keeper into a report for a bowl of barley.

A small grey man had told him at a spring what that hand was. He had not much cared to be told, and it had been true. He laid the hand on his knee again, palm down, and he looked at the room and did nothing to it.

A keeper was eating, and the bench had made room. That was all that was in front of him.

There was a paper under his hand, where it had stopped, and it was not the only one. He carried, in oilskin, three sheets under one seal, and they were what he had told a loft that he would not show; and beside them lay a receipt in a small upright hand, unsealed, which said that certain things had been read in part and understood in no part. He had read that sentence a good many times on the road, and he thought it the more exact document of the two.

And he carried one thing that was not paper.

A boy had given it to him on a bank, at dusk, across a yard of turf. He had it by heart, as he had always had a finding: there was a place, which a healer had found; it had been read twice in a day; nothing had come back. He did not know what had read it. A man of his training could set out the possibilities, in their order, and on the road that morning he had begun to; and he had reached the second, and seen that it was a draft, and stopped.

He was not accustomed to holding a thing that he had not been given the whole of. He found that he could do it. It was like the leg.

Out under the arch, a clerk in an apron had come to the bench across the way. Four people got up. A card went into a cap, and the four went off up the hill with frames on their backs, and not one of them had been asked so much as a name.

He watched them out of sight. The lowest thing in that district went through its walls on the least ink. He set that beside the keeper's bowl, as a fact about a mechanism, and could not have said why he had troubled to keep it.

A bowl came down on the table in front of him.

It was the large young man who had set it there, and he had six others on a board. Vastin felt for a copper. He was told, as he had been told on four evenings running, to put it away.

"I had not known," he said, "that a man could be fed for four days and still be owed nothing."

"You've not been fed. You've been counted." Brom set the seventh bowl at the table's end, by itself. "It's different. Eat it while it's hot."

# Chapter 24 — Noon

Karis did not choose.

The day, when it came, was the two hundred and eighty-third. She had the second box unroped before there was any sun, and its lid off, and she knelt on the boards of the loft with fifty-two sheets in three heaps before her: eleven, and twenty-nine, and twelve. They were her own first copies, the ones that she had drawn on her knees under a ring of stones, and on a stair, and in a drowned hall, with a lantern held low. Cael had carried them a long way. He had not seen them out of their wrapper since a cold term.

"I had meant to take him the best," she said. "I lay awake over which they were: there are three from the Stair that came out clean, where the cutting was deep, and one from the Hall that I am proud of." She squared the third heap with the side of her hand. "And then I saw what I was doing, which was choosing what a man was to think before he had looked. If I show him my four best, I have shown him four things that a careful person can draw; so I shall take him all of it, with the bad sheets in their places."

"To a cook-house counter," said Brom.

"One, to the counter, since I am not a fool; and the rest under my arm, in their order, where he can see how thick the roll is."

She had a square of oilcloth for them, and a tape; and Seln watched her tie it, from the ladder's head, with a carrying-frame on his back that he had hired for the day and a keg lashed to the frame. He had been down to the boards before the lamp was out and had come back with a card in his cap, and the card's edge was buff.

"Lamp oil," he said, when Lira looked at it. "It is for a chandler inside the third wall: one hand, counted in at both gates and out again before the fourth bell. I shall have the better part of a forenoon up there, with a reason to be seen in every lane; and by tonight I shall know how a load goes to that yard."

"And us?"

"You will rope three boxes for a stair, as though you knew that there was one, and Brom will keep off that foot. At noon there will be two of you at the cook-house, and no more." He turned his eyes on Karis, and then on Cael. "One at the counter, who has something to show; and one in the wood-yard, with a bowl in his hand, who is nobody. The yard is where porters are fed, through a hatch, and its door stands open to the room for the heat, so that a person may hear from there and need not be seen."

"Why is it him?" said Lira. There was no complaint in it; she wished to have it right.

"Since he has asked," said Seln. "He asked me last night, on the ladder, when the rest of you were asleep."

Cael had. He had lain with a strange short word going round in him until the lantern was out, and then he had got up, and found a grey man sitting on a rung in the dark, as he had known that he would.

"I want to see him," he said now. "That's all it is. I've a line in the book about him and I've never seen his back, and I'd like to see how he looks at a thing before I'm in a hole with him."

Karis considered that, with her roll held against her chest, in both arms, like a child that she had been told not to drop.

"You may see his back," she said. "I should be obliged if he did not see your face. He is said not to care for being spoken to, and I do not know what he would make of being looked at."

"I'll look at the logs."

"You will look at him. I know you. Do it from behind something."

Vastin had been standing at the gable all this while, under the blanket, with a strap of the tarred case in his hands. He had found a place where the stitching had begun to go, and he was working at it with an awl that he had borrowed of the stable.

"A practitioner of the Bronze, in a class which the registry ranks beneath its notice, who has gone down by one door for longer than a porter can remember," he said, to the strap. "I certified this town on four occasions, and his name was on a roll that was laid before me each time, and I did not once ask who he was."

Nobody answered that, and he did not appear to want it answered.

---

The cook-house was built against the second wall, hard beside the gate, and its back was the wall itself.

Cael saw that from the wood-yard. It was a single long room, low, with a counter running down the far side of it; and behind the counter the pale stone went up bare into the smoke, with pots hung on iron pins that had been driven into its joints. Three keepers in blue coats were eating at a table by the street door. Along the counter stood clerks, and a groom or two, and a woman in a good cloak with a child.

The yard lay at the room's lower end, and it was only a space between the cook-house and the next building, roofed over with boards and stacked to the height of a man with split logs. There was a hatch in the wall, and a boy at the hatch, to whom Cael gave a copper, and from whom he had a bowl and half the copper back. A dozen porters stood about among the logs, eating on their feet; and he found a place by the doorpost, with a stack at his shoulder, where he could see the whole length of the counter end on.

The nearest man to him at that counter was not five strides off.

He stood at the very end of it, by the yard door, where the draught was, with his back to the room and his face to the stone. He was not tall. He had grey hair, cut short and badly, as a person cuts his own; and a coat that had been brown and was now the colour of dust, with dust worked into every seam of it, paler than any that the street could have put there. His shoulders were round; and there was a bowl in front of him, from which he was eating steadily, with his eyes shut.

Cael looked at that for some time. The man did not blink them shut and open them, as someone does who is tired. He kept them closed, and found the bowl with his spoon by habit, and chewed, and rested them as a carter rests a horse.

His hands were square, with the nails pared to the quick, and they were as pale as roots.

Nobody stood next to him. There was room for two at that end of the counter, and the clerks had left it, as the carters on a bench had made room for a keeper; but this was the other kind of room, and Cael knew it well. It was the space that people leave about a person whom they have long since stopped trying to talk to.

Then Karis came in by the street door.

She was very small in that doorway, with the light behind her, and a roll of oilcloth held against her chest. He saw her look down the room and find the dusty coat. She did not hurry. She came along behind the clerks with her chin level, as she had once walked up to a broker's plank with a hide; and she stopped at the counter's end, in the place that nobody took.

She did not speak. She undid the tape, and drew out the uppermost sheet, and laid it on the counter beside his bowl, and squared it to the counter's edge with one finger.

The spoon went on to his mouth, and came back.

It was the paper that he heard, Cael thought, or the breath of it settling. The grey head turned a little, and the eyes opened; and they were not as Cael had expected, since they were red at the rims, and wet, like the eyes of a man who has been all night over a small flame. They went to the sheet.

He did not read it.

Cael had watched a great many people read. He had watched registrars do it, and magisters, and a small pale woman who went along a line as a hen goes along a row of seed; and this man's eyes did not go along anything. They went to the foot of the sheet first, and stayed there, and then they climbed, as Brom's had climbed a pier on a river bank, from the footing upward, stopping at whatever seemed to be holding weight. He might have been looking at a wall.

It took him no longer than it takes to swallow. He laid the spoon down.

"He did not ask what I thought. I have been waiting for somebody to ask me that for as long as I can remember, and the people who asked did not believe it, and I had grown used to that. He did not disbelieve me; he simply would not hear it yet, and I do not know what a person does with that."

"Eats," said Brom.

He had been across the yard already. He had a bowl in his hand, from the hatch, with a spoon standing in it, and he set it on the boards between her boots. Then he stood there, as he had told a healer at a fork that he would.

Karis looked at the bowl. She looked up at a great deal of Brom.

"You are going to remain there."

"Till it's empty."

"I see." She picked up the spoon. "It appears that I am under several people's terms today."

Lira had the card, since she had lifted it off Karis's knee by its edges, as it had been held, and carried it to where the light came round the blanket.

"*Matter*," she read, with her lips. "*Its bearer, one. Hands—*" She stopped. "There's a figure. And his name, at the top, and a rank, and a number as long as my arm. And a day." She turned it over. I worked for that paper harder than I've worked for anything." She did not touch the pocket. "And this one's a scrap of card with a number, and it goes higher."

"I'm thinking about it. Don't push."

He did not push. He had seen her look at a blue coat from a smith's yard, the day before, with both her hands held still; and he thought that whatever she was working at had not finished with her yet.

Seln came up the ladder a little before dusk, with lamp oil on his sleeve and no frame.

He did not sit; he took a straw from the nearest pallet, and went down on his heels in the middle of the loft, and drew in the dust of the boards while they stood round him.

"The third gate," he said, and made a stroke. "From it, the street goes on, straight, and opens into the square within eighty yards, and that is the way which a person is told." He drew it, and then he laid the straw across it. "We do not take that. Under the wall, on the right hand as you come through, there is a lane, which loads use; and a keeper will point you to it if he is asked where loads go, and I should let him."

"And then a road round," said Brom.

"There are two. Carts go to the left, by a street that runs the whole way about the crown, half a mile, to the yard's lower gate; and half way along it there is a bar, and a beam, where every load is weighed, and the weight is written against an owner." His eyes came up. "It is a book."

"And the right?"

"The lane runs under the wall for two hundred paces, and there is no bar on it, since no cart could use what is at its end." He drew five short strokes, one below another, like the rungs of a ladder. "It is a cut, between two buildings, that goes down the back of the bluff to the same yard: a stair of five flights, which I made twenty-two steps. It is a stride and a half wide, and the walls on either hand are three men high and have no door in them, nor any window. Nobody stands in it. I went down it with a keg, and up again empty, and I did not meet a soul."

"Stairs," said Brom. He was looking at a bound leg.

"I have been down stairs before," said Vastin.

"Twenty-two. Down. With a knee that doesn't bend."

"I shall take them as they come. I should prefer that to being weighed."

Nobody said who was to carry.

Cael marked it, as he marked anything that people took care to step round. There was a figure on that card. There were three boxes, and they had three backs under them. There was a tarred case, which had ridden on one shoulder since a fork-stone, where nobody had asked for it and nobody had forbidden it. And there was a man with a straw in his fingers who went ahead of every load and had never yet been counted with one.

It could be left until morning. He thought that it would not be any easier in the morning.

He wrote the day by the lantern, when the pot had gone round.

*Two hundred and eighty-third day. Treswick, the district under the first wall. A hundred and thirty-six. Saw the man, from a wood-yard, at five strides. He looked at one sheet for as long as it takes to swallow, and he did not read it as anything of mine has ever been read: he asked it nothing, and he started at the bottom. He wouldn't hear what she thinks they are, and he wouldn't have a name, and he never looked at the yard. I'd wanted that, and I find I'd wanted the other as well. Entered.*

*S. has a way to the yard that crosses nothing I've written about. A stair. Tomorrow, the second bell, on a card with a figure on it.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

Shadow had lifted while he wrote, and he had told it no without raising his pencil.

He read the entry over, and it was thinner than what he had seen. He had gone to that yard to learn how the man looked at a thing; and he had learned it, and did not yet know what to do with the knowledge. Every reading that had ever been made of him had begun at the top, with a name, and a day, and a word, and had gone on down the sheet looking for whatever would bear the word out. At a cook-house counter he had watched a pair of sore eyes begin at the foot of a drawing, where the weight came down, and climb, and ask the paper for nothing that it had not offered. He did not know whether a person could be looked at so. He did not know whether he wished to be.

The loft was quiet. Brom slept as he always slept, like something felled, with seven bowls stacked by his head, and Lira lay on her side with her back to the gable. Vastin stood at it, since he would not lie down while anybody else was awake, and the awl was still going in his fingers, in and out of a strap, by feel.

Karis had not lain down either. She sat against the boxes, with the roll across her knees and both her hands flat on it.

Cael shut the book, and went and sat on the boards beside her.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I should. I have been told so by a healer, in front of witnesses." She did not look round. "I walked into a cook-house today with fifty-two sheets, and a man looked at one of them and stopped eating; and I have not been so happy since I was a child, and I am not able to tell it from terror. I did not know that they could be the same."

"He stopped on the paving, after. A step outside the door. You'd gone to pick up your sheet."

Her head came round.

"He stopped?"

"As you did at the bridge. He stood for three breaths, and then he went up that hill like somebody who's left a pot on."

She took that in, and he watched it go into her, slowly, like water into dry ground; and her hands closed on the oilcloth.

"Thank you," she said. "I should not have known that. He would never have told me."

"No. I don't think he tells."

They sat, and below them a horse shifted its weight from one hip to the other, and sighed.

"I asked you into this," said Karis, "and I have not forgotten it for an hour. If he looks at all three floors tomorrow and tells me that I have fifty-two sheets of a mason's pattern-book, then I have walked you under a station for it."

"You asked. I took a slip. It was my hand on the paper."

"That is generous, and it is not quite true."

"It's true enough to sleep on."

"You are frightened of him," she said.

It was not a question. She had said it as she would have read a date off a seal.

"Yes."

"So am I. I do not think that it is of the same thing."

"No. You're afraid that he'll look at them and say you're wrong."

"And you?"

He had not meant to say it. But he had told one thing on a bank, two nights since, to a man who had earned it, and he had found that the telling had not killed him.

"I'm afraid that he'll look up," said Cael.

She did not ask him what he meant. She was the one person he knew who never asked for the sentence after the one that she had been given. She only moved the roll, a little, so that the end of it lay across his knee as well as her own; and they sat so, under a slate roof, with fifty-two sheets between them, until the lantern guttered and Vastin at last put down the awl.

# Chapter 25 — The Slate Town

The second of the five gates was the one with the child in it, and Oryn came to it after dark on the third day, a good deal greyer than she cared to be seen.

She had walked nine miles along the hill-foot since noon on legs that had wanted a bed. She had told a carter that she did not put her hands to hard work tired; and she had then spent an hour inside his wrist and set out on a road. It was not the first time that she had broken that rule. It was the first time in a long while that she had broken it before the day's second gate, with the gate still in front of her.

The child found her before she had the mule tied.

He was three, or thereabouts, with a head of hair like a dandelion, and he had been a thing to worry over before the snow: a chest that whistled, and a mother who had walked him out to the bench twice in one round to ask whether it was the kind that passed. Oryn had said that she would hear him again before the cold. Then there had been seventeen days on a mountain, and a road shut behind them.

He ran at the mule, shouting, and was caught by the back of his smock.

"He's been doing that since he could stand," said the mother, laughing, and a little out of breath. "You never came, and I thought every night that I'd hear it stop."

"Bring him here."

She heard him on the bench, by the gate-house lantern, with two fingers on a wrist no thicker than a spoon and her ear against his back. He would not keep still, and she did not need him to. The river in him ran quick and clean. The whistle had gone down into a small low hum, a long way off, like a kettle in another room.

"He's mended himself," she said. "They do, at that age, as often as not. Then I'm sent for to be told so."

"So I needn't have feared."

"You'd every need. You couldn't know which sort it was, and nor could I, from the far side of a shut road. Fear's what you had instead of me." She let the boy go, and he went straight back to the mule. "I'm sorry that it was all you had."

It was then that the gate-house door opened.

That hold kept its keeper outside the wall. There was a room built against the wall's outer face, on the road side, with one window and a bed in it, since somebody must sleep on the wrong side of a bar to hear who knocks at night. An old woman stood in its doorway with her sleeves rolled and her hands held away from her apron.

"You're the healer," she said. "I'd have sent a boy up the road for you this morning, if I'd known there was a road with you on it. It's my son's wife. She's been at it since first light, and it's not coming. I've caught every child in this hold for thirty years, and I can feel a shoulder where there ought to be a head."

Oryn stood up. The yard went grey at its edges, and came back.

"Lying across."

"Square across. I've tried to walk it round, and I've not the hands for it any more."

She washed to the elbow at the butt, and it took her longer than it should have. The old woman watched her do it.

"You're spent," she said. It was one tradeswoman's finding to another.

"I'm spent. I gave it to a wrist this morning, and I'd do the same again, and that's no use to you at all. I told a man last night that I don't put my hands on this sort of thing tired." She dried them, a finger at a time. "A child doesn't wait on what I've told people."

The room was hot, and smelled of tallow, and there was a young man in the corner who had been told to keep out of the way and could not find any. The woman on the bed had her eyes shut. She was perhaps twenty. She opened them when the pain let go of her, and looked at a strange square face in the lantern-light.

"I'm a healer," said Oryn. "My name's Oryn; I walk this road. Your child's lying crosswise in you, with its back to the door, and I can turn it. I do it from outside, with my hands on your belly, slowly, in between; it'll hurt you some, and a good deal less than what you're doing now." She waited until the eyes had hold of hers. "May I?"

"Yes. Yes. Do it."

She laid her hands on, and counted, and went in.

There were two rivers.

She had felt it a hundred times, on a hundred benches, and it had never yet become an ordinary thing. There was the woman's, wide and driven, like a millrace with a week of rain behind it. And inside that, in a bed that was no bigger than a loaf, there ran a second, as quick as a bird's, twice as quick as anything grown, going its own way entirely. It did not know that anything was the matter. It lay across the current of its mother like a boat that has swung broadside in a stream.

She did not hurry. She could not have hurried, with what she had left in her; and a child was not a thing that she would have hurried in any case.

Her hands asked. The Path eased what they asked of, a little, so that a wall which had been set like a fist went slack under her palms for the length of a breath; and in that breath she walked the small boat round by the width of a finger. Then the pain came back, and the wall shut, and she stood with her hands exactly where they were and waited for it to pass over. She did that forty times, perhaps, and lost the count, which she never did.

Twice the quick river faltered.

Both times she stopped as she was, with nothing moving in the room but a tallow flame, and gave it what she had been keeping back for her own walk to the next gate. Both times it picked up, and ran on, and she breathed.

The head came down into its place a little after midnight. After that it was the old woman's work, and the young one's; and Oryn sat on the floor against the wall with her hands in her lap, because she could not have stood, and kept two fingers on an ankle to hear the rivers through to the end.

It was a daughter. She came at first light, in a great hurry at the last, and she was very angry about it.

Oryn heard the cry from the bench outside, where they had carried her a cup. She had not been able to lift the cup. She sat with her back against field-stone and watched the hill go from black to grey, and listened to a person who had been in the world for less than a minute telling it what she thought.

Two rivers in one body; and she had never in her life had any trouble in telling which was which. Each had its own bed. She could have drawn the banks.

She had met one body in which ten ran together with no bank to any of them. In the middle of the ten there had been a place where nothing ran at all, and everything went round. She had called it a stone, in her own head, on a carter's wrist, and she had called it a join. Sitting here with her hands as heavy as wet sacks, she knew that it had been neither of those, and that she had only reached for the two nearest words. She did not have a word. She had a child, an hour old, that had lain turned the wrong way in the dark and could not have told anybody so; and she had a boy three days south who was walking into a town with a thing in him that he could not read. She had not read it. She had sent him in with a rule about a square, since a rule had been the only thing that she owned to give him.

It was three weeks, less three days. She did not like to owe.

The old woman came out and stood beside the bench, with her arms folded, looking at the light.

"You were grey when you came," she said. "You're the colour of that wall."

"I'll be some other colour by noon. Is she feeding?"

"Like a calf."

"Then leave me be for an hour, and tell that bench that I'll see it when I've slept. And somebody's to keep the boy off my mule."

There was a white frost in the inn yard at dawn on the two hundred and eighty-fourth day, and the three boxes stood in it on their ends.

Brom had roped them again in the night, for a stair. Each had a second becket now, low down, so that it would sit high between the shoulders and leave a person's eyes clear for his feet. He went along the three of them once more in the half light, a knot at a time, as he had gone along them at a ferry's shed. Oryn's empty bowl was wedged under the lashing of his own, mouth down.

"It's coming," he said, when Lira looked at it. "She said all of it."

Karis had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other. She had the card inside her left glove, against the palm, and she had looked to see that it was there four times since the ladder.

Seln stood by the pump, with his hands in his sleeves. He had nothing on his back.

"The card has a figure on it," he said. "Three of that figure are plain, and they are standing beside their loads; and I am not the fourth. I shall say why once, since you will otherwise think it a nicety."

Nobody had asked him. Cael thought that he had been getting it ready since he drew a stair in the dust.

"A hand is a thing that a clerk looks at for the space of a count. I was in a registry's service for fifteen years, and for the most of them I was looked through; but it was clerks who looked through me, and a clerk remembers furniture. There may be one man in that yard who has seen me walk a file from one desk to another, in a house above Ostrand or in some other. If he finds such a face among the porters, he will not ask me who I am. He will ask himself what the rest of you are, to be worth it." He drew a breath. "I should cost you the count. And there is this besides: when a door has shut on five people, somebody ought to be standing where he can see who comes to look at the door. That person cannot be on a card."

"So you'll be on a roof," said Lira.

"I shall be where I ought to be. If you see me, I have done it badly."

That left the case.

It leaned against the pump's trough, tarred and black, with its strap new-stitched in three places, and it had ridden one shoulder since a fork-stone, though nobody had asked for that. A healer had forbidden it, twice, and had been waited out; and the person who kept a book had been told so on a road, to his face, and had neither given leave nor refused it.

Cael looked at the case, and so did Brom. He saw Karis begin to turn her head, and stop herself, since she had been raised to think it rude to look at a person whom one was about to ask for something.

"Yes," said Vastin.

He had said it before a mouth was open. He stood at the foot of the ladder, with both hands on his stick and the frost round his boots, and he looked at none of them in particular.

"You are one short of a figure, and I am standing here. I have carried that case for three days against a healer's express instruction, and I do not propose to hand it to somebody else at the foot of a hill because the carrying has acquired a name." He bent, hooked the strap with the stick's head, as he had at a stone, and brought it to his shoulder. "I have been counted once already in this town, by a man with beads. I found that it could be survived."

"It's not the gate," said Cael. "The gate's a thumb. It's the yard door. There's a man there with a pen, and if he looks up—"

"He will be told nothing by me. I have been practising; you may have observed it, and I am told that I do it very ill."

"If he asks who you are."

"Then I shall look at my boots, as a hand does, and somebody with more wit will answer him." The old mouth moved. "I am aware of what I am being entered as, since I have signed a great many sheets with that column on them. It is a narrow one, and it has never yet had anything in it but a number."

"Nobody's ordered it," said Brom.

"No. I heard no order. I heard a count that came out one under, and I have never been able to leave such a thing alone."

Cael did not answer. He had asked that man a great many things in his own head, across a table, over the years, and some of them in anger. It had never once entered his mind to ask him to carry.

"Twenty-two steps," said Brom. "Down."

"I can count them. It is the one accomplishment that I have brought with me."

They went up the street at the second bell.

Cael put his boot on the first of the slanted setts with a box on his back and a card going before him in a small pale woman's glove. He marked it, as he had marked a sill, since two days ago he had stood at that kerb and not stepped. It was the same stone; he had only become a different sort of thing to it.

The blue coats were waiting, as they waited for everything.

Karis gave up the card. She did it well, he thought: she did not offer it, or explain it, but only held it out and looked at the wall. The keeper with empty hands took it. He looked for the figure, as he had looked for a hundred figures since the gate opened. Then his eyes went back up the card, to its head, and stopped there.

"Lower archive," he said, to his fellow. "Here, look at this. Matter for the lower archive."

"There's no such card."

"There's one in my hand. It's his seal; the old one, with the corner gone." He turned it over, and turned it back. "I've had this coat nine years, and I've not seen one of these come up the hill."

It was not a question, and nobody answered it. *There?* said Anchor, of the tall arch and its two grooves. Cael told it no.

The keeper shrugged. He lifted his pen. He pointed its feather at Karis, and his lips moved; and then at a box with a big man under it, and at a box with a girl under it, and at Cael. Cael watched the feather come. It rested on him for no longer than on the others. He was a cap.

It came to the old man last, and there it stayed.

It did not stay for long. It was perhaps the length of a breath. But the keeper who held it read people for his bread, and he had in front of him a porter with a stick, and a bound leg, and the lightest load of the four, who stood up under it a great deal too straight.

Vastin was looking at his boots.

He was doing it badly. Cael could see that he was. A person who has looked at his boots all his life lets his head hang, and this one held his at an angle, with care, as a thing that he had been told the trick of; but his right hand was shut on the ash, and it did not move an inch toward his coat.

"You've a lame one," said the keeper.

That was not a question either. It was Karis who took it.

"He carries the light one."

"He'll want to. It's a hill, from here." The feather dropped. He made a stroke on his sheet, a single one, and tore the corner off the card and gave the card back. "Go on."

Vastin went under the arch with the case on his shoulder and his eyes on the setts. It was a gate that he had ridden through on four occasions, with a keeper standing at each jamb and the board held out to him, open, so that he might see for himself how it was kept; and he went through it now as one stroke in four, on the wrong side of the pen, and Cael heard him let his breath go when the shadow of the arch was behind them.

Lira went under the arch second.

Cael had her back in front of him, and a box on it, and above the box the back of her head. He could not see her face. He saw what her head did. As she came level with the board that hung from the second keeper's neck, where a line stood for every stranger who had walked through that gate today on his own paper, her chin turned toward it by the width of a finger.

There would be names on that sheet, in a clerk's hand, each with a district and a rank; and there was a pocket in her coat with such a line folded in it, sealed, which a station at Ostrand had made out to her, and which she had worked harder for than for anything else that she owned. She had only to put the box down.

Her chin came back. She shifted the beckets, and went on up the hill.

It was quieter inside the second wall, and it was cleaner, and for the first hundred yards that was all that he could have said of it.

Then he began to see what Iron bought.

The roofs were slate, every one. The street had a gutter down each side of it, with water running, and no dung. The windows had glass in them. At a corner there was a cistern with a brass spout, and a girl was filling a jug at it; and she did not put a copper in any dish, since there was no dish. A row of children's voices came out of an upper window, chanting something all together, and he caught the shape of it before the words: it was a list, and it went up by steps, and at the top of each step there was a colour.

Nobody looked at them.

It was not as the outer street had not looked, where everybody was carrying and a box was only a thing in the way. People here were not carrying anything. They walked with their hands free, in good cloth, at their own pace; and their eyes went over four backs under loads as eyes go over a row of posts. A woman drew her skirt aside for Brom without breaking her talk. Cael had been stared at by such people for the whole of his life since a Kindling. He had not known that there was a way of standing so low that they could not find him.

Karis had not looked at a single slate. She walked with her face turned to the house-fronts on the upper side, where the street had been cut into the hill and the footings of the houses stood on the bluff's own rock; and twice she put out two gloved fingers, in passing, and drew them along a bed of pale stone at the height of her hip, as another person might have touched a dog.

"Left foot," said Brom, to himself, on the steep of it. He was favouring two toes.

The street climbed harder past the cistern. It had been laid in long shallow steps there, a stride to each, with a raised kerb for the grip of hooves. Behind him the stick had begun to come down out of time. Cael did not turn round; he slowed, by a little, as one slows a line for the last on it, and heard the breath at his shoulder catch up, and shorten.

"I am aware of what you are doing," said Vastin, very low.

"I'm tired. It's a hill."

"It is courteous of you to be tired at my pace."

There was a house on the right hand with a board beside its door.

He would have gone by it. It was Lira who slowed, in front of him, with her head turned; and he read what she was reading. The board was black slate, ruled in chalk, as every board in that town was, but it had no loads on it: it had hours, and against each hour a colour. *First bell: buff. Second bell: blue.* And at the foot, in smaller letters: *Cards, after the fourth, at the side door, as the house's means allow.*

There was a bench under the board. Nobody was sitting on it at that hour.

"That's hers," said Lira. She did not say whose, and did not need to. "That's her column. They've hung it on a door."

"It is the standing order for a district house," said Vastin. He had come up level, and stopped, with both hands on the stick, and he was looking at the board as he had looked at a bridge with five piers. "I have read it in the code, where it is the ninth article of a schedule. I had never seen it in chalk."

"Does it read different in chalk?" said Brom.

"It reads the same. It is a good deal harder to walk past."

They walked past it. There was nothing else to be done with it. But Cael carried it up the hill with the box, a square woman's voice saying that it was not cruelty and was only how a heap was sorted; and he thought of a girl on a kerb with a basket of eggs, and a child who had taken hold of a stick with both fists.

The third wall was the oldest, and it showed.

It was lower than the others, and thicker, and its stone had gone the colour of a tooth. The arch in it was round, and narrow, and not tall enough for a loaded wagon. There was one keeper under it. He was an old man, in a long pale coat that had been made for somebody broader, and he sat on a stool with a board on his knee.

He read the card twice. He read it as Karis read a seal, from the stamp inward.

"Lower archive," he said. He had a dry, exact voice. "I had heard that there was such a card. My father kept this gate before me, and he told me that he had passed one, once, when he was young." He looked up, at three boxes, and at a tarred case, and for rather longer at a courier's flat of waxed leather with a stamp across its face. "That is a good deal of matter."

Karis said nothing. She looked at the wall.

"It is not my business what it is. My business is how many." He lifted his pen, and it had no feather; he used the point. It went to her, and to Brom, and to Lira; it came to Cael, and he felt it on his breastbone like a finger, and it went on. It came to the stick, and the bound leg, and it did not linger there at all, since that old man had seen a great many porters, and he had long ago stopped expecting them to look like anything.

"One, and four," he said. He made his stroke, and tore the second corner off the card.

"Where do loads go?" said Karis.

It was the only thing that she had been told she might ask. She asked it of the wall.

"Not by the square. They'll not thank you for boxes on that paving, with the steps swept." He pointed with the pen, through the arch and to the right. "Under the wall. There is a lane. Follow it till it goes down, and then go down with it, and you will come to a yard. Mind your lame one on the steps. They are older than he is."

"Thank you."

"Go on."

They went under the round arch, one behind another, and it was so low that Brom bent his head.

*There?* asked Anchor. It asked very softly here, as a person lowers his voice in an old room. Cael told it no.

Then he was through, and he stood inside the third wall of Treswick, on the crown of the bluff, with a box on his back.

He had his eyes on the ground. He had put them there under the arch, before the light changed, and he held them there now as he would have held a rope. He saw setts, small ones, laid in a pattern of fans. They ran away in front of him between two rows of very old doorsteps. A long way up that street, eighty yards perhaps, they stopped being grey, and went white, where the sun came down upon something wide and open and paved.

There was no sound from that end, and he had thought that there would be. There was only a brightness lying on the stones, and at the top of his sight, where he would not lift it, a paleness in the air that was not the sun's.

It was a hundred paces off. He could have walked to it while a person counted to sixty. Hesk's book lay against his ribs, under the box's strap, with two sentences in it that a square woman had read with her lips moving; and he found that he was glad, for the first time, that she had made him write them. A thing that he had only said would have been arguing with him by now.

He turned right.

A small quick shape came up on his left side as he did it, with a box on its back, and settled there, between him and the street's end. She did not look that way herself. She had her eyes on the lane, and her chin level, and she said nothing whatever; she only walked where a wall would have been, if anybody had thought to build him one.

# Chapter 26 — The Iron Door

The lane ran under the third wall for two hundred paces, and they were the longest that Cael had walked since a cut on a moor.

It was a narrow way, cobbled, with the wall's inner face on his right hand and the backs of old houses on his left. Nothing in it was meant to be looked at. There were water-butts, and ash-heaps, and a line of washing; and every forty paces, between two houses, there was an alley.

He had not been told about the alleys.

Each was no wider than a door. Each ran straight, from the lane, away to his left, between blind walls; and at the far end of each, a hundred yards off, there stood an upright strip of white. It was the same white that had lain on the setts at the head of the street. The square was at the end of every one of them. It lay along that side of the lane, the whole way, like a lit room beyond a row of keyholes.

He kept his eyes on Brom's heels.

It was harder than the rise had been. On the rise the thing had been a thumb's breadth of light at a day's distance, and he had only needed to turn his head. Here it came at him sideways, at the edge of his sight, a bar of brightness that swung across the cobbles in front of his boots at each opening and was gone. He did not need to look. He had only to walk through it. He found that the muscles of his neck had set like a rope with a load on it.

At the first alley Lira's shadow fell across the brightness before it reached him.

She had come up on his left at the gate, and she had stayed there. She was a hand shorter than he was, with a box that stood up above her head; and at every opening she was between it and him for the two strides that it took to pass. She did not look down the alleys. He could see the side of her face, and it was turned to the lane ahead, and her chin was level. She went by each strip of white as she would have gone by a dog that she did not mean to notice.

Nobody had asked it of her. He had not asked it on a road, or in a loft where a blanket hung on two nails, and he did not ask now. He counted the alleys instead, since he counted anything. There were four.

At the third there was a sound.

It came down the alley from the white end, small and clear in the quiet: a broom, going on stone, in long even strokes. Somebody was sweeping a great many steps. The keeper at the arch had said that they were swept. Under the broom, once, he thought that he heard a child's voice, high up and a long way off, counting.

He did not turn his head. He went through the bar of light on the cobbles, and through the shadow that a girl laid over it; and the broom went on behind him, stroke after stroke, and grew faint.

"The lane is not on the plan," said Vastin.

He spoke low. He was a stride behind, with the stick coming down on the cobbles and the case on his shoulder, and he had not looked down an alley either, though nobody had required it of him.

"There is a plan of this town, filed at the seat, which I have had unrolled for me on four occasions. It shows three walls, two gates, a square, and the principal street. The registry is drawn as a block with one door." The stick came down. "I do not find upon it any lane by which a load might reach that building without crossing the paving. I should have said, before today, that there was none."

"There's got to be," said Brom, from in front. "They burn wood up here. Somebody brings it."

"That is exactly the consideration which the plan omits."

Karis had said nothing since the gate. She walked nearest the wall, and her right shoulder all but brushed it, and twice Cael saw her tilt her head back and look up its whole height, as she had looked at a bluff from six miles off. She was counting courses.

He did not see Seln in that lane, or on any roof above it, though he looked at every roof-line that did not lie to his left. He took a certain comfort from that. It meant that the thing was being done properly.

Then the cobbles tipped, and the lane began to go down.

---

It turned as it fell. The houses ended on the left hand, and the wall on the right dropped to a parapet no higher than his waist, where the crown of the bluff broke off; and for the length of ten yards there was nothing on that side but air.

Lira stopped.

She set her box down on the parapet's top, and held it there by one becket, and looked.

The whole town lay under them. They had come round to the bluff's northern edge, and the ground fell away so steeply below the parapet that Cael could have dropped a stone onto a slate roof. He saw the second wall from above, going round the hill's waist like a belt, with the blue gate in it no bigger than a keyhole. He saw the roofs change below that from slate to tile and thatch. He saw the outer wall, and the long shed of the rope-walk laid against it, and the river coming round under the quay; and far out, small and dark across the brown water, a bridge on five piers.

It went down in three steps. She had said so, from a rise, three days ago. *They've built the ladder out of stone.*

He watched her, and not the town.

He knew what the top of a thing did to Lira. He had seen it on the wall of a house above Ostrand, and on a roof there, and on a mountain. Her weight went forward onto the balls of her feet. Her chin came up, and something lit behind her face, as a lamp is lit in a room. She had wanted to stand at the head of that ladder for as long as he had known her. She had been told that it was where she belonged by everybody who had ever had the raising of her; and there was a paper in her coat which said that she had set her foot on it.

He waited for her weight to go forward.

It did not. She stood flat on both heels, with one hand on a box, and she looked the whole of it over, from the parapet to the bridge, slowly, with her lips moving. She had looked at a factor's fence so, on a hillside, when she was asked what it would take to mend.

"Two gates," she said at last. "And a view."

Nobody spoke.

"That's all that it is. I've been trying to see what else there was since the first one." She did not take her eyes from it. "It's three walls, one inside the next. You go through a gate and the roofs get better. You go through another, and there's a tap that you needn't pay at. And when you're right at the top, what you've got is this. You can see everybody who isn't."

"It's a good view," said Brom.

"It's a very good view. I'd have given everything I had for it. I very nearly did; I'd my foot on it." Her left hand had come out of its pocket, and she looked at that for a moment, the long thin fingers that had been in wool for the better part of half a year, and shut them. "And I'm stood on it, and it doesn't want anything of me. I thought that it'd pull. I've been waiting for it since the rise. I had it all ready, what I'd say to myself when it did."

"And it doesn't," said Cael.

"It's a hill with a tax on it." She said it with a kind of wonder. "You can't want a place the same once you've been its porter. I don't think you can, anyway. I've carried fifty-two sheets up the inside of it, and seen who fetches its eggs."

She swung the box off the parapet, and got her arms through the beckets, the right and then the left.

"It's worth the look," she said. "I've looked."

Karis had turned to her, with the flat under one arm. She did not say anything, since she never did when a thing was finished. She only moved aside, so that the girl with the box should go first into what lay beyond the parapet.

It was the cut.

It opened in the wall of the last building, a slot a stride and a half wide, and it went down. Cael saw the first flight: five steps of dark worn stone, and a landing, and the head of the next. The walls went up on either hand, three times the height of a man, without a door or a window or so much as a drain. The sky was a strip at the top. It was like looking down a well that had been laid on its side.

Brom went in first, and his box all but brushed both walls.

"I shall go before you," said Cael.

The stick had stopped at the slot's mouth. The old man was looking down the first flight, and at his right leg, and he was doing a sum.

"You are behind me, in the order."

"I'm going two steps below you. If the knee goes, it goes onto a box, and the box is full of paper."

"You are proposing to stand where I should fall."

"Yes."

"That is not a thing that I can properly require of a person who is carrying the weight that you are."

"You've not required it. I've said where I'm walking."

The grey eyes rested on him. Then Vastin shifted the case higher on his shoulder, and set the stick's foot on the first tread.

"So you have," he said.

He went down as a man goes down who has thought it through. The stick went first. Then the bound leg was swung out and lowered, quite straight, like a plank let down on a rope; and then the sound one bent, and took the whole of him, and followed. He did that for every step. Cael went backward below him, with a hand on either wall and his heels feeling for the treads, and the box dragging at his shoulders.

Lira counted aloud, from the landing beneath. Five. Nine. Thirteen.

It was on the fourth flight that the stick slipped.

The tread there had been worn into a dish by more feet than anybody could have reckoned, and it was wet. The ash skidded an inch. The straight leg came down with nothing to steady it, and the whole long weight of the man tipped forward into the slot.

Cael had both heels down before he knew that he had set them. Compression had come up into them of its own accord, as it did when a load was on its way, and his frame creaked like hemp. He told it to wait.

The load did not come. A raw left hand, which had not opened flat since a ferry's strap, struck the wall and held there; and the old man hung on it, with his shoulder against the stone and his breath gone, a foot above the box.

"Seventeen," said Lira's voice, from below, a little higher than before.

"Seventeen," said Vastin. He got the stick under him again. "I had it as seventeen."

He did the last five without a word. At the bottom he stood with both hands on the ash, quite grey, and looked back up the slot at the strip of sky.

"Twenty-two," he said. "The man can count. I shall tell him so."

---

The cut let them out through an open gate into a yard, and the yard was full of carts.

It was a long paved place, sloping, shut in on three sides by sheds and on the fourth by the back of a very large building. A wagon of split logs was being unloaded at a dock. There was a beam on a post by the lower gate, with a pan hung from it and a man beside the pan holding a slate. Nobody looked round at four backs and a small pale woman coming out of a hole in the wall. Loads came into that yard all day.

Cael looked at the building, since it was the only thing there that he had come for.

He looked at it from the ground upward. It was of the pale stone, mortared, and it went up five storeys, with a row of small barred windows to each. It had no ornament on this side. A registry does not trouble about its back.

Karis had stopped again. He saw where her eyes were, and followed them down.

The lowest three courses were dark.

They ran the whole length of the wall's foot, under the pale, close-jointed and without a thread of mortar; and where a pale block had been set upon a dark one, the mason had cut it to lie snug, one stone at a time. She did not say what it was. She had said it once already, on a river bank. Her gloved hand went out, and came back, and closed on the flat.

"Later," said Brom, very gently. He had seen it too. "It'll be there."

There was a low door in that dark footing, and a square hatch beside the door with a ledge, and a bench along the wall for people who waited. Karis went to the hatch. She laid the card on its ledge, with two corners gone. A hand came out of the dimness and took it in, and nothing else happened.

"You're to wait," said a yard-man, going by with a sack. "He's a load to enter. He does them in their order."

So they sat on the bench.

Brom lowered his box to the paving and himself beside it. Lira sat on hers. Vastin stood for a moment, measuring the bench with his eye, and the post at its end; and then he set his back to the post and let himself down, with the leg laid out in front of him, and shut his eyes. It was the third time. He had not asked anybody whether he might.

Cael took Hesk's book out of his coat.

He had not meant to write until night. But he had a quarter of an hour, and a thing that he had seen at a parapet, and he knew by now what became of the things that he left until night. He set the day at the head, and the place, which was the registry's yard at Treswick; and he looked at that for a moment, since he had never in his life expected to write those four words together of his own will.

Then he wrote what he had seen.

*Lira looked at the whole ladder from the top of it and it didn't pull. She priced it. She's carrying a box.*

It was three short lines in the plain hand, and it was the largest thing that he had entered since a fork. He had known her for longer than he had known anybody but an old man in Denvash, and he had watched her go up a great many walls. He had half believed, at a board in Lowmarch, that the first blue coat would have her, and that he would lose her to a keeper reading her name aloud. He had said nothing of it, to her or to the book, since a person does not enter a fear about a friend where the friend will read it.

She was reading it now, upside down, across his knee.

"I didn't price it," she said. "I only said what it was."

"That's pricing."

"Brom prices. I look." She put her finger on the last four words. "That's true, though. You can leave that."

He left it. He shut the book on the pencil, and sat with it between his hands.

Somewhere above him, on the far side of five storeys of pale stone, there was a square. He did not know how many yards off it lay; a building's thickness, and a flight of steps. He had come round it by a lane with four keyholes, and down a slot, and he sat now with its whole registry between himself and it, on the low side, among the firewood. He had not set a foot on its paving. He had not lifted his eyes to what stood over it.

It had been easy to write, at a fire. It had cost him something to keep, in a lane. He thought that the healer had known the difference when she asked for it in pencil.

The hatch's shutter moved.

---

The low door opened, and the clerk came out with a book.

He was a man of no particular age. He had a grey coat with ink on its right cuff and none upon the left, and a narrow book bound in green cloth, and a pen. He was pale, as people are who are kept indoors, and he blinked once at the daylight. Cael did not learn his name that day, or on any day after it.

He had the card in his fingers. He stood on the sill and read it again, slowly, though he must have read it at the hatch. Then he looked at the small pale woman who had brought it.

Cael had stood up. So had the others; he heard the stick find the paving.

This was the place. A quiet man had told them so on a ladder's head, and had not been able to shorten it. There was a line in that green book, and a pen above the line, and a person holding the pen who was neither forbidden to ask nor required to. A hundred clerks, Seln had said. Eighty would.

The clerk's eyes left Karis. They came along the bench.

They went to Brom, and to the box at his feet with a bowl wedged under its lashing. They went to Lira, who was looking at a cart. They came to Cael; and Cael, who had been read by registrars since he was fourteen, and had learned to meet each of them in the eye so that it should cost them something, looked down at the toes of his boots.

He had not known that it would be so hard. It was the only thing that he had ever had to set against a pen, and he gave it up, and was a hand.

The eyes went on. They reached an old man who stood very straight, with a stick, and a bound leg, and a tarred case; and there they rested.

Nobody moved in that yard, by the bench. A log fell off a wagon, somewhere behind them, and rang on the stones.

The clerk's mouth had opened.

Cael saw it from under his brows. He saw the breath taken, and the pen come up an inch from the page; and he saw, at the edge of his sight, a raw hand close on the head of an ash stick until the linen round it creaked. Vastin was looking at the ground. He was doing it a little better than he had done it at a gate.

The clerk shut his mouth.

He looked down again at the card, at the seal in its corner, which was old, and had a piece gone. Something went over his face, and Cael could not have sworn to what it was. It was not suspicion. It was not much like curiosity either. If he had been made to put a word to it, he would have said that a man who had kept a book with one name in it, for a great many years, had at last been handed a second line to write, and did not mean to spoil it by learning anything that he would then be obliged to set down.

He opened the green book on his forearm.

He wrote for some time. He looked at the boxes, and counted them with his eyes, and wrote. He looked at the case, and the courier's flat, and the calfskin, and wrote those. He did not ask what was in any of them. Then his pen moved to the right, to a narrow column, and made a single mark there; and then to a narrower one, at the very edge of the leaf.

He wrote *four*.

He blotted the line. He did not turn the book round for anybody to sign, since there was no place in it where a hand could have signed. He stepped back from the sill, and stood aside, with the green cloth shut against his chest.

Lira had her box up before the door was clear.

Cael got his arms through the beckets. He had been a name with a word after it on every sheet that the Compact had ever kept of him; and he had spent his life since a Kindling on other people's wrong side of a counter, reading that word upside down. He went past a clerk of the oldest registry on the Line, into its foundations, as the quarter part of a figure in the last column of a green book. The man did not look at him as he went by. He was looking at the line that he had written, with something very like satisfaction.

---

Inside the door there was a passage, and then a stair, and they went down.

It was a short flight, of the pale stone, and it brought them into a vault that ran away under the building for farther than the lamps showed. There were lamps: iron ones, in brackets, a long way apart. There were pillars as thick as Brom, and between them stacks of bound paper tied in tape, and broken stools, and a dead clock. A registry had been putting things down here for a long time that it did not want and could not burn.

Overhead there were feet.

He heard them through the vault's crown, dull and many, going to and fro: five storeys of clerks, at their desks and between them. A whole town was up there, above that again, with its gates and its coats and its chalked boards, sorting everybody who came within reach of it by a colour. It had a book at every door. It had looked at him four times since a bridge and seen a back.

The clerk had come down behind them with a lantern. He did not lead; he only walked at the rear, as a man does who is seeing a load to its place.

The pale stone ended.

It ended at a pillar, as cleanly as a floor ends at a step. Beyond it the vault's walls were dark, and close-jointed, and there was no mortar in them at all. The air changed in the same stride. It had been cellar air, damp, with mice in it. This was dry, and very cold, and it had no smell that he could name.

Karis made a sound. It was hardly more than her breath.

She had taken her right glove off, without setting down the hide, and Cael did not know how. Her bare palm was flat against the dark wall. She walked so, the whole way down that last stretch, with her fingers trailing over joint after joint, like a person who has been told of a thing all her life and is at last allowed to touch it.

At the end there was a door.

He had expected something great. It was not. It was low, lower than the round arch had been, and broad, and it was made of plates of black iron laid one over another like slates. There was not a lock on it that he could see, or a handle. It stood in a frame of the dark stone; and on either side of that frame the registry's pale masonry came up, and stopped, each block cut to lie against the dark, as a tower had been cut to a bridge.

*There?* said Anchor.

It did not ask as it asked at a gate. It asked once, very low, and he told it no.

He had not touched the iron. He stood two strides off it, with a box on his back, and felt the cold come off the plates onto his face.

The clerk went by him and knocked.

He knocked with his knuckles, three times, and the sound that came back was not the sound of a door. It was deeper. It went away behind the iron and down, and it was a good while in going.

Something was drawn, on the far side, with a long scrape. The plates swung inward.

There was a man in the opening, with a lamp held low at his knee. He had a coat the colour of dust. He stood at the head of a stair that went down into no light whatever, and his red eyes did not go to any face. They went to the card, which Karis held out to him, and then they went to the loads.

"Three," he said, in the voice like a hinge. "And the case."

He counted boxes. He did not count the people under them. He stepped back against the stair's wall, to leave the way clear, and waited.

Karis went in first, with her bare hand held before her like something that she was carrying. Brom bent nearly double to follow, and the bowl under his lashing scraped the iron. Lira went. The stick went, a tread at a time, and its owner did not look round at a clerk who had very nearly asked him a question.

He was the last.

He stepped over a sill of black iron that was worn bright in the middle. The cold closed on him to the knees, and then to the chest. It came up that stair like water rising, and it was older than cellars. Nothing in it moved. He stood on the first tread, in the round of one small lamp, and looked down, and there was no bottom to see.

Above him lay a registry, and above the registry a square. Three walls stood round that, and two gates, with a pen at each; and at the very top of it all, where he had not once lifted his eyes, there was a thing that had been built to read whatever was brought near it. Not one stone of the whole reached lower than this door.

Cleon pulled the iron to.

It shut with no more noise than a book makes. The clerk's lantern went out of the world, and the feet overhead, and the last grey thread of the day.

The tiers closed, and the dark opened.

# Chapter 20 — Wayposts

The hold came off at the first hedge.

It was Seln who took it off. He had gone down the western side of the rise thirty yards ahead of the boxes, as he went ahead of everything; and at the foot, where the ruts ran in between two banks of thorn, he stopped and let the file come up to him. He had not done that on any road since the Line.

"We may talk," he said.

Nobody did. They had come two hundred and eighty-one days through a country where a road was a place for hands and not for mouths, and the habit had gone deeper into them than any of the six had known.

"Who says?" said Brom at last. His own voice seemed to startle him.

"The hedge does. A hound does not lie up where a plough goes, and a thing that hunts what a Path gives off wants a mile of nothing round its den." Seln nodded at the bank. It had been laid within the year, with the stems cut half through and bent over and woven between stakes. "Somebody tends this. That town works these fields to the foot of the moor, and it will keep a watch that rides as far as its fields go. There will be posts."

"So we're inside," said Lira.

"We are not. The river is the Line here, and the sill lies under the gate. This is ground that the town uses and does not own." He put his hands into his sleeves. "I had better say the rest now, since it will be wanted before night. The hold was one rule in two halves, and only one of the halves is lifted. You may speak. Nobody declares."

"There's nothing to hear it. You said so."

"There is nothing to hunt it. Out there a declaration draws a thing with teeth. In here it draws a clerk. A Path that is opened on a town's ground is opened in somebody's district, under somebody's eye, and he is paid to write down what he saw and whose it was." The mild voice did not alter. "I should take the teeth, for choice. A hound does not send a copy up."

Lira laughed. It was one short breath through the nose, and it was the first sound of the kind that Cael had heard on a road since a meadow at Lowmarch.

"So we can talk, and we can't do anything," said Brom.

"That is a town."

"I'd forgotten that I didn't like them."

They went on between the hedges, and for half a mile nobody used the leave that he had been given. Cael found that he had a great deal to say and no notion of how a person began. He had not been silent all those days; they had talked at every fire, behind every wall, and through a whole cold term in a back room. It was only on a road that the mouth was shut, with the sigils in their pockets and his ten lying closed, and he had left off noticing it as a carter leaves off noticing his own wheels. He noticed it now, as it lifted. It was like setting down the box.

The road found its tongue sooner than they did. A cart came out of a field gate ahead of them, piled with dung and steaming, and the man at the horse's head was whistling. He whistled the same four notes over and over, badly, and did not leave off when he saw six strangers under loads. He looked at the boxes, and at the beckets, and pulled his horse over to let the weight go by, as one carrier does for another. He had given Cael the same length of glance that he gave the hedge.

After him there was a boy with a switch and nine geese, and a woman under a yoke, and two men mending a hurdle; and not one of them gave the six more than a glance, which took in the loads and priced the distance. Cael counted them, since he counted anything. He had been stared at on roads since he was fourteen, by people who had been told what to look for, and for the better part of a year he had walked where there was nobody to stare. This was a third thing. He was a back with a box on it. He thought that he could have borne a great deal of that.

Vastin went beside him on the ditch side, a shoulder off. The stick came down a little after each of Cael's strides, and the tarred case knocked softly against a brown coat, and neither of them spoke; of the six they were the two who found the leave hardest to use. Cael had kept one question from that man since a board at Lowmarch. He thought that the man was keeping something too. He walked like somebody carrying a full bowl.

---

The first post stood where a lane came in from the north. It was a dressed stone as high as Cael's waist, squared, with a figure cut deep in its eastern face, and under the figure three short bars, one above another.

"Nine," said Lira. "They've counted it for us."

"A town of the second schedule is bound to post its approaches at intervals of—" Vastin stopped. Nobody had asked him. He looked at the stone for a moment. "It is a milestone," he said.

"There," said Seln, without turning his head.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read, civilly, of the post, as it had asked of every rut for three days. Cael told it that it need not know. Brom lowered his box to the verge, and rolled the left shoulder, and looked at his right boot as a man looks at a debtor.

Lira had her box off before him. She set it down on the grass and sat on it; and she sat with her back to the west.

Cael would not have marked it, had he not been doing the same thing himself for three miles. Since the foot of the rise the town had dropped behind the hedges, and come up again, and dropped; and each time that it rose he had put his eyes on the lowest wall and held them there, since there was a small pale light above the highest roofs that did not flicker. It was work. It was like carrying a second box.

She had never once looked up. He went back over the morning, and he was sure of it. On the rise she had looked at three walls and called them a ladder. After that she had come down two miles of open road with the whole bluff in front of her, and that quick chin had stayed level the whole way, on Brom's heels, and on a hedge, and on a boy's geese. Lira looked at the top of everything. She had looked at the tops of walls since a house on a bluff, to see what it would cost to stand there.

Nobody had asked it of her. A healer had set a term at a fire, for one person, and had made him write it; and a girl on a meal-sack had heard, and had taken a share of it without a word, as she would have taken one end of a load.

He did not thank her. She would have asked him what for, and made him say it, and then told him that it was a boot.

"What?" said Lira.

"Nothing. You're sitting on fifty-two sheets."

"Then they'll be flat."

---

He fell back beside Karis when the road went on.

She walked with the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as she had since a landing, and her eyes had not left the bluff since it came up over the hedges. She was not looking at its walls.

"It is bedded," she said, before he could speak. "The rock. Do you see the lines in the face of it, under the lowest wall, where the river has cut? Each of those is a floor that was laid down and then covered. I have been trying to count them for a mile, and I lose my place at the seventh."

"At the spring you said what he was. The man under it." He shifted the box. "You said a historical-Path practitioner, and I let it go by, since I'd a mule to fetch. What does one do?"

"I shall tell you what a catalogue says, and you will remember that it is a catalogue." She did not look at him. "The registry keeps a class that it calls historical studies. It is a small one. The Paths in it are of no use on a floor or in a field, and so nobody has troubled to rank them high; I do not know which of them he has. What they have in common is that they are turned on made things. A wall. A seal. The binding of a book. Such a person can say how a thing was put together: what was laid first, and what rests on what, and whether two things came from one hand."

"And what it means?"

"No. Every account of them is careful to say so in its first line. He reads how a thing is built. He does not read what it says."

"That's half."

"It is the half that I have not got." Her voice had gone thin, as it did when she came near the edge of what she had. "I have dated three floors past anything that I can lay paper beside. I have forty-one kinds of sign in one order. And I cannot tell you which of them is holding the others up. I do not need a man to tell me what it says, Cael. I shall do that, if it can be done. I need one who can tell me how it stands."

She went on counting beds of rock, and he let her go ahead.

He turned the phrase over for a furlong, with the box riding on his shoulders. *How a thing is built.* He had not thought to ask before what kind of reader sat under that town. He had only known that there was one more of them.

Then he went over what he was carrying there, a stride at a time; and it was not the box.

There was a station on that crown. Oryn had told him what such a thing was, at a fire, in the plainest words that she owned: it was the one building in a town that was made to read whatever was brought near it. He had stood on the plate of one, once, and it had taken eleven seconds over him and written a word. He had a term in Hesk's book about this one, and he meant to keep it. But the term kept him off a square. It did not tell him what he was keeping off it.

He did not know. There was a place under his breastbone where nothing moved. It had been read three times, on the boards of a loft and at the edge of a marsh, and not once since a square woman stood over him in the grey of a morning and gave him seven words.

*Write it down and stop touching it.*

He had done both. It was a hundred and sixty-one days since he had laid two fingers on his own wrist.

He had kept that order as he kept few things, and he looked at it now as he had learned to look at a slip on a board: for what it said, and not for what he wished that it said. She had said *touching*. She had said what she meant by it, too, standing over him: that a person who has found a place in himself goes back to it every night with his fingers.

He knew what his fingers had been. Three times he had gone down the middle of himself and straight at that place, to learn whether it would let him in. Three times it had turned him; and each time he had fetched the ten up to it, one after another, like a man who tries every key on a ring in a door that has no keyhole. That was touching. He had been asking it something.

There was another way to go. He could let the reading take its own road, and follow where it bent, and ask the place nothing whatever. A person could walk the whole way round a pond and never put his hand in.

He was honest enough to see what he was about. He had an order from the one person whose orders he kept, and he was reading it for the leave that he wanted in it, with its author three weeks and a moor away from telling him that he had read it wrong. A registrar would have been proud of him. He would write that down as well.

But he was walking toward a thing that read, with a thing in him that had never been read; and the healer who owed him that reading had said herself that she did not know what the one would do to the other. He did not see how a person kept a promise about a place sensibly without once looking at what he was keeping away from it.

He lengthened his stride until the second box was beside him.

"At the next post I'm going to lie down behind a hedge for a quarter of an hour."

"That's a long time to lie behind a hedge," said Lira.

"I'm going to read. Myself. As she showed me."

She did not turn her head. Her pace broke by half a stride, and mended.

"Is it the thing that she told you to leave alone?"

"It's beside it. I'll go round, and I'll not go in, and I'll take nothing up to it."

"And she'd call that leaving it alone."

"I don't know what she'd call it. She's gone north with a mule."

"So you've settled what she'd say, and she's not here to say different." There was no unkindness in it. She hitched the beckets higher on her shoulders. "Write down that I asked. When you write it. I'd like it in there that somebody did."

"I'll write it."

"And I'll sit on the bank. If Brom comes round that hedge and finds you flat on your back with a hand on your chest, he'll have you over his shoulder before you can open your eyes, box and all."

---

The second post stood beside a brook, where a plank bridge had been thrown over for people on foot and the carts went through the water. It said six. They had done three miles in an hour and a half, and it was a stick that had set the pace.

Brom sat down on the plank's end and had his right boot off inside a breath. He held the bound foot out over the water without wetting it, and frowned at two toes, and counted on the fingers of one hand to eight.

Vastin did not sit. He put his back against the post and both hands on the head of his stick, and stood with the bound leg thrust out before him, as he had stood at every halt for two days. A man in that binding could get himself down to the ground. It was the getting up again that could not be done without another person's arm; and Cael had watched him measure that at a fold, and at a fork, and at the top of a rise, and choose his feet every time.

There was a gate into the field behind the hedge, hung on a post of the same dressed stone as the mile-mark. Cael lifted its latch. He set his box down inside, on a headland where the grass had not been ploughed, and lay flat in the lee of the bank with his boots toward the brook.

Lira's back came against the far side of the hedge. He heard her settle, and begin to pull the heads off grass.

He laid his right palm on his breastbone. He put the first two fingers of his left hand on the inside of his right wrist, where the healer put hers; and he let his breath go long, as a sleeper's does, and counted.

At ten the short reading gave him the moving. It was his own gait, quick and slow, and a good deal quicker than it ought to have been in a person who was lying down.

He went on beyond ten, into the deep reading. They came up out of it one at a time, as they always had, like the furniture of a room when the eyes have grown used to the dark.

Wind was first, since it was the oldest, and it had always leaned toward a girl. It was a dry hiss in his feet, like pale grass going over in a breath of air. It was good for ten strides, and it had to be paid for at the end of them with a locked beat in which he could not have lifted a heel. He had spent it two days ago in a cut, running away from the people whom he was guarding.

The read went round beneath it, under his soles, low and even: a millstone heard through a floor. That one was Brom's, and it worked for its keep. It would tell him the weight of anything within a pace and a half, and which way the weight was going; and if he leaned on it long it took his near sight for an hour.

Something ticked in his right hand, as a lamp-wick ticks when the oil is poor. That was Ember, a single point in a single place, and the heat of it came home to the hand that held the thing.

Pressure drew and filled under his breastbone, slowly, like a bellows under a tired arm. It went out of him narrow and deep, and it came back at him off anything curved.

Compression made no sound of its own. He found it, as he would have found a rope in a dark barn, by leaning on it: his whole frame creaked, very faintly, like hemp under a load. It wanted both heels down, and ground worth sending a load to.

There was the held note, thin and steady, like a nail that has been driven and has not quite done ringing. Anchor had asked at every sill and gate since Ostrand whether he would not like something set across. He had told it yes twice since the Line, and no more times than he could count.

Higher than that, in his right arm, a wet finger went round the rim of a glass. Blade would put an edge on steel for as long as a struck match burns, and it was the steel that paid.

The ninth he knew by what was missing. By day Shadow lay shut under its seal, and it made no sound whatever; he could tell where it was only as a person tells where a rug lies in a dark room, because the floor does not ring there. It would lift at dusk, as it lifted every dusk. He would tell it no.

Under everything lay Storm, the heaviest, a sea turning over a long way off, so low that he had it in his teeth before he had it in his ears. It was laid a lane at a time, and every lane came with its own bill.

And the tenth was the thing that he was going along with. It had no sound. He knew it as a walker knows his own stride. It had been a healer's, and it read whatever flowed in a living body under his hand, his own or another's. For another's the rule stood over it in two people's writing: *ask first, every time*.

That made ten. He had called them fragments since he was fourteen, and pieces, and entries in a list. Lying in the grass he had them as the going-along had them, which was none of those. They were ten currents, and not one of them had a bank. Each ran on into the next as water runs into water, and a healer had found that out before he did, with both her hands, and had told him so on a ridge.

---

He went down toward the middle.

He did not go at it. He had gone at it three times, and it had cost him an order. He chose one current, the hiss of Wind, and gave the going-along to it as a leaf is given to a stream. He did not steer.

A long way short of the place, the Wind began to lean.

It was farther out than he had ever marked, since on every other night he had been looking ahead, at the place itself. There was no check in the lean, and nothing piled. The Wind bore off as a road bears off round the shoulder of a hill, which has gone that way for as long as there has been a road. He leaned with it. He went by on the one hand, and did not look in; the lean eased, a long way beyond, and the hiss ran on toward his feet as though nothing had ever lain in front of it.

He came back and took the millstone, and it went by on the other hand. He took the wick. He took the bellows, which lived closest of the nine, and bent least far and most sharply. He took every one that had a sound, in its turn, and none of them touched the place or slowed for it. Then he let the going-along go by itself, with nothing to ride on, the whole way round, as a man walks the bounds of a field, until it stood again where it had set out.

It was the first time that he had been all the way round.

He lay and counted what he had, and it was a good deal. He knew where the place was. He knew how far out the leaning began, for each of the nine. He knew that the way round was worn smooth, with never a ripple in it; and he knew now how far it was about. He went down that column with something like pride, as a small pale woman went down a row that agreed.

Then he saw what kind of column it was.

Every line of it was about a thing that moved. It told him where the Wind leaned, and how the millstone went by. There was not one line in it about the place. There never had been. *Doesn't flow. Doesn't change.* He had written those on a leaf, and had taken them for findings about the place; and they were not. They were only the instrument's way of saying where it left off.

The going-along felt current, and it had never done anything else. It had been a healer's means of telling how the rivers ran in a body, quick or slow or choked, and it knew a river only by going along with it. Set it where nothing ran, and it did not come back and say *empty*. It did not come back and say *full*, either. It had nothing to go along with, and so it said nothing at all; and for the better part of a year he had been writing that nothing down in pencil, with the day at its head, as though it had been an answer.

It was a cord on a stone. A man might run a cord round a boulder in a field, and knot it, and carry the knot home with him, and know to the width of a finger how far it was about; and he would know no more of what lay inside the boulder than he had known at breakfast.

He took his fingers from his wrist, and put his right hand down to push himself up.

It came down on the foot of the gatepost. The going-along had not been called home yet. It went down his arm to the palm, as it had gone to a healer's wrist when he had her leave, and there it stood. The stone gave it nothing to travel on. He had always known that it would not; the tenth did nothing whatever on a dead thing, and he had never troubled to think about that.

What held him there, half risen, with his weight on that hand, was that he knew the feel of it.

It was the same nothing. To the tenth of his fragments, a gatepost and the middle of himself came back alike.

He did not care for that at all. He made himself hold it at arm's length until he could see what it was worth, and it was not worth much. It did not say that the place in him was stone, or dead, or of any sort. It said that this instrument could not tell the two apart. Karis kept a rule for exactly this, and had said it twice over a cord in a frozen yard, and he had not understood until now what it cost her to keep. He had learned something about the tool. He had learned nothing about what the tool had been laid on.

He called the going-along home, and it came, and lay down. He was cold through, as he always was after it, and as empty as if he had hauled on a rope all morning.

He needed an instrument for what did not move.

He knew of two. One stood on the crown of the town ahead of him. It had been built to read whatever was brought near it, and he had written in pencil that he would not go within a square's width of the thing. The other sat underneath the same town, with a short strange name, and read how things were built. Whether a man who could tell how a wall stood could tell how a boy did, Cael had no idea.

He lay a moment longer, looking up through the hedge at a sky with nothing in it, and found that he feared the second a good deal more than the first. He had only to keep away from the first. He would have to walk up to the second and ask.

---

Lira was on her feet when he came through the gate, with a stem of grass between her teeth.

"You were as long as Brom's boot," she said. "Did you go in?"

"No. Round."

"And?"

"It'll be in the book tonight. You can read it there."

"You'll not say it?"

"I've not got it in the right order yet. It'll be in order by tonight." He got the box up by one becket, and she steadied the other, without being asked, with her right hand. "I didn't touch it. That part I can say now."

She looked at him for the space of a stride. Then she took the stem out of her mouth and threw it at the brook, and went to get her own box.

The road ran straighter from the brook, between hedges that were older and thicker, and it began to be a town's road. There were carts in both directions. At five miles there was a farm that stood close on the verge, with a woman beating a rug in its yard; at four there was a lane with a signboard at its mouth, and a painted hand on the board, and Seln read whatever stood under the hand without breaking his stride.

The town came up with every furlong. He kept his eyes on its foot, and he learned the foot of it very well. The lowest wall ran clear round the bluff, as far as he could follow it, with square towers at even distances, and he counted eleven of those before the river hid the rest. There was a gate in it with a tower on either hand. A bridge went up to the gate, long and low, and there were things crawling on the bridge that he took for wagons.

He did not raise his eyes above the second wall. He knew where the third was, and what stood within it, as a person knows where a dog is lying in a room.

"That's a deal of wall," said Brom, from ahead. "Somebody's paid for that by the yard. Three times."

"The third is the oldest," said Karis. "It will be the smallest, and the best laid. Each of the others was built when the one inside it grew too full."

"Then it's a tree. With rings."

"It is very like a tree." She sounded pleased. "I had not thought of putting it so."

"I've split enough of them."

The stick had taken its place again on Cael's left, on the ditch side. It had not lost a stride on him in six miles. He could hear what that was costing, since the breath beside him came short at each planting of the bound leg, and the tarred case had been shifted twice to the other shoulder and twice back.

"I saw a man go through a gate, at that brook," said Vastin.

He said it to the road in front of him, at about the fourth mile, in a voice pitched for one hearer.

"He was gone for some time. He came back the colour of that road, and a young woman who had been set to keep the hedge asked him three questions and was content with half an answer." The stick came down. "I say so in order that you may know it was seen. I have found that people on this side of the Line prefer to be told. I do not ask what it was."

"No," said Cael.

"I should have asked, once. I should have asked it in writing, and required the answer under two hands by the week's end."

"I know. I was there."

"So you were."

They went another hundred yards. A cart went by them with a load of slates, and the carter touched his cap to the boxes.

"I shall want a quarter of an hour of you before that town," said Vastin. "Not on the road. Somewhere that a man can stop. I have carried a thing three hundred miles that is not in any flat, and I find that I should like to set it down before I go through a gate behind you."

Cael turned that over. He had kept a question for that man since a board at Lowmarch, and had thought himself the only one of the two who was carrying anything.

"Tonight," he said. "There'll be a halt. They'll shut those gates at dusk, and we'll not be at them by dusk, at this pace."

"At my pace. You may say it."

"At your pace."

"Thank you." The old man drew a breath, and let it out. "It is a curious thing, to be the slowest in a company. I had never had the opportunity."

The third post came up out of the afternoon, a long way ahead, where the hedges ended and the river meadows began. It was too far off to read; but there was a figure on it, and under the figure three short bars.

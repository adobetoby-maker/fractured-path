## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —

- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult

- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 19 — Different Verbs

The fork-stone stood where the ruts parted, a slab of the dark rock as tall as Brom, set on end by people who had left no other mark on that moor.

They came to it at dusk on the two hundred and eightieth day. One pair of ruts went on past it to the west, and began at once to climb a long bare rise that shut out the sky on that side. The other turned north, along the moor's edge, toward the blue hills. Somebody had cut a hand into each face of the stone, with the fingers pointing, and a hundred cold terms had worn both hands nearly smooth.

The fold lay a little way down the northern branch, in a dip. It was a drovers' pen, round, of dry stone laid shoulder-high without a grain of mortar, and three parts of it were open to the sky. The fourth had been roofed with slabs. In that corner there was a hearth built into the wall, with a flue, and beside the hearth a stack of cut peat as high as a man's waist.

"You burn what you want, and you cut as much again before you go, from the hag behind," said Oryn. "It's been the rule here since before the stone was stood up. A fire with a wall round it and a roof on it isn't on a road. I've slept here on thirty rounds."

Brom built it, laying the peats on end as she showed him, and Karis set one point among them without being asked; and the thing caught slowly, with a sour brown smell that Cael had never met. It was the first flame that the seven of them had sat by since a hold on a shoulder, and only the second since the river.

They did not all sit. Vastin stood in the gap of the fold, where the hurdle went, with his back against the stone and the bound leg out before him. He had come six miles from the spring on that stick, at the tail of the column, and had been offered a mule twice; and he had the grey look of a man who is counting what is left in him and finding it sufficient by a very little.

Seven bowls went round, and the mule had the open part of the pen to herself, where she stood with her rump to the wind.

Oryn ate standing, at her stride and a half, and when her bowl was empty she set it down on the wall's top and pushed neither sleeve up.

"I'm not an archive person," she said.

It was said to the fire, and nobody had asked her anything.

"That's the head of it, and I'll give you the rest in order, since I've had long enough to arrange it. You're going under a town to read. I've nothing to do down there. I can't read a stone; I'd not know a stratum from a stair. I mend what's put in front of me, and then I walk to the next. So you'll not be thinking that I'm coming, any of you, and I'll not have to watch you not ask."

"Nobody'd thought it," said Brom.

"You laid seven bowls."

"I've laid a bowl for you since a heap of stones. I never thought that it meant you'd go down a hole."

She looked at him, and the corner of her mouth went down.

"There's a thing that none of you has asked me in two hundred and eighty days, and I've been glad of it. I'll say it once, since it bears on what I want of him." She did not look at Cael. "I wasn't made out here. I was made in a city, inside the Line. It doesn't matter which; they're all one city, with different rivers. There was a house there for the sick, with forty beds and six of us to tend them, and I'd my rank from it."

"Iron," said Lira. "The fourth."

"Provisional. I never stayed to have it made anything else." She rubbed her palms together slowly. "Every bed in that house had a sheet on a board at its foot. There was the name on it, and the complaint, and what had been spent. And there was a column for the patient's tier."

"For the patient's," said Karis. Her pencil was not in her hand.

"Not for the healer's. For theirs. I filled that column in for a long while before I asked what it was for; I thought that it was like the name. Then there was a week of fever in the low town, and the house was full to the stairs, and I found out." She spoke as she spoke a finding. "We'd so much in us, the six of us together, as I've so much in me tonight, and it had to be spent on somebody. It went down the column from the top. There was a Gold in a bed by the window with a wrenched wrist. He had what he wanted on the first day. Then the Silvers had theirs."

Nobody at the fire moved.

"There was a woman brought in from the river sheds on the third night. She'd no tier that anybody could find. She'd never been stood on a plate, or her paper was lost; I never knew which. So there was nothing to put in the column." Oryn's eyes had gone to the peats. "A sheet with an empty column goes to the bottom of the heap. It's not cruelty. It's only how a heap is sorted."

"Did she die?" said Lira, very low.

"She did not. I'll not have you think it worse than it was. I went to her at night, off the sheet, with what I had left, and it was enough. And in the morning the woman who kept that house showed me the board, where there was a spending and no tier written against it. She said that a healer who spends off the sheet has stolen from whoever stood next on it." Her spoon was in her hand, and she turned it over. "She was right. That's the thing that I've never got past. By that house's sums she was right, and I couldn't fault a line of them. So I went to look for some other sums."

"You left," said Cael.

"I walked out of a gate in the coat that I stood in, and I've not been through one since."

---

She let them have that for the space of three breaths, and Cael did not need it explained. He had been a sheet with an empty column since his Kindling; and he understood now why a healer who had never asked him for a tier had once looked at his hand, on a mountain, as a person looks at a shut press.

"So I know what leaving is," said Oryn. "I've done it the once. It's when you go and you don't mean to come back to it, and you don't tell them where you'll be. I'll not have this mistaken for that."

She had turned from the fire, and her face was as level as he had ever seen it, and a good deal less tired than it had been at the cut.

"Leaving and routing are different verbs. I'm doing the second."

"Say the route," said Brom. He had his thick fingers ready, to count it on.

"North by that rut at first light. There are five gates up along the hill-foot that I've not seen since before the snow, and one of them has a child in it that I was to have heard a cold term ago. Then down the far water to the ford, and over, and on to the mill from above. It's my own round, walked the wrong way about. The mill has waited all the cold for me, and it'll wait as long again and say that I'm late." Her eyes went to the small pale woman on the meal-sack. "How long does a person want, to read a hole under a town?"

"I cannot tell you. I have never been let into one."

"Three weeks," said Seln.

He had said it to the peats, and everybody at that fire turned to him, since he did not give figures that he had not counted.

"A provision of that kind is granted by the week. I have seen the form. In the first week the keeper of a place decides whether he believes what he has been shown. In the second there is work. And nobody who has waited years for a thing has ever left it at the end of a second week." His hands were in his sleeves. "I should not put my name to it. I should be surprised to be wrong by more than four days."

"Three weeks is five gates and a ford, at my pace, with a day in hand," said Oryn. "That'll put me at Millrace. I'll be at the mill's bench when you come down off that road, and if you're late I'll hear of it from a carter."

"And if you're late?" said Lira.

"Then somebody's needed me worse than you do, and you'll wait."

She came round the fire then. She stopped at her stride and a half from Cael, and he saw her mouth open; and he knew the sentence before it came, not by its words but by its shape, since he had watched it arrive in her three times and be shut in.

This time she did not shut it.

"You said a station, at that board. He said it first, by the fire, and then with a bowl turned over. I know stations. There's one on the crown of every city inside the Line, with steps going up to it and a square in front, and I've stood in such a square with a line of children and watched them go up one at a time." She did not push up her sleeves. "It's the one thing in a town that's built to read whatever is brought near it. And I've had my hands on you, a long while back, and I found a thing that I made you write down and leave alone. I've not touched it since. I don't know what it is."

"Nor do I," said Cael.

"No. So I don't know what a station does with it. That's the whole of my reason, and it's no better than that. I've no finding. I've a thing that I can't read, and a thing that reads, and I'd not walk any patient of mine past the one with the other in him until I knew." Her voice had not risen. "You go inside, you don't go near the station. Not the steps, not the square. You promise me in that log of yours, because I've seen what the log does to you."

Nobody spoke, and Karis had lowered her head. Vastin, in the gap, was looking at the healer with an attention that Cael could not read; he had certified those stations, and he said nothing.

Cael did not ask her what she meant about the log, since he knew. A thing that he had only said, he could weigh again in the morning. A thing that he had written in Hesk's book was done; he had kept a count of uses for a whole cold term because a small pale woman had seen him write the number.

It was what she was asking for. She did not want his word; she wanted the one form of it that he had never broken.

He took the book out of his coat, and opened it on his knee, toward the fire. He set the day at the head, since he could not write anything without it. Then he wrote what he had been asked for, and it took him a good deal longer than the length of it deserved.

"Read it," said Oryn.

He read it to her, aloud, with the peat-smoke in his eyes.

*The fork. Oryn north; five holds; routing, not the other verb. Her term, given at the fire, taken in the log because that's the form she'll take it in: inside Treswick I do not go near the station. Not the steps. Not the square. Written here so she can read it, and so I can.*

He turned the book round, and held it out to her across the stride and a half.

She did not take it; she bent, with her hands on her knees, and read it where it lay in his, from the day to the last word, with her lips moving. He had written *inside*, and he had not seen that until he heard himself say it. There was no slip any longer between himself and that word; he had put it down in pencil, in the plain hand, as a place where he would be.

"That'll do," said Oryn. She straightened. "I can read that. I couldn't have read a promise."

---

She priced them after that, one at a time, while the fire was good.

It was what she did at every gate before she left it, and Cael had seen it done at a mill and at a heap of stones. She did not sit to it. She went round the fold at her own distance from each, and asked before she put out a hand, and said what she found to the person and to nobody else; but the fold was small, and all of them heard all of it.

"Arm," she said to Lira. "May I?"

"Yes."

The thick fingers went round a forearm that was no longer quite as thin as a rail. Her lips moved to ten.

"Fifteen days. It held a hound yesterday, by what I'm told, and it's none the worse. It's slow, and it'll be slow for as long again. They've stone floors where you're going, and stairs. Don't fall on it."

"I've been not falling on it for fifteen days."

"Then you've the habit."

Brom had his boot off before she reached him.

"That's a foot. Ten days, I said, and it's nine now. The shoulder's another woman's knot, and I can't read through it, and she's the far side of a river. You'll be a month and more from her." She put one finger on the drover's turn, as the old woman had, and took it away. "If it tells you anything, you put down whatever you're carrying. You don't undo that. You find somebody with a bench."

"There'll be none that'll touch me in there. I'm Copper in a book."

"Then you'll put the thing down and leave it down, which is harder." She had gone on to the meal-sack already. "You, I can find nothing the matter with. You've not slept a night through since a ferry, and you'll go down under a town and forget that people eat. I can't mend that. Somebody's to put a bowl in front of her and stand there."

"I'll stand there," said Brom.

"I know that you will."

She stopped before the sill of the hearth, where a small grey man sat with a case on his chest.

"You've never had my hands on you, and you've never offered, and I'll not ask; you'd say no, and I'd sooner not make you say it." Seln inclined his head by a very little. "I'll price what I can see from here. You've carried that on the one shoulder for longer than I've known you, and it's pulled you a finger out of true. Change shoulders."

"I shall consider it."

"That's a no in a clean shirt."

She crossed the fold to the gap.

Cael watched her do it, and she did not go any quicker than she had gone to the others, or any slower, and she stopped at the same distance.

"Knee. May I?"

"Yes," said Vastin.

She went down on her heels, and ran two fingers under the binding's upper edge, and under the lower, and pressed with her thumb beside the joint while she looked at his face and not at her thumb.

"This is the first day. There are twenty-nine after it. It's stood a spring and six miles of road, and it's no worse than I left it, which is more than you deserved." She sat back. "Stick; binding; no kneeling. No walls. You'll want to carry something tomorrow, when those boxes come off my mule. You're not to."

"I shall take that under advisement."

"You'll take it under your arm, with the stick." But she had not said it sharply. She had given him as long as she had given a girl's arm, to the breath, and he had marked it; Cael saw him look after her as she rose, as a man looks who has been handed something across a counter and can find no price on it.

"You priced him like one of us," said Lira, when the healer came back to the fire. She said it without any edge. She only wished to have it right.

"A knee's a knee. I told you what I think of that column."

That left one, and she had kept him for the last.

She stood before Cael at her stride and a half, and she looked at his hands. They lay on Hesk's book.

"You, I've not read. I said at my own plank that when I put my hands on that again I'd know what I was reading for, and I'd have slept. I don't know yet. And I'll not do it tired, at a fork, with a round in front of me."

"That's on me," said Oryn. "It's the one thing I'm walking up that road owing, and I'll pay it."

"Then it'll keep," said Cael.

"It'll keep three weeks. I'm not easy about it. I'd sooner you knew that I'm not."

She took up her bowl from the wall's top, and looked into it, and found it empty.

"Three weeks, and the mill. And you'll not come to me there with what you think that I ought to hear." Her eyes came up, and went along the six of them, and they were as level as a plank. "Bring me whatever walks out of that archive with you. All of it."

Cael had a thing in him, since noon, that had walked out of a row of paper and that he had not written down. He heard those three words fall on it; and he said nothing, and was aware of a small grey man at the hearth who said nothing either.

---

The boxes came off the mule on the two hundred and eighty-first day, as soon as there was light to rope by, and the frost was white on the fork-stone.

Brom unroped them himself. He had lashed them on the brown back at a ferry's shed with the beckets outward, so that they might go up any road; he had not said then that the last of that road would be on people's shoulders, and he did not say it now. He took the heaviest on his left side, where the webbing was not. Lira took the second by its beckets, using both arms. The third stood on the frozen turf.

A raw hand in linen went out toward it, from the side where a stick was.

"Twenty-eight," said Oryn, without turning round. She was drawing the mule's girth.

The hand stopped.

"I was not about to lift it. I had thought to steady it, while he—"

"It's steady," said Cael. He got the third box up by one becket, and Brom steadied the other long enough to swing it to his shoulders. "It's been steady since Ostrand."

Vastin put his hand back on the stick. He did not look pleased, and he did not argue; it was a middle state, and Cael thought that the man had not spent much of his life in it.

There was not a great deal else to be done, since Karis had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other, as she had carried them off a landing. Lira had the second box. The tarred kit case remained by the fork-stone. The healer's pannier stayed on the mule, with her linen in it, and a bow hung beside it with its one bolt.

"She'll look for you at every halt," said Brom. He had the mule's nose in his big palm. "She's had three boxes on her since the snow."

"She's a mule. She'll look for where the barley came from." Oryn took up the cheek-strap. "I'll tell her that it's gone under a town."

She did not say goodbye, since she had never said it to anybody, at any gate. She looked at each of them once, in no order, and at a bound knee last. Then she stood a moment before Cael, with her free hand shut, at her own distance.

"Not the steps," she said. "Not the square."

She turned the mule's head to the north.

They watched her go down the rut and past the fold, a square figure in an old coat with a small brown beast beside her. She walked as she walked to any gate, without haste, and she did not look back. Cael had heard why, once, though not from her. A healer who looks back has only doubled what she is carrying.

"It's six bowls," said Lira.

"It's seven," said Brom. "I'll lay hers. Somebody can carry it empty for three weeks, and I'd as soon that it were me."

When the northern rut had taken Oryn and the mule out of sight, Vastin went back to the fork-stone. He picked up the tarred kit case by its strap without asking anybody and came after the other five.

They took the western ruts, and the box rode badly on Cael's shoulders for the first furlong, and then he found the way of it, as he had on a stair with something heavier. At a quarter of a mile he turned.

He had not meant to; but the road had begun to climb, and from where he stood the whole of the fork lay below him in the early light, with the stone at the parting, and the round pen, and a thread of blue going up from its corner where the peats still smouldered. There was nobody on the northern rut for a long way. Then he found them, very small, where the moor's edge bent: a dark upright speck and a lower one. They were not hurrying, and neither had turned its head.

He looked for as long as it took Brom to get twenty strides ahead of him. Then he shifted the box, and went up after the others.

The rise took them the whole of the forenoon.

It was a long bare swell of the moor, a mile and more from its foot to its head. The ruts went up it aslant, as ruts do when they have been cut by loaded carts, and the wind came over the top of it into their faces. Nobody spoke, since it was a road, and the hold lay on all of them; and there was a box on three backs, and a man on a stick behind, who would not be waited for and could not be left.

Cael walked with his eyes on the ruts.

He had a good deal to carry besides the box, and he went over it, a stride at a time, as he would have gone over a count. There was a job, done and paid, with a hundred and thirty-eight marks to show for it. There was a wall that he had stood behind for the space of one count, and an eleventh thing that had not come of it. There was a mark in a small ruled square, which was on five sheets and an iron stake and which nobody could read. He had a name, short and strange, for a man who sat under a town. He had two sentences in a book, in his own hand, about a flight of steps and a paved place.

And there was a day. He had not written that, and he did not go over it. He only knew where it lay, as a person knows without looking where the stair is in his own house.

They came to the top a little before noon.

The ruts went over a lip of bare rock and began to go down; and the whole country opened in front of him, as it had opened once from a shelf, on the second day past the Line, when it was the east that he was looking at.

This was the west. The moor fell away in long brown folds to a plain, and the plain was green. There were fields on it, with hedges, and he had not seen a hedge for two hundred and eighty days. A river came down out of the north and went across that green in loops, shining, and in the nearest of the loops the land stood up in a bluff.

There was a town on the bluff.

He counted it, since he counted anything: it went up in three steps. A wall ran round the foot of the bluff, grey and long, with a great many roofs behind it. Higher, where the ground steepened, there was a second wall, and the roofs inside that were of slate. The third was at the top, close and pale, about the crown of the rock. It was a day's walk off still, and he could have covered the whole of it with his thumb.

"Three tiers," said Lira, at his shoulder. She had set her box down. "They've built the ladder out of stone. I'd wondered what it would look like from outside."

Nobody else said anything, and Karis had stopped a yard in front of them all. She stood with a calfskin under her arm and the wind pulling at her coat, and looked at a place whose name she had read once on the spine of a catalogue that she was not let to open. Seln was counting gates, with his lips. Brom lowered his box to the rock and rolled his left shoulder, and studied those three walls as he would have studied any wall.

There was a light on the crown.

It stood above the highest roofs, at the very top, where a town of that kind keeps the one building that reads. It was pale, and very small at that distance, and it did not flicker as a fire does. It burned as steadily in the full noon as a lamp burns in a shut room.

Cael did not look at it.

He had seen that it was there; and he turned his eyes from it as he would have turned them from a hound at forty yards, without haste, and set them on the lowest wall, and kept them there. It cost him a little; and he found that it was a thing which a person could do, and that it would want doing a good many times.

A stick came up over the lip behind him, and stopped.

Vastin stood with both hands on its head, getting his breath, with the bound leg a little out. He looked at the town for some time. He had gone in at those gates, no doubt, on a horse, behind a clerk, with a seal; and he looked now as a man looks at a house that he once had the keys of.

He said nothing, and nobody asked him to.

Cael turned to Karis. He had not known until he was speaking that he meant to.

"The oilcloth. Have you got it where you can reach it?"

She looked at him. Then she shifted the hide, and put her gloved hand into the breast of her coat, and brought out a square of oilcloth no bigger than a man's palm. She opened it on the top of Brom's box, along its own creases, and held down the corner that the wind wanted.

There was one sheet in it, of the Compact's ordinary desk paper, in a square upright hand. It lay the wrong way up to him. A schedule of housings ran down it, as dull as a tally of sacks; and half way down, between two strokes of a pen, there was a title that nobody had ever met, and the name of the town in front of him.

He had kept his hand a hand's width from that page for the whole of a lamp, so that he might still say no to it. He had ruled on a slip instead, at a plank, in daylight. He had walked three days up a road behind a job. Somewhere on that road the no had gone, without his having marked the stride; it had gone at a spring, perhaps, between two hands laid flat on a rock.

He put out his right hand, and laid it flat on the page.

It was only paper, cold, and a little rough, and the wind moved it under his palm.

"Did it bite?" said Lira.

"No."

"I said that it wouldn't."

He lifted his hand. Karis folded the cloth over the sheet without a word, and put it back inside her coat, where it had ridden; and her small pale face had nothing in it that could have been written down.

He got the box onto his shoulders again.

They went down off the rise in file: a quiet man in front, and a girl with a case in her left hand, and a big one with a box, and a small one with a hide. A boy of nineteen came after them, who had been taken to a great many registries in his life, and sent for by some, and filed in all of them. Last came an old man on a stick, who had been a marshal and was learning to be nobody. Cael had never before walked toward such a place because he had chosen to. He kept his eyes on the lowest wall.

## Rhythm calibration (measured, 2026-10-01)

Book 1 Movement 1 — the first movement drafted from this brief — overshot the formula in
the short direction: sentence mean 8.8 words (target 14.6), 41% of sentences at five
words or fewer (target ~28%), 0.3% at forty-plus (target ~3.3%), Flesch-Kincaid grade 2.8
(target 6.8), about 106 dialogue tags per 10k words (target ~41). Paragraph median (15)
and scene spacing were close. So, when drafting:

- Let thought, action and description run in full, well-built sentences. Compound and
  complex sentences with a clear hierarchy are the house texture, not the exception.
- Join a run of three or four clipped statements into one sentence when they are one thought.
- Keep short sentences for beats that land — a recognition, a hit, a turn — not as default.
- Give each chapter a few deliberately long, readable sentences (forty words or more)
  where an action or a thought earns the length.
- Drop "he said" / "she said" when the paragraph already makes the speaker clear.
- Keep short paragraphs (median ~18 words) and scene breaks about every 950 words.

## No scripted prose surgery (2026-10-01)

Rhythm and length repairs are done by reading, sentence by sentence, never by a script that
splits sentences or paragraphs at clause or sentence boundaries. Two repairs did that and
each needed a recheck to find the splits that broke a thought. Where the formula and a
character's speech disagree, keep the speech: one-to-three-word dialogue lines and the short
landing beats of a fight are not drift to be repaired.

## Source-reuse gate tightened to 8 words (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

The 10-word gate let distinctive source phrasing through in 6–9-word fragments (the first
movement's opening line, for example, echoed the current edition's). Measured at 8 words, movements
written after authors began self-checking run 1–6 shared runs per 10k; the worst ran ~30. From now
on `ed.sh overlap` and every editorial review use runs of **8 or more** words. Every listed run that
BOOK_MAP does not protect is rewritten — re-composed, not a synonym swapped. Names, Path names,
ranks and plain facts of canon will sometimes share a short run; re-compose the sentence around them.
Movements closed under the 10-word gate get one book-level 8-word sweep at book completion.

## Draft from your own event list, not from the source page (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

Book 3 Movement 6's review found the retold scenes in ch42–44 to be close paraphrase: 23–32% of
sentences followed a source sentence word for word in order, with words varied enough to pass the
8-word gate. Everything the author invented was clean. The cause is drafting with the source open.
So, for every movement:

1. Read the source chapters the packet names, once, for what happens and who these people are.
2. Close them. Write a private event list in your own words — what happens, in what order, who
   wants what, what changes. Keep it in your working notes, not in the manuscript.
3. Draft from that list and the book map. Do not reopen the source chapters while drafting.
   Choose your own entry point into each scene and your own order of beats; the source's scene
   shape is not canon, only its events are.
4. Only protected wording (BOOK_MAP) is copied, exactly, from the map — not from the source page.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

> **OPEN:** Whether Warden Coss survives the series.

> **RUMOR (source: Compact Registry):** [SHATTERED] classifications indicate dangerous instability.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

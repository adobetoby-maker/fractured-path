## 5. Book-wide repetition — the worst ten

Counts are case-insensitive lexical/formula counts over all sixty manuscript files. Some are deliberate motif counts, not sixty separate defects; the issue is cumulative audibility.

1. **Counting language — 518.** `count/counted/counting/counts` is the book's governing mind, but by the archive it becomes the default transition, observation and emotional defence (`chapter-01.md:17`; `chapter-27.md:285`; `chapter-59.md:1`). Preserve actual ledgers and tactical counts; reduce reflexive count narration where no figure changes.
2. **Role epithets — 377.** `old man` 239, `small pale woman` 60, `big man` 60, `quiet man` 18. They protect point of view and anonymity early, but named intimates still receive the labels late (`chapter-38.md:135`; `chapter-44.md:7–23`; `chapter-60.md:289`). The closing six-person epithet roll is the clearest overreach.
3. **`flat` — 275.** This includes the courier's *flat*, flat hands, flat ground and flat delivery (`chapter-17.md:3–43`; `chapter-44.md:311–315`; `chapter-52.md:27–43`). The noun is canonically necessary; the gesture/adverb uses need thinning.
4. **`as a/an man/person/woman/boy/girl ...` — 176.** These explanatory comparisons often restate an already visible action (`chapter-15.md:25`; `chapter-44.md:315`; `chapter-52.md:29`). They are the most audible sentence-level mannerism.
5. **Entry language — 134.** `enter/entered/entry/entries` properly belongs to Cael and the offices, but it spreads into ordinary emotional narration (`chapter-21.md:131–163`; `chapter-44.md:145–151`; `chapter-60.md:17–35`).
6. **Price/bill language — 96.** Brom's idiom and fragment costs justify much of it; other viewpoints borrow it often enough to blur speaker texture (`chapter-05.md:217`; `chapter-40.md:49–65`; `chapter-44.md:189–213`).
7. **`did not/didn't look` — 90.** Looking away is a real covenant, but the same negative gesture serves fear, courtesy, privacy, tactical discipline and grief (`chapter-15.md:173`; `chapter-31.md:71`; `chapter-60.md:241`). Keep deliberate Cleon/Cael look-away beats; vary routine blocking.
8. **`nobody said/spoke/answered` — 52.** Silence repeatedly receives its own explanatory tag after the scene has already made it audible (`chapter-05.md:109`; `chapter-40.md:23`; `chapter-54.md:249`).
9. **`He found that` — 48.** The phrase delays direct realization and recurs across viewpoints (`chapter-15.md:197`; `chapter-39.md:47`; `chapter-60.md:97`). Most can simply state the discovery.
10. **`not X ... but Y` — 43.** This heuristic count excludes cross-sentence antitheses; the construction is strongest at genuine epistemic turns and weaker in ordinary qualification (`chapter-39.md:19`; `chapter-40.md:43`; `chapter-44.md:315`). Preserve pole language and research limits; cut routine reversals.

Secondary tallies confirm the same texture: finger-width expressions 38; `told ... no` 36; `for a moment/while` 31; hands on stick/ash 23; thumbs in belts 19; `squared` 18; `lay down` 13; quarter-turns 0. `He told it no` is especially audible when adjacent to the 518-count/134-entry system (`chapter-34.md:13–15`; `chapter-36.md:343`; `chapter-42.md:295`).

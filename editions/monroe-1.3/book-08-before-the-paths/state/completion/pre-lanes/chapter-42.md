# Chapter 42 — Five Piers

Brom swept the handcart a second time before the light, though he had swept it at dusk, and stood its shafts against the stable wall with their ends level.

"She said swept," he said. "She never said by whom, or how often."

The woman of the half-door came out to look at it with a candle. She looked at the wheels as well, and at the floor of the end stall, where an old man had folded his blanket in four and laid it on the manger. She said that she had kept lodgers over those horses for eleven years, and had never before been handed back a stall tidier than she had let it. Then she counted them out under the arch by their feet, as she had counted them in, and went back to her bread.

It was the two hundred and ninety-fifth morning past the Line. They had lain thirteen nights in that loft, and Cael had not once gone in at the painted door.

The rope-walk was awake before the street was. A lantern burned at the head of the long shed, where the wheel stood, and another far down it, as small as a star; and between the two a line of people walked backward in the half dark, with the yarn running out between their fingers. Brom went along the pegs as he had gone along them thirteen days before. He did not need to hunt. He stopped at the coil that he had stopped at then, and took a fathom off it, and bent the end back, and looked in.

"Four," he said. "Round a heart."

"It's the same that you looked at," said the woman with the tarred hands. "I told you it'd be the same going out. I've had it by since."

"You'd no call to do that. I never put money on it."

"You hung my fathom back as you found it. I've sold rope in this shed to men who'd not do as much for their mothers." She had the coil down already, across her two arms. "Sixty yards. It's the price that I named."

He counted it onto the head of a tar barrel: two marks, in silver, out of the purse that lay inside his shirt beside a thumbed slip. He watched the coil while he did it, and Cael saw the frown go out of his face a little at a time, like a knot being worked loose with a spike. That debt had ridden in him since before the snow. It had gone over a river and come back, and up a moor, and into a town, and through eleven days under a registry. Here it lay at last, sixty yards of the right kind, across a woman's forearms.

"A hundred and twenty-eight in hand," he said.

Then he looked at his own arms, which he could not lift above the elbow, and at a box that stood beside his boot.

"I can't," he said. "I've been turning it over since the fire. It's the weight of half a man, and it'll not go on a back that's got a box on it already." He said it without shame, as he would have said that a plank was short. "I asked you last night who hauls."

"You've no need to ask. Look behind you."

There was a dray in the lane. It was a long low thing on four wheels, with two brown cobs in its shafts, and it was loaded to the rails with coils, tarred and white, under a sheet that had not yet been lashed down. A lean man with a straw in his mouth was going round it, trying each knot.

"That's for the western landing," said the woman. "Three days down the moor road. There's a broker there who's sold three-strand for as long as I've laid it, and a fortnight since he sent up for four. Forty coils. He wrote that a big man from over the water had stood in front of his stack with a full purse, and called his rope the wrong kind, and gone away with the purse; and that he'd not have it said to him twice."

Brom had gone red to the ears.

"I never called it wrong. I said that it wasn't what the man had asked for."

"He's taken it hard, whatever you said. That's forty coils that I'd not have laid." She jerked her chin at the lean man. "It's his road, and you'll ask him. But I'd think that a coil could ride with its own kind, to the place where it's wanted."

The carter had heard. He came round the tail of the dray and looked Brom over slowly, from the boots upward, as a man reads a waybill.

"You're the wrong kind of excellent," he said.

"I've been told so."

"They've had it on that landing for a fortnight, for a saying. A lad drops a keg, and they tell him that he's the wrong kind of excellent." He took the straw out of his mouth. "Throw it on. I'll not charge a coil that's going where I'm going."

His eyes went past Brom then, and found an old man standing on one leg and a stick, with a tarred case under his arm.

"And that?"

"That walks as far as he's able," said Cael. "After that he doesn't."

"I have a copper," said Vastin. "I have had it ready since last night, in the expectation of being asked what I should cost to carry. I should prefer to pay it."

"There's a tail-board. It's a copper a day to sit on, and you'll get down for every hill; I'll not have my cobs pull what can walk. And you'll hold your tongue on the moor. I'll not need to tell you why."

"You will not," said Vastin.

He counted the copper into that brown palm, and it was kept. Cael saw the old shoulders ease by the width of a finger. It appeared that this road, at least, was going to take his money.

---

The gate was opened at first light, as two painted boards had promised, and they were in the arch with the first of the carts.

The keeper in undyed grey sat on his high stool with the frame on his knee. He looked at feet, and wheels, and hooves, as he had looked at them thirteen days before, and his thumb moved the beads: eight hooves, and four wheels. He came to the boots, and Cael counted five pairs with him. The thumb went on to the tail-board, where a pair of boots hung that did not touch the ground, and it stopped.

"Those are feet," said the keeper, to himself. "They're on wheels."

"He came in on them," said Lira. "You moved a bead for him. I watched you do it."

The keeper looked at her, for the first time. It was plain that nobody argued with that frame from one cold term to the next. Then he moved two beads along the upper wire, slowly, as a man pays a debt that he had hoped was forgotten.

"Six out," he said. "Six in, the day I had you. It comes even."

Nobody at the sloped desk wrote a word of them. A load on a back was the back's, coming or going.

Cael stepped over the sill.

It was one long stone, worn hollow in the middle, with a socket at each end. *There?* asked Anchor, in its thin held note, of the two sockets, and he told it no. He had crossed that stone thirteen days ago to walk into the oldest registry on the Line, and he crossed it now with no name of his in any book of that town. He had not known that such a thing could be done. He did not think that it could be done twice.

He counted the piers. He had counted them coming in, twice, since Karis would ask; he counted them now for a reason of his own. There were five. They stood in the water like the knuckles of a hand laid flat, each with its bay above it where a person on foot might stand aside. The thing behind his eyes had come round to the first of them before his boot was on the roadway.

It wanted that bridge badly. It was a thing of dark stone laid dry, each block cut to its neighbour, by somebody who had meant every joint. *Bears*, the thing would have said, of each pier in turn. He could feel the word waiting, five times over, like five steps going down into water.

*One*, he said instead, and looked at the first pier's cutwater, and at nothing else of it. *Two.*

It was his own count, and it was made with his own eyes, which could not do two things at once. The lean came up short against it, as a dog comes up against a door that has been shut without unkindness. At the third pier the grit had not come. At the fifth he knew that he had found a thing which he could use. He had spent his life counting what was in front of him so that he need not feel it, and for once the habit was worth something to somebody.

Karis had stopped in the third bay.

She had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other; and her bound hand lay open on the parapet's dark cap, palm down, in its linen. She was not measuring anything. She only stood, with her head a little on one side, and looked along the roadway at two grooves that were deeper than a wheel's rim.

"I was told that it would be here on the way out," she said. "It is here."

"I said that," said Brom. He had stopped the whole file behind her, box and all, while the dray went on.

"I have not got a week, and I have not got a sheet for it." She took the hand away. "I know more about what stood here before the walls than any person living. I have it from a skin, and a board, and three floors that are a week east of this. I have not so much as drawn the thing that I walked over to reach them. I should like that entered against me."

"It's entered," said Cael. "Draw it going home."

"Which way is that?"

He had no answer for her, and she had not wanted one. She shifted the hide, and went on.

He was in the last bay when he understood that he was free to turn round.

It came to him quite plainly, between one stride and the next. A healer had made him write a thing in a book at a fork-stone: that inside Treswick he did not go near the station. He had kept it for thirteen days, to the letter, down a lane with four white alleys in it, and through a night when a thread of light on a loft's boards had not been steady. And the gate was behind him. The river was the Line, and he was over the river. The promise had been for inside, and he was out.

He could stand in this bay, as any carter might, and look back up the whole of that hill to the top of it.

A girl with a box on her back had come up on his left side. She did not look at him, and she did not look anywhere else. She had kept her chin level down every yard of this road, coming in, without being asked, and it was level now.

He did not turn.

It was not the promise. That was paid, and she would hear at a mill that it had been. It was only that he had seen a light go unsteady once, for a count of one, and had written *hold* beneath it. A person who has ruled *hold* does not go back to stare at the thing, to learn whether it will do so again; that is not holding.

"Five," he said aloud, to Lira. "It's still five."

"I'd have been surprised," she said, and went by him off the bridge.

---

The road ran on its causeway for three miles, with a ditch on either hand, and a white mist stood in the river meadows as high as the cobs' bellies.

It was a town's ground. A person might speak on it, as far as the first hedge, and for a long while nobody did. The dray set the pace, which was a stick's pace or a little better. The lean man walked at his near cob's head, and the six came after his wheels in the order that they had learned, with a quiet man gone on ahead into the white. Cael walked last, behind the tail-board.

So he had an old man in front of him, riding backward, for the whole of twelve miles.

He had not thought of that when the copper was paid. Vastin sat on the board's edge with the bound leg laid along a coil and the tarred case across his thighs, and both raw hands upon the stick's head; and since he faced the way that they had come, he faced Cael. There was two yards of road between them. It did not grow or shrink.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the three-mile post.

It stood at the western end of a carters' pull-in, where a hedge stopped and a bank ran on beside the ditch. Cael knew that bank. A man had let himself down upon it, in the dusk, where he could not rise again without a boy's arm, and had said why he once declined to read that boy a second time. They had called it ground afterward. It had not been friendship, and it was not now.

*No*, he told the thing. It had asked at a milestone, as it asked at every line that anybody had ever drawn across a road. The man whom it had come out of looked over his head at a town, and did not know.

"I have ridden up to that gate on four occasions," said Vastin. He spoke low, though the ground allowed more. "Each time I was met at the bridge. I certified what I was shown, and I rode down again, and I did not once see it go small behind me. One does not, from a saddle. One is looking at the next district."

"What's it look like?"

"It looks like a hill with three walls on it. I had supposed that it would look like more." He considered it a while longer. "I was eleven days in the bottom of it, as a hand, on a card that I could not have signed. I find that I knew it better from a stall than I ever did from the registrar's table. I do not say so to be thought humble. It is a finding, and it does not flatter the office."

Cael said nothing to that. He had the fair copy of a leaf inside his coat, against a pair of brass legs; and he had two things under his breastbone that the man in front of him had never been told of, of which one was the man's own.

That was the weight of the morning, and it was all in those two yards. He had entered a wall once, at this man's asking, and had entered more than was asked for. He could not pay it. A thing of that kind could not be told by itself; whoever heard how the eleventh had come would have heard how all of them came, and that was not Cael's to hand over. It belonged to four others who walked in front of the dray, and to a healer.

"You are thinking that I am owed something," said Vastin.

Cael's step did not alter. He had been trained to that by a healer's hand on his chest.

"I've a face, then."

"You have very little. I sat opposite it for three days once, with a plate, and I came away with nothing. But I have had a fortnight of it now from the side, which is another matter." The grey eyes had come down from the hill. "I am not asking. I said in a loft that I know no more of what you are than the plate did, and that I do not ask; and I have not found it harder to keep than a good many orders that I was given. I say only that I have noticed that I am not told. I enter it without complaint. I kept six people's worth of such things for a living."

"It's six people's," said Cael. "That's why."

"I had supposed so. Then it is in better keeping than most of what I was ever owed."

He turned his eyes to the hill again. It was the whole of what passed between them on that road, and Cael found it harder to walk behind than a silence would have been.

The second post stood by the brook, where a plank had been thrown over for people on foot. There was a gate into a field there, hung on dressed stone, and a headland of unploughed grass beyond it.

"That's your field," said Lira. She had dropped back beside him. "You lay in it for a quarter of an hour, and came out looking like a man who's lost a tooth."

"I'd lost an answer."

"It's the same face." She had slipped her left arm out of its becket while she spoke. The box hung from the right shoulder alone, and she let the left hand take the other becket low, at her hip, and carry there. "Thirty. That's her month. I've been slow for the whole of it, as I was told to be."

"And now?"

"Now it's an arm," said Lira. "She said so. I'm only the first to hear."

---

The hedges ended at the foot of the rise, a little before noon, and the road's rule came down on them there like a lid.

It was Seln who put it on. He had been thirty yards ahead of the cobs all the morning. Where the last laid hedge gave out, and the ruts ran up onto open ground, he stood in the road with his right hand flat at his side, palm down, until the whole file had seen it. Nobody needed the words. The lean man spat out his straw. The girl who had talked for three miles about a broker's face shut her mouth on the rest, and every sigil on that road went into a pocket.

Cael felt the twelve of them settle, as hounds settle at a threshold that they know. Wind had his feet already. The read asked, civilly, what the ruts weighed. He went round the house and shut the doors.

"Hill," said the carter, without turning his head. It was the one word that he had spent since the hedge.

The old man got down.

He did it as he had learned to do everything in the last fortnight, by his hands, a little at a time, with the sound foot finding the road first and the stick after it. The case he left on the coils. Nobody offered him an arm, since he would have had to refuse it aloud. But a big man with a box on his shoulder had fallen back to walk on his right, and Cael was on his left, as he had been once before on this very road, going the other way.

It was a mile and more to the top, aslant, with the wind coming over it into their faces.

That was new. It had blown out of the west on every day that they spent upon that moor, and had been at their faces going in; it had gone round in the night, and it was at their faces coming out. Lira looked up at the grey sky with the look that she kept for unfair rulings.

Cael counted the plantings of the stick, since he had nothing else to count. Each of them cost a breath. He had walked behind that sound, and beside it, ever since a ferry's planks, and he knew its two voices: the dull one, when the leg was only tired, and the short one, when it was hurt. This was the dull one. It had been the short one all the way down these same ruts, fourteen days ago, with a case on the shoulder above it that a healer had forbidden.

They came to the top.

The dray had stopped there, to let the cobs blow, and the lean man had a hand on a wet neck. Behind them, a long way below, lay a green plain with hedges on it, and a river in loops; and in the nearest loop a bluff stood up with three walls about it in steps.

Nobody turned. Seln had gone over the crest already. Lira stood beside the near wheel with her back to the west, tightening a becket that did not need it; and Karis had the hide against her chest, and was looking at the cobs. Cael looked at them too. A horse's ears are a good thing to look at.

It was the old man who turned.

He did it on the stick, the whole of him, since the knee would not let him do it by halves. He stood on the crown of the road with the east wind in the skirts of the brown coat and looked back, for the space of three breaths, at the last town on the Line. He had certified it four times under his own seal; and he had left that seal in a tray, a long way south of here, with nothing written to go beside it.

His right hand came off the stick. It went up a little way, toward the breast of the coat, where a pen would have been. Then it stopped, and hung, and took hold of the ash again.

He turned his back on the plain, and held up the hand for the tail-board.

The fork-stone stood at the rise's eastern foot, as tall as Brom, with a worn hand cut into each of its faces. One hand pointed along the ruts by which they had come. The other pointed north, where a second pair went away under the moor's edge toward a line of blue hills.

Those ruts were empty. There had been frost in them and rain since a mule went up, and no print was left of four small hooves or of a square woman's boots. There was only the fold a little way along, with its roofed quarter and its stack of peat, where six people and a healer had sat round a hearth while a term was written in a book.

Brom did not stop. But as he went by the stone he put his left hand up, as far as it would go, which was to his own chest; and he laid two fingers on a thing that rode under the lashing of his box, which was a wooden bowl.

---

After the fork the moor took them, and there was nothing to do for six miles but walk into the wind.

It was a country for people who had made their peace with one colour. The swells went away eastward, brown beyond brown, with the ruts drawn over them like a pair of pencil lines; there was no tree, and no wall, and the only thing that stood higher than a sheep was the dray. The wind came out of the east steadily, with nothing in it but cold. It found the gap between a collar and a neck. It leaned on a box as a hand leans, and by the second mile Cael had the ache of it across both shoulders.

He kept the file, behind two brown boots that hung from a tail-board.

Nobody had spoken since the hedge. He had forgotten in thirteen days how loud that silence was. A town's noise is a blanket; a road's quiet is a room with every door open, and a person who carries twelve things that would each like to answer has the minding of all the doors. The quietest of them would lift at dusk. He could feel where it lay, as a person feels where the cold place is in a bed.

He had gone west along these ruts with ten.

He went over that as he walked, since the road asked for nothing but his feet. There had been a boy on this moor fourteen days ago with a date in him that he had not written down, and a page that he had not touched, and a promise in a book. That boy had been afraid of a gate. He had thought that the worst thing a town could do was to write his name.

It had not written it. It had done a good deal more than that. It had let him hold a lamp for ten days over the oldest paper in the country; and at the end of the ten, a small pale woman had read out, once, what the whole of it had been built against. He had two things more in him than he had carried up this road, and a paper in his coat, and a sentence that he had said aloud by a fire. He was the same weight on the scale. He did not think that he was the same load.

Seln's fist did not go up once in six miles.

That was a thing to be entered too. Eight had laired round a sink on this moor, and five of them had been dragged out of a cut by a hold's people, and three had gone through rotten ice. It seemed that nothing had yet come to learn the road in their place. A wheel could go over that ground now from a river to a town. Five people had seen to that with a rock and a knife, for twenty marks; and an old man had done the rest of it with a knee. There had been thirty of them at the outer gate that morning.

The outcrop came up out of the east a little after the middle of the afternoon: a block of the dark plated stone as big as a barn, a furlong south of the road, with a thread of green going away from its foot.

The carter knew it. He turned his cobs off the ruts without a sign from anybody, as a man turns in at his own gate; and the dray went rocking over the bents toward a basin that somebody had built round with stones.

Karis had quickened her step.

She had not meant to, and Cael saw her check it. There had been a slab at that spring, twelve feet long and waist-high, lying level on its own rubble in the lee of the rock. She had called it a table. She had laid six years of an office's paper out upon it under river pebbles, and nothing had stirred but a corner now and then by the width of a finger.

The wind was in the east today.

It came straight along the outcrop's face, with nothing between. The slab lay as it had lain, grey and level and bare; and a dead stem of bracken went over it while they watched, end over end, and off the far side. There was a row of pebbles yet at one edge, set out by size. Two of the smallest had been blown down into the grass.

Karis looked at it for some time, with the flat under her arm.

Then she walked round the end of the rock, to the western face, which had been the weather side on every other day of that year. It was in shelter now. There was no slab there; there was only wet turf, and a litter of fallen plates, and not a level place the size of a sheet of paper.

She sat down on one of the plates, with her back to the stone, and laid the flat across her knees, and did not undo its straps.

---

They ate cold in the wrong lee, with their backs against the rock, and the carter ate with them.

He had turned his cobs out on the green by the overflow. He took the seventh bowl from Brom as a thing that the road owed him, and he had it empty before the old man had begun; and then he lay down under his own tail-board with his cap over his eyes. Nobody lit anything. It was a furlong off the road, and a person might have spoken there. Nobody did that either, beyond what the bowls wanted. They had six miles of wind in their ears.

Cael went round to the basin with the pot.

Seln was there before him.

He stood at the water's edge with his hands in his sleeves and his back to the slab, looking down into the basin; and it was so exactly where he had stood on the two hundred and eightieth day that Cael's foot stopped of itself. He had been at that slab then, with a row of paper under his hands that counted a sheet short. He had looked up, and found a small grey back turned to him at the water. And when he looked down again there had been a sheet lying before him on the rock, which had not been there, with a registry year at its head and a day.

He had laid that day against the first line of his own file. He had done it twice, a figure at a time.

He had written none of it. He had said none of it, to a healer or a girl or a man with a lamp. In the fifteen days since, a leaf had been read aloud in a gallery which made a good many things look different, and he had not once let himself carry that sheet across to stand beside it. That was for the clean page, if it was for any; and a woman had laid that page down bare, with a bound hand, and had said that nobody was to rule a line.

The grey back had not moved.

Cael knelt at the basin's lip, upwind, and dipped the pot. The water was as cold as a knife. Neither of them said one word. He did not know what that man had made of a date, and he had never asked. It came to him, on his knees in the bents, that he was not the only person at this spring who kept a thing out of his book.

He carried the pot back round the rock.

Karis had not opened the flat. But she had the reader's roll of card out of her coat, with its turn of brown tape; and beside it, on her knee, her own notebook at the fourth line. She sat bent between the two, in what was left of the light, with her bound hand holding the one flat and the pencil going in the other; and her lips were moving.

"Six," she said, under her breath, as he went by. It was not said to him. "The first. The fourth. And the last."

"You said that you'd not guess."

"I am not guessing. I am looking at how sure I am of three." She did not lift her head. "It is another kind of work, and it is the only kind that I am allowed tonight. Go away."

He went, and sat down against the third box, and took out Hesk's book. The brass legs slid from it into his lap, shut, with the cork upon their points. He had carried them for three days and had not once opened them upon a distance. A ruling of his own stood in the way, and it was a hundred and twenty-seven days old. Whatever measured had read one way in four hands and another in his.

*Two hundred and ninety-fifth day. The spring, a furlong south of the border road; nineteen miles and some from the gate. Out at first light by the outer arch: six out, as six in, and none of us written. Sixty yards of four-strand, two marks. A hundred and twenty-eight. The coil rides on the rope-walk's dray, with forty of its kind for the landing. So does Vastin, for a copper a day of his own; he walks the hills. Sixteen of thirty. Lira: thirty. She says that it is an arm.*

*Five piers. I did not look back from the bridge. The promise held to the gate and is done; the not looking was mine.*

*Known: a carter at a rope-walk knew Brom by a thing that a broker said three days' road from there. I have been written down since I was fourteen. It is not the same as being known, and I do not yet know what it weighs.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

The light was gone before he had ruled the line. Shadow lifted in him, as it lifted at every dusk, and the other eleven dropped away under it like a yard going quiet under snow.

He told it no, and it lay down.

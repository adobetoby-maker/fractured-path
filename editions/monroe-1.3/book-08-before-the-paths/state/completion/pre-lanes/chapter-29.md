# Chapter 29 — The Cart and the Board

The dray came into the yard behind them, on the two hundred and eighty-sixth day, by the carts' gate at the bottom.

Cael marked it only because it was the heaviest thing there. It had two wheels as tall as Lira, and one mule between the shafts, and it was loaded to the rails with roofing slate, stacked on edge in straw. A lad of twelve or so walked at the mule's head. A man in a good grey cloak and a broad hat walked beside the tail-board, with a tally in his hand; and there was a square of slate painted on the dray's side, which was some broker's sign.

Loads came into that yard all day, and Cael had learned in two mornings how it took them. Whatever had wheels came up from the bottom, past the beam, where a man with a slate wrote a weight against an owner; whatever had legs came down the slot in the wall, as they had, and was written nowhere until it reached a hatch. The yard sloped between the two a good deal more steeply than a carter would have chosen.

The four of them had come out of the cut a moment before, and Karis had gone to the hatch with the card. Brom had set his box down by the bench. He was looking at the dray as he looked at any load, to see how it had been put together.

"That's too much for one axle," he said. "On a slope."

It was the near wheel that went. Cael saw the pin jump from its hole and ring on the stones; and after that the wheel walked off its arm, quite slowly, as a drunken man leaves a doorway, and rolled away down the yard by itself.

The dray dropped on that corner; its bed tipped, and the whole stack of slate slid to the low side in one long clattering rush, like a dresser of plates going over. The mule screamed and plunged. The lad had let go of its head and jumped back toward the dock, as anybody would have; and the bed's rail came down across his boot and held it against the kerb.

He did not cry out; he stood bent over, with both hands on the rail, and said the same thing three times in a small surprised voice.

"It's on my foot. It's on my foot."

Two yard-men were running already, with a pole. The man in the cloak was shouting for a lever, and pointing. They went where he pointed, to the high side, where the other wheel stood a foot clear of the ground, and got the pole's end under the axle.

"Not there," said Brom.

He had not raised his voice, and it carried all the same, as a bell carries. He was walking, not running, with his eyes on the heap of slate; and Cael, who had watched him read a wall a hundred times, saw him read this.

"You'll roll it. It's sitting on that corner like a box on one becket. Lift the high side and the whole of it goes over onto him." He had come to the low corner. He crouched, and looked along the under side of the bed, and at the stub of the axle, and at a boot. "Leave that pole. Put it down. All that's holding it off his leg is the slate that slid, and the slate's not done sliding."

It was not. A single plate shifted in the heap while he spoke, and the rail came down by the width of a thumb, and the lad's breath went in through his teeth.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read.

It was the third of the ten that he carried, a piece of Brom's own Iron come to him at second hand, and it asked under the soles of Cael's feet, low and even, as it had asked of every rut for nine days; it asked also which way the weight was going, since that was the half of its trade. It could have told him to a stone. It could have told him which slate would move next.

He told it no. He stood in a registry's yard, twenty feet from a hatch with its shutter up. A girl with eggs had been turned from a gate in that town for the wrong hour on a card; he did not care to learn what was written against a porter who was seen to know a thing that he had no way of knowing.

And Brom had not asked for it; Brom had his head on one side, and was looking.

"Lira. His belt, at the back. Both hands, if you've got them. Not till I say, and when I say, you take him straight toward yourself and you don't stop." He did not look round. "Cael. The wood wagon. Two splits, thick, as long as your arm; and one of those men to drive them. Under the axle first."

"And you?" said Lira.

"I'm going to pick it up."

---

He got down on one knee beside the kerb, and put his left shoulder under the corner of the bed.

It was the good one. The right was in four yards of webbing, under his shirt, with another woman's knot upon it, and he held that arm across his thigh as a man keeps a tool out of the way. He set his left foot flat, and his hand on the stones.

Cael had the splits. He had taken them off the wagon's tail without a word to anybody. A yard-man had come with him, since the big stranger plainly knew what he was about; they knelt at the axle's stub with the wood ready. From there Cael could see the side of Brom's neck.

He watched it for the grey.

He had seen it come up a dozen times: on sprung oak, and in a hall, and on a road with hounds. It began under the collar and went over the skin like frost over a pane, and when it had gone over, a blade would not mark the man. A dray would have been nothing to him then. He would have stood up under it as under a sack.

It did not come. The neck stayed the colour of a neck, and the cords stood out in it.

Brom had a sigil in his pocket that would have made a stone of him. He had a certificate, somewhere, that said *Copper*, with a finding pinned to it that nobody had signed. He was twenty feet from a clerk who wrote down whatever was brought to his ledge; and he took a dray of slate on his bare shoulder, as a carter's son might have, with nothing to help him but his legs.

Cael had priced that man a great many ways since a yard above Ostrand. He had priced him by what he could carry, and by what he would take on his own skin for somebody else, and by a figure that a board had given him and a house had declined to sign; and he saw now that every one of those sums had had the grey in it. He had never once asked what the man was worth with the Path shut. It appeared to be a dray of slate.

"Now," he said, through his teeth.

The corner came up, by no more than two fingers; the slate groaned in the straw, and the rail lifted off a boot.

Lira had the belt in her right hand and the left hooked beside it, a beat behind, as it was behind in everything. She threw her whole weight backward. The lad came out from the kerb like a cork, and sat down on the paving with her; and his boot stayed where it was, under the rail, empty.

"He's clear!"

"I know he's clear. It's shifted."

It had. The lifting had tipped the heap again, and a dozen plates had run down into the corner all together; and what Brom had raised by two fingers was now coming down upon him with a good deal more than he had taken up. Cael saw his knee begin to open. He could not set the thing down. The kerb was still under the rail, where a foot had been, and Lira's hands were on the stones beside it.

"Wood," said Brom.

The yard-man drove the first split under the axle's stub with the heel of his hand, and Cael the second beneath the bed's rail, end on, and kicked it home. The dray settled on them. It creaked, and took the oak a little into its timber, and was still.

Brom came out from under it on his hands, and stood up slowly. He tried to lift the left arm, and it went as high as the elbow would carry it, and stopped; and he looked at it with the deep patient dislike that he had once given to a hound.

"That's gone," he said. "A week. Might be two."

Somebody was saying *easy, easy, stand*, over and over, in a low level voice, and it was not a carter.

Cael turned, and the mule was standing. It had been throwing itself about in the shafts a moment since, and every plunge had rocked the bed. It stood now with its head down and its sides going, and an old man had hold of its cheek-strap. He had the stick in his right hand. The left, which would not open flat, had shut on the leather; shutting was the one thing that it could still do well. He was looking at nobody; he had held a ferry's stern so once, in a flood, with a turn round a post.

He had not given one order. Cael went back over the whole of it to be sure. Three men had been shouting in that yard, and a fourth had been obeyed by all of them; and the one person there who had commanded for forty years had seen a beast that wanted holding, and had gone and held it.

The lad had his toes in both hands, and was counting them aloud. There were five, and they bent.

At the hatch a pen had been going through all of it. Cael looked that way once, between the axle and the bench, and saw the green book open and a grey sleeve moving. It came to him that the clerk had written *the same*, and a figure, and blotted them, while a dray came down ten yards from his ledge, since none of it was matter for the lower archive and he had no line to put it on.

"What tier?"

It was the man in the cloak. He had come round the tail-board, and he stood in front of Brom with his hat brim down, so that Cael saw of him only a shaved chin and a pair of clean hands. The voice was a quick, dry, townsman's one.

"Copper," said Brom.

"A Copper would have done that with the grey on him, and I'd have thought it no more than his work. You did it in your shirt." The chin lifted an inch. "I watched your neck. You never lit."

"It's a registry's yard."

"So it is. My master keeps eleven on his doors, and there's not one of them will shift a keg until he's lit up to do it, in front of whoever's passing; and then it's a line in somebody's book, every time." The clean hands turned the tally over. "The house is the third on the brokers' street, with the slate above its door. There is a guard's post at the store we keep inside the second wall. It's a standing pass, sealed, by the month. It's a bed in the house, above the wall, and the house's coat. Four marks, and found."

Nobody spoke. Lira had got up off the paving.

It was a real offer, and Cael knew what it weighed. There was a house in Velmere that had wanted exactly this for its son, for as long as it had had him: a coat with somebody's colour on it, and a place inside a wall, and a figure at the month's end that could be written in a letter. A panel had sat once to tell that house what he was worth. Here a stranger had told him in a yard, after watching his neck.

Brom looked at the dray, propped on two logs. He looked at the hatch, where a small pale woman stood with a card, and had not once turned her head.

"I'll think on it."

"Ask for the factor."

The man touched his hat, and went to see to his slate.

Brom walked back to the bench, and bent to his box, and put his left hand to the becket, and the arm would not take it. He stood a moment; then he turned his other side to it, and swung the box up, in one heave, onto the right shoulder, over the webbing.

"That's the one she tied," said Lira.

"It's a box."

"She said that if it told you anything—"

"It's not said a word. Cart was heavier."

Cael had his mouth open, and found that there was nothing in it that would serve. He was the one who ruled on slips. He could not rule on a shoulder; it had never been his, and the man under it was looking at him with perfect mildness.

"It goes in the book," he said.

"Put it in. Put in that it carried."

---

Lira went below the stick that morning, on the stair inside the iron.

She had said that she would, at the door, with the lamp in her hand; and she had looked at Brom's right shoulder as she said it, and then at Cael. So he came down last but one, with nothing to do but watch a box ride where a healer had said that nothing was to ride. It sat well. Brom carried it as he would have carried it on the other side, and if the knot spoke to him, he did not pass on what it said.

They were on the second flight, with the light a long way beneath, when the big man spoke.

"You think Karis is right? That it's all built on something?"

Cael took a tread before he answered.

"I think we're about to find out."

"Good. I'd hate to have turned down a real job for a wrong hunch."

Cael missed a tread, and caught it.

"You told him you'd think on it."

"I did think on it. I thought on it from the dray to the bench." The box shifted. "It's a fair post. I'd have taken it at sixteen and written home the same night."

"And now?"

"Now I've sixty yards of rope to buy going out, and a bowl to carry to a mill." He went down three steps. "He counted my neck. I've been counted by better; Pike never asked me what I was. Mind that landing."

That was all of it. Cael had been afraid, on a climbing street, of a blue coat that would read a girl's name aloud. He had not thought to be afraid of a grey cloak; and it had come and gone in a yard, while he knelt at an axle, and he had not been asked to do anything about it at all.

There was something new at the third table.

He saw it from the gallery's mouth, with the carried lamp still thirty strides ahead of him: a long dark thing stood on its end against the table's edge, beside the Hall's fourth sheet, with a fold of sacking on the floor at its foot. It was a plank. It was of oak, as black as the shelves, as long as a man's arm and two hands broad; and as the light came up he saw that one face of it had been cut.

Karis had stopped a yard from it, with both arms full, and she did not go nearer.

There were four rows. They ran across the board's width, small and square and deep, and he knew them as a person knows the letters of his own tongue in a strange hand: he had seen their kind on three floors, by lantern, in her pencil. At the end of each row the cutter had made something else, a cluster of little pecks and rings; and after the cluster, a few signs more.

"That is not paper," she said, hardly above a breath.

"No," said the voice from the desk. A leaf was turned face down. "It was on the bottom shelf of the west gallery, under a heap of quarrymen's wedges, with its face to the wall. I've had it ten years." The chair scraped. "Lamp."

Cael took the ring from Lira, and went down on his knee, a hand to the left of a plank, and let his breath go long.

---

The man had three bowls on the table's end, and a reed.

The bowls held counters: white pebbles in one, and black in another, and in the third a heap of small red squares that had been broken from a tile. The reed was as long as his forearm. He did not touch paper with his skin, and Cael had seen by now that he did not mean to; the reed did it for him, from a hand's breadth off.

"Top row of that sheet," he said to the chair. "I'll point. You lay. White where I say white."

He pointed, and Karis laid. A white pebble went down on the first sign of the row. Three black followed it, on the next three. Then red, and red; and white again; and black. She set each one below its mark, not upon it, so that her own pencil should not be hidden. It went the length of the row, twelve signs, and when it was done there were twelve counters in a line across the foot of her drawing: two white, seven black, three red.

"Now the board."

The reed went to the plank's first row. He said the colours again, and she laid them along the table's edge beneath it; and Cael, who had the best seat in the gallery for it, saw the second line of pebbles grow beside the first. White. Three black. Red, and red.

They were one line, laid twice.

Karis saw it at the fourth counter; her hand stopped in the bowl, and went on.

"What's the white?" said Lira, from the floor.

"It bears." The reed touched the air over a white pebble. "Take that out and the row comes down. There's nothing left for the rest to hang on."

"And the black?"

"Hangs. It's carried. You could change one of those for another kind, and the row'd still stand up; it'd only be carrying something different." The reed moved. "The red's nothing. It holds nothing and nothing holds it. It's there so that the row fills its stone from edge to edge, as a mason packs a joint."

"How d'you know?" said Brom. He had come to stand at the table's far corner, with his right arm folded and his left hanging. "They're scratches. One's as deep as the next."

"How do you know which wall of a house you can put a door through?"

Brom thought about it honestly.

"You go and look at what's sitting on it."

"That's the whole of it. I look at what's sitting on what." The red eyes had not left the board. "I've a Path that lets me look a good deal harder than you can. That's all the difference there is between us, and it's the dullest one on the roll."

Cael knelt two feet from that Path, and felt nothing whatever.

He had stood beside Brom when the grey came up, and beside a wall on a moor that had roared like a forge with its door open. This made no sound at all. The man only looked, from the foot of a thing upward, without blinking, while the water ran down beside his nose; and whatever it was that he did, it went into the oak as quietly as ink goes into paper. It was the stillest thing that Cael had ever known a person do with what a plate had given him.

He could not have said what he had looked for in it. A Path, as he had met them, was a thing that was done to somebody: each of the ten in him had come out of a blow, or a throw, or a pair of hands laid on to mend, and he had learned every one of them by what it cost the person at the other end. This was done to nobody. It was only a man attending, as hard as a man can, to something that another man had made.

The reed had gone back to the sheet, and it went along the white pebbles, the first and the second, and came to rest.

Cleon said, "They're orders."

Karis had risen from the chair, and did not seem to know that she had. She stood with a black pebble shut in her glove, and she had gone so white that Brom took a step.

"Karis. Sit down before you go over on his paper."

"I said no names," said the man, sharply.

"I never gave it to you. I gave it to her."

"I heard it."

"Then you'd best forget it."

"I don't forget things. It's why I asked." But he had turned his head, for the first time that morning, a very little toward the chair; and he waited until she was in it again.

"Orders to do what?" said Brom.

"I can tell you these are orders. I can't tell you what they order. For that you need the language, and the language is in the text, not the stone. That's her work, not mine."

He laid the reed down across the bowls.

"A white one stands at the head of every run, and all that follows hangs from it, and goes toward an end. A list doesn't do that. A prayer doesn't. The only writing I've ever met that's built so is the kind that tells a man to do a thing, and what to do it to, and how many times." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and it did them no good. "That's the bones. I've no more than bones. I never have had."

Vastin had not moved from the nearest bay. He spoke to the board, and it was plain that he had not meant to speak.

"To do a thing; to what; and how many times." The stick's foot turned on the rock. "I have drawn some thousands of papers in that form, and had as many drawn for me. I have followed fifty-two of them down a stair for three days, on other people's backs; and it did not once enter my mind that I was looking at my own trade."

"You'd not have known it," said Lira. "It's in scratches."

"I should have known the shape. It is the shape that I am supposed to be good at."

"You did the bridge," said Brom. "The grey one, with the five piers. You'll have had a look at that."

"Twice. It was the first thing I did when I came here."

"And the river under it?"

"It doesn't work on a river, and it doesn't work on a man, and it doesn't work on a tree, because nobody chose where the branches went. It costs me my eyes for an hour after and nothing else."

He said it as a carter states his rate, and then he went up for his bowl.

---

Karis counted while he was gone.

She did it without a pebble, on a leaf of her notebook, going from sheet to sheet of the Hall's twelve with the pencil held upright. She had forty-one kinds of sign, and had had them for the better part of a year; and she knew now, of the twelve that stood in one row, which kind bore and which was carried.

"I shall want every row," she said. "He will have to go through all fifty-two with that reed, and I shall have to lay each one and write it, before I can say how many kinds are white."

"That's your week," said Brom.

"It is a good deal of my week."

The man came down within the hour, and it was done as she had said. He went along the Hall's table through the afternoon with the reed, and she laid, and wrote; and between the sheets he went back to the plank. The second of its rows stood on the Stair's ninth sheet, nine signs long, and the third on the Ring's sixth. He found each as a carpenter finds a joint in the dark, by going to where it ought to be. Each time the reed came back to the plank, and the pebbles went down along the table's edge in the order in which they had gone down on paper, white and black and red.

The fourth row was the shortest, of seven signs, and it stood near the foot of the Stair's twenty-first sheet. When he had found it he straightened, and put both hands in the small of his back. Cleon looked from the plank to the three tables, and at the plank again.

"Same make."

Cael kept the flame where it was wanted, and did not hear what Karis answered. He saw her glove come down upon the table's edge, flat, an inch from her own pencil, where she had been told that nobody was to lean; and the man beside her looked at the glove, and for once did not say so.

He had been listening to another thing since noon. It had been said over his head, to a big man, as a rate is stated; and one piece of it had gone into him like a splinter. It was the piece about a man.

He had come to that town with one question. He had written at a fire that he was about to hand it to a stranger, and had been afraid of it for two days, in a loft, with a small pale woman's roll across his knee. He need not have been. The stranger had told a gallery, without being asked, that he could not take it.

It ought to have been a relief; and he knelt on the rock, and felt for the relief, and it was not there.

He wanted it tried.

He looked at that squarely, with his breath going long and the flame standing up. Two feet from his right hand there was a pair of sore red eyes that could see what held a thing up. They had found it in a cutting that nobody living had made, off a drawing, in the time that it takes to swallow. He had a place under his breastbone that five readings had come to and none had entered. He had been told, in so many words, that those eyes were for walls and seals and boards, and not for boys. He believed it. He wished, all the same, that the grey head would turn two feet to the left and look.

He did not say so, since there was nobody to say it to. The man took no more note of him than of the iron arm that his own lamp hung from, and Cael had asked to be exactly that.

He put the wish where he put a cord, and a hair, and a date. He would write it tonight, and he would write that he had not spoken it.

"Left," said the rough voice.

He was there already.

When the bell went they roped the boxes, and Brom took his on the right shoulder again, and nobody said anything about it. On the long stair Cael counted the steps behind that box, twenty-seven, and the twenty-two after them in the open air; and the big man did not once shift it to the other side, since there was no other side to shift it to.

The dray was gone from the yard when they crossed it. Its two logs lay by the dock, where somebody had thrown them, and the wheel had been found. Brom looked at the place in passing, as a man looks at a piece of work that he has finished and been paid for.

He wrote it in the loft, under the day.

*Brom: the left shoulder wrenched, under a dray in the registry's yard, with no Path opened. He is carrying on the right, in the old healer's knot, against Oryn's word at the fork. I did not tell him to put it down. I couldn't have made him. Owed at the mill, to two healers, by me as much as by him. Entered.*

*Cleon has a board. He put it against her fourth sheet and it was the same line twice. Some signs bear, and some are carried, and some are nothing; she'll not say how many of each until all fifty-two are laid.*

*He says what it won't work on, and I'm one of the things. I wanted it tried all the same. Not said.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

# Chapter 15 — The Loudest Thing

He understood it in the time that it took them to cover the first five strides, and it was far too late to be of use.

They had learned the road. Nothing that had ever declared at the top of that glass had stayed there. It had gone down the hill backward, with a man hanging on its tailboard and a Path blazing in him, and it had come to rest at the foot; and three times the grey things had run to the head of the cut, and found the sound already sixty strides below them. They did not go any longer to where a declaration was made. They went to where it ended.

And at the foot today there were a small pale woman, and a healer, and a mule.

He had put them there himself. He had called it the last place in that road that anything would come to.

They came down the ice as he had never seen a beast go on ice. They did not gallop. They ran low, with their legs spread and their claws out; and where the runnel bent a hand's breadth they let their hindquarters swing wide, and caught themselves, as a boy does on a slide of his own making. They were as high as calves at the shoulder. There were eight of them, strung out over ten strides of glass, and the sound was like a handful of nails drawn down a slate.

He had come two strides below Brom's stone by then, and he stood there, as he had told six other people to stand. Brom was a post above him, with the rock against his thigh. Something on the scree had gone as still as the scree itself. That was the doctrine, and it was sound. They would go by. They would arrive at the foot, where nothing was loud, and they would stand there and swing their heads. Then one of them would try the nearest quiet body with its teeth.

Karis knew how to be a post. So did Oryn. He was not certain what a mule knew.

There was a brown coat at his elbow.

Vastin had not looked at the hounds since the first of them came over the bank. He was looking down the cut. Cael, who was two feet from him, saw exactly where. A mule's long ears stood against the light, sixty strides off, beside a small figure with something bright in its hand, and a square one with its arms bare. Then the grey head turned, and he looked up the hill at what was coming. His face did not change at all. It was the face with which he had read a case through to its last sheet.

"I beg your pardon," he said, quite low; and Cael knew afterward that it had been said to him.

He stepped out into the road.

It was one stride, bad leg first, off the dead turf and onto the frozen ruts at the runnel's edge. He set his two feet, a little apart, with the stiff leg straight. He took the ash stick in both his wrapped hands and brought its foot down on the road between his boots, once, as a man knocks at a door that he has the right to open.

There was a sound like a mallet on a cask. It did not come from the stick.

It came out of the road. Cael felt it through his own soles, since for that one instant the ground under him had been told something and had answered. From the place where the stick stood, a line went across the cut. He saw it run. It went over the frozen ruts and split the runnel's glass from side to side, as straight as a thing ruled, and struck the foot of the north bank. All along that line the ice was white, in a crack no wider than a hair.

Then the air stood up from it.

He had no other words for the thing, then or later. There was no light. Nothing was thrown, and nothing went anywhere. One moment there was a road going up a hill, and eight hounds on it. The next there was a face across the road from bank to bank, as tall as the scree. He could see it only as he might have seen a pane of clean glass, by what was on it. The frost that the pack had kicked up was drifting down the cut ahead of them. It came to that place and stopped, and lay flat on nothing.

It was two feet from his face. He could have put out his hand and touched it, and he did not.

The first hound was three strides off and running.

---

It struck with its whole weight, shoulder first, and the sound was that of a sack of wet meal thrown against a barn door.

The thing did not give by the breadth of a hair. It did not ring, or shake; the frost that lay on it never stirred. The hound went back up the hill on its side, with all four legs working, and the second went into it, and the third into the second. Behind those the rest were braking with everything that they had, sliding on their haunches down the glass. One after another they came against that face in a slither of grey bodies, and were stopped.

Eight of them had come at it running, and not one had got through. It held.

Cael had seen Paths used on floors for half his life. He had seen fire run between nine points, and a girl leave one place and arrive in another, and a Gold lay the air down in lanes; and every one of those had been a thing that went out and did something. This did nothing whatever. It was a plane, as flat as a rule and as thin as a thought. It had been drawn on the ground and stood on end. It kept the ground's own refusal to be walked through, and it would have been all one to it whether a moth struck it or a cart.

He looked at the man.

Vastin had not moved. That was the first cost, and Cael read it off a pair of boots. They stood on the frozen road exactly as they had been set, and they looked as though they had been let into it. The line that crossed the cut ran under both soles. Each time that a body struck the plane, forty feet off or four, something came down through that brown coat like a blow on a post. The stiff leg took it and shook.

"Can you walk it?" said Cael. "Forward. Can you push them up the hill with it?"

"No." It came through shut teeth, very distinct. "It does not go anywhere. I told that girl so. It stands where I stand, and I may not lift a foot; if I shift so much as a heel, the whole of it is gone."

"For how long?"

"I have never been asked to find that out."

That was the second cost, and it was arriving already. It was going out of him as heat goes out of a forge when the door is opened, steadily, with nothing kept back. Cael's own Arbiter had been dark since the day that it woke, and yet he felt it from two feet away. He had stood beside Brom when the grey came up, and beside Karis with nine points alight, and neither had been a tenth of this. Every hound in that cut could feel it from the far side of the hill. It was the loudest thing that had been done on that road in the memory of the den.

It did not slide away down the ice, either, and it did not stop. It stood.

They knew it in two breaths. He saw the grey heads leave the plane, one and then all, as a flock of birds turns. There was nothing in that hard clean face to bite. The thing that they had come for stood at its southern end, behind it, in a brown coat. There was no going through. There was a bank.

The first of them set its forefeet on the scree at the plane's end, a little below Brom's stone.

Brom dropped the rock on it. He did not swing. He had said that a rock only wanted lifting and letting go. He lifted the thing left-handed to the height of his chest, with no grey on him anywhere, and let it go edge downward. The hound lay down on the plates as a dog lies down by a hearth. It did not get up.

That was one, and it had been done without a sound that a Path would have made. Not a grey head turned to it. They went by a large still man as they would have gone by a boulder, onto the bank above him, seven of them, scrabbling.

Then the bank began to move.

"Yours," said Lira's voice, somewhere over Cael's head, not loud.

She was sitting on the scree, ten feet up, with her heels dug in. He had not seen her go there. She had a plate of the dark stone in her two hands, as broad as a trencher. She sent it down the slope flat, like a boy skimming a pond; and where it struck, the whole skin of the bank let go. A yard of scree went out from under two hounds together and carried them down to the road in a rattle of plates, on their sides, with their legs going.

She had the next stone up before they had found their feet. She lifted it with both arms. The left came a little after the right, as it did in everything, and the slope did not care which hand had been late.

"It runs," she said, between her teeth. "I told you. Nothing with four legs comes up this in a hurry."

But there were seven, and one bank, and it was thirty feet from the road to the lip. A thing that could not climb it quickly could still climb it. Cael saw one of them go wide of her, up to the left, where the plates were bigger and lay still. He saw another, which she had sent down to the road, come in under Brom's stone on the low side, and shut its jaws on the nearest thing that was quiet.

It was Brom's right boot.

The big man did not make a sound, and he did not answer. He stood on one foot and a hound, with a sack on his left arm that he had wrapped for this and had not been asked for. He looked down at the thing with an expression of deep and patient dislike.

---

The one that had gone wide came over the top of the bank's shoulder, above the place where the plane ended, and dropped onto the road inside it.

It was no more than a length from Cael. It did not so much as turn its eye to him; he was a quiet body, and there was a loud one two feet beyond. It went by his knee like a grey wind and took the old man by the stiff leg, a little below the joint, and shut its teeth.

Vastin made a sound in his chest, and that was all.

He did not lift the foot. Cael saw the boot stay where it had been set, on the line, with the whole weight of a beast as heavy as a calf dragging at the leg above it. The bandaged hands went white on the stick. From bank to bank the plane stood as flat and as still as it had stood before, with the frost lying on it. Beyond it a hound that the slide had carried down was running at the bank again.

Cael had his knife out. He did not remember drawing it.

Everything that he owned came to him then at once, and each of the ten had its reason. *Edge?* said the ninth, of the steel in his fist, and it would have gone through that grey neck like a wire through cheese. Pressure had been gathering since the first hound came over the bank. It lay under his breastbone as tight as a held breath, narrow and deep, and he had learned in a shed what it did afterward. It would go through the hound, and on, into a man's knee. *There?* asked Anchor, of the place where the plane met the scree, in its thin note. A point held there would have shut the bank like a gate, and it would have taken every other thing that he had while he held it. Storm showed him the air of the cut as a lane already half laid, from the head to the foot, and what a lane would do to six hounds; and after it he would be ten counts late at everything, in the one place where a beat mattered.

He could have used any of them. The road's rule was broken already, by a better man than himself, for a better reason than he had ever had. No hound in that cut would have turned its head for one point of Ember more.

That was what stopped him, and it stopped him as a hand stops a child at a door.

They would not have turned. He could have made himself as loud as he pleased, here, at the wall's end, and it would have altered nothing whatever. The sound that they wanted was here already, and he would only have been adding to it. Seven hounds were trying to get round a wall. Not one of the seven had any wish to go through it. Why should it? Everything that it had come for was standing at the end.

He saw the whole fault then, as he had once seen the fault in a bout from a library chair.

A wall was a fine thing if what the other side wanted lay behind its middle. This one had nothing behind it at all but sixty strides of empty road, and at the far end of that, too small and too quiet to be felt, the three whom it had been raised to cover. The only reason that the pack had for crossing that line was the man who held it up.

Somebody else would have to be the reason, and it could not be done from here.

"Mine," said Lira.

She had come down the bank. He never knew how; there was a rush of plates, and she was on the road beside him with the long knife in her right hand. She did not strike at once. She put out her left hand first, the thin one, which had been out of grey wool for fourteen days. She took the hound by the loose skin behind its skull.

It was slow. He watched the fingers close in their order, the first and then the next. But what they closed on did not get away from them. The hand that could not yet hit a thing held it, as it had held a reed and a stave and a carter's boot. With the head held, she had all the time that her right side needed.

It was done in one stroke, close in, with the whole of her quick shoulder behind it, and it was not a thing to look at. The jaws came open. The hound went down across the old man's boots.

"Two hands," said Lira. She was breathing hard, and she looked at the left with something very like astonishment. "It held."

"That's two," said Cael. "There are six."

Vastin had not looked down. There was blood, dark, soaking through the frieze below his knee, and his face had gone the colour of the ice; but his eyes were on the bank, where four grey shapes were coming over the plates again, more slowly, having learned where the ground was bad.

"I have it," he said. "I shall have it for some little while yet. I cannot tell you how long, and I cannot go to it or from it." He drew a breath through his nose. "If you have a use for a wall, I should be obliged if you would find it soon."

---

"I've a use for it," said Cael. "Don't let it down. Whatever comes at it."

He looked for a place, as a girl on a mounting block had taught him to look: not where he wanted to fight, but where he would as soon be a post for a count.

It was behind him, on the north side. The low bank ran down the cut there, inside the plane, with its top crusted in loose plates; and ten strides off, where the bank stood highest, there was a place on it as far from Vastin's end of the wall as the cut allowed. A straight line drawn from the south scree to that place went through the plane a little north of its middle.

He had never in his life spent a burst to get away from his friends. He had spent every one of them, since the Line, to get at something. Each time he had landed like a parcel, with a whole count for the thing to do as it liked with him.

*Away*, he thought. *Not at.*

He told Wind yes.

There was a crack like a wet sheet shaken out, and the cut jumped. He had left the road and come to the bank, with the ten strides taken out of the middle. The plates slid under his boots, and held. Then the lock came down on him.

He had known that it would. He stood on a low bank of scree in the shadow of a hill, with one arm half out for his balance, and for one whole count there was nothing in him that answered. He could not shift a foot or shut his fist on the knife. Even his head would not turn.

He did not need it to. He had landed facing them.

Every grey head on that south bank had come round. He saw it as a man sees a thing in a dream, slowly and all at once: four hounds on the plates, and two on the road beneath, one of which had that instant let go of a boot, and each of them with its muzzle swung to the place where a burst had ended. It was a new sound. It was sharp, and it was nearer than the old one by the width of a road, and there was no bank in front of it at all. There was only the road, going down.

There were six left. They came off the scree in one slide. The bank that Lira had loosened came with them, a grey river of plates, and it put them onto the runnel's glass already running, six of them, straight at him, down a hill.

He stood behind another man's wall and could not so much as lift a hand. He had trusted his feet and his skin to a great many things in his life: to a count, to a read of a pace and a half, to his own judgment of a floor. He had never before trusted them to something held up by another person's knee, and a person whom he had known as an officer.

The first of them reached the line.

The plane took it at the full stretch of its run, and this time there was no braking, since a beast cannot brake on ice with a hillside of stone coming down behind it. It struck, and dropped at the foot of that flat face, and did not stir. The others went in on top of it. They went in sideways and end over end, in a heap as high as his waist, with the plates rattling down upon them. A stride in front of his own boots, the frost on that clean face shook once and lay still.

"One," he said, aloud, to nobody, as a girl with her arm tied had once said it for him.

He could move.

The ice went in the same breath. The crack that the wall had made lay across the runnel like the cut of a knife. Under a heap of that weight, the glass on the uphill side of it broke up in plates of its own, and what was beneath came up black. There was water under it. The seep had been running there all the cold term, a stride wide and a hand deep, under its own roof.

Two of the heap were standing in that water when they found their feet.

He saw what it did to them. Water hid a hound from what it hunted, and hid what it hunted from the hound; a small exact voice had read it out at a ford. They stood to the hocks in the runnel with their heads lifted, and they swung them, slowly, to one side and to the other, like things that have been wakened in a strange room. The loudest thing on that road was twelve feet from them, and they had lost it.

A small grey man came over the north bank above the wall.

Cael had not known where Seln was until that moment. He stepped down into the heap as he would have stepped off a kerb, with a short knife held low. Nothing in it knew that he was there, since he gave off no more than a stone does. He bent to the nearest of the two in the water, with one hand on its neck as a man steadies a horse, and it folded at the knees. He did not look at it afterward. He had done it as he did a paper.

Brom had got down off his stone, with one foot dragging and a rock in his left hand. He lifted it over the other, and let go.

---

That was five.

Cael counted them from the bank's top, as he counted any room. There was one on the plates under Brom's stone. There was one across the road inside the wall, where a pair of boots had stood all this while with a hound lying over them. There was one at the foot of the plane, that had broken on it. Two lay in the black water.

Three had come out of the heap alive.

They had been last into it, and so uppermost. They got clear of the plates on the uphill side with their coats full of grit, and they stood for a breath on the frozen ruts with their heads low. Not one of them looked at the wall. Karis had it in her fifth line. Five of their kind had been killed in that place in less time than it takes to say so, and by nothing that made a sound. The only sound in the cut was a thing that could not be bitten.

The first of them turned to the north bank, and the other two went with it.

They went over the worn place and down, running, as they had come up. Cael, who stood on that same bank forty feet lower, saw the whole of it. Fifty-five yards of scree went by under them like water. At the bottom lay the bowl, round and grey in its shadow, with the scrapes about its rim. They did not go to the scrapes. They went straight out onto the ice, the three abreast, to get the breadth of it between themselves and the hill.

It had borne them all the cold term. He had known that grey for what it was two hours ago, from a lip of rock, and had thought nothing of it.

It let them through together, without any great noise. There was a long soft tearing, and a slab went down on one side and came up on the other, turning; and after that there was a piece of black water the shape of a slate, with a little grey ice rocking on it. He watched it for as long as he could have held his breath. Somewhere under the lip of rock the water went on going down into the hill, as it always had.

Nothing came up.

"Three," he said. "Into the sink." He found that he was not glad of it, and he did not try to be.

When he turned round, the wall was still standing.

It stood across an empty road, from bank to bank, with a dead hound at its foot and the frost lying flat on it. The man who held it was looking at him. He had not been able to see over the north bank, and he had not shifted a heel to try.

"That's all of them," said Cael. "Eight. You can let it go."

"You are certain."

"I counted."

Vastin shut his eyes. He did not do anything that Cael could see. The frost that had been lying on nothing fell, in a thin straight line, across the road and the broken ice. There was a hill with a cut through it, and cold air. Up on the scree a plate shifted and ran a yard, and stopped.

He did not fall. Cael had thought that he would, and was half way down the bank to him. But the man had both hands on the stick. He bent over it until his forehead was almost on his knuckles, and stayed so, with his feet where they had been. They had not moved since the stick came down. He lifted the left boot now, an inch, and set it down, as if to learn whether it was still his.

"I am perfectly well," he said, to the road. "I shall stand a moment. I should prefer not to be helped to sit."

"Nobody's helping you to do anything," said Brom. He had lowered himself onto the edge of his stone, which was a thing that he was allowed. He had his right foot out before him with the boot still on it. The upper was torn across from the lacing to the welt. "It had my boot. I said that I was wrapped."

"You wrapped the arm," said Lira.

"It didn't want the arm."

Cael stood on the road between them, with his knife in his hand and nothing on it. His left palm stung. He had spent one grain of fire and one burst, and neither had touched a hound.

He waited then for something else, and he did not know for a breath what he was waiting for.

Each of the ten had come to him so. He had stood within reach of somebody who was using a Path in earnest, in a fight that the person meant. Afterward, or in the middle of it, there had been a new thing in him, asking its question. He had just stood two feet from the largest declaration that he had ever felt. He had put his whole skin behind it.

Nothing came. He had ten, as he had had that morning. He could not have said why some arrived and some did not, and he did not try; he put it where he put a cord, and a hair.

Down at the foot of the cut a mule had begun to walk up the road, on the dry edge, with a small woman at her head. In front of them both, coming a good deal faster, there was a square figure with her sleeves pushed up.

# Chapter 35 — Lean

The tall book lay open on the table's bare end, and the ninth sheet of the Stair lay beside it, foot to foot, in the round of the carried lamp.

Cael had the best place in the gallery for both, as he had for everything, and the worst way up. From his knee he saw a page of the cramped brown hand, line under line, with a figure at the end of each: some clerk of the old town had set down what came in at a gate on one day, and what was paid on it. A hand's breadth off lay Karis's hard clean pencil, in rows, with the white pebbles and the black still lying where she had laid them that forenoon.

The man had not begun at once. He stood with his hands behind him and his eyes shut, as he had stood on the first morning while she laid out fifty-two sheets. Karis stood on the rock where he had put her. She was a head shorter than he was, and she had come up a little on her toes without knowing it, to have her eyes where his would be.

Then he opened them, and looked at the book.

He looked from its foot. Cael had moved the flame before the grey head moved, and it was there. The eyes went up the brown page slowly, without a blink, and the water came into them, and they went up the sheet beside it. Then, which they had never done in eight days, they went back and forth between the two.

He spoke while he looked. That was new as well. He had always looked first, and said afterward what the looking had found, in as few words as would carry it.

"A record," Cleon said, "sits."

His chin had gone toward the brown page.

"A load came in at a gate. It was paid on, and when the day was done a clerk put it down. He might have begun at the head of his column or the foot of it; it'd be the same tally. There's not a mark on that leaf that's waiting for anything."

The eyes came across to her pencil, and the water ran.

"An instruction *leans*."

He said it more slowly, as a man says a thing that he has only ever said to himself.

"Whoever cut this set each sign where it is for the sake of the one after it. Take them out of their order and you've a pile of chippings. Leave them in it, and you're handed on, sign to sign, whether you can read a letter of it or not. I don't read it. I'm walked down it."

He looked from the one to the other once more, the book and then the sheet.

"This is a stair."

Nobody in the gallery moved.

Brom had come up as far as the table's corner, and stopped there, with his arms hanging. Lira sat against a table's leg with her chin on her knees. On a box by the nearest bay, a long grey man had turned his head toward the two things on the table, and his hands had gone still on the ash.

Karis was not looking at the sheet any longer. She had turned, on the spot where she had been put, and her eyes were going down the gallery: over the Hall's twelve, and the long table with its three rows, and the Ring's eleven beyond. She looked at them as she had once looked at a bluff from six miles off, counting.

"The strata lean too."

She had said it very low, and to herself.

The man made a sound in his chest. It was not the grunt that he gave for a thing well answered, and it was not the other, which had been nearly a laugh. He had heard a thing that he had not thought of; Cael could tell so much from the set of his shoulders, and no more. He did not take it up. He bent again to the sheet, and one square pale finger came out over the paper, an inch above her pencil, as it went above the lines of his own page in the mornings.

The finger began at the low left corner, and went up, a row at a time, and across.

Cael had the light there before it, and did not have to think of where. The finger came to the fifth row. It went along the signs, the white and the three black and the red, without touching one of them, slower and slower; and above the last sign of the run it stopped.

"There," said the man.

It came at that word.

He did not know it for what it was; he knew only that something at the tail of his eye had altered, and he took it for the lamp. A dark thing stood propped against the table's edge, with its head a little back, and it had four rows cut in it; and all four had gone aslant, the same way, like the rails of four stairs seen from below. He looked at his own hand: the ring hung from two fingers, and the flame stood up like a painted one.

So he looked at the sheet, where the finger was.

Nothing on the paper had stirred; the signs stood where her pencil had put them, square and small, each in its place. But the fifth row had a way to it now, as a field of barley has a way when the wind has gone over it and every stalk still bows a little toward where the wind went. The white sign at the head stood up straight, and all that followed it hung from it, and inclined, each toward the next. At the row's end the lean came down flat, like a foot arriving on a floor.

*Goes*, it said, of that row. It said it without any voice at all; it was only there, as the weight of a stone is there in the hand that lifts it.

He knew it then, and he knew whose it was. He had been a hand to its left for six days.

He had read every paper of his life from the wrong side of somebody's desk, with its head toward the person who owned it. It was as though a hand had reached across, without a word, and turned the sheet about, so that for once it lay facing him.

He put his eyes on his own knuckles, and kept them there. There was a table under that sheet, with pegs in it, and a ring of brass in his fingers, and a desk six strides off, and a stair of dark stone that somebody had laid; and the new thing inclined toward each of them in turn, mildly, like a head coming round. He did not let it look. He had been given a pair of eyes that were not his, in a room that belonged to the man they came from, and he did not know yet what they cost.

It was the twelfth.

---

"Red," said the rough voice. "At that one. And at that. He's finished, at those; he begins again after."

It was said to the chair by the wall. The finger had gone back to the head of the sheet, and it was coming down the rows now, stopping above each place where a run ended. At every stop Karis laid a red square of broken tile below her pencil, between one sign and its neighbour.

She had asked for his joins, and he was giving them to her; he could not have done it with a pebble five days ago. He had needed to say aloud, to somebody who stood in his boots, what he had stood in them and seen for thirty years; and it seemed that the saying had loosened the rest.

"Five," she said, of a group. Her voice shook, and she let it. "That is one word. It is five signs long, and it is one word, and I have four of its letters."

"Then you've a book to lay it against."

She was at the tall book before he had done. Cael held the flame for her, a hand to the left, and watched her pencil go down the old clerk's column, hunting a word of five with those four in their places. She found it in the time that it takes to draw a long breath; and she found it again, twelve lines lower, and only then did she write.

"Fifteen."

Cael knelt, and kept the light where it was wanted; and he found that he had put it there, twice, before the finger moved.

He had done as much for three days, and it was not the same. He had moved the lamp then because the eyes always went one way, and he had learned the way as a groom learns a horse. He moved it now because he could see where the row was going. A made thing was looked at from where it stood upon the ground, toward where it went, and a light set anywhere else lay across the grain of it. He was glad to have that; it explained six days of *left* and *up a finger*.

It also made him a thief, or would by the morning.

A man had shown a stranger the inside of his trade, for the first time in thirty years, because she had asked him honestly for something that he could not hand her; and the boy who held his lamp had come away with a piece of it. He had not reached. He never did reach. But he would kneel at that elbow tomorrow, and the day after, with the man's own way of looking folded behind his eyes; and each time that the finger went along a row, he would know where it was going before it went.

That was not a thing that had been taken once, in an alley, and now sat idle. It would be taken again every hour, for as long as the lamp was his. He would have to tell him. He did not see how; he saw only that it would not keep.

"Sixteen," said Karis. She had dust on her cheek, and her left hand had come up out of her lap and was holding the tall book's leaf flat, on its bound palm, as though it had forgotten that it was hurt. "It is a score. It is the old word for twenty, and there are a hundred of them on this page."

"He counted by the score, then," said Brom, from the table's corner. "Like a drover."

"I am not saying how he counted. I am saying what I can spell."

The thing leaned at the tall book, when her hand turned the leaf. *Sits*, it would have said of that page, if he had let it: he could feel the word waiting, flat and level, like a board laid on two trestles. It leaned at the lamp-ring, and at the joint of the table's leg, a foot from his knee, where one peg did the work and another had been put in for show. He told it no each time. It was an easier thing to refuse than Shadow, which pulled, or the wall, which asked; this one only offered, like a clerk holding out a sheet that need not be taken.

Near the bell it offered one thing more, and that was not a table's leg.

He had come to that town with a place under his breastbone which five readings had gone round and none had entered, and he had written at a fire that he wanted an instrument for what does not move. He had one now, resting an inch behind his eyes, as quiet as a folded rule. It would take no time; he need not so much as shift his knee.

*Write it down and stop touching it.*

A healer had put that on him, on a plank, a long way east. He had got round her words once already, at a waypost, by going about the place and not into it; and he had been ashamed enough of that to write it, and to owe her the telling. He did not get round them now. She would be at a mill inside of eight days, and she could be asked.

*Not until she's heard it*, he said, to the thing behind his eyes; and it lay down, having only ever offered.

"Seventeen," said Karis, when the flame had begun to smoke. She said it as a person says the name of a town that she has walked to.

"Of twenty-three," said the man.

"Yes."

"Two days."

"I know what I have. I did not know this morning that I should have it so quickly." She looked up at him, from the chair. "You have never shown that to anybody."

"Nobody wanted his joins."

He went to his desk to write the card. A long way off, at the stair's foot, something small and flat was struck once, like a spoon on a cup.

---

The old man had gone to the stair a quarter of an hour before the bell, by himself.

He had said that he would. A person who must climb as he had to climb did well to begin early. He had gone off down the gallery on the stick, hand over hand along the black uprights, with a civil refusal for each of the three who offered an arm. He had left the tarred case leaning at the foot of the first flight. Cael heard him before he saw the place: a slow scrape, and a breath, and a scrape, somewhere up in the dark of the first flight.

"He's on the first landing," said Lira. "On his seat, backward. I'd not have thought he could."

She had the lamp, and she had set her foot on the bottom tread. Brom was behind Cael, under his box, and Karis behind Brom, with her notebook shut against her chest and seventeen letters in it.

Cael went up two steps, until his mouth was at the height of her ear.

"Twelfth. His. Lean."

He had said it under the scraping. The old man was nine steps above them, with his back to the climb, making noise enough for three; they used the voices that a road had taught them, all the same.

The lamp did not move; he would have known it if it had, having become a judge of such things. Only her head came round, slowly, and her eyes went over his face in that brown light as they went over a page of his book.

"From him," she said, hardly above a breath. "Down there, with the pebbles. He never laid a hand on anybody."

"He showed her how he sees it. I was a hand off."

"When?"

"The fifth row. You were sitting against the table."

"I thought you'd gone white. I thought it was your knee." She looked up the stair, at the dark that was scraping, and down again. "Does it ask? Like the other one?"

"It doesn't ask. It leans. It's leaning at this stair now, and I'm not letting it."

Brom had heard, as he heard everything on a stair, having nothing to do there but carry. He let his box down upon the first tread, by his knees, and sat on it.

"That's two in two days," he said. "You'll want a bigger book."

"I'm going back down. I'm going to tell him."

Nobody answered at once. Above them a boot's heel knocked on stone, and there was a pause, and a breath.

"You've one up there that you couldn't tell," said Lira. She did not say it unkindly; she had read two lines in a book, the night before, and had read them twice. "You wrote that you can't tell the taking without the rest, and the rest isn't yours. It's six people's."

"It is. Three of them are on this stair."

He had been turning it over since the fifth row, and he gave it to them as it stood, with the weak places showing.

"That one came once, and it's shut, and I've not touched it. To tell him that much, I'd have to tell him all of it, and I can't take it back out of him afterward. This one's different, and it's not because I think better of the man. I'm at his elbow every day. He says a word and I'm there before it, and from tomorrow I'd be there because I can see what he sees. He made it a term that nobody tells him what a thing is before he's looked. He's looked for a week at a lamp, and it's not a lamp."

"He does not want to be told things," said Karis.

She had come up beside Brom's knee. Her face was white and tired, and she was holding the notebook as she held anything that she meant to keep.

"He wants to look. He said so at a counter, before he would let me say one word of what I thought. But I do not think that he would thank us for letting him go on looking beside a thing that he had not been shown." She drew a breath. "I shall sit in that chair tomorrow. I should not care to sit there knowing what he does not. He has given me his joins."

"Seln told a clerk the truth in a yard," said Brom. "It cost him his face. He said that it was the only way he'd found that stayed right afterward." He looked at his hands. "The fellow's not a counter. He's kept all that for thirty years with never a ticket on a shelf. Tell him what's his. I'd not tell him ours."

"I'll tell him how this one came. No more than that."

"Then that's three," said Lira. "You'll have the other two to face. One's on a roof, and one's got a mule." She shifted the ring to her other fingers and back again. "Go on. I'll hold his light at the top till he comes for it. Leave the box."

He slid the beckets off. Brom shifted along, and laid one forearm across the lid of the third box, with a purse in it under five thumbs, as a man lays his arm along a gate.

"I've a hand on it," he said.

Cael went back along the cross-way, into the middle gallery, with no lamp at all.

---

It was a hundred and ten strides, and he walked them toward one small light.

The desk's lamp burned at the far end, on its iron arm, as it had burned on the first morning; and for most of the way it showed him nothing but itself. The shelves went by on either hand as walls go by in a dream. The new thing leaned at every bay. He kept his eyes on the flame, and counted.

The man was not at his desk. He stood at the third table, in the spill of that good light, with the black plank tipped toward him on its foot and the reed in his fingers. He was looking at its first row, from below, and he did not turn at the sound of boots.

"You've a quarter of an hour, and you've left your light," he said. "If she's forgotten a book, it'll keep."

"It's not a book."

Cael had stopped at the table's end. He had never stood in front of that man without a ring in his fingers, and his hands hung strangely; he put them at his sides.

"You said no names, and I'll give you none. You said that nobody was to tell you what a thing was until you'd looked. You've looked. There's a thing about the hand that's held your lamp, and I'd sooner you had it from me tonight than found it out tomorrow."

The reed had stopped, and the grey head did not come round.

He told it in the order in which he had it, which was the order in which it would have to be written: what the thing stood beside, and whose it had been, and how much of it he had; and then, since that was what the man would want, how it had come. He said that he had no Path, and that a plate had told him so. He said that pieces of other people's came to him all the same, when he stood near somebody who was using what he had in earnest. He did not reach for them, he said, and could not stop them; and there had been eleven that morning.

"There's a twelfth. It came an hour ago, at the fifth row, when your finger stopped. It's a piece of yours. I can see which way a run goes. I looked at that row, and at your board, and I've looked at nothing since."

"You did what."

It was not asked; it was said flat, to the oak, as a man says the weight of a thing that has been set down on his foot.

"I can see the lean. I couldn't this morning."

The plank went back against the table's edge. He set it there with both hands, slowly, as he would have set down a full jar; and still he had not turned.

"That's not in the classification system. That's not *supposed* to be possible. That's — "

His voice had risen. Cael had not heard it rise in eight days, for a dray or a cut hand or a lamp that moved; and it had gone up now like a clerk's who has turned a leaf and found a figure where no figure can be.

"No," Cleon said, to himself. "No. I'll not say that one. Not down here. Not to you."

Cael did not ask him for the word.

He could not have named it. He knew only that the man had it, whole, ready behind his teeth, and had judged it too heavy a thing to hand across a table to a boy; and he found that he would rather be spared a word by that man than be given one by most.

The square hands had opened on the table's edge, and shut. Then the man walked to his desk, with one hand before him, by habit. He reached up to the shelf under the iron arm. He took down the cup, and the jar, and poured. He carried the cup back along the tables, and set it on the bare corner of the third, a long way from any paper, an inch from Cael's left hand.

He did not say what it was for; it was water, and it was the first thing that he had ever given to the person who held his lamp that was not an order.

Cael drank the half of it. He had not known that his mouth was dry.

The man stood at the table's corner, where he had set the cup down, with his face to the plank. He spoke to the oak.

"I'm going to pretend you didn't tell me that," he said, "for your sake, not mine. But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you can apparently take my whole trade by watching me work it for a minute."

The reed lay along the table's edge, and he squared it with one finger, to the grain.

"Everything I read is *made*. Stone, ink, seal — somebody built it, so I can see how. If that fragment ever reads something for you that shouldn't be *made* and reads it anyway — "

He stopped. The square finger had come off the reed.

"Don't tell me. Don't tell anyone. Just remember I said it."

He went back to the carving.

It was done as a door is shut, without any haste. He took up the reed, and bent to the plank's first row, from its foot; and the water was running in his eyes already, though he could not have begun to look.

Cael stood where he was a moment longer, since something had happened in the middle of that which he wished to be certain of.

For eight days those red eyes had gone over him as water goes over a stone in a ford. He had been a hook, and a hook is not looked at; he had asked to be exactly that, and had minded it, and had written that he minded. They had not gone over him tonight. When the cup was set down they had come to the edge of him. They had come as far as a sleeve, and the wrist of the hand that held the light; and there they had stopped, and the man had turned his whole head away to the oak, as a person turns from a lit window that is not his to look in at.

It was not the old not-seeing. Cael had been unseen by a good many people who had the training for it, and he knew the one from the other as he knew a shut door from a wall. This had been done on purpose, and done for him.

He set the cup down where it had been put, with the half still in it.

"Second bell," said the man, to the plank, as he said every evening.

---

Brom was still on the box, at the stair's foot, in the dark.

"Well?"

"He knows."

"And?"

"He's at his board."

The big man asked nothing else. He got up, and turned his bound shoulder to his own load, and swung it there in one heave; and Cael found the third box by its beckets and got it onto his back. The tarred case stood against the wall where it had been left, and he took it up by its strap.

There was a little brown light at the top of everything, three flights up, where a girl stood holding a lamp for a man who had not yet come for it. It showed him the first landing. It showed him, three steps above that, a pair of boots, and a leg thrust out as stiff as a bolster.

"I had hoped to be higher," said Vastin.

He sat on a tread with his back to the climb and his face to the two who were coming up. His breath was short, and he had the stick laid across his thighs.

"I left the case. I regret it. I took it up at a fork, against a healer's word, and I have not set it down for anybody since; but I find that I cannot go up a stair on my hands and carry a thing in them."

"I've got it."

"I should prefer that you had the half of it." The left hand came out, the one that would not open flat. "That will hold a strap. It is very nearly all that it will do."

So they took it up between them.

Cael held the case by its foot, and the old man had the strap's end shut in his fist. At every tread the two raw hands went to the stone, and the sound leg thrust, and the whole length of him was hitched one step higher into the dark, backward, with the case drawn after him like a thing being brought up out of a well. They were face to face, and Cael could not see the face. He could hear it breathe, a yard above his own, and he bore up the weight of a cord and a mallet and one stake, so that it should not pull upon that hand.

He had a thing to read, and it had waited since the fifth row.

The lines had stood behind his eyes for an hour, as each of the eleven had stood, and he had not looked at them. He had had a lamp to hold, and then a thing to say. He took them now, on a stair, a tread at a time, as he had taken the last upon a pallet: slowly, from the top, in the plain words in which they would have to go into Hesk's book.

*Rune-adjacent. Cleon. Partial. Rune family. A made thing's lean — the direction a maker put in it; which mark bears, which is borne, which is nothing; whether a run sits or goes. Structure, not meaning: it will tell me a thing is an instruction and will not tell me what it instructs. Made things only. Not a river. Not a man. Costs the eyes, after. No use in a fight. Engagement field: inscriptive — the second field that is not a fight, after clinical; given, not taken. Tier equivalent unknown. Deployment: none. Twelfth.*

Twelve.

He went over what was new in it, a step at a time, since there was nothing else to be done on those steps. It had a field that he had never met. Nine of his had come out of fights and said so; one said *clinical*, and that had been a healer's hands. Here was a thing that had come to him off a sheet of paper, across a table, from a man describing what he saw. The lines had found a word for that. They had found two more that he read three times over. Every other piece that he carried had been got by standing where somebody was hurt, or hurting. This one said that it had been handed to him.

There was a line which he did not need to puzzle at. He had heard it stated like a carter's rate, and he had watched it paid for eight days, in water running down beside a man's nose.

He put two lines of his own under the twelve, since they were owed, and since he wished to have them by heart before there was a pencil.

*He looked away. Kept.*

*Not a man.*

The second was the notice's own, said over. He set it by itself because it was the one that he would have to stand upon.

"You are very quiet," said the voice above him. "I had supposed that I was the one who lacked the breath for talk."

"I'm counting."

"So am I. It is eighteen."

They rested on the second landing. Below them, quick flat feet were coming up in the dark without a hand to the wall; and the old man drew his leg in as far as it would come, and Cael stood against the stone. The man in the dusty coat went by between them and the wall. He went by with his face turned to it, though there was not light enough on that landing to have known one face from another.

---

It began on the last flight.

Cael had set his boot on the nineteenth tread, with the case, and the lamp was above him, eight steps up, in Lira's hand. It was a small brown flame in a smoked glass. He had knelt beside it for six days. It went into his eyes now like a handful of sand.

He shut them. That was worse; they burned behind the lids, and the water came out from under, hot, and ran down beside his nose. He opened them upon a smear. There was a blot of light, and a dark upright thing that might have been a girl, and where the steps should have been there was nothing that he could have set a foot to.

*Costs the eyes, after.* He had read it a flight ago, and had thought that he understood it.

"You have stopped," said Vastin.

"My eyes have gone. They'll be back. It's an hour, about."

He had not lied to the man by the width of a hair. He stood on a tread that he could not see, with a box dragging at his shoulders and a strap in his fist, and waited to be asked how he came to know the length of it.

"I do not ask," said the old man. There was a silence, and a scrape, and the case drew upward by the height of one step. "There are eight more. Each is of the same rise; I have had occasion to learn them today. Put your right foot up. Higher. There."

He went up the last flight of that stair blind, on an old man's word.

It was given a step at a time, in the level voice in which a clerk reads a column: the right foot, and the left, and that the tread was dry. For eight days Cael had gone two steps below that stick, so that there should be something under it if it fell. He had not thought to find the thing turned about. He bore up his end of the case, and put his boots where he was told, and was brought up out of the dark by a person who could not stand.

"The sill is iron, and it is bright. Lift for it."

He lifted. A hand that he knew took him by the sleeve, and a lantern that he could not look at went by his face, and a key turned.

"What's wrong with your eyes?" said Lira, at his ear.

"It bills. It said that it would."

"And him? Down there? What did he say to it?"

"He gave me a cup of water."

It was true, and it was the only part that he had leave to give her. She waited for the rest. He felt her wait, the whole length of a vault that he crossed with his lids shut and her fingers in his sleeve; and he let her go on waiting, which he had never in his life done to that girl before.

Cael did not tell anyone. He remembered.

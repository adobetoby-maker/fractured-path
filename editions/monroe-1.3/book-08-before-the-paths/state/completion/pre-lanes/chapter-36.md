# Chapter 36 — First Bell

His eyes came back at the pump.

He had come down half a mile of carts' street with his lids shut and one hand on the tail-board of a handcart, pushing when he was told to push. In the lane a girl's shoulder had gone beside his own through four alleys, as it always went. It was the one evening in that town when he could not have looked at the crown if he had been paid for it; and he had found that funny, under a wall, with the water running off his chin, and had not said so.

Brom worked the handle for him, low, from the wrist, and the cold took the sand out by degrees. By the time the pot was called at the half-door he could tell a lantern from the thing that it lit, and he went up the ladder on his own feet.

There were six bowls in the loft, and a seventh that had gone down to a stall.

"He's got straw to his chin," said Lira. She had carried it. "He said that he was obliged to the horse for the loan. I think he meant it."

Cael ate, since Brom was watching him do it; and then he took out Hesk's book, and held it a long way from his face, and wrote the day.

*Two hundred and ninety-first day. Treswick; the loft; one in the stall. A hundred and thirty-four. By the carts' street, both ways, on wheels: a weight chalked at the bottom gate to the name on the card, and to nobody else. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He ruled a line, and wrote the twelfth's lines under it, in their order, as he had got them by heart on a stair, and added the two that were his.

Then he sat with the pencil over the leaf for some time.

There was a thing that he had not written, and it was the largest thing of the day. A man had said it to a plank of oak, with his back turned, after a cup of water; and he had said what was to be done with it. Cael knew what a line in that book was. Lira read the book; and a thing set down there had been said to her, as surely as if he had leaned across two pallets and put it in her ear.

He did not write it. He had kept one other thing out of those pages on purpose, at a spring, eleven days ago; he marked that this made two, and that he could not write that either.

He wrote what he might.

*Twelfth. Rune, from the reader, at his own table, while he showed Karis how a row goes. Told: Lira first; then him. He is the first outside the six who has it from my mouth how one came. Brom and Karis and Lira gave their word for it on the stair. S. and Oryn to be told. V. is not told, and I have now said to a stranger what I could not say to the man it's owed to. That's heavier than it was this morning. It stands.*

*It bills the eyes. Near an hour.*

Lira took the book across the gap, and read with her chin down; she came to the foot of the leaf, and went on looking at the place below the last line, where there was nothing.

"That's not all of it," she said.

"No."

"He said something."

"Yes. It's not mine to put there."

She gave the book back, and she did not look pleased, and she did not ask again; he thought that it had cost her something not to, and was sorry, and could not mend it.

Seln had heard the whole of that from the ladder's head, where he sat with his feet on the second rung, as he sat every evening.

"You have a twelfth," he said. "And you have told a registered practitioner of the Compact, beneath its oldest registry, how you came by it."

"How I came by that one. Nothing of the rest, and nothing of any of you. I asked the three who were there. I couldn't ask you."

"What did he do with it?"

"He gave me a cup of water, and went back to his board."

The mild eyes rested on him.

"That is not all that he did."

"It's all that I've leave to say."

"Then it is all." Seln put his hands into his sleeves. "I should have said yes, on that stair. I should have been a good deal longer about it than a big man with a box. He has kept a hundred and ten strides of paper for thirty years, and has not put a ticket on a bundle; I have considered that fact for five days on my own account." He was silent a moment. "It is very nearly the only thing in this town that I trust."

Karis looked up from her notebook, by the boxes. She had seventeen letters, and a strip of linen on her left hand that wanted changing.

"He showed me his joins," she said. "I do not think that a person who would do that would sell a boy."

"Nor do I. I only like to have thought so slowly."

He did not go back to watching the yard; he looked at Cael a little longer, and then at the blanket on the gable, and at the dark under the slates.

"There is the other matter. The boots are at the painted door."

Nobody moved.

"He came a quarter of an hour behind your cart. He stands under the lamp, as he stood last night, with his hood up. Last night he stayed until you were in, and walked away by the ring road, and I let him go, since I had a man to carry." The voice did not alter. "Tonight I have not. When he leaves, I mean to learn where it is that he goes."

"Not alone," said Cael. "That was ruled this morning. You said it back to me."

"I did. I had hoped that I might be excepted from that one as well. I find that I am not." There was the very least movement at the corner of his mouth. "One man walking behind another is a thing that is seen. Two are a street. I want a second pair of feet, and I had thought to ask for hers."

"I can walk," said Lira.

"You can run, which is what I am afraid of; and you have a shoulder the colour of a plum."

"Then it's me," said Cael.

He had not known that he would say it until it was out. He had been looked at for nine days, from a doorway and a kerb and the head of a stair, by people who had been told what to look for; and he found that he wished, once, to do the looking.

"It is you whom they came to see," said Seln. "I have weighed that. If he turns and finds you behind him, he will have seen a porter walking in a street at night, which is no more than he has seen already." He got up off the rung. "You will open nothing."

"Nobody opens anything."

"I say it because it will be dark, and I know what you carry that likes the dark."

---

They went out by the door where the muck went.

It was at the stable's far end, a low thing on leather hinges, and it gave upon a lane no wider than a barrow. They passed the end stall to reach it. There was a long shape in the straw there, under two blankets, with a bound leg laid out along a plank; and its eyes were open.

The old man did not speak; he looked at the two of them, and at the door, and then one raw hand came out from under the blanket and shut, slowly, into a fist.

It was the second of the four hands that a road had taught him. *Seen.*

"He learns," said Seln, in the lane, very low. "I had not been sure that he would."

They stood at the lane's mouth, a house's length from the carriage-arch, and Cael was taught the trade in three sentences.

"You will wish to look at him. Do not. Look at the place where he will be in twenty strides, and let him walk into it." The small grey man did not turn his head while he talked. "Never be the nearest thing that moves. And when he stops, you do not stop. A man who halts when another halts has signed his name; you go by, and give him to me, and when I go by I give him back."

"And if he looks round?"

"Then you are going home. You have been going home the whole time."

That made two people whom Seln had ever taught, and Cael did not say so. He stood in the mouth of a muck-lane and looked along the house-front, at the round of yellow light under a painted door's lamp.

There was a man in it.

He stood on the step, where a person stands who wishes to be seen, beside a book on a stand that he had not written in. His hood was up, and he wore a long coat of no colour. Cael looked at the boots, since he had been told of them four times: a horseman's, cut high, and at each heel a square of paler leather where a spur's rest had been taken off with a knife.

*Away?* said the hiss in his own feet. It was not a thing that Wind asked often, standing still.

He told it no. And at his knees, where it had stood since the lane under the wall, the other thing lifted its head.

Shadow had come up at dusk, as it came every dusk, and he had told it no in a lane while his eyes ran. It had not lain down; it was full night now, in a town of doorways, and he was going to follow a man through it. There was a seal on that thing, and a reason for the seal which had not altered since Ostrand; and he had never felt it lean so against a shut door.

The man under the lamp stepped down into the street.

He did not look to left or right; he went away up the house-fronts at a stroll, toward the open place, with his hands behind him, like somebody who has dined.

"Count thirty," said Seln. "Then follow me, and not him."

The open place was empty. There were forty feet of trough, with the pipe running into its upper end, and not a beast at it, and the smith's two fires were banked. The man in the hood went to the pipe, and bent, and drank from his hand. He drank for a long time, and stood afterward with the water dripping from his fingers and looked at nothing, in the middle of a paved space with no cover on it for sixty yards.

Cael stood in a doorway and looked at a place twenty strides beyond him.

It was a great deal harder than not looking at a crown. The crown did not move.

A light came round the corner by the chandler's, low, jogging, with a rumble of small wheels. It was the watch: two men in leather caps, and a handcart between them, and a lantern slung from a pole at its front.

Seln's hand came up beside his coat, flat, palm down. *Stand.* Then two fingers.

They fell in behind the cart. It was done as a thing is done on a road, at a walk, without any word; and Cael understood it only when he was there. A watch looks where its own lantern falls; and anybody who turned to see that light would see the light, and two caps, and behind them a darkness in which a pair of porters might have been going home.

The watchmen did not turn round at all. One of them was telling the other about a goose.

They crossed the open place so, twenty strides behind a hood, to the mouth of the brokers' street. There the cart swung right, for the ring road; and the man with the cut boots went straight on.

It was as wide as a field, and there was nothing in it. Every slate had been wiped, and every bench stood empty against its wall.

"In an empty street nobody hides," said Seln, into his collar. "He goes home. You have the right-hand houses; I have the left. Sixty yards. Do not match his step."

Cael went down the right-hand side of the brokers' street, in the open, as a porter goes who has been kept late and has a bed at the bottom of the hill. His boots made a noise, and he let them. He looked at a place twenty strides ahead of a long coat, and the coat walked into it, and he chose another.

*What does it weigh?* asked the read, of a tread sixty yards in front of him that he could have had through the soles of his feet, heel and toe, for as long as he liked.

*No.*

*There?* asked the thin held note, of the lane's mouth half-way down, where the man was turning.

He told that one no as well, and he had begun to count them.

---

The lane went down toward the river, and it smelled of tar and new hemp.

Cael knew where it led before he saw. He had stood in that place with a big man, nine days ago, by daylight, and watched him look into the cut end of a rope as a person looks into a flower. It had been full of people walking backward then.

It was a shed, open all down one side, and it ran away along the inside of the town wall for three hundred yards. There was a great wheel at its head, and beyond the wheel a floor of trodden boards as straight as a ruled line, under a roof on posts, with coils hanging the whole length of the wall like hats on a row of pegs. A lantern burned at the far end, very small, where the tar yard was.

The man in the hood walked in under the roof, past the wheel, and went on down the middle of the boards.

Seln's fist had shut; then the palm came down flat, and stayed.

They stood at the corner of the last house, and Cael could hear the boots on the planking, unhurried, going away.

"It is a pipe," said Seln. He scarcely moved his lips. "Whoever goes into it behind him stands against that wheel and the sky for three hundred yards. Whoever goes round it by the open side has lost him for as long; and there is a quay at the bottom, and a lane up from the quay, and a wicket in the town wall for the tar-men. He has three doors out of the far end, and we should not know which."

"He knows that."

"He chose it because of that. I should have." The grey eyes went along the roof. "I shall stay at the wheel, in case he has only gone in to come out again. You will go round by the open side, behind the drying-racks, to the barrels. Go softly, and be there before he is. And when you are there, you are a barrel."

"And if he comes out past me?"

"Then let him. Count where he goes. I shall find you."

Cael went.

There was a strip of beaten ground along the shed's open side, and a line of racks on it, taller than a man, hung with yarn. He kept the racks between himself and the boards, and ran on the outer edges of his feet, as he had been taught on a different road by a different person, and he did not run fast.

*Away?*

Ten strides at a burst; thirty bursts; and a crack at the end of each like a carter's whip, in a town asleep. He told it no without breaking step.

He made one mistake, and it was not with his feet.

He glanced along the shed, to judge how much of it was left; he only meant to measure. But it was three hundred yards of a thing that somebody had built to go in one direction, with every post and plank and peg of it laid toward the far end; and the new thing behind his eyes came round to that as a weathercock comes round, before he had told it anything.

*Goes*, it said. The whole roof leaned away from him, down the length of the wall, toward a lantern.

He shut it, and it had been open for the space of a blink. A grain of sand came into each eye and sat there, and he ran the last hundred yards with his lids half down, and was angry, and had not the time to be.

The tar yard was a square of cobbles above the river, with barrels stood on end in rows and a black kettle on a cold hearth. He went in among the barrels and found two that stood a little apart, and put himself between them with his back to the wall.

Then he waited for a man to come out of the end of the shed.

He counted, since there was nothing else to do with the time. A person at a stroll goes three hundred yards in a count of two hundred and forty, or thereabouts. He gave it three hundred, and then four.

Nobody came.

The lantern burned on its post at the head of the quay steps, and under it the river went by with a noise like cloth being drawn over a table. He could see the shed's mouth, a square of blacker dark, twelve yards off; and nothing came out of it, and there was no sound of boots on boards.

At five hundred he understood.

The man had stopped. He was standing somewhere in that long roofed dark, among the coils, with his hands behind him, and he was doing what Cael was doing: he was listening for a second pair of feet to come down the boards after him, or round by the racks; and he was prepared to stand there until he heard them, or until it was morning.

Cael had been told what to do when a man stops. He had not been told what to do when the man stopped out of sight, and stayed, and there was no walking by.

He knew one thing that might serve, and he let his breath go long. He counted it, to ten and to ten again, as a healer had taught him on a plank; and he gave over wanting to be anywhere.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the shed's mouth.

It was a good place for it, and he saw that plainly. A line stood from that post to the wall would have kept a man in a pipe like a cork in a bottle, until a grey porter came down from the wheel and they had him between them. It would have roared across half the district, and he told it no.

And Shadow asked.

It had not once left off, and it asked now as water asks at a sluice. He was standing between two tar barrels at night, in a black coat, making himself a part of the dark by holding his breath; and there was a thing under a seal that would have made him so in truth, to any eyes at all, between one heartbeat and the next.

*No*, he said. He had said it to that one twice a day since Ostrand. He said it now as a person leans on a door with somebody leaning on the other side.

At seven hundred and ten the boards spoke.

---

The boots came down the last of the shed without haste, and the man walked out of its mouth into the yard and stood still.

He was five yards off. Cael could have counted the buttons on the long coat, if there had been light to count by, and there was none on that side. The lantern was at the man's back, and it made of him a flat black shape with a hood on it and nothing under the hood at all.

The hood turned.

It went round the yard slowly, as a lamp is carried round a room: over the kettle, and the hearth, and the rows of barrels on end. It came to the two that stood apart, and rested there.

Cael did not breathe, and he did not want to be elsewhere. He had learned that in the corners of halls, and at the edges of yards, and it had never been asked of him by anybody who stood so near.

He counted ten. The hood went on round, as slowly as it had come, to the wall and the quay steps; and the man went down the steps.

At the foot, for the length of two strides, the lantern was on him from above. Cael saw a pair of horseman's boots go through its light, with a square of paler leather at each heel. He saw gloved hands, clasped behind, and the back of a hood.

He did not know whether he had been seen. He thought, afterward, that the man had looked at the place where a follower would be standing if there were one, and had neither found him nor needed to.

"You did not come through after him," said a voice at his shoulder. "That was well judged."

Cael had not heard a step, and he let his breath go.

"He stood in there. I counted seven hundred."

"He stood for eleven minutes. I heard him not walking, which is a sound that I know." Seln was looking down at the quay. "He is in no hurry at all. I begin to dislike that more than I should dislike a man who ran."

The quay was long and bare. Six flat boats lay along it, nose to tail, with their sweeps shipped. Beyond them the great wheel of the hoist stood up against the water, and beyond that again a cable went out over the river into the dark.

Cael looked at the cable, and his stomach went cold.

"There's a ferry."

"There is. It has no crew before first light. But a man who had one waiting would be on the other bank in a quarter of an hour, and we should be standing here."

The man in the hood walked the whole length of that quay, and went by the six boats, and laid a hand on the mooring post of each as he passed, as a person touches the heads of children. He stopped at the cable, and looked along it at the far shore; and he stood so long that Cael began again to count.

Then he turned his back on the river, and went up a flight of steps between two warehouses, toward the hill.

"He has shown us the ferry," said Seln. "He wished us to think of it."

They followed by the steps. There was a lane above, and at its head the ring road, lying under the second wall like a ditch under a dyke; and there the man turned right, and went along it at his stroll, with the pale stone going up on his left hand higher than a house.

Cael knew that road. He had walked the whole round of it on his second day in the town, by himself, and had not once lifted his eyes above the top of that wall. He did not lift them now; he looked at a place twenty strides ahead of a long coat, on the cobbles, and went home.

It was a quarter of the way round the bluff, and it took the better part of an hour, and the man did not once look round. He stopped twice: once to do up a boot, with his back to them, for a long minute; and once for no reason, in the middle of the way, with his hands behind him. Each time one of the two walked on past him, on the far side, with his chin down; and each time the other had him.

The watch's cart came toward them, by the chandler's, with its lantern jogging. The man in the hood lifted two fingers to it as it passed, and one of the leather caps nodded.

"They know him," said Cael, in the dark of a warehouse door.

"They know that he may be out. It is a different thing, and it is the thing that matters." Seln's voice was as mild as ever. "You and I have gone round this town tonight like mice in a wall. He has gone round it like a man taking the air."

The road brought them out, at last, by the back of the smith's, into the open place where the trough was. They had come the whole way round to within eighty yards of their own beds.

The man crossed the open place, and went by the smith's two cold fires, and set his foot on the one street that climbed.

"No nearer," said Seln, and drew Cael into the smith's doorway. "Look where we are standing."

It was a deep doorway, well back from the lamp at the corner. A man had stood in it five nights ago, at an hour when nobody has business in a doorway, and looked at the carriage-arch of an inn.

At the head of the street, two hundred yards above them, a brazier burned red in the arch of the second wall. The grating was down; it filled the arch from the keystone to the paving, a net of black iron with the fire behind it, and in front of the fire stood two shapes in long coats.

The man in the hood walked up the middle of the street to them.

He did not show anything at once; he went to the brazier, and held out his gloved hands to it, and stood so, with his back to the whole district, for as long as it takes to say a rhyme. One of the keepers spoke. The man turned his head, and answered, and put a hand into his coat; whatever came out of it was held low, in the palm, and was looked at, and put away.

A small door opened in the foot of the grating.

He stooped to it. And in it, with one boot through, he turned round.

The brazier was beside him, and its light came up under the hood. There was something pale there, two hundred yards off, no larger than a thumbnail held at arm's length, and it was turned down the street toward a smith's doorway. It stayed for the space of one breath.

Then he went in, and the small door shut.

---

"I'm going up," said Cael.

"To do what?"

"To see where it stops."

Seln looked at him, and did not say that it was foolish. He said, "A person may walk up a street. Do not speak first, and do not stand as though you were waiting to be let in."

So Cael walked up it, in the middle, as the other had.

It was two hundred yards, and he had gone up them on eight mornings with a card in front of him and a box on his back. He had never done it with his hands empty. The brazier grew as he climbed, and the two long coats took on a colour, which was dark blue.

*This far?*

It had never asked so loud. Every other place where it had asked had been a doorway that he chose to see as a line. This was a line. Somebody had cut a groove across a road, a hand wide, and lined it with iron, and hung a net of bars above it to come down at dusk; and it had come down. The thing in him knew its own kind. It asked in the voice of a bar going home into two sockets, and it very nearly did not need an answer.

He stopped a hand short of the iron.

The keepers had watched him come. One of them had a pike, grounded, and the other a board on a cord round his neck, and they were not unfriendly. The one with the board looked him over, from the cap to the boots, and saw what there was to see.

"First bell," he said, quite kindly. "And you'll want your card. There's no kerb-work to be had up here before the light."

Cael nodded. He did not speak, since he had been told not to, and since there was nothing that he could have said to that man which would not have been a lie or a great deal too much.

He stood at the groove for as long as a porter might, who has come up early by mistake.

He could have crossed it; he looked at that squarely, with the fire on his face. There was a thing at his knees that would have had him through the small door on anybody's heels, and neither of those two would have known that the air had moved. There was a hiss in his feet which made nothing of walls. He had twelve things in him, and not a keeper in that arch could have named one.

He could not cross it lawfully, and that was another matter, and it was the matter that the groove was for.

By day he might go through as the fourth part of a figure on another person's card, for a named door and a stated purpose, and be counted out again by dusk; by right he might not go through at all. Iron walked under that arch, and showed a paper, and was written. There was a colour for Iron, and a paler one for the wall above. A plate had put a word after his name that had no colour. There was no card in the district with an edge for what he was; and a man with his spurs cut off had just put a hand into his coat and brought out the one thing that Cael would never own, which was leave.

He had been stopped by lines for as long as he could remember being anything. They had all been written ones, in files, in rooms that he was not let into. He had never before been able to stand at one, and see the iron, and measure with his eye how wide it was.

It was a hand wide, and he found that he was glad to know it.

*No*, he told the eleventh; and he turned his back on a brazier, and went down the street.

---

Seln had not stirred from the doorway.

"Well?"

"He's Iron, or he's got a paper that does as well. He showed it low. They opened the little door." Cael leaned on the smith's jamb. His legs had begun to tell him how far they had been. "That's all. We walked three hours to learn that a man can go through a gate."

"Eleven guards on one broker's doors can go through that gate at night, and every Iron in the district. I do not call it a finding. I call it a wall." Seln was looking up the street at the red point of the fire. "But I shall tell you what I have, since you have earned the half of it. He was not learning whether he was followed. He knew that from the trough. He took us round by the one shed where we must divide, and stood eleven minutes in it; he showed us a ferry; he showed us a watch that nods to him; and then he went to bed, through a thing that we cannot pass, and turned in the door so that we should be sure of having seen."

"Why?"

"So that we should know how much time they have. That is the whole of it. They need not take you in a stair. They may stand under a lamp for a month."

Cael thought about that, with the cold of the doorway at his back.

"He wanted me to do something," he said. "At the barrels. Or at that gate. He stood five yards off and looked at where I was, for a count of ten. It'd have been the easiest thing I've ever done, to be gone."

"Yes."

"I don't think it was the stair they shut, two nights ago. I think it was this. They want it shown, and they'll wait."

"Then they have been shown a porter, twice." The small grey man came out of the doorway. "It was worth one night to learn that it is not worth two. I shall not follow him again; and I should be obliged if you did not."

They went back by the muck-lane. The fist came out from under two blankets as they passed the end stall, and opened, palm upward, on the straw. *Nothing?*

Cael turned his own hand over, empty, and the old man shut his eyes.

There was a greyness over the roofs by then, and he washed at the pump, and the tar would not come off his palms; he had not known that he had leaned upon a barrel. He wrote three lines by the last of the lantern, with the book held at the length of his arm.

*Night of the 291st. Followed the boots, with S., round the whole of the lower town. All of them asked; Shadow asked for three hours. One look that I didn't mean, with the twelfth, at the rope-walk: a grain in each eye. Not counted. Nothing opened.*

*He may go through the blue gate at night. I may not go through it at all, except as a figure. It is a hand wide.*

*S.: they have time. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

The second bell was three hours off. He lay down in his coat, and slept like a thing dropped.

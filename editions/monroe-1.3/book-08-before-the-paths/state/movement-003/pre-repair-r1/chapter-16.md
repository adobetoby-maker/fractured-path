# Chapter 16 — A Month

Oryn came up the cut on the dry edge, at the pace that she kept for a gate with a bell ringing, and she stopped at her stride and a half and looked the whole of it over before she spoke.

It took her three breaths, in which Cael saw her eyes go to five grey shapes, and leave them, since they were past her trade. They went to a girl who was wiping a long knife on a tuft of bents with her left hand; to a small man who had come out of the broken ice wet to the shins and was looking at nothing; to a large one sitting on a stone with his foot out. They came to Cael, and passed over him as they passed over a gatepost. Then they stopped.

"That's first," she said.

Vastin had straightened. He had his back to the south bank and both hands on the stick, and below his right knee the brown frieze was black and wet from the joint to the boot.

"I am in no immediate—"

"I'll say when it's my turn to be told." She did not go nearer. "Three days ago, at a board, I opened my mouth about that leg, and you answered a thing that I'd not yet put. You said that you offered it to nobody. So I said that I still hadn't asked, and I let it lie, since that's my rule and I keep it." She pushed her left sleeve a little higher. "This is the asking. There are teeth in that, and under the teeth a joint that's been walked three hundred miles and then stood on by eight of those. I want my hands on it. And I want two fingers on your wrist while I'm there, to learn what's left in you. May I?"

He did not answer at once.

Cael watched him look for the form of it. A man who had spent his life granting leave, and refusing it, under his hand and a seal, was being asked a plain thing by a woman on a road who had no office to ask it from. He looked at her bare forearms. He looked, for an instant, at the five who stood about him, and it came to Cael that he was waiting to hear whether any of them would answer for him.

Nobody did.

"Yes," said Vastin.

It was the first thing that Cael had ever heard him grant to anybody without a clause hung on the end of it, and the healer would not have even that.

"To what? I'll not take a nod for it."

"To your hands upon the knee. And upon my wrist." He drew breath. "I said that I offered it to nobody. I find that being asked is another matter."

"It generally is. Sit on that stone."

"I had rather stand."

Brom, who was on the stone, had begun to get up from it. He lowered himself again, slowly, and looked at the healer.

"Then stand," said Oryn, "and you'll stand still, and you'll put your weight on the big one's shoulder and not on that stick. I'm not kneeling on a frozen road to have you fall on me."

He did as much of that as he could bear to, which was to lay two fingers on Brom's shoulder, as a man touches a banister that he does not mean to use.

She knelt, and slit the frieze with a small knife, from the knee to the boot's top, and laid it back; and she told him what she was going to do before she did it, as she told every person who had ever lain on her plank. There were four holes and a tear, none of them deep, since a stiff leg in a thick coat is a poor mouthful. Those she would close. It would take her a quarter of an hour, and it would feel like cold water poured into the bone. The joint was another matter. It had been wrung like a cloth, and she did not mean to spend on it.

"A thing that's torn, I can shut. A thing that's tired wants what I can't give it faster than it'll take it for itself. I'll bind that, and it'll have to do its own work."

"I understand the distinction."

"I'd be surprised if you didn't. Now hold your tongue."

Her thick fingers went round the leg, above the place and below it, and her eyes shut. Cael had lain under those hands. He knew what was passing along that shin now, slow and exact. Tide, the tenth of his own, lay in him as still as a pool, and did not so much as ask; it knew its owner's work when it was near. He had been handed the reading without the mending, a ruler and no saw. Watching her now, he could have said to a breath where along that shin her attention stood, and he could not have done one thing about what it found there.

When she had done, she was grey about the mouth. She had spent on four small holes what a child at some northern gate might want of her within the week, and she had weighed that before ever she knelt, as she weighed every spending; it was why the joint was to have linen and a month, and nothing more. She took linen from the mule's pannier without getting up, and two laths, and bound the knee from above the joint to below it, turn upon turn, until it would bend no more than a post. Then she reached up, and he gave her his wrist, and her lips moved to ten.

"You're at the floor," she said. "I've felt a well like that in men who'd held a roof up off their own children. There's nothing in you to draw on. If I'd tried to spend on that joint, you'd not have had the wherewithal to take it."

"How long?"

"A month."

She got to her feet by Brom's other shoulder.

"Thirty days in that binding, and on that stick. You'll not kneel on it. You'll not run. And you'll not stand another of those on it, for anybody, until I've had my hands on it again and said so."

"I shall be on the road tomorrow."

"I've no quarrel with roads. A road's walking."

"As to the last, I cannot undertake—"

"I didn't ask you to undertake it. I told you what it costs. Stand a wall on that leg inside the month, and you'll keep the stick for the rest of your days." She had turned to Brom already. "Boot."

"It's a boot," said Brom.

"It was. Off. May I?"

"Aye."

The upper had been torn across from the lacing to the welt, and two of his toes had gone the colour of a plum. Nothing was broken. She bound them together, and told him that he would know about it for ten days and walk on it all the same; and then she lowered herself onto the stone at his side, with her head down, and her lips moved to fifty.

---

The factor came down from the lip while she was counting, leading a mule.

It was a hold's mule, old and sour, with a sack over its eyes, and the factor took it by the cheek-strap and walked it into the cut at the foot, between the dead carts, on the dry edge. Cael understood that he was watching the only test that such a person trusted. They came up the whole hundred strides, past six people and a healer, with a pair of pale eyes going to each grey shape in turn. They went as far as the black wet rock at the head. They turned there, and came down again.

"Five," said the factor, at Brom's stone. "I said five."

"There were eight," said Cael. "Three lay under your north bank, where nobody could have counted them. They went back over it, and down, and out onto the ice. It let them through."

The crook was leaned against the bank. The factor climbed the low side with the ease of a person who has done it since childhood, and stood on the top with both hands in the apron's belt, looking down fifty-five yards of scree.

"That's rotten. It's been rotten a week; I'd not have put a lamb on it." There was a long silence. "I never saw that water give back anything that went in."

"So it's clear," said Brom.

"It's clear because it's empty, not because it's safe," said Seln.

Nobody added to it, and the factor came down off the bank.

"Empty's what I posted. I never asked anybody for safe. I've lived on this shoulder since I was born, and that seep'll have its glass back by morning." A hand went into the apron. "There's another den'll find it, in a year, or in five. They'll have to learn it from the beginning, as these did; and by then I'll have had thirty carts of salt up."

The twenty was counted out on the top bar of the gate, in four heaps of five, and a thumb was set to the foot of Pike's slip, where it left a mark like a crook. Cael had been paid at a good many gates by then, and he had learned to read a hold by how it paid. The factor at the axe-head had counted twice, like a man who has been cheated before; this one counted once, and did not look at the heaps afterward, like a person who has got exactly what was wanted and means to think no more about the price.

"A hundred and thirty-eight in hand," said Brom. He put the slip inside his shirt with the purse. "That's his, to draw the tack on. I'll have it after."

"I've a thing to ask, and it's not about the money." The factor had not gone in. The pale eyes were on Vastin, who stood by the gatepost on his stick with the leg bound stiff. "I was on the lip. I saw eight of them run into the air and stop. I've had Paths through this gate for forty cold terms, and I never saw that, and I'd know what to call it if a carter asks me."

Nobody answered for him, since it was his to say or to keep.

"It is called Iron Wall," said Vastin. "It is on a roll, with a rank beside it which would mean nothing on this road. I had not declared it in a great many years. I should be obliged if a carter were told that it was declared by a fool, in the wrong place, and that he was got out of it by five people who never made a sound."

"I'll tell them what I saw," said the factor. "It's all I ever tell anybody."

Karis had her notebook open on the gate's bar.

"I have read of that Path," she said, to the leaf. "There is a description of it in a manual of engagement fields, with a diagram, which I had always supposed to be drawn wrong. I saw it from the foot. It looked as though eight animals had run into the end of the world."

"What did you put?" said Lira.

"That a plane stood across a cut, from a line that cracked the ice. That it stood for as long as he did not move, and went when he let it. That eight struck it and none passed." Her pencil went on. "And I have added a sixth line to my five. *It goes to where a declaration ends, if it has learned that the declaring moves.* I had the three wrecks under my eyes from the foot for the better part of an hour, and I did not read them either."

"None of us did," said Cael.

"No. I enter it against all of us. I enter it against myself first."

---

The hold fed them in the long house at dusk, on mutton and barley, at a board by a fire that a man could have stood in.

There was no rule against a fire under a roof, behind a wall as thick as a cart. Cael sat with his back to it and felt the cold of the cut go out of him a little at a time. Shadow had come up to his knees with the dark, as it did at every dusk, and he held it there; the man whom it had come from sat two places down, and had not spoken since the ice.

Vastin had not sat at all. There was room on the bench, since Brom had left it, and he stood by the door-post instead, with the stick and the bound leg, and ate what was brought him from a bowl held in his hand, as a man eats at a muster. Lira watched him do it for some time.

"He's not sat since the cut," she said, under her breath.

"If he sits, he'll have to get up again," said Brom. "In front of us. With that. So he's not sitting."

Cael thought that it was very likely the whole of it, and he did not think the less of the man. It was Brom who spoke next, aloud, across the board, since he could not leave a figure unsaid.

"It held, though. Eight of them, running. I've seen walls that wouldn't."

"It held, and they went round it," said Lira.

"That is exact," said Vastin.

He had set his bowl on the sill. He spoke to the fire, and it was plain that he had been arranging the thing since the hound was lifted off his boots.

"I shall tell you what that Path is for, since you have seen what it is not for. It is a Path of the tribunal floor. It is taught for fields; and a field is ground where a thing has been declared, a hearing, or a square, or a gate with a crowd at it. Everybody on a field knows where the line is. They are there because they want what lies on the far side of it: a door, or a prisoner, or a paper under seal. I have stood on eleven such. Nobody on any of them ever went round me. There was nothing round me that they wanted."

"And today there was nothing behind you that they wanted," said Seln. It was the first thing that he had said. "Only yourself."

Cael had read of such floors in a library above Ostrand, in the lists of other people's hearings, and had never thought to ask what stood at the edge of them. It appeared that an officer had stood there, with a stick, for the whole of a working life; and he had been so sure of being the thing in the way that he had never had to learn what a wall was worth when nobody wished to pass it.

"Only myself," said Vastin. "I drew a line across a road and stood upon it. It did all that such a line can do, and it served no purpose whatever, until a boy went and stood behind the middle of it, where he could not lift a hand, and made himself worth the crossing."

"I'd seen it hold eight," said Cael.

"You had seen it hold once. I have known a good many officers who would not have put their skin behind a thing on that evidence."

He turned from the fire then, and looked at the board, one face after another, in the order that he kept.

"The wall held the line," Vastin said. "And someone else had to be the reason they wanted the line."

Nobody answered him. The fire ticked.

"I have never once in forty years needed someone else to be the reason."

"You do now," Cael said.

Vastin received it as he had received the price of a loaf. He bowed his head by a little, toward the voice, and it was not the short stiff bow; it was a good deal less practised than that.

"I know fields. I don't know packs." Vastin took up the stick. "I'll learn or I won't."

He said it without any sourness, as a clerk enters a balance that he does not yet know how to settle. Then he asked the hold's woman, who was passing with a pitcher, where a guest might sleep who was on nobody's sheet, and paid her a copper for the answer, and went out by himself into the yard.

"He paid again," said Brom.

"He'll pay till somebody tells him that he needn't," said Lira. "And then he'll want to know by what rule."

Oryn had been standing by the hearth's end with her bowl. She watched the door until it had shut.

"He took every word of that," she said. "About the month. He heard it, and he told me that he couldn't undertake it. And he's not knelt since, or gone faster than a walk, or leaned on anything but that stick." Her spoon went round the bowl. "I've had patients who said *yes* to everything and did none of it. I never had one before who said *I cannot undertake*, and then did it to the letter, as if it were a thing that he'd thought of himself."

---

Cael wrote it in the byre loft, on his knee, by the light that came up between the boards from the hold's lantern.

The others were down already in the hay, where he could hear Brom turning his foot about to find a way that it would lie. He began as he began every entry, with the day, and with what could be counted.

*Two hundred and seventy-ninth day. The shoulder hold. The cut is clear of eight: five killed, by a rock twice, by a knife twice, and once by a thing that I shall come to; three through the ice of the sink. Twenty paid at the gate. In hand, a hundred and thirty-eight. Brom's right foot, two toes, ten days. Vastin's right knee: four holes shut by Oryn, the joint bound, on a stick for a month by her word.*

*I planned it wrong. They do not go to where a thing is declared. On that ice they go to where it stops. I put Karis and Oryn and the mule at the foot and called it the safe end.*

He stopped there, with the pencil's point on the leaf.

He had gone back over it a dozen times since the ice. Eight hounds would have come down to the foot of that cut, and found three quiet things, and stood. They would have tried one of the three with their teeth. He believed that it would have held; a healer and a researcher knew how to be posts, and a mule answered with nothing that a hound hunted. He believed it. He had not been asked to watch it tried, since a man with a stick had put a road's width of refusal in front of it, on his own judgment, with a knee that he knew would not carry him. The rule had been broken, and he had broken a rule himself, twice, for a calf and for a carter. He could not find it in him to enter the breaking as a fault.

He wrote the rest as it came.

*Vastin fought. He rooted the road and nearly got eaten for it — a wall's no good if you're the only thing making noise behind it. He knows fields. He doesn't know packs. He'll learn or he won't. Watched an Iron Wall go up two feet from me. Filed it. Note the day: an Archmarshal took a wound out here and there was no one to report it to. He looked almost free. Almost.*

He read that over; and the last of it he had seen in the yard, an hour ago, and had not known until he wrote it what he had seen.

The man had come out of the long house with his copper spent. He had stood under the lantern at the byre's door, looking down at the linen on his knee, and his right hand had gone to the breast of the brown coat, where a pen would have been. Cael knew the gesture by now. An officer hurt on duty wrote it up within the day, no doubt, under two hands, with the circumstances; there would be a sheet for it, and a tray. The hand had found a receipt in a small woman's writing, and nothing else. It had stayed there a moment. Then it had come away empty, and Vastin had looked up at the black shoulder of the hill, and for the space of three breaths his face had had nothing in it that Cael had ever seen there: no room that he was being fair to, and no file.

Then the knee had reminded him, and he had gone in.

*The point: Ember, one grain, at the mark. In earnest. The left palm, though I held nothing.*

*Wind: one burst, away, ten strides, to the north bank inside the wall. The lock was spent behind another man's wall. It cost me nothing but the count, and I was more afraid in that count than I have been since a hall.*

*Nothing came of standing by it. Ten.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He ruled a line; and below him, in the hay, somebody had not gone to sleep either.

"You've written that it was your fault," said Lira.

"I've written that I planned it wrong."

"It's the same sentence in a clean shirt." The hay rustled. "I was on that lip as well. I looked at three carts for two hours and thought about my arm. Write that."

"It's not mine to write."

"Then write that I said so, and put my initial against it. She does it. I don't see why I shouldn't."

He wrote it, with an L., and shut the book.

---

On the two hundred and eightieth day they went up through the cut as soon as it was light, on the dry edge, in file.

The seep had been at work in the night, and where a heap of hounds had broken the glass there was a new skin already, clear as a window, with the crack still showing white beneath it in a line from bank to bank. The factor's people had dragged the five away, and nothing else in that place was any different. Cael walked past Brom's stone, and past the spot where a stick had come down, and did not put his boot on the line.

Vastin stopped there.

It was only for a breath, while he stood with the bound leg a little out and looked at a hair's breadth of white under new ice, as a man looks at his own name in a book where he had not expected to find it. Then he came on, a step at a time, with the stick doing what the knee could not.

"The mule's carried worse than you," said Oryn. She had the cheek-strap, and the three boxes sat on that brown back as they had sat since the landing. "Two of those would shift to the other side."

"She is carrying what she was asked to carry. I shall walk."

"I said a month on a stick. I never said that the stick had to go uphill."

"You said that a road was walking," said Vastin; and the healer shut her mouth, and Cael saw the corner of it go down, which was most of the way to a smile.

Beyond the cut's head the ruts came out onto the open shoulder, and the whole country changed. It was high moor, bare and brown, rolling away westward in long swells like a sea that had stopped. The wind came over it steadily from the west, with nothing in it but cold, and it had not left off since the first of them topped the rise. There was no tree. Far off to the north a line of blue hills stood up, with snow on them still. Somewhere under that western sky, not two days off, the country that kept no books came to its end; Cael had crossed that edge once, going the other way, by another road, with his name in every ledger behind him, and he had not turned his head to look at it then either.

Lira walked with her left hand out of her pocket. It was the fifteenth day, and she was carrying her own knife-belt in it, coiled, for no better reason than that it was something to carry.

They had gone perhaps four miles when Seln's two fingers went up, and then his whole hand, pointing off the road.

There was an outcrop of the dark plated stone a furlong to the south, as big as a barn, and under its eastern face the ground was green, where a spring came out. It rose in a basin that somebody had built round with stones, a long while ago, and ran over the basin's lip and away down the moor in a thread of rushes. Beside it a single slab of the rock had fallen flat from the face. It lay level, waist-high on its own rubble, twelve feet long and half as broad, with the outcrop standing between it and the wind.

Karis had stopped in the road.

She looked at that slab as Cael had seen her look at a wall of cut courses in a marsh. It was off the road by a furlong; a person might speak there. It was in daylight, and dry. And there was not a bench near it, or a meal-sack, or anybody's elbow.

"That is a table," she said.

"It's a rock," said Brom.

"It is the table that I have been looking for since the night before last. I said that I should count in daylight. I have carried six years of an office's paper for two days up a road and through a fight, and I have not yet turned a sheet of it." She had the flat out from under her arm already. "I want the forenoon. I want pebbles, a great many, about the size of a hen's egg, since there is a wind. And I want every person here who can read to wash his hands in that basin first."

"That knee wants the forenoon as well," said Oryn. "I'd have stopped you at the next water, whether there'd been a table or not."

Vastin opened his mouth.

"I am not telling you to sit," she said. "I know better by now. I'm telling you where the mule's stopping."

Cael looked at the road, which went on westward over the next swell and out of sight. Somewhere beyond that swell there was a fork, and beyond the fork a rise. He had ruled on a job, and the job was done and paid, and nobody had asked him this morning which way they were walking. They had simply walked.

He went and washed his hands.

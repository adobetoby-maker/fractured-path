# Chapter 41 — The List of Parts

"Fifteen," said the old man, from the mounting block, before anybody had wished him a good morning.

It was the two hundred and ninety-fourth day, and the yard was grey, with the ashes of a fire in an iron basket and eight bowls stacked on a board beside the pump. He had got himself out of the stall on the stick and one shoulder. The tarred case lay across his thighs already. It had lain in the straw at his elbow on every night since he came down a ladder on a rope; nobody had given it into his keeping, and nobody had asked for it back.

"Of thirty," said Cael.

"I am obliged. I find that I like it said by a second person."

Cael had slept, which surprised him. He had said a thing on his feet beside that basket, with six faces lit from below, and afterward he had ruled a line in a book and written nothing under it; and he had supposed that it would keep him awake. It had not. It lay in him this morning where it had been set down, like a box that has been carried into a room and is no longer on anybody's back.

There was a day's work between him and a gate, and he was glad of it.

The card for it said four, in the hand of a man who had sat on a bucket with his eyes shut. Brom ran the handcart out under the arch. They went up the hill as they had gone on three mornings, with a big man leaning into the shafts and an old one riding backward on the paper; the keepers in blue counted one, and four, and tore their corner, and the old keeper at the round arch asked whether the lame one meant to make a habit of it.

"I am told that it is the last time," said Vastin, to the cobbles.

In the lane a girl kept the square's side of him at each of four alleys. On the carts' street he looked at the roofs. He knew by now exactly how a promise sat in the neck. He knew, too, that this was the last morning on which he would have to keep it, since by the same hour tomorrow he would be on the far side of a river, where it did not run.

The clerk wrote *The same*, and *four*, and blotted them, as he had on every morning but the first.

Then there was the iron, and the twenty-seven, with an old man going down them on his seat and a boot's heel in Cael's palm at every tread. At the stair's foot the lamp's ring was hung on his fingers, without a word, by somebody who did not look at him.

The skin lay on the slope of the desk.

It had been taken out from under its jar before they came. The upper board of beech stood against the rock; and the man in the dusty coat was at the desk with the lower board in one hand and a third, which Cael had not seen before, in the other, like a cook who means to turn a cake.

"I'll not put a finger on it," he said. "Nor a reed. It's lain on that side since before I was born, and I don't know what the under side of it will bear."

He laid the one board over the leaf, and slid the other beneath, and turned the whole between his two flat hands. Then he lifted away what was now the top.

Cael looked, since the lamp was in his hand and the lamp went where the looking went.

The back was paler than the face. No dark stuff had been rubbed into it, or none was left, so that the scored lines showed only as a low sun shows a footprint, by their shadow. There were not many. He counted nine, each of them short, standing one under another down the left side like a column of figures. Beside the fourth, a little below the middle, the skin was thin and rough, and the grain of the beech showed through it.

He knew that place. It was the back of the one that a knife had been to.

The thing behind his eyes had come round, as it came round to every joint and peg in that gallery, and it leaned, mildly, at nine scratched lines that somebody had ruled first.

*No*, he told it.

It was easier than it had been. He had been caught by it three times in four days, at a rope-walk and a rivet and a table's leg, and each time he had learned a little more of how it was refused. He did not push it down, as he pushed Wind. He gave his eyes something of his own to do. So he counted the uprights of black oak between the desk and the nearest bay, which were four, as they had been yesterday; and the thing leaned at nothing, and no grit came.

"Lamp," said the rough voice. "Left. Lower than that; it's not inked. I'll want the light nearly along the skin."

He knelt, and laid the flame almost level with the desk's slope, a hand to the left, so that every scratch on that leaf threw its small shadow to the right. The man bent to it from the foot, and the water began to run from his eyes.

"Nine," he said. "And it's a list."

---

It took the whole of the forenoon to learn how little a list gives away.

Karis had the second chair, with a board across her knees and her notebook open upon the board. She read the first line through in the time that it takes to lace a boot; she had every one of the twenty-three letters now, and a hand that had pressed like a mason's did not blur. Then she sat looking at what she had written.

"I can spell it," she said. "I can very nearly say it aloud, as a child says a word in a tongue that it has not been taught. It is a noun, and then a number, and the number I can read." Her pencil tapped. "The noun stands on the face as well. It is the first of the four things that he says were made."

"And the second line?" said Cael.

"The second of the four, and a number. The third and the fourth are the same." She looked down the column. "After those there are five that the face does not name. I have their letters, and I have not one of their meanings. A tally-book counts cattle, and hides, and loads of stone; whoever kept those books had no occasion to write down the parts of a thing like this."

"So it's a bill," said Brom. He had come up the gallery with an empty box on his shoulder. "So many of this, so many of that. I've had them nailed to a crate."

"It is a list of parts, with their count. I shall call it that, since it is what I can see." She did not look up. "I am not going to tell you the numbers. They will be on my page tonight, in words; but a number without its noun is the best bait for guessing that was ever laid, and I have four people in this gallery who can guess."

"Five," said Lira.

"I had not counted myself. I am the worst of them."

The man had not sat down. He had fetched a sheet of his own from under the desk's slope, a square of the stiff stock that his cards were cut from, and a pen; and Cael saw, with some surprise, that he meant to write.

He did not read as she read. He set his pen at the foot of the first line and drew what was there, sign for sign, as a joiner copies a moulding, without once asking what it said; and wherever a run came down flat he made a small tick beneath it, in red, from a second pot.

"She'll take hers away, and it'll be right," he said, to the skin. "And in a month she'll want to know whether a stroke was his or the knife's, and she'll be three hundred miles off. So there's to be one that's drawn and not read. That's mine. It goes with her."

"I had not asked it of you," said Karis.

"No. You'd not have."

That was all, and they went on, two pens at one leaf. There was a quick small hand that turned a dead tongue into words, and a slow square one that turned it into nothing and kept its shape. Cael held the flame along the skin for both, and moved it up a finger as each line was done. He did not need to be told. He had known for three days where that man's eyes would go next, and he kept his own upon his knuckles while he knew it.

Behind him the tables were being stripped.

The fifty-two sheets had lain there for eleven days in three heaps, as they had lain upon three floors. They were to go up at the bell, and a clerk was to write that they were gone. Lira lifted them one at a time, flat on her two forearms, the slow arm and the quick, while Brom held each box's lid. On the third box, six strides from the desk, sat the old man, with his bound leg along a plank. He had the tarred case for a desk, and a leaf of Karis's notebook upon it.

She had given it to him herself, at the stair's foot, with a pencil.

"You drafted an order at a spring," she had said. "Each row to be taken up singly; a schedule to be made of every sheet as it is turned; and the schedule to be signed by two hands. I told you that it was quite a good order, and that I had nobody to sign the second half." She had held out the leaf. "I have somebody now. I should like them counted into their boxes by a person who has done it before."

He had looked at the pencil for a good while before he took it.

"The Fallow's, eleven," said Lira now, from the first table. "That's the last of them."

"Eleven," said Vastin, and made his stroke. "I have eleven. The box may be shut." He turned his head a little, toward the big man with the lid. "I should be obliged if it were said back to me."

"Eleven," said Brom. "And I never thought to be asked."

Cael heard the pencil go down again, and he did not turn round. He had seen that man stopped at a spring in the middle of an order, by nothing more than a small grey person's eyes. It seemed to him a large thing, and one that nobody in the gallery was going to remark upon, that the same order had now been asked for; and it was being carried out to the letter, with a stub of pencil on a kit's case, by the man who had not been let to give it.

---

They ate their bread at noon by the nearest bay. The man went up for his bowl, and came down, and sat his hour with his eyes shut.

It was after the hour that they came to the fourth line.

Karis had left it. She had gone from the third to the fifth without a word, as a person steps over a broken tread, and had done the five that she could not turn; and only when the ninth was written did she bring her pencil back up the column to the place where the skin was thin.

"I cannot do this with a glove," she said.

She drew the right one off. Then she looked at her left hand, which lay on the board's edge in its strip of a big man's shirt, and drew a breath through her nose; and she put the pencil into it.

"That's sewn," said Brom, from the tables.

"It is sewn across the heel. I do not write with the heel." She had the brass strip from Ternhall in her right hand now, and she laid it along the line, to keep her eye from wandering into the one above. "I have two hands, and one line that wants both of them. I shall be slow. I should be obliged if nobody watched."

Everybody watched. Cael saw the linen darken a very little, at the palm's edge, before she had made three signs; and he saw that she had seen it, and had gone on.

The line was a bad one. Somebody had scraped a word from the leaf's face, long ago, and had borne down to do it; and where the edge had gone, the skin was no thicker than the film on a pan of milk. What had been scored on the back at that place had lost its depth. A stroke that lay a hair deep on either side of it was there no more than the scratch that a nail leaves on a slate.

"There's his," said the man. The reed was in his fingers, an inch above the leaf. "And there's the knife's, coming through from the other side. They cross. You'll want to tell which is which before you call any of it a letter."

"I know that I shall."

"The first of it's sound. It's the word you had on the face, for the mark." He did not say it as she would have said it; he said it as a man names a beam. "That's what bears, and there's only the one. Two runs hang from it." The reed moved. "One there, of five. One there, of six, and that's the one in the thin. And after the pair of them, his number."

"*Three*," said Karis. "I have the number."

"They hang level. I'll tell you that for nothing, since you can't see it and I can. He's not put the one above the other. Whatever those two are, the mark's set over both the same."

On a box, six strides off, a pencil had stopped.

Cael did not look round. He looked at his knuckles, and at the flame, and he heard an old man decline to finish a sentence for the second time in two days; there was no sound in it but breath, let go slowly, and then the small scrape of a stroke made on a tally. It appeared that the count of the Stair's had come to twenty-nine, and that nobody at the desk had heard anything else.

"The first run I can read," said Karis. She was writing with the bound hand, very small, and her lips were white. "It is the people at a kindling, as the face has them. The second run is six signs, and it is in the thin; and of the six I am certain of three."

"Which?"

"The first, and the fourth, and the last."

"I'd have given you the first and the last. I'd not have sworn to your fourth."

He had straightened. He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and bent again, to his own square of card, where the same line stood in his own slow drawing. He looked from it to hers, and he was a long time about it.

"Your hand's better than mine on the fourth line," said Cleon.

He took up his pen, and struck his drawing of that line through with one stroke, and held the card out along the slope.

"Put it in on mine. As you've got it, and no better. Where you're not sure, leave me the scratch, and don't make a letter of it."

She took the card. She looked at him for a breath; and it was the first time in eleven days that anybody in that gallery had seen her at a loss for the small exact thing to say.

"It is three signs of six," she said at last. "I could have five by supper, if I let myself. I have a word in my head that would fit the three like a key." Her eyes had gone down to the thin place. "I had a word in my head once before, under a glove, and it was the wrong one. I shall not tell you what this one is."

"Good," said the man. "Then I'll not have to forget it."

---

The bell went with his finger on the last line.

It had been there some time. He had drawn all nine, and had gone back to the foot of the ninth, to see whether it came down flat or was only broken off by the skin's edge. He stood so, bent over the slope, with the water running from his eyes and one square finger in the air above the last scratch on that leaf, when the wire stirred at the stair's foot and the small flat stroke came up the gallery.

"It ends," he said. "He finished. There's no tenth that's been cut away." He straightened. "That's the one thing that I've been sure of since noon."

Nobody hurried. The boxes stood ready by the cross-way, three of them, roped, with a tally lying on the lid of the third under a stub of pencil. It had two hands' marks at its foot, a K. and a square upright V. Cael had seen the old man look at his own letter after he had made it, as at a thing that he had not expected to be asked for again.

The leaf went back upon its face. The man turned it between the boards as he had turned it in the morning, and laid the upper board home, and set the jar upon the board.

Then he held out his hand, behind him, to nothing.

It was not for the lamp to be moved, and Cael understood that a breath late. The hand was open, with the palm upward, and it waited; and he put the brass ring into it.

The fingers shut. The man took his own lamp up, for the first time in ten days, and the thumb shifted on the ring, of itself, until the flame hung a hand to the left of the thing that he was about to look at, which was the floor.

He had not looked at the boy. He had not done so since he poured a cup of water and set it on a table's corner.

Cael got up off his knee, which had a callus on it now, like a tiler's. He had been a lamp in that gallery since the third hour of the second day. He had been told *left*, and *up a finger*, by a man who did not know his name and had never asked what he was; and it came to him, standing there with his hand empty, that it was the only work he had ever done inside a wall which nobody had written down. No form had a line for it. He had held a light where it was wanted, and had learned which way the looking went; and a week of another person's whole life's work had been done by it.

He found that he would have liked to say so. He did not. He had been looked away from for his own sake, and the least that he could do was to let it be done well.

The old man had asked for his quarter of an hour again, and had been given it. They heard him above them on the second flight when they came to the stair's foot with the boxes: two hands set down, and a breath, and a scrape. He was at the head when they reached it, sitting against the iron with the case in his arms.

"Twenty-seven," he said. "Unassisted. I mention it for the tally."

"It's entered," said Lira. "Shift your leg."

Quick flat feet came up behind them, with a brown light. The man drew the bar. He did not swing the iron at once. He stood with his hand on it, in the narrow place at the stair's head, with five people and three boxes pressed along the wall; and he held out a square of card-stock, rolled, with a turn of brown tape about it.

Karis took it in her right hand. The left she kept against her coat.

"I do not know how to enter this week," she said. "I have been trying since the yard. I have a page for what the leaf says, and a page that is clean. Neither of them has a line for the man who gave me every join in fifty-two sheets, and would not hear what I thought they were. I shall have to write something at the head; and I should like it to be a thing that you would put your name to."

"Work. Not a finding."

"That is not enough."

"It's what it was. I stood where I've stood for thirty years, and said which way a thing leaned. You found out what it said." The red eyes were on the roll of card in her hand. "A finding's yours. You'll have to carry it up into the light and be believed, and I've told you what I think of that. Work's a thing that a man can go back down to in the morning."

"Then I shall write *work*," said Karis. Her voice was not quite steady. "And I shall write whose."

"Write what you like. It's been on worse."

He put his shoulder to the iron, and it swung inward on the vault, where a clerk's lantern waited among the pillars. She had gone a step over the sill when he spoke again, to her back, in the voice like a hinge; and it was drier than Cael had ever heard it.

"Mind the stair. It leans."

She stopped.

They had come up the only stair in sight, all twenty-seven steps of it, and it lay behind them in the dark. Cael watched her take that in, and then watched her take in the rest. There had been a row of signs on a sheet, with each one hung from the one before; and a man had said that he did not read such a thing, but was walked down it. She had fifty-two sheets of that in three boxes, and she was about to walk them east.

"I shall mind it," she said, and did not turn round. "Thank you."

The iron went to behind them, and they heard the bar.

---

The clerk had his green book open on the hatch's ledge, and for once he did not know what to write.

Cael watched him find it. The pen had set down *the same* on that leaf day after day, and tonight the same was not so. The tables below were bare. Three boxes stood upon his paving which would not come back in the morning, and there was no card for the morning in anybody's hand. His eyes went along the stones at the height of a boot, and found seven that stood and one that hung. Then they went back to the form.

He wrote slowly. *Withdrawn by its bearer*, said the long column; and the narrow one said *four*. He blotted both. Then, with the pen still lifted, he ruled a line beneath the whole of it, across both columns, as a man rules off an account that has come out even.

"He's shut us," said Lira, under her breath.

"He's glad to," said Brom. "Look at his shoulders. That's a man who'll sleep tonight."

The clerk had asked them one thing in eleven days, and he did not ask a second. But when the key had turned in the low door he stood a moment on its sill, with his book against his chest, and looked along the dock at an old man being got onto a handcart. His mouth opened a very little, as it had on the first morning. Then he shut it, and went in.

Seln was at the painted pole, with a hired frame at his feet. He fell in at the cart's tail, like a porter who has been paid to, and said what he had to say to the setts.

"There was nobody on the kerb, and nobody in the smith's door. I have been down to the quay and looked at the bridge from below. It is one cart wide." He walked a few strides. "A person who wished to see us leave this town need not follow us about it. He need only stand at one end of that."

"Then he'll see six people and some boxes," said Cael.

"He will see how many, and which way. It is all that I should want, in his place."

The yard was nearly dark when they had the cart swept. Brom did it himself, with a birch broom from the stable, and stood the shafts against the wall as he had found them. Then he looked at the three boxes in their row, and at his own two arms, which hung.

"I can carry mine, on the right, as I have," he said. "She's two, and so's he. That's the boxes. She's the flat and the hide." He nodded toward the mounting block. "And there's twelve miles of that road before the first hedge ends, and a hill after it, and I've nothing to put under him."

"I shall walk for as long as I am able," said Vastin. "I shall say when I am not. I undertake that much, and I do not undertake a yard more."

"That's no use to me. I want to know where I'll be standing when you say it."

"There's carts on that road," said Lira. "We met thirty."

"Going in. I'll ask at the walk in the morning, when I'm there for the rope. A man who sells cordage knows who's hauling." Brom rubbed his jaw with the back of his wrist. "It's not a mule. I'd give a good deal for the mule."

Lira had her left arm out before her, and was turning the hand over and back in the last of the light.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "She told me a month of slow. I've counted every one."

He looked at it with her. It was thinner than the right, still, all down the forearm, and its eight marks were pale; but it turned without a hitch, and when she shut the fist there was no tremor in it that he could see. Twenty-nine days ago it had come out of a sling, and could do no more than hold a boot.

Cael went up the ladder, and sat down against the third box, and wrote by the lantern.

*Two hundred and ninety-fourth day. Treswick; the loft, and one in the stall; the last night of both. A hundred and thirty. The back of the leaf: a list, nine lines, a thing and its number to each. Two copies, hers and the reader's, and his goes with her. The fourth line is the mark's; of its last six signs, three are sure. Fifty-two sheets counted into three boxes under two hands, K. and V. The clerk wrote* withdrawn*. Vastin: fifteen of thirty, and the stair alone. Karis wrote with the sewn hand. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

*A lamp, ten days. It is entered nowhere but here.*

He read that last over, and let it stand.

In the morning there was a gate, and a bridge on five piers, and a road that ran twelve miles between hedges to the foot of a rise; and somewhere beyond that rise, a long way north and east, a square woman was coming down a far water toward a mill. She had a brown mule on a rope, which would have carried three boxes and thought nothing of it.

She was four days off, and there was still no mule.

# Chapter 41 — The List of Parts

"Fifteen," said the old man, from the mounting block, before anybody had wished him a good morning.

It was the two hundred and ninety-fourth day, and the yard was grey, with the ashes of a fire in an iron basket and eight bowls stacked on a board beside the pump. He had got himself out of the stall on the stick and one shoulder. The tarred case lay across his thighs already. It had lain in the straw at his elbow on every night since he came down a ladder on a rope; nobody had given it into his keeping, and nobody had asked for it back.

"Of thirty," said Cael.

"I am obliged. I find that I like it said by a second person."

Cael had slept, which surprised him. He had said a thing on his feet beside that basket, with six faces lit from below, and afterward he had ruled a line in a book and written nothing under it; and he had supposed that it would keep him awake. It had not. It lay in him this morning where it had been set down, like a box that has been carried into a room and is no longer on anybody's back.

There was a day's work between him and a gate, and he was glad of it.

The card for it said four, in the hand of a man who had sat on a bucket with his eyes shut. Brom ran the handcart out under the arch. They went up the hill as they had gone on three mornings, with a big man leaning into the shafts and an old one riding backward on the paper; the keepers in blue counted one, and four, and tore their corner, and the old keeper at the round arch asked whether the lame one meant to make a habit of it.

"I am told that it is the last time," said Vastin, to the cobbles.

In the lane a girl kept the square's side of him at each of four alleys. On the carts' street he looked at the roofs. He knew by now exactly how a promise sat in the neck. He knew, too, that this was the last morning on which he would have to keep it, since by the same hour tomorrow he would be on the far side of a river, where it did not run.

The clerk wrote *The same*, and *four*, and blotted them, as he had on every morning but the first.

Then there was the iron, and the twenty-seven, with an old man going down them on his seat and a boot's heel in Cael's palm at every tread. At the stair's foot the lamp's ring was hung on his fingers, without a word, by somebody who did not look at him.

The skin lay on the slope of the desk.

It had been taken out from under its jar before they came. The upper board of beech stood against the rock; and the man in the dusty coat was at the desk with the lower board in one hand and a third, which Cael had not seen before, in the other, like a cook who means to turn a cake.

"I'll not put a finger on it," he said. "Nor a reed. It's lain on that side since before I was born, and I don't know what the under side of it will bear."

He laid the one board over the leaf, and slid the other beneath, and turned the whole between his two flat hands. Then he lifted away what was now the top.

Cael looked, since the lamp was in his hand and the lamp went where the looking went.

The back was paler than the face. No dark stuff had been rubbed into it, or none was left, so that the scored lines showed only as a low sun shows a footprint, by their shadow. There were not many. He counted nine, each of them short, standing one under another down the left side like a column of figures. Beside the fourth, a little below the middle, the skin was thin and rough, and the grain of the beech showed through it.

He knew that place. It was the back of the one that a knife had been to.

The thing behind his eyes had come round, as it came round to every joint and peg in that gallery, and it leaned, mildly, at nine scratched lines that somebody had ruled first.

*No*, he told it.

It was easier than it had been. He had been caught by it three times in four days, at a rope-walk and a rivet and a table's leg, and each time he had learned a little more of how it was refused. He did not push it down, as he pushed Wind. He gave his eyes something of his own to do. So he counted the uprights of black oak between the desk and the nearest bay, which were four, as they had been yesterday; and the thing leaned at nothing, and no grit came.

"Lamp," said the rough voice. "Left. Lower than that; it's not inked. I'll want the light nearly along the skin."

He knelt, and laid the flame almost level with the desk's slope, a hand to the left, so that every scratch on that leaf threw its small shadow to the right. The man bent to it from the foot, and the water began to run from his eyes.

"Nine," he said. "And it's a list."

---

It took the whole of the forenoon to learn how little a list gives away.

Karis had the second chair, with a board across her knees and her notebook open upon the board. She read the first line through in the time that it takes to lace a boot; she had every one of the twenty-three letters now, and a hand that had pressed like a mason's did not blur. Then she sat looking at what she had written.

"I can spell it," she said. "I can very nearly say it aloud, as a child says a word in a tongue that it has not been taught. It is a noun, and then a number, and the number I can read." Her pencil tapped. "The noun stands on the face as well. It is the first of the four things that he says were made."

"And the second line?" said Cael.

"The second of the four, and a number. The third and the fourth are the same." She looked down the column. "After those there are five that the face does not name. I have their letters, and I have not one of their meanings. A tally-book counts cattle, and hides, and loads of stone; whoever kept those books had no occasion to write down the parts of a thing like this."

"So it's a bill," said Brom. He had come up the gallery with an empty box on his shoulder. "So many of this, so many of that. I've had them nailed to a crate."

"It is a list of parts, with their count. I shall call it that, since it is what I can see." She did not look up. "I am not going to tell you the numbers. They will be on my page tonight, in words; but a number without its noun is the best bait for guessing that was ever laid, and I have four people in this gallery who can guess."

"Five," said Lira.

"I had not counted myself. I am the worst of them."

The man had not sat down. He had fetched a sheet of his own from under the desk's slope, a square of the stiff stock that his cards were cut from, and a pen; and Cael saw, with some surprise, that he meant to write.

He did not read as she read. He set his pen at the foot of the first line and drew what was there, sign for sign, as a joiner copies a moulding, without once asking what it said; and wherever a run came down flat he made a small tick beneath it, in red, from a second pot.

"She'll take hers away, and it'll be right," he said, to the skin. "And in a month she'll want to know whether a stroke was his or the knife's, and she'll be three hundred miles off. So there's to be one that's drawn and not read. That's mine. It goes with her."

"I had not asked it of you," said Karis.

"No. You'd not have."

That was all, and they went on, two pens at one leaf. There was a quick small hand that turned a dead tongue into words, and a slow square one that turned it into nothing and kept its shape. Cael held the flame along the skin for both, and moved it up a finger as each line was done. He did not need to be told. He had known for three days where that man's eyes would go next, and he kept his own upon his knuckles while he knew it.

Behind him the tables were being stripped.

The fifty-two sheets had lain there for eleven days in three heaps, as they had lain upon three floors. They were to go up at the bell, and a clerk was to write that they were gone. Lira lifted them one at a time, flat on her two forearms, the slow arm and the quick, while Brom held each box's lid. On the third box, six strides from the desk, sat the old man, with his bound leg along a plank. He had the tarred case for a desk, and a leaf of Karis's notebook upon it.

She had given it to him herself, at the stair's foot, with a pencil.

"You drafted an order at a spring," she had said. "Each row to be taken up singly; a schedule to be made of every sheet as it is turned; and the schedule to be signed by two hands. I told you that it was quite a good order, and that I had nobody to sign the second half." She had held out the leaf. "I have somebody now. I should like them counted into their boxes by a person who has done it before."

He had looked at the pencil for a good while before he took it.

"The Fallow's, eleven," said Lira now, from the first table. "That's the last of them."

"Eleven," said Vastin, and made his stroke. "I have eleven. The box may be shut." He turned his head a little, toward the big man with the lid. "I should be obliged if it were said back to me."

"Eleven," said Brom. "And I never thought to be asked."

Cael heard the pencil go down again, and he did not turn round. He had seen that man stopped at a spring in the middle of an order, by nothing more than a small grey person's eyes. It seemed to him a large thing, and one that nobody in the gallery was going to remark upon, that the same order had now been asked for; and it was being carried out to the letter, with a stub of pencil on a kit's case, by the man who had not been let to give it.

---

They ate their bread at noon by the nearest bay. The man went up for his bowl, and came down, and sat his hour with his eyes shut.

It was after the hour that they came to the fourth line.

Karis had left it. She had gone from the third to the fifth without a word, as a person steps over a broken tread, and had done the five that she could not turn; and only when the ninth was written did she bring her pencil back up the column to the place where the skin was thin.

"I cannot do this with a glove," she said.

She drew the right one off. Then she looked at her left hand, which lay on the board's edge in its strip of a big man's shirt, and drew a breath through her nose; and she put the pencil into it.

"That's sewn," said Brom, from the tables.

"It is sewn across the heel. I do not write with the heel." She had the brass strip from Ternhall in her right hand now, and she laid it along the line, to keep her eye from wandering into the one above. "I have two hands, and one line that wants both of them. I shall be slow. I should be obliged if nobody watched."

Everybody watched. Cael saw the linen darken a very little, at the palm's edge, before she had made three signs; and he saw that she had seen it, and had gone on.

The line was a bad one. Somebody had scraped a word from the leaf's face, long ago, and had borne down to do it; and where the edge had gone, the skin was no thicker than the film on a pan of milk. What had been scored on the back at that place had lost its depth. A stroke that lay a hair deep on either side of it was there no more than the scratch that a nail leaves on a slate.

"There's his," said the man. The reed was in his fingers, an inch above the leaf. "And there's the knife's, coming through from the other side. They cross. You'll want to tell which is which before you call any of it a letter."

"I know that I shall."

"The first of it's sound. It's the word you had on the face, for the mark." He did not say it as she would have said it; he said it as a man names a beam. "That's the bearer, and there's only the one. Two runs hang from it." The reed moved. "One there, of five. One there, of six, and that's the one in the thin. And after the pair of them, his number."

"*Three*," said Karis. "I have the number."

"They hang level. I'll tell you that for nothing, since you can't see it and I can. He's not put the one above the other. Whatever those two are, the mark's set over both the same."

On a box, six strides off, a pencil had stopped.

Cael did not look round. He looked at his knuckles, and at the flame, and he heard an old man decline to finish a sentence for the second time in two days; there was no sound in it but breath, let go slowly, and then the small scrape of a stroke made on a tally. It appeared that the count of the Stair's had come to twenty-nine, and that nobody at the desk had heard anything else.

"The first run I can read," said Karis. She was writing with the bound hand, very small, and her lips were white. "It is the people at a kindling, as the face has them. The second run is six signs, and it is in the thin; and of the six I am certain of three."

"Which?"

"The first, and the fourth, and the last."

"I'd have given you the first and the last. I'd not have sworn to your fourth."

He had straightened. He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and bent again, to his own square of card, where the same line stood in his own slow drawing. He looked from it to hers, and he was a long time about it.

"Your hand's better than mine on the fourth line," said Cleon.

He took up his pen, and struck his drawing of that line through with one stroke, and held the card out along the slope.

"Put it in on mine. As you've got it, and no better. Where you're not sure, leave me the scratch, and don't make a letter of it."

She took the card. She looked at him for a breath; and it was the first time in eleven days that anybody in that gallery had seen her at a loss for the small exact thing to say.

"It is three signs of six," she said at last. "I could have five by supper, if I let myself. I have a word in my head that would fit the three like a key." Her eyes had gone down to the thin place. "I had a word in my head once before, under a glove, and it was the wrong one. I shall not tell you what this one is."

"Good," said the man. "Then I'll not have to forget it."

---

The bell went with his finger on the last line.

It had been there some time. He had drawn all nine, and had gone back to the foot of the ninth, to see whether it came down flat or was only broken off by the skin's edge. He stood so, bent over the slope, with the water running from his eyes and one square finger in the air above the last scratch on that leaf, when the wire stirred at the stair's foot and the small flat stroke came up the gallery.

"It ends," he said. "He finished. There's no tenth that's been cut away." He straightened. "That's the one thing that I've been sure of since noon."

Nobody hurried. The boxes stood ready by the cross-way, three of them, roped, with a tally lying on the lid of the third under a stub of pencil. It had two hands' marks at its foot, a K. and a square upright V. Cael had seen the old man look at his own letter after he had made it, as at a thing that he had not expected to be asked for again.

The leaf went back upon its face. The man turned it between the boards as he had turned it in the morning, and laid the upper board home, and set the jar upon the board.

Then he held out his hand, behind him, to nothing.

It was not for the lamp to be moved, and Cael understood that a breath late. The hand was open, with the palm upward, and it waited; and he put the brass ring into it.

The fingers shut. The man took his own lamp up, for the first time in ten days, and the thumb shifted on the ring, of itself, until the flame hung a hand to the left of the thing that he was about to look at, which was the floor.

He had not looked at the boy. He had not done so since he poured a cup of water and set it on a table's corner.

Cael got up off his knee, which had a callus on it now, like a tiler's. He had been a lamp in that gallery since the third hour of the second day. He had been told *left*, and *up a finger*, by a man who did not know his name and had never asked what he was; and it came to him, standing there with his hand empty, that it was the only work he had ever done inside a wall which nobody had written down. No form had a line for it. He had held a light where it was wanted, and had learned which way the looking went; and a week of another person's whole life's work had been done by it.

He found that he would have liked to say so. He did not. He had been looked away from for his own sake, and the least that he could do was to let it be done well.

The old man had asked for his quarter of an hour again, and had been given it. They heard him above them on the second flight when they came to the stair's foot with the boxes: two hands set down, and a breath, and a scrape. He was at the head when they reached it, sitting against the iron with the case in his arms.

"Twenty-seven," he said. "Unassisted. I mention it for the tally."

"It's entered," said Lira. "Shift your leg."

Quick flat feet came up behind them, with a brown light. The man drew the bar. He did not swing the iron at once. He stood with his hand on it, in the narrow place at the stair's head, with five people and three boxes pressed along the wall; and he held out a square of card-stock, rolled, with a turn of brown tape about it.

Karis took it in her right hand. The left she kept against her coat.

"I do not know how to enter this week," she said. "I have been trying since the yard. I have a page for what the leaf says, and a page that is clean. Neither of them has a line for the man who gave me every join in fifty-two sheets, and would not hear what I thought they were. I shall have to write something at the head; and I should like it to be a thing that you would put your name to."

"Work. Not a finding."

"That is not enough."

"It's what it was. I stood where I've stood for thirty years, and said which way a thing leaned. You found out what it said." The red eyes were on the roll of card in her hand. "A finding's yours. You'll have to carry it up into the light and be believed, and I've told you what I think of that. Work's a thing that a man can go back down to in the morning."

"Then I shall write *work*," said Karis. Her voice was not quite steady. "And I shall write whose."

"Write what you like. It's been on worse."

He put his shoulder to the iron, and it swung inward on the vault, where a clerk's lantern waited among the pillars. She had gone a step over the sill when he spoke again, to her back, in the voice like a hinge; and it was drier than Cael had ever heard it.

"Mind the stair. It leans."

She stopped.

They had come up the only stair in sight, all twenty-seven steps of it, and it lay behind them in the dark. Cael watched her take that in, and then watched her take in the rest. There had been a row of signs on a sheet, with each one hung from the one before; and a man had said that he did not read such a thing, but was walked down it. She had fifty-two sheets of that in three boxes, and she was about to walk them east.

"I shall mind it," she said, and did not turn round. "Thank you."

The iron went to behind them, and they heard the bar.

---

The clerk had his green book open on the hatch's ledge, and for once he did not know what to write.

Cael watched him find it. The pen had set down *the same* on that leaf day after day, and tonight the same was not so. The tables below were bare. Three boxes stood upon his paving which would not come back in the morning, and there was no card for the morning in anybody's hand. His eyes went along the stones at the height of a boot, and found seven that stood and one that hung. Then they went back to the form.

He wrote slowly. *Withdrawn by its bearer*, said the long column; and the narrow one said *four*. He blotted both. Then, with the pen still lifted, he ruled a line beneath the whole of it, across both columns, as a man rules off an account that has come out even.

"He's shut us," said Lira, under her breath.

"He's glad to," said Brom. "Look at his shoulders. That's a man who'll sleep tonight."

The clerk had asked them one thing in eleven days, and he did not ask a second. But when the key had turned in the low door he stood a moment on its sill, with his book against his chest, and looked along the dock at an old man being got onto a handcart. His mouth opened a very little, as it had on the first morning. Then he shut it, and went in.

Seln was at the painted pole, with a hired frame at his feet. He fell in at the cart's tail, like a porter who has been paid to, and said what he had to say to the setts.

"There was nobody on the kerb, and nobody in the smith's door. I have been down to the quay and looked at the bridge from below. It is one cart wide." He walked a few strides. "A person who wished to see us leave this town need not follow us about it. He need only stand at one end of that."

"Then he'll see six people and some boxes," said Cael.

"He will see how many, and which way. It is all that I should want, in his place."

The yard was nearly dark when they had the cart swept. Brom did it himself, with a birch broom from the stable, and stood the shafts against the wall as he had found them. Then he looked at the three boxes in their row, and at his own two arms, which hung.

"I can carry mine, on the right, as I have," he said. "She's two, and so's he. That's the boxes. She's the flat and the hide." He nodded toward the mounting block. "And there's twelve miles of that road before the first hedge ends, and a hill after it, and I've nothing to put under him."

"I shall walk for as long as I am able," said Vastin. "I shall say when I am not. I undertake that much, and I do not undertake a yard more."

"That's no use to me. I want to know where I'll be standing when you say it."

"There's carts on that road," said Lira. "We met thirty."

"Going in. I'll ask at the walk in the morning, when I'm there for the rope. A man who sells cordage knows who's hauling." Brom rubbed his jaw with the back of his wrist. "It's not a mule. I'd give a good deal for the mule."

Lira had her left arm out before her, and was turning the hand over and back in the last of the light.

"Twenty-nine," she said. "She told me a month of slow. I've counted every one."

He looked at it with her. It was thinner than the right, still, all down the forearm, and its eight marks were pale; but it turned without a hitch, and when she shut the fist there was no tremor in it that he could see. Twenty-nine days ago it had come out of a sling, and could do no more than hold a boot.

Cael went up the ladder, and sat down against the third box, and wrote by the lantern.

*Two hundred and ninety-fourth day. Treswick; the loft, and one in the stall; the last night of both. A hundred and thirty. The back of the leaf: a list, nine lines, a thing and its number to each. Two copies, hers and the reader's, and his goes with her. The fourth line is the mark's; of its last six signs, three are sure. Fifty-two sheets counted into three boxes under two hands, K. and V. The clerk wrote* withdrawn*. Vastin: fifteen of thirty, and the stair alone. Karis wrote with the sewn hand. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

*A lamp, ten days. It is entered nowhere but here.*

He read that last over, and let it stand.

In the morning there was a gate, and a bridge on five piers, and a road that ran twelve miles between hedges to the foot of a rise; and somewhere beyond that rise, a long way north and east, a square woman was coming down a far water toward a mill. She had a brown mule on a rope, which would have carried three boxes and thought nothing of it.

She was four days off, and there was still no mule.

# Chapter 42 — Five Piers

Brom swept the handcart a second time before the light, though he had swept it at dusk, and stood its shafts against the stable wall with their ends level.

"She said swept," he said. "She never said by whom, or how often."

The woman of the half-door came out to look at it with a candle. She looked at the wheels as well, and at the floor of the end stall, where an old man had folded his blanket in four and laid it on the manger. She said that she had kept lodgers over those horses for eleven years, and had never before been handed back a stall tidier than she had let it. Then she counted them out under the arch by their feet, as she had counted them in, and went back to her bread.

It was the two hundred and ninety-fifth morning past the Line. They had lain thirteen nights in that loft, and Cael had not once gone in at the painted door.

The rope-walk was awake before the street was. A lantern burned at the head of the long shed, where the wheel stood, and another far down it, as small as a star; and between the two a line of people walked backward in the half dark, with the yarn running out between their fingers. Brom went along the pegs as he had gone along them thirteen days before. He did not need to hunt. He stopped at the coil that he had stopped at then, and took a fathom off it, and bent the end back, and looked in.

"Four," he said. "Round a heart."

"It's the same that you looked at," said the woman with the tarred hands. "I told you it'd be the same going out. I've had it by since."

"You'd no call to do that. I never put money on it."

"You hung my fathom back as you found it. I've sold rope in this shed to men who'd not do as much for their mothers." She had the coil down already, across her two arms. "Sixty yards. It's the price that I named."

He counted it onto the head of a tar barrel: two marks, in silver, out of the purse that lay inside his shirt beside a thumbed slip. He watched the coil while he did it, and Cael saw the frown go out of his face a little at a time, like a knot being worked loose with a spike. That debt had ridden in him since before the snow. It had gone over a river and come back, and up a moor, and into a town, and through eleven days under a registry. Here it lay at last, sixty yards of the right kind, across a woman's forearms.

"A hundred and twenty-eight in hand," he said.

Then he looked at his own arms, which he could not lift above the elbow, and at a box that stood beside his boot.

"I can't," he said. "I've been turning it over since the fire. It's the weight of half a man, and it'll not go on a back that's got a box on it already." He said it without shame, as he would have said that a plank was short. "I asked you last night who hauls."

"You've no need to ask. Look behind you."

There was a dray in the lane. It was a long low thing on four wheels, with two brown cobs in its shafts, and it was loaded to the rails with coils, tarred and white, under a sheet that had not yet been lashed down. A lean man with a straw in his mouth was going round it, trying each knot.

"That's for the western landing," said the woman. "Three days down the moor road. There's a broker there who's sold three-strand for as long as I've laid it, and a fortnight since he sent up for four. Forty coils. He wrote that a big man from over the water had stood in front of his stack with a full purse, and called his rope the wrong kind, and gone away with the purse; and that he'd not have it said to him twice."

Brom had gone red to the ears.

"I never called it wrong. I said that it wasn't what the man had asked for."

"He's taken it hard, whatever you said. That's forty coils that I'd not have laid." She jerked her chin at the lean man. "It's his road, and you'll ask him. But I'd think that a coil could ride with its own kind, to the place where it's wanted."

The carter had heard. He came round the tail of the dray and looked Brom over slowly, from the boots upward, as a man reads a waybill.

"You're the wrong kind of excellent," he said.

"I've been told so."

"They've had it on that landing for a fortnight, for a saying. A lad drops a keg, and they tell him that he's the wrong kind of excellent." He took the straw out of his mouth. "Throw it on. I'll not charge a coil that's going where I'm going."

His eyes went past Brom then, and found an old man standing on one leg and a stick, with a tarred case under his arm.

"And that?"

"That walks as far as he's able," said Cael. "After that he doesn't."

"I have a copper," said Vastin. "I have had it ready since last night, in the expectation of being asked what I should cost to carry. I should prefer to pay it."

"There's a tail-board. It's a copper a day to sit on, and you'll get down for every hill; I'll not have my cobs pull what can walk. And you'll hold your tongue on the moor. I'll not need to tell you why."

"You will not," said Vastin.

He counted the copper into that brown palm, and it was kept. Cael saw the old shoulders ease by the width of a finger. It appeared that this road, at least, was going to take his money.

---

The gate was opened at first light, as two painted boards had promised, and they were in the arch with the first of the carts.

The keeper in undyed grey sat on his high stool with the frame on his knee. He looked at feet, and wheels, and hooves, as he had looked at them thirteen days before, and his thumb moved the beads: eight hooves, and four wheels. He came to the boots, and Cael counted five pairs with him. The thumb went on to the tail-board, where a pair of boots hung that did not touch the ground, and it stopped.

"Those are feet," said the keeper, to himself. "They're on wheels."

"He came in on them," said Lira. "You moved a bead for him. I watched you do it."

The keeper looked at her, for the first time. It was plain that nobody argued with that frame from one cold term to the next. Then he moved two beads along the upper wire, slowly, as a man pays a debt that he had hoped was forgotten.

"Six out," he said. "Six in, the day I had you. It comes even."

Nobody at the sloped desk wrote a word of them. A load on a back was the back's, coming or going.

Cael stepped over the sill.

It was one long stone, worn hollow in the middle, with a socket at each end. *There?* asked Anchor, in its thin held note, of the two sockets, and he told it no. He had crossed that stone thirteen days ago to walk into the oldest registry on the Line, and he crossed it now with no name of his in any book of that town. He had not known that such a thing could be done. He did not think that it could be done twice.

He counted the piers. He had counted them coming in, twice, since Karis would ask; he counted them now for a reason of his own. There were five. They stood in the water like the knuckles of a hand laid flat, each with its bay above it where a person on foot might stand aside. The thing behind his eyes had come round to the first of them before his boot was on the roadway.

It wanted that bridge badly. It was a thing of dark stone laid dry, each block cut to its neighbour, by somebody who had meant every joint. *Bears*, the thing would have said, of each pier in turn. He could feel the word waiting, five times over, like five steps going down into water.

*One*, he said instead, and looked at the first pier's cutwater, and at nothing else of it. *Two.*

It was his own count, and it was made with his own eyes, which could not do two things at once. The lean came up short against it, as a dog comes up against a door that has been shut without unkindness. At the third pier the grit had not come. At the fifth he knew that he had found a thing which he could use. He had spent his life counting what was in front of him so that he need not feel it, and for once the habit was worth something to somebody.

Karis had stopped in the third bay.

She had the flat under one arm and the hide under the other; and her bound hand lay open on the parapet's dark cap, palm down, in its linen. She was not measuring anything. She only stood, with her head a little on one side, and looked along the roadway at two grooves that were deeper than a wheel's rim.

"I was told that it would be here on the way out," she said. "It is here."

"I said that," said Brom. He had stopped the whole file behind her, box and all, while the dray went on.

"I have not got a week, and I have not got a sheet for it." She took the hand away. "I know more about what stood here before the walls than any person living. I have it from a skin, and a board, and three floors that are a week east of this. I have not so much as drawn the thing that I walked over to reach them. I should like that entered against me."

"It's entered," said Cael. "Draw it going home."

"Which way is that?"

He had no answer for her, and she had not wanted one. She shifted the hide, and went on.

He was in the last bay when he understood that he was free to turn round.

It came to him quite plainly, between one stride and the next. A healer had made him write a thing in a book at a fork-stone: that inside Treswick he did not go near the station. He had kept it for thirteen days, to the letter, down a lane with four white alleys in it, and through a night when a thread of light on a loft's boards had not been steady. And the gate was behind him. The river was the Line, and he was over the river. The promise had been for inside, and he was out.

He could stand in this bay, as any carter might, and look back up the whole of that hill to the top of it.

A girl with a box on her back had come up on his left side. She did not look at him, and she did not look anywhere else. She had kept her chin level down every yard of this road, coming in, without being asked, and it was level now.

He did not turn.

It was not the promise. That was paid, and she would hear at a mill that it had been. It was only that he had seen a light go unsteady once, for a count of one, and had written *hold* beneath it. A person who has ruled *hold* does not go back to stare at the thing, to learn whether it will do so again; that is not holding.

"Five," he said aloud, to Lira. "It's still five."

"I'd have been surprised," she said, and went by him off the bridge.

---

The road ran on its causeway for three miles, with a ditch on either hand, and a white mist stood in the river meadows as high as the cobs' bellies.

It was a town's ground. A person might speak on it, as far as the first hedge, and for a long while nobody did. The dray set the pace, which was a stick's pace or a little better. The lean man walked at his near cob's head, and the six came after his wheels in the order that they had learned, with a quiet man gone on ahead into the white. Cael walked last, behind the tail-board.

So he had an old man in front of him, riding backward, for the whole of twelve miles.

He had not thought of that when the copper was paid. Vastin sat on the board's edge with the bound leg laid along a coil and the tarred case across his thighs, and both raw hands upon the stick's head; and since he faced the way that they had come, he faced Cael. There was two yards of road between them. It did not grow or shrink.

*This far?* said the eleventh, of the three-mile post.

It stood at the western end of a carters' pull-in, where a hedge stopped and a bank ran on beside the ditch. Cael knew that bank. A man had let himself down upon it, in the dusk, where he could not rise again without a boy's arm, and had said why he once declined to read that boy a second time. They had called it ground afterward. It had not been friendship, and it was not now.

*No*, he told the thing. It had asked at a milestone, as it asked at every line that anybody had ever drawn across a road. The man whom it had come out of looked over his head at a town, and did not know.

"I have ridden up to that gate on four occasions," said Vastin. He spoke low, though the ground allowed more. "Each time I was met at the bridge. I certified what I was shown, and I rode down again, and I did not once see it go small behind me. One does not, from a saddle. One is looking at the next district."

"What's it look like?"

"It looks like a hill with three walls on it. I had supposed that it would look like more." He considered it a while longer. "I was eleven days in the bottom of it, as a hand, on a card that I could not have signed. I find that I knew it better from a stall than I ever did from the registrar's table. I do not say so to be thought humble. It is a finding, and it does not flatter the office."

Cael said nothing to that. He had the fair copy of a leaf inside his coat, against a pair of brass legs; and he had two things under his breastbone that the man in front of him had never been told of, of which one was the man's own.

That was the weight of the morning, and it was all in those two yards. He had entered a wall once, at this man's asking, and had entered more than was asked for. He could not pay it. A thing of that kind could not be told by itself; whoever heard how the eleventh had come would have heard how all of them came, and that was not Cael's to hand over. It belonged to four others who walked in front of the dray, and to a healer.

"You are thinking that I am owed something," said Vastin.

Cael's step did not alter. He had been trained to that by a healer's hand on his chest.

"I've a face, then."

"You have very little. I sat opposite it for three days once, with a plate, and I came away with nothing. But I have had a fortnight of it now from the side, which is another matter." The grey eyes had come down from the hill. "I am not asking. I said in a loft that I know no more of what you are than the plate did, and that I do not ask; and I have not found it harder to keep than a good many orders that I was given. I say only that I have noticed that I am not told. I enter it without complaint. I kept six people's worth of such things for a living."

"It's six people's," said Cael. "That's why."

"I had supposed so. Then it is in better keeping than most of what I was ever owed."

He turned his eyes to the hill again. It was the whole of what passed between them on that road, and Cael found it harder to walk behind than a silence would have been.

The second post stood by the brook, where a plank had been thrown over for people on foot. There was a gate into a field there, hung on dressed stone, and a headland of unploughed grass beyond it.

"That's your field," said Lira. She had dropped back beside him. "You lay in it for a quarter of an hour, and came out looking like a man who's lost a tooth."

"I'd lost an answer."

"It's the same face." She had slipped her left arm out of its becket while she spoke. The box hung from the right shoulder alone, and she let the left hand take the other becket low, at her hip, and carry there. "Thirty. That's her month. I've been slow for the whole of it, as I was told to be."

"And now?"

"Now it's an arm," said Lira. "She said so. I'm only the first to hear."

---

The hedges ended at the foot of the rise, a little before noon, and the road's rule came down on them there like a lid.

It was Seln who put it on. He had been thirty yards ahead of the cobs all the morning. Where the last laid hedge gave out, and the ruts ran up onto open ground, he stood in the road with his right hand flat at his side, palm down, until the whole file had seen it. Nobody needed the words. The lean man spat out his straw. The girl who had talked for three miles about a broker's face shut her mouth on the rest, and every sigil on that road went into a pocket.

Cael felt the twelve of them settle, as hounds settle at a threshold that they know. Wind had his feet already. The read asked, civilly, what the ruts weighed. He went round the house and shut the doors.

"Hill," said the carter, without turning his head. It was the one word that he had spent since the hedge.

The old man got down.

He did it as he had learned to do everything in the last fortnight, by his hands, a little at a time, with the sound foot finding the road first and the stick after it. The case he left on the coils. Nobody offered him an arm, since he would have had to refuse it aloud. But a big man with a box on his shoulder had fallen back to walk on his right, and Cael was on his left, as he had been once before on this very road, going the other way.

It was a mile and more to the top, aslant, with the wind coming over it into their faces.

That was new. It had blown out of the west on every day that they spent upon that moor, and had been at their faces going in; it had gone round in the night, and it was at their faces coming out. Lira looked up at the grey sky with the look that she kept for unfair rulings.

Cael counted the plantings of the stick, since he had nothing else to count. Each of them cost a breath. He had walked behind that sound, and beside it, ever since a ferry's planks, and he knew its two voices: the dull one, when the leg was only tired, and the short one, when it was hurt. This was the dull one. It had been the short one all the way down these same ruts, fourteen days ago, with a case on the shoulder above it that a healer had forbidden.

They came to the top.

The dray had stopped there, to let the cobs blow, and the lean man had a hand on a wet neck. Behind them, a long way below, lay a green plain with hedges on it, and a river in loops; and in the nearest loop a bluff stood up with three walls about it in steps.

Nobody turned. Seln had gone over the crest already. Lira stood beside the near wheel with her back to the west, tightening a becket that did not need it; and Karis had the hide against her chest, and was looking at the cobs. Cael looked at them too. A horse's ears are a good thing to look at.

It was the old man who turned.

He did it on the stick, the whole of him, since the knee would not let him do it by halves. He stood on the crown of the road with the east wind in the skirts of the brown coat and looked back, for the space of three breaths, at the last town on the Line. He had certified it four times under his own seal; and he had left that seal in a tray, a long way south of here, with nothing written to go beside it.

His right hand came off the stick. It went up a little way, toward the breast of the coat, where a pen would have been. Then it stopped, and hung, and took hold of the ash again.

He turned his back on the plain, and held up the hand for the tail-board.

The fork-stone stood at the rise's eastern foot, as tall as Brom, with a worn hand cut into each of its faces. One hand pointed along the ruts by which they had come. The other pointed north, where a second pair went away under the moor's edge toward a line of blue hills.

Those ruts were empty. There had been frost in them and rain since a mule went up, and no print was left of four small hooves or of a square woman's boots. There was only the fold a little way along, with its roofed quarter and its stack of peat, where six people and a healer had sat round a hearth while a term was written in a book.

Brom did not stop. But as he went by the stone he put his left hand up, as far as it would go, which was to his own chest; and he laid two fingers on a thing that rode under the lashing of his box, which was a wooden bowl.

---

After the fork the moor took them, and there was nothing to do for six miles but walk into the wind.

It was a country for people who had made their peace with one colour. The swells went away eastward, brown beyond brown, with the ruts drawn over them like a pair of pencil lines; there was no tree, and no wall, and the only thing that stood higher than a sheep was the dray. The wind came out of the east steadily, with nothing in it but cold. It found the gap between a collar and a neck. It leaned on a box as a hand leans, and by the second mile Cael had the ache of it across both shoulders.

He kept the file, behind two brown boots that hung from a tail-board.

Nobody had spoken since the hedge. He had forgotten in thirteen days how loud that silence was. A town's noise is a blanket; a road's quiet is a room with every door open, and a person who carries twelve things that would each like to answer has the minding of all the doors. The quietest of them would lift at dusk. He could feel where it lay, as a person feels where the cold place is in a bed.

He had gone west along these ruts with ten.

He went over that as he walked, since the road asked for nothing but his feet. There had been a boy on this moor fourteen days ago with a date in him that he had not written down, and a page that he had not touched, and a promise in a book. That boy had been afraid of a gate. He had thought that the worst thing a town could do was to write his name.

It had not written it. It had done a good deal more than that. It had let him hold a lamp for ten days over the oldest paper in the country; and at the end of the ten, a small pale woman had read out, once, what the whole of it had been built against. He had two things more in him than he had carried up this road, and a paper in his coat, and a sentence that he had said aloud by a fire. He was the same weight on the scale. He did not think that he was the same load.

Seln's fist did not go up once in six miles.

That was a thing to be entered too. Eight had laired round a sink on this moor, and five of them had been dragged out of a cut by a hold's people, and three had gone through rotten ice. It seemed that nothing had yet come to learn the road in their place. A wheel could go over that ground now from a river to a town. Five people had seen to that with a rock and a knife, for twenty marks; and an old man had done the rest of it with a knee. There had been thirty of them at the outer gate that morning.

The outcrop came up out of the east a little after the middle of the afternoon: a block of the dark plated stone as big as a barn, a furlong south of the road, with a thread of green going away from its foot.

The carter knew it. He turned his cobs off the ruts without a sign from anybody, as a man turns in at his own gate; and the dray went rocking over the bents toward a basin that somebody had built round with stones.

Karis had quickened her step.

She had not meant to, and Cael saw her check it. There had been a slab at that spring, twelve feet long and waist-high, lying level on its own rubble in the lee of the rock. She had called it a table. She had laid six years of an office's paper out upon it under river pebbles, and nothing had stirred but a corner now and then by the width of a finger.

The wind was in the east today.

It came straight along the outcrop's face, with nothing between. The slab lay as it had lain, grey and level and bare; and a dead stem of bracken went over it while they watched, end over end, and off the far side. There was a row of pebbles yet at one edge, set out by size. Two of the smallest had been blown down into the grass.

Karis looked at it for some time, with the flat under her arm.

Then she walked round the end of the rock, to the western face, which had been the weather side on every other day of that year. It was in shelter now. There was no slab there; there was only wet turf, and a litter of fallen plates, and not a level place the size of a sheet of paper.

She sat down on one of the plates, with her back to the stone, and laid the flat across her knees, and did not undo its straps.

---

They ate cold in the wrong lee, with their backs against the rock, and the carter ate with them.

He had turned his cobs out on the green by the overflow. He took the seventh bowl from Brom as a thing that the road owed him, and he had it empty before the old man had begun; and then he lay down under his own tail-board with his cap over his eyes. Nobody lit anything. It was a furlong off the road, and a person might have spoken there. Nobody did that either, beyond what the bowls wanted. They had six miles of wind in their ears.

Cael went round to the basin with the pot.

Seln was there before him.

He stood at the water's edge with his hands in his sleeves and his back to the slab, looking down into the basin; and it was so exactly where he had stood on the two hundred and eightieth day that Cael's foot stopped of itself. He had been at that slab then, with a row of paper under his hands that counted a sheet short. He had looked up, and found a small grey back turned to him at the water. And when he looked down again there had been a sheet lying before him on the rock, which had not been there, with a registry year at its head and a day.

He had laid that day against the first line of his own file. He had done it twice, a figure at a time.

He had written none of it. He had said none of it, to a healer or a girl or a man with a lamp. In the fifteen days since, a leaf had been read aloud in a gallery which made a good many things look different, and he had not once let himself carry that sheet across to stand beside it. That was for the clean page, if it was for any; and a woman had laid that page down bare, with a bound hand, and had said that nobody was to rule a line.

The grey back had not moved.

Cael knelt at the basin's lip, upwind, and dipped the pot. The water was as cold as a knife. Neither of them said one word. He did not know what that man had made of a date, and he had never asked. It came to him, on his knees in the bents, that he was not the only person at this spring who kept a thing out of his book.

He carried the pot back round the rock.

Karis had not opened the flat. But she had the reader's roll of card out of her coat, with its turn of brown tape; and beside it, on her knee, her own notebook at the fourth line. She sat bent between the two, in what was left of the light, with her bound hand holding the one flat and the pencil going in the other; and her lips were moving.

"Six," she said, under her breath, as he went by. It was not said to him. "The first. The fourth. And the last."

"You said that you'd not guess."

"I am not guessing. I am looking at how sure I am of three." She did not lift her head. "It is another kind of work, and it is the only kind that I am allowed tonight. Go away."

He went, and sat down against the third box, and took out Hesk's book. The brass legs slid from it into his lap, shut, with the cork upon their points. He had carried them for three days and had not once opened them upon a distance. A ruling of his own stood in the way, and it was a hundred and twenty-seven days old. Whatever measured had read one way in four hands and another in his.

*Two hundred and ninety-fifth day. The spring, a furlong south of the border road; nineteen miles and some from the gate. Out at first light by the outer arch: six out, as six in, and none of us written. Sixty yards of four-strand, two marks. A hundred and twenty-eight. The coil rides on the rope-walk's dray, with forty of its kind for the landing. So does Vastin, for a copper a day of his own; he walks the hills. Sixteen of thirty. Lira: thirty. She says that it is an arm.*

*Five piers. I did not look back from the bridge. The promise held to the gate and is done; the not looking was mine.*

*Known: a carter at a rope-walk knew Brom by a thing that a broker said three days' road from there. I have been written down since I was fourteen. It is not the same as being known, and I do not yet know what it weighs.*

*Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

The light was gone before he had ruled the line. Shadow lifted in him, as it lifted at every dusk, and the other eleven dropped away under it like a yard going quiet under snow.

He told it no, and it lay down.

# Chapter 43 — Here

There were sheep on the high moor, and that was the first thing which told Cael that the country had changed while they were under a town.

He saw them at the third mile on the two hundred and ninety-sixth morning: a scatter of grey backs on the swell ahead, with their heads down in the bents and the east wind laying their wool over. At the fourth mile the ruts began to draw in toward a black place in the rock, and a spare grey person stood beside them with a crook.

It was the factor. There was the leather apron, and the slate on its cord, and the pair of pale eyes that had gone along six people and a healer at a gate and counted them. They counted now. They went from a quiet man, who had come up level with the cobs, to four with loads; and they rested last upon the tail-board, where a pair of raw hands lay on an ash stick.

"Five," said the factor. "And the one that walked behind. He's not behind today."

"He's paid for where he's sitting," said Brom.

"I'd not doubt it. He paid my woman a copper to be told where the straw was." The crook went out toward the moor. "Those are mine. I've not had a ewe on this top since the first frost, and I'd three hundred lambed in the yard for want of it. I've had thirty-one carts up that cut in a fortnight, where I told you thirty. # Chapter 44 — A Wall's Count

Teague counted them off the scow, from the window on the rise, as he counted anything that came over that river.

There were six, and there was no mule; and he marked the mule's absence first. A crew that went west with a beast and came home without one had sold it, or eaten it, or lent it; and that crew did not sell what belonged to a healer. So she was off on her own road, with the beast under her, and they had carried three boxes on their backs for as long as she had been gone.

They carried them poorly: the big one had his on the right shoulder, which was the bound one, and neither of his arms came up above the elbow when he shifted it. The small pale one walked with her left hand laid flat against her coat. The girl had both her arms, which was new since the thaw; she used the left as a person uses a tool that has just come back from the smith. The boy with the book was sound, and came last but one, as he always had.

Last of all a stick came off the planks, and a stiff leg after it, and then the old man.

Teague looked at him longer than at the rest.

He had been home from the shoulder hold for a week. He had taken that salt up himself, behind the first carts, when he might have sent it with the bow and saved his own boots; the bow had said so, and had been right. But a cut that had eaten three carts in one cold term had been opened in an afternoon by the eleventh crew on Pike's cloth, and Teague had wished to see the ground.

There had been little to see, since it was a wet road between two banks. He had stood in it for a quarter of an hour, and then he had gone up to the gate, where a grey factor with a crook was counting sacks; and he had asked what was done there, and by whom.

He had been told about a rock, and a knife, and a boy who lit a thing no bigger than a barley grain and walked away from it. He had expected as much, since those five fought as he would have had his own fight, with nothing showing. Then the factor told him the rest.

Eight had come down that road at a run, and had stopped in the air.

He had made the factor say it twice. The old man with the stick had stepped out, and struck the ice once, and there had been a thing across the cut from one bank to the other, as high as the scree. Eight hounds broke on it. It stood for exactly as long as the man never lifted a foot; and when it went, he went down with it, like a tent with the pole drawn.

Teague had never seen an Iron Wall. He had heard of them, as a man who works the holds hears of what is kept behind the walls of towns. They belonged to tribunals, and a person did not come by one in a meadow. Whoever carried such a thing up a border road on a stick had been somebody, a long way from here, and had left off being it; and Teague was not such a fool as to ask what.

He had asked something else, and it was what a captain needed to know. Could the man move?

He could not. That was the whole of it, and Teague had carried it home down a moor, turning it over.

The Force went out along a line and was done. He had been paid for that for the better part of his life, and it had made him first on a sheet of sailcloth: he could put his palm to a thing that six men could not shift, and it shifted. It was a thing for roads: a block came down on a ledge, or a wheel went into a ditch, or a gate would not open; and he arrived, and it was settled, and the wrist kept the account.

A wall did not arrive. It stayed. It could not come to the trouble; the trouble had to be brought to it, and whoever stood behind it had to be worth the bringing. He had seen that much from a factor's telling; the boy with the book had seen it from two feet away, and had gone and stood there.

So a wall wanted a count. It was the first thing that such a tool asked of whoever meant to use it: how many are behind? A captain who did not know that figure before the thing went up would learn it afterward, by who was left outside.

He had it counted before he was off the moor. There were their five, and the old man made six; there were his own three, and himself; and it made ten.

# Chapter 46 — Not Flow

He found her above the weir, at the last of the light, sitting on a stone with her hands in the race.

She had spent on a shoulder and a hand that evening, and had read an arm and a knee; and she sat as she had sat on a road after four holes in a leg, with her head down and her lips moving. He waited until they stopped, while the water came down the cut channel beside her, fast and brown, toward a wheel that had not yet been shut off for the night.

"You've a thing to say," said Oryn, without turning. "You've had it in your boots since you came down that notch, and you walked as if one of them had a stone in it."

"Two things. I wrote that you'd have them from me at this mill, before anything else was said."

"Then say them. I've a loaf to eat before I'm fit for you, and I'll hear it while I eat."

He had the book out, and he did not open it; he had found on a ridge that he did not need to.

"You told me to write it down and stop touching it. On the two hundred and eighty-first day I read myself twice, with yours: once in a field by a brook, and once at a milestone, in the dusk." He kept it level. "I went round the place, and not into it, and I told myself that round wasn't touching. I knew what you'd meant when you said it. I read your words for the leave that I wanted. I'd do it again, and I'm not asking you to say that it was right."

She had taken her hands out of the water, and she dried them, a finger at a time, on the skirt of a dead man's coat.

"What did you get?"

"The same nothing. But I found out what the nothing was. Yours feels what moves; it was all that it ever felt, in you or in me. Where nothing moves it comes back empty, and it would come back empty from a gatepost. I'd been calling that a reading of the place, and it was only the end of the tool."

"I could have told you that."

"You did tell me. You said that you'd never felt such a thing in anybody, and I heard a finding. It was a limit."

She was silent for a little while, with the loaf in her lap, and then she broke it.

"I set a rule on you for a healer's reason," she said. "A person who's found a sore place goes back to it every night with his fingers. You kept my words, and you went round what they were for, as a carter goes round a toll by way of the field; and that's worse than breaking it outright, since it's what clever people do." She chewed. "And then you carried it seventeen days, and came down a ridge to tell me so before I'd laid a hand on you. That's the other half of it, and I'll take the two together. It's entered. What's the second?"

"There are twelve. You had ten at the fork."

Her jaw stopped.

"The eleventh's the old man's. It came in a stair, under the town, while he held a wall for us on that knee, and he doesn't know. I can't tell him how it came without telling him how all of them came, and that's six people's to give."

"That's a debt."

"It's written as one."

"And the twelfth?"

"There's a man under the registry who reads how a made thing stands: which stone bears, and which is carried. He was showing Karis the inside of it, and I was holding his lamp." He drew breath. "It wasn't taken. It turned round in me, as a sheet is turned on a desk. He's been told, since it was his; he said that it wasn't supposed to be possible, and then he poured me a cup of water."

"What does it do?"

"It leans. It'll lean at a table, or a bridge, or a hinge, and I've to stop it looking. It gives one word, and it bills the eyes for an hour afterward. It reads only what somebody made, and he was plain about that. Not a river, and not a tree." He made himself finish. "Not a man."

Oryn had turned on her stone, and she looked at him as she had not yet looked at him that day, the whole of him, from the boots.

"You've a place in you that doesn't move," she said slowly. "And you've been handed a thing that reads what doesn't move."

"Yes."

"Have you turned it there?"

"No. It offered, on the first afternoon, and I said not until you'd heard."

"I've heard." She ate the last of the crust. "I'll tell you what I think, and it's a healer's, as it was before. The man who owns that tool told you what it's not for, and a man generally knows his own tool. That's his word. Here's mine. "After, you're on a road, with your own eyes to spend, and I mend what's brought to me. I don't keep people." She got up, stiffly, with a hand on the stone. "But not on my plank."

"You know how it goes, and I'll say it again, since it's the form. You breathe like a man asleep. She stood over him with her sleeves pushed up and her thick hands hanging. "I'll talk, since it's how I keep my place. And this time I'm not stopping at twenty minutes. "I don't know. I've never gone so far into anybody who wasn't dying." She looked down at him. "May I?"

"Yes."

Her left hand slid in under the back of his neck, broad and warm, and took the weight of his head; and her right came down flat upon his breastbone.

He counted breaths, since it was the only count that she had left him. A man asleep draws twelve to the minute, and he began at one.

It came as it had come once before, on a scrubbed table. It did not go down into him, as his own read went down into the ground; it went along. He felt it leave her right hand somewhere about the thirtieth breath, and set out toward his feet without haste, as a joiner runs a thumb down the edge of a plank to learn whether it is true. It turned where there was no more of him, and came up the other side, and arrived under his neck, and set out again. There was the old hitch in it, a shade late upon his right side; he knew that now for a taped heel, favoured so long that it had got into everything she did.

"That's skin," she said, from a long way off. "That's breath. Those are the three that I taped; they've been leaned on since, and they've forgotten it. That's a heart that's had a hard week."

He let them go by, and did what she had taught him on that table, which was nothing at all: every door in the house left standing as it happened to stand. He had thought it then the hardest thing that he had ever been set; and he had done it since on a road, on every day, with ten of them and then with twelve.

"Now yours."

She went among them slowly, and named each as she came to it by what her hands met; and he lay in the dark behind his eyelids and heard himself told off like a load on a landing.

"That one's quick, and it's hardly got a bank; I know whose it was. That's a deep narrow one. That's the heavy one, the workhorse, down in the soles, and here's something that waits, right through the frame. A hot point, by the left hand. A thing with a lid on it, which I'll not lean on; it's lifted since dusk, and you're keeping it down, and I'll let you, since it's that or have it up." She breathed. "Three lanes, like a ploughed field. A fine thin one that wants a corner to sit in. A short sharp one. And mine."

He was at the hundred and fortieth breath, and Tide lay in him as still as she stood, since it knew its owner.

"That's ten, and I had ten. Now where's the old man's?"

He did not answer, since he was not to.

"There. I'd have walked past it. It doesn't run at all; it stands in the stream like a post, and everything parts for it. That's eleven." Her hand had not shifted on his chest. "And there's a little flat thing behind your eyes, laid by, like a rule folded on a shelf. It's doing nothing. That'll be the reader's."

She was silent for six breaths, and he felt the slow current pass down him once more and return.

"Twelve," she said. "I'll not ask how many more you mean to bring me."

---

"Now the middle," said Oryn.

It was the two hundred and sixth breath. He had been on that plank for seventeen minutes, and she had only just finished with what he carried.

He felt her go to it, as she had gone once before. Her current came up his left side toward the hollow under the breastbone; and where it should have passed through, it parted.

He had not had a picture for that until three days ago, and he had one now. He had stood on a bridge and counted piers so that he need not look at them, and he had seen what a brown river does at a cutwater. It does not stop there, and it does not go in. It divides upon the stone, and runs down either side, and joins again below; and a person on the bank can tell the whole shape of the pier by nothing but the water.

"There," she said, very low. "It's as it was. Everything goes about it."

She did not leave it, this time. She had come as far as this before, and had said what she met, and gone out again; tonight she stayed. He felt her send the slow limping current round it on the left hand, and then on the right, and then, which she had never done, round it from above and from beneath. She went about that still place as a person goes about a house which has every shutter closed, trying each wall in turn with the flat of a hand.

"There's no bank," she said. "It's not a bank that stops me. There's nothing there to push on. I come up to it, and then I'm past."

He drew the two hundred and fortieth breath. That was twenty minutes, and she had said that she would not stop at twenty.

"It's not small. I'd thought that it was small."

She went round, and round again. Her voice had sunk to almost nothing. The heel of her hand on his breastbone eased and settled with each passing, and he lay beneath it and kept every door as it stood.

It was at about the three hundredth breath that the twelfth turned.

He had not bidden it, or so much as thought of it, since he had been counting, and the count was all of him that was awake. It had lain where she said, a little flat thing behind his eyes, folded; for seven days it had leaned at whatever a hand had made, at pegs and rivets and the piers of a bridge, and each time he had given his eyes some other work, and it had come up short.

His eyes were shut now, and there was nothing that he could give them to do.

And something was being drawn in him, under her hands, which had never been drawn before. It was an outline. Her current went round the place on every side, and round again; and the thing behind his eyes came about to that, mildly, as a head comes round in a dark room toward the only sound in it.

It did not look outward. It leaned in.

*No*, he began to say; and he found that he could not say it without saying it with the whole of himself. A refusal was a holding, and she would feel a jaw go hard. She had felt it once, at the hundred and eighth breath, on another table, and had told him that he was helping.

He could not shut that door, and he was not to open it, and so he left it as it stood.

It leaned, for the space of three breaths, at the middle of him.

*Still.*

There was no voice in it; it was only there, as the weight of a stone is there in the hand that lifts it. It was not *nothing*. He knew how the thing said that: flatly, of a red square of tile, or of a table's idle leg. It was not *sits*, which it said of a page. It was a word that he had never had of it before; and he had used that very word himself, in a log, for longer than he had owned the thing which now said it.

Her current made one more round, coming up from beneath, and parted, and joined.

And the lean came down flat, as a foot arrives upon a floor.

*Set.*

His heart struck once, hard, against the heel of a healer's hand.

He let the breath out slowly, like a man asleep, to the count, and he drew the next. He had stood on a river bar with a hound's teeth in the sack on his arm, and had not answered it, and that had been easier than this. He kept his jaw loose, and he kept his two hands lying open on the plank, with the palms upward, and did not let one finger of them close.

*Set*: as a stone is set, or a stake. He knew three places where he had met that word. A thing had come into him in a stair, not as a filling, but as a post is stood in its hole; a small pale woman had found it under her own glove, when she had wished for a name; and it stood in the fourth line of a list on the back of a skin, hung level with the people at a kindling.

There was a next word. He could feel the shape of it, as a tongue feels the gap where a tooth has been.

He did not let it come. He put it down, as he had seen an old man put down a sentence in a gallery, with one hand off the stick; and he lay under a healer's palm and went on with his count.

"Something turned over," said Oryn.

It was a murmur, from very far away.

"Not in the middle. Out at the edge of you. One of yours, in its sleep." Her hand had not moved. "Leave it. It's nothing to me."

He left it. He was at three hundred and eleven, and he had never in his life lain so still.

---

She came out of him at the four hundred and ninetieth breath.

He knew it by her hands: the one on his breastbone lifted first, and then she drew the other from under his neck, a little at a time, and let his head down upon the wood. She stood for a moment with both of them hanging; then she felt behind her for the keg, and sat on it heavily, and put her elbows on her knees.

He had seen her grey, and he had not seen her so. Her face in the lantern was the colour of the ash in a dead ring, and her cropped head hung, and her lips went to fifty, and began again at one.

Lira had come off the bench. She stood at the lean-to's post with a cup of water from the race, and she did not offer it until the second count was done.

"That's forty minutes," said Oryn at last, and drank. "I said that I'd go as deep as I went, and that's it. There's a floor to me, and I've had both feet on it this quarter-hour."

Cael had sat up. The lantern had begun to hurt him a little at the edges, already, and he looked at the turf of the roof instead.

"You'll want it plain," she said. "I've only the one way of giving it."

She set the cup down upon the shelf, among her things.

"There's a place in you I can't read because it isn't *flow*. I've spent my life reading flow. Whatever that is, it's not the kind of thing my Path was built for."

The race ran by in the dark, and nobody said anything.

He had heard her say before that a thing was past her hands. She had said it on a hillside, of a girl's arm; and she had told him afterward that when she said so he was to believe her, and not stand looking at her as though she had kept something back. He believed her. She had gone round that place for the better part of half an hour, at every height, with all that she had, and she had not come out and said that it was nothing. She had said what she was, and where she ended.

It cost her to say it. He saw that, and he thought that it had cost more than the forty minutes. A person who has read every body that was ever laid before her, for thirty rounds of a country, had sat down on a keg in front of a boy and a girl and told them the size of her own tool.

It was a whole finding, and it was hers.

He had another. It lay behind his eyes in two words, and the two findings did not say the same thing. Hers said that there was no flow in that place for a Tide to feel. His said that something stood there which had been put.

He looked at the roof, and kept his own.

It was not the first thing that he had kept back. He had kept a date from everybody, at a spring, and a warning. But he had never before kept a thing from the person whose hands had drawn it out, with those hands hardly cold upon him; and he found that it had a weight which the others had not.

"That's all?" said Lira. She was not asking him.

"It's all that I've a right to. I could dress it for you. I could say that it's a stone, or a knot, or a door; I thought all three on a road north, between one gate and the next, and they're none of them true." Oryn rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "I know what I read, and I know when I'm not reading. I'll not make a guess sound like a finding because a boy's come a long road for one."

"I didn't come for one," said Cael. "I came to hear yours."

"Then you've heard it."

Lira had not moved from the post. She had her chin up, and Cael knew what was coming, since he knew that girl. A thing had been asked for at a fork-stone, in so many words, and she had carried the asking for eighteen days as she would have carried a count.

The paper stayed where it was. Cael had put his hand to his coat when the girl began, and he took it away again.

He was not sorry. A leaf had been read once, in a gallery, by the one person who could read it; and the woman on the keg had spent herself to the floor that night upon the one thing that she had come down a river for. She had drawn a line round her own table. It was the same line that a small pale woman had drawn on a desk with a bound hand: what a person had found went on one page, and what a person had been told went on another.

"Go to bed," said Oryn. "The pair of you. I'm going to sit here until I can stand."

---

His eyes began to pay before he was across the yard.

It came as it had come on a stair under a registry: first a dryness, and then sand, as though somebody had thrown a handful of the road into his face. By the door of the cart-shed the mill's one lantern had a ring round it, like a moon in mist, and he did not look at it twice.

They had been given the cart-shed, with last year's hay thrown down between the wheels. Ten people lay in it, and a big man's breathing went up and down at the far end like a slow saw. He found his place by his hands, and lay down on his back with his arm across his face, and let the water run.

It ran for a long time. Somewhere beyond the hay a broad man turned over on his bad hip, and grunted, and a boy said three words in his sleep which might have been a count. He had not turned the thing; it had turned of itself, and he had not looked at anything, with his eyes or without them. It billed him all the same, to the grain, as a toll-keeper charges a cart that was pushed through his gate by somebody else.

"You're awake," said Lira.

She was a yard from him in the dark, and by her voice she had not lain down.

"Yes."

"You went stiff, on the plank, somewhere past the middle of it. She didn't see; she'd her eyes shut. I hadn't."

He did not answer at once. He could not have told that girl a false thing, and there was no need to; he had only to find the true one that was his to give.

"Something came," he said. "I didn't ask for it, and I've not got a name for it that I'd put my hand to."

"Is it bad?"

"I don't know what it is."

"That's twice now that you've given me a clerk's answer and looked sorry about it." The hay rustled; she had put her back against a wheel. "I'll not dig. I dug at you all the time that we were on a bluff, and all I ever turned up was what you'd have told me in a week if I'd let you alone. You'll tell me when it's a thing."

"On the road."

"If there's road," said Lira, and he heard her lie down.

He listened until her breathing had gone long. She had asked him a thing, and had been given less than the whole of it, and had said that she would wait; and he did not know many people who could do the third.

He could not see to write. He took the book out and wrote by feel, as he had on a moor, with the pencil's butt against his thumb to keep one line from running into the next.

*Two hundred and ninety-eighth day, night. Millrace; the cart-shed. A hundred and twenty-seven. Told Oryn, before she read me: the two readings at the brook and the post, and why; the eleventh; the twelfth. Then she read me, asked first, for forty minutes, as deep as she goes. Her finding is hers. I have it word for word, and I am not copying it here, since she can say it.*

*One thing more came, under her hands, that nobody asked for. I am not writing what. Reasons, in order.*

*One. It was not bidden. She said that nothing was to be turned while she was in. I turned nothing, and it turned. I'll not hand a healer the thing that broke her term and call it a finding.*

*Two. It is one look, by a tool that was never meant to look at a man. A count that one hand has made is a hope, and I have nothing to lay it against.*

*Three. What she found is whole. If I set a second word beside it tonight, it stops being what she found, and becomes half of what two people think.*

*Four is not mine to write.*

*Ruling: hold. Deployment since the Line: thirty-two.*

He shut the book on the pencil.

The fourth lay in him where it had lain for seven days. A man with red eyes had poured a cup of water, under a town, and had said what he was to do if the thing ever read something that ought not to be made, and read it all the same. He was not to tell that man, and he was not to tell anybody. He was only to remember that it had been said.

He had remembered, and it had been easy, for a week, since it had been a sentence about a day that might never come.

It had come at about the three hundredth breath, on a plank, beside a stopped wheel.

He lay with his arm across his eyes, and he did not go back to the place. He had been told once what a person does with a sore spot that he has found, and he kept his hands off it. But the sentence had moved. It no longer stood upright, where he had put it; it had begun to lean, like every row on a sheet that somebody had meant, and he knew which way.

They forded with the first of the light, on the two hundred and ninety-ninth day, and the woman on the flat stone counted them across.

She had a good deal to count, and Seln stood on the far bank, where he had been before anybody else was awake, and did it with her, by the heads.

"Twelve," he said. "And a mule."

There were the six who had come down a border road: a quiet man, and a girl, and a big one whose arms came up as far as his shoulders that morning, and a small pale woman, and a boy with a book, and an old man on a stick. There were four from the house on the rise. A captain had his right wrist wrapped in linen under the leather. A tall man carried his spear upright, and a broad one eased his weight onto the right leg in the water; and a boy went over last, with his eyes on a girl's feet.

There was a lean old woman with a staff and a board, who was on nobody's count and had said so.

And there was the healer. She came down to the ford leading the brown mule on its rope, with her one pannier across the withers and a rolled strip of canvas; and she stopped at the water's edge and looked at Brom.

"She'll carry three," said Oryn. "She's carried them before. Put them on the low side first."

Brom lifted the boxes himself. He did it one at a time, slowly, with both hands, to the height of that brown back, and he looked at his own two arms after each as a man looks at a pair of tools that he had given up for lost. The mule stood for it with her ears back. When the third was on, with the flat strapped over all and a sack of the mill's meal for a counterweight, he laid his palm on her neck for the space of a breath; and it was the first time in twelve days that he had been able to reach it.

"That's a healer's road and a researcher's whole archive on one beast," he said. "I'd not care to be the mule."

"She's had worse company," said Oryn.

"Twenty," said the old man, to nobody.

"Of thirty," said Cael.

"I am obliged." Vastin considered the far bank, and the track that climbed away from it toward a notch. "I am informed that I am to walk a stride behind a man who is slow to answer. I find it the first posting in some years which I understand."

Karis had not crossed.

She was on the near bank, kneeling at the flat stone that the wheels aimed for, with the calfskin spread on it under four pebbles; and she had pinned a clean sheet to the skin's eastern edge with two thorns out of the hedge. The Ternhall brass lay along the pencil line, through four rings, and ran on over the join.

"I have made the office's figure again," she said, when he came back through the water to her. "By daylight, with nobody talking, and it is what it was." She held out her right hand. "I shall want them once more."

He gave her the brass legs, shut, with the cork on. She opened them upon the hall's ring and the fourth, as she had done at a table; she closed them by a hair, and swung one point round along the strip, and it came down on the clean paper. She pressed, and it left a prick in the sheet no bigger than a grain of sand.

Then she took the pencil in her left hand, the bound one, in its new linen, and drew.

She began in the middle of the fourth ring, where the carter's skin ended, and the line went over the join of hide and paper without a tremor. It ran along the brass's edge to the prick, and it did not stop there. She carried it on, a hand's breadth beyond, to where the sheet gave out, and lifted the pencil only at the paper's edge.

"You've gone past it," said Cael.

"I do not know that it ends at the point. I know that an office measured as far as that, and wrote a figure. I am not going to draw a stop that the paper does not give me." She blew the lead off the sheet. "It is a line. It goes where it goes."

She gave him back the dividers, and he put them into the book.

They went up out of the green valley in the file that had been set under an ash, with a mule and two healers in the middle of it. At the notch's head the ruts divided. Teague did not look along the pair that went back to a plank and a cloth. He lifted two fingers, and the tall man turned his spear the other way.

Cael walked at a captain's shoulder, a stride back.

He had a healer's finding in him, word for word, and it was whole; he had two words of his own behind his eyes, which did not agree with it, and which he had told to nobody. He had a sentence from under a town that had begun to lean. Ahead of him a quiet man went small into the pale grass, along a line that a woman had ruled past the place where the figures stopped.

It ran east, off the edge of everything that she had. He went where it went.

## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of
other named characters, not a true multi-POV structure. If you want to signal "feels like
Iron Prince," don't build alternating-chapter dual POV — keep the lead's POV overwhelming
and let other characters get glimpses, not chapters.

## 8. Supporting craft signals (secondary, use to taste)

- **Filler-word discipline:** "that" runs hot (92.1 per 10k words) — a known, common genre
  tic, not a target to imitate; if anything, tighten below this. Other fillers ("just,"
  "almost," "felt," "seemed") sit in the 5–25-per-10k band — moderate, not aggressively
  self-edited, not sloppy either.
- **Adverb density:** ~161 -ly adverbs per 10k words — fairly high; this is a book that
  lets adverbs carry tone rather than insisting on pure show-don't-tell. Not a craft flaw
  to correct, a genre-register signal to optionally match.
- **Dialogue-tag (reporting-verb) density:** ~41 per 10k words — moderate dialogue presence,
  consistent with an action/interiority-forward book rather than a dialogue-driven one.
- **Combat-vocabulary density:** ~22 per 10k words (strike/block/dodge/blade/impact/etc.) —
  present throughout, not just in set-piece fight chapters.
- **Readability:** Flesch Reading Ease 72.3, Flesch-Kincaid grade 6.8 — accessible YA/adult
  crossover register, short-to-medium sentence construction, low jargon-density outside the
  progression-vocabulary spikes.
- **Repetition discipline:** common connective 3-grams ("out of the," "one of the," "for a
  moment") sit at 2–4 per 10k words — normal English-prose background rate, not a
  distinctive tic to replicate. Don't chase these; they're noise, not signature.

# Chapter 60 — Alignment

Karis asked for the room before she had taken off her coat.

The inn's keeper was a slow heavy man with a cloth over his shoulder, who had drawn their jugs for a hundred and twenty days and had never been asked for anything else. She told him what she wanted: a dry floor, and a door that shut, and a table that nobody ate at. It was for paper, she said. She would have it until the thaw.

"There's the store behind the chimney," he said. "It's warm; it has the back of that fire for a wall. I keep meal in it." He looked at the three boxes, which Brom and Seln had brought in out of the snow. "Six, for the cold term. And you may lie at this end of the hearth for the standing-money, the five of you, as long as there's paper in my store."

She took the purse out of her coat. Cael had seen that woman pay for a cart, and a boot, and a kid; each time she had opened her notebook first, and done the sum, and done it again beneath. She did not open it now. She put her fingers into the neck of the bag and laid six marks on the board, one upon another, with her eyes on the door behind the chimney.

"Ninety-six in hand," said Brom, who had done it for her.

"I know what it is. I did not need to count."

It was a narrow room with one small window, and it smelled of barley. There was a table in it of scrubbed deal. The keeper moved his meal-sacks to one end, and by the time the hearth had been banked for the night she had made the other end her own.

Brom set the kit on the table. Its case lay there in tarred canvas, with the strap coiled on the lid; inside were a cord knotted at the fives, stake thirty-two with the stroke and the bar and a bright turned point, and a mallet. The stake had never been driven. Beside it Karis put a dead man's ledger, shut. The three boxes stood against the warm wall, and each of them had one leaf in it that was the same as its fellows.

Then she took out the sheets.

She laid them in three heaps along the deal, and did not try to make their edges agree; the Fallow's were long strips, and the Stair's were leaves, and the hall's were whatever a person could tear from a roll with her teeth. There were eleven in the first heap. There were twenty-nine in the second, and twelve in the third.

"Fifty-two," said Lira, from the door. She had counted them as they went down.

"Fifty-two sheets. Forty-one kinds, at the two that I could do properly. Three perimeters of one size, and a fourth that is twice it. I had three boxes of other people's stone when I crossed the Line. I should not have said that there was anything east of it to put in them."

She set her lamp in the middle, and lit it with a point, and unrolled the hide.

Cael did not go to the hearth with the others. He sat down at the meal-sack end of that table, with Hesk's book, while she worked.

*Hundred and twenty-ninth day, night. A back room with a door on it, in the lower inn at Lowmarch. First snow. Ninety-six in hand. Nine lines in Pike's book, and no number against the season. Eleventh on the cloth. Deployment since the Line: twenty-seven.*

He ruled a line beneath it. What came next was the inventory, which he made at the start of anything: every fragment that he carried, in the order of its coming, with the Path that it stood beside and the person who had given it. He had last made one in a loft, on the night that he was nineteen. This was the first that he had ever written under a roof which he meant to come back to, and it was to stand as the count for the whole of a season past the Line; so he wrote it slowly, and left nothing out.

*Ten confirmed fragments.*

*Wind-adjacent — Lira. Pressure-adjacent — the Bronze from Ardenmere. Iron-adjacent — Brom. Compression-adjacent — Reydan. Ember-adjacent — Karis. Shadow-adjacent — Seln; source protected; the seal held past the Line as it held inside, and was used twice out here where no one who could carry it west could see it, and that is the seal's whole logic and it is unchanged. Storm-adjacent — Daeva. Anchor-adjacent — unnamed; the hand with no face; opened a door on a rim and held a floor in a hall, and I own a piece of the craft the system uses to hold people and have used it twice this year, once each way. Blade-adjacent — Ephram. Tide-adjacent — Oryn; partial; Iron; self and contact; the tenth, and the first that does nothing in a fight, and the only one whose engagement field says clinical, and the only one with a rule on it in her hand and mine: ask first, every time. Deployment notes current on all ten.*

*One anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent. Not an entry any more; a location. The still place. Read three times. Doesn't flow. Doesn't change. Everything goes around it. Not the Quiet. Not a fragment. Older than the log. Observation continues.*

*Reydan will get his answer. Daeva will get her rematch. Both are going to need a map.*

He shut the book on it. On the far side of the lamp a strip of brass was going down a calfskin, a hand's length at a time.

The last ferry crossed at noon on the hundred and thirtieth day, with two fingers of snow on its rail.

The ferryman had been at the shelf since first light with a pole and an axe, to cut her a lane. Half the meadow stood on the landing to see it. The scow went out along its cable slowly, shouldering the broken ice, with the gelding walking his round in the shed and the boy calling the turns. On the far bank a man in a hat with one side cut away stood waiting beside a mule.

It was a quarter of an hour before the boat came home again. It brought a wet canvas bag, and the bag went up the bank to the plank under the eave, where Pike had set out his book for the last time before the thaw.

He sorted it aloud. He had done so with every bag that Cael had seen come over, each piece given out twice, so that people who could not read should know what was theirs.

"For the smith; from his brother, by the hand. For the smith." A folded sheet went along the oak. "For the house on the rise, a bill; they'll not want it said twice, and I'm saying it twice. For the landing: two, under his own seal, and I've not looked at them. For the landing, two." The broker's clerk took those. He did not meet anybody's eye. "For the woman with the hides, from a tanner. For the hold at the top of the north water. For Teague's old woman, from her daughter, and it's the first that ever came for her by this boat. For Thornwater, to go up tomorrow with the carter."

That made eight, and there was one thing left in the bag. Pike drew it out with his single hand, and looked at it, and looked at it again.

It was long, and narrow, and wrapped in oilcloth, with a knot on it that Cael would have known in the dark.

"For the boy with the book," said Pike. "From a town I never heard of. For the boy with the book. That's nine."

He held it across the plank. Cael took it, and pulled the one end of cord that would undo that knot, and the oilcloth fell open on the oak.

There was a strip of brass in it. It was a hand longer than the one from Ternhall, and a little broader, and it had been rubbed with something so that the snow-light ran along its edge like water. Folded round it was a leaf from an order-pad, with a few lines on it in a square upright hand.

*Nineteen. I'm told it's late; the ferry's fault, not mine. Your grandmother said a man who catalogues everything eventually needs to draw a line through it. This one's true to a hair. Mind the shape. — H.*

He read it standing in the snow.

An old man in Denvash had cut that leaf from his pad a long while ago, and had rolled a ruler in it for a boy who was going where there were no instruments. It had lain in a bag on the wrong side of a river for a month and more. And in that time the boy had walked off the end of another person's brass for three days and come to a marsh, and had stood at a fence of iron with a line going through it. He had not written one word of that west. The old man could not have known that there was a line. He had only known the boy.

"He says that it is true to a hair," said Karis, at his elbow. "I should like to see it against mine."

"Tonight. I've two letters to write, and that boat's not going to wait for me."

He wrote them at the end of Pike's plank, on two leaves torn out of Hesk's own book, with the horn ink-pot and a borrowed pen. He had owed both for a long while, and he had been waiting for a thing to say; and he had not expected, when it came, that it would go into so few words.

*Nineteen, past the Line, and there's a roof. It's an inn. The shape you said to mind is a line, and I've got the tool for it now. — C.*

The second took him longer, since it was to a woman who had kept a ledger with his name in it, in a yard, and had never once shown it to a seat.

*Vell. There's a second ledger on the continent with my name in it, kept by a one-armed man in a town on no map, and this week he stopped numbering me. He said his book was wrong about us by being too small. You said once that somebody should have a record that doesn't answer to the Compact. There are two now. The session still stands. — C.*

There was a third that he owed, to a friend on a bluff who lost to him every time he ran it. He had no line for that one yet. It would go by the first boat of the thaw, and he wrote in his own book that it was to.

He folded the two, and put no place on either, and gave them to the ferryman's boy with a copper apiece. The clerk's packet went into the same bag. Seln watched it go down the bank.

"It will say that a crew kept what it was sent for," he said. "It cannot say why, since he does not know. I find that I can bear that."

The scow went over once more, and came back empty. Then the ferryman and his boy took her lines in. They drew the hauling rope off the drum, fathom by fathom, stiff with ice, and coiled it in the shed; and the gelding was led away up the bank to the smith's stable with his blanket on.

"That's her shut," said Brom.

The snow was coming down on the landing, and on the bare cable, and on the far bank, where there was no longer anybody at all.

It had been the hundred and twenty-second morning, eight days before, when Oryn left five people standing at a heap of chock-stones and took the left-hand road.

She did not turn her head at the notch. She had left a good many gates in thirty rounds, and had found out in the first of them that a healer who looks back has only doubled what she is carrying.

The jenny went as she had always gone, which was badly. She led as a gate swings. For the whole of the first morning Oryn's right hand kept going out to a small brown cheek that was two days behind her on another road; it met a grey ear like an oar, and came back. She let it.

She did her sum as she climbed, since she did it every morning, and it came out as she had given it to five people at a heap of stones. There was a bench at the mill that she had left a day short, and two gates beyond it that had not seen her at all. There was the ford with the oxen. Beyond the ford was the gate that had given her the coat, and beyond that the child.

She had sent word for the child. No word had come back to the mill before she left it, which meant that the breathing had not changed, or that the carter had been slow. She could not tell which. She put it with the things that could only be carried, where it did not get into her hands.

Then she let herself go over the other, since she had four days and nobody to see her do it.

She began with what her hands had, as she had been taught. They were full again. She had mended nobody for three days, and she could feel the purse of her reserve sitting heavy and whole, as it had not sat since a mill. They had left a shoulder in the keeping of a woman who knew more about holding than she did, and she had a drover's turn to show for it, which she could lay now herself.

It was the other way about that she had no practice in. He had been in *her*.

She had felt him go along. She would have known that step anywhere now, quick and then dragging, like a person walking a road with a stone in his boot; and while it went, his voice had told her what the same step had found in himself.

She had said the only true thing that she had. In eleven years she had put her hands into more bodies than she could number, the hurt and the whole and the dying, and every one of them had been all current, fast or slow, standing behind a break or running free. She had never met a still thing in a living person. The dying went still all at once, and everywhere. This was neither of those.

She did not ask herself what it was. That belonged to a small pale woman with three boxes, and Oryn had no box. She had given him a healer's sentence about it, and she would have said the same to a carter with a scab.

There was a thing that she had said at the marsh's edge, and she looked at that last.

*So are you.* It had come out of her crooked, and she had heard it come. She went over it now, strictly, with her knuckles ready. He was on the route. That was the whole of what the words said, and they were true of a miller's man and an old woman on a hurdle. She had gone into a dish of water after a shoulder, since she was nearest. He had gone into a hole after her. Neither of those had anything to say to a morning on a plank at Thornwater, when a boy lay still and let her spend.

They were two leaves. She would not have the one laid over the other, by him or by herself. She had not forgiven him. The question stood where it had stood, and it had got company.

---

The gap took the four days that a carter's skin had given it.

It was a high bare way between two hills, with nothing on it but frost and a few sheep that belonged to nobody, and she slept each night with her back against the jenny's belly and her feet in her pannier. On the fourth afternoon the ground began to fall. There was a valley under her with a pale road in it, going east and west, and she knew that road; she had gone round a ring of stones by it, thirty times, with a mule.

She came down to Thornwater's eastern gate at dusk on the hundred and twenty-fifth day. The bar was drawn for her before she had called.

"It's yours when you're here," said the steading's woman. She had a lantern, and she looked the healer over with it as she would have looked over a cart that was late home. "You're thin. You've been looked for."

"By the bench?"

"By the bench. And by one that walked."

She led the jenny in under the gate-walk. A lean man stood up there with a long bow at the length of his arm, and he lifted two fingers from the stave. She had never known him do that for her. She understood, crossing the yard, that it was not for her: it was for a girl with one arm, who had sat on those planks beside him and shot at a sack, and he was asking after her in the only way that he had.

"Her arm's whole," said Oryn, upward. "It held a stair, which it shouldn't have. It's held nothing since."

The two fingers lay down again on the wood.

There was no brown mule at the trough. She had known that there would not be, and she looked for it all the same, and was annoyed with herself. The back room had been swept. Her strip of canvas lay along the sill as she had left it, and somebody had put a cold fowl on the plank under a cloth.

There were seven of them under the back room's window in the morning, in the frost, and the first was sitting bolt upright on the bench's end with a stick across her knees.

She was a very small old woman. Oryn had mended that knee at a mill, thirteen days before, and had seen its owner go away up a valley on a hurdle between two grandsons. She came over the low place every year before the snow, to her daughter's hearth in this long house; and Oryn had told her, as the hurdle was lifted, that she would see the knee here, after the thaw.

"I said after the thaw," said Oryn.

"You said it to a woman lying on a hurdle. I never agreed to it." The old woman rapped the knee with her knuckles. "I rested it ten days, as I was bid. Then I came over to my daughter's, as I do, and I came on my own two legs, to find out whether the thing was yours or mine. It's a day and a half. I did it in two. I'd one of the boys with me to carry the bread."

"On a stick."

"I never leaned on it. I brought it to hit him with."

Oryn got down on her heels in the frost, and put both hands round the joint. Her lips went to three. It was all there: the old banks, worn as smooth as a doorstep, and the current going through the place where a ladder had been as though no ladder had ever stood in it. She had promised that knee a look after the thaw, and it had walked over a hill to be looked at before the snow.

"It's held," she said.

"I know that it's held. I've just walked a hill on it. I came to have you say so."

That was one of seven. She did the rest as she always did them, and two of them took a mending apiece, and at the end of it she sat on her keg with her head down for a count of fifty.

The word came on the second day, with the last of the grain carts.

It was a long stooped carter whom she knew, fetching for the gates up the water, and he had been as far as the ford with the oxen and a gate beyond. He gave it to her in the yard, as he had been given it, in the order of the road. The mill had had nothing worse than a crushed thumb. The two gates above it had buried nobody. At the ford a man had lost a finger to a chain and kept the hand; they had bound it themselves, badly, and it had not gone bad. And at the gate where the little one was, the breathing was no different. She coughed at night, and slept after.

Oryn heard the whole of it standing.

Three weeks, then, and seven gates, and nobody dead of it. She did not take that for a reason. A person who has crossed thin ice once has not learned that ice will bear; she has been lucky, and knows it by the cold in her stomach afterward. But the round was whole. Every gate on it was where she had left it, with everybody in it that she had left there, and waiting.

She owed that bench two days more. Then she would go north by the wicket.

---

It was the steading's woman who told her about the carter, on the evening of the hundred and twenty-eighth, with the sky gone the colour of a slate.

"He goes down to the board at first light, with my bark. He'll not come up again this side of the thaw, if that does what it looks like." The woman jerked her chin at the north. "If you've anything to send to that crew of yours, it's tonight."

"They're not mine."

"No. You've said." She set a strip of clean bark down on the scrubbed plank, and a pot of the hold's ink, and a pen cut from a goose quill. "There it is, all the same."

Oryn looked at it after the door had shut.

She did not keep a book. She had said so to the pale one across a fire: a healer who needs a page to know who is owed has stopped looking at them. She had never in thirty rounds set down a word about any person whom she had had her hands in. But those five were two days south, with a saddle between that would be shut by morning, and she could not look at them. There was a girl down there with an arm in a knot of hers, who had been told *first snow*; and by the feel of that sky she would be looking at it out of a window tomorrow.

She could not read an arm from here. She had said as much in a yard, of a nearer gate than this.

So the arm would wait until she could have it between her two hands, and that would not be before the thaw. The girl knew who was to say when. There was nothing in that to write.

What was there, then? She went at it as she went along a bench, a person at a time, and told each the truth in a sentence.

The boy would be carrying her round. He had been told at a bar of iron that he was to have it and do nothing with it, and he had never learned to do nothing with a thing. He should hear how it stood.

The small one had been in a loft when Oryn said that an old woman's knee did not care what was under a floor. She was a person who liked to know how a thing came out.

And there was what a round of three weeks had cost, which was the sum that all five of them would have been doing since a fork. It was not a kindness to tell them. It was a finding, and she gave people their findings.

That left what she wanted, and she looked squarely at it, as she had on a road out of Oxhollow and under a cold stone. There was a calfskin with a strip of brass on it that pointed somewhere. Wherever it pointed, there would be an edge; and at the edge, somebody ought to be standing on the right side with her sleeves pushed up. She had said it in a loft, and she had been shown twice since what it was worth. And he was on the route, which meant one thing and no more.

She went over what she was leaving out, as she would have gone over a bench at the end of a day, to be sure that nobody had been missed.

There would be nothing in it about a plank. That stood where she had put it, and a strip of bark was no place to move it from. There would be nothing about a still thing under a boy's breastbone; she had told him to leave it alone, and she would not be the one who put a finger on it from two days off. She would not ask after a mule. And she would not tell five people that she had been glad of their bowl, since she had eaten out of it standing, on purpose, for nine days, and they had every one of them seen her do it.

She dipped the pen. She wrote slowly, in a hand that had not been used since a healing house's morning list, and she did not write one word that she would not have said aloud at a gate.

It came to four lines, and a letter for her name.

*Route's whole. The knee held. Nobody died of three weeks. After the thaw: bring the map, and lie still when I say. — O.*

---

The carter came over the saddle on the hundred and thirty-first morning, with a hand's depth of snow on the road and his eyebrows white, and he had a strip of bark for Pike and another that was not for Pike at all.

"From the healer," he said, at the inn's door. "She was writing. I never saw her write. I'm to say that it's for the five of you and that there's no answer wanted."

Cael took it to the hearth. There were not a dozen people in that long room so early. Brom was laying out the bowls at their end of it, as he had at every halt, and Lira sat on the hearthstone with her right hand full of kindling. Karis had come out of the room behind the chimney with the hide under her arm, and Seln stood at the far end of the fire, by the small window that looked down on the landing.

It was a hand that Cael had never seen. It was square, and stiff, and it pressed, like a person lifting something heavy. He read the four lines out, as they stood, a stop at a time: the route, and a knee, and what three weeks had cost; and then the thaw, and a map, and what he was to do when he was told.

Brom had six bowls in a row on the board. He looked along them. He had put the last one down a little apart, as he always did; and he did not take it back.

"Six, by letter."

It was Lira who said it. She had turned on the hearthstone, and her eyes had gone past him to that window, where the snow was lying a hand deep on the sill. Then they came down to the grey wool across her own breast, and to a knot that was not hers.

"She'll say when," she said.

"After the thaw," said Brom.

Karis had the hide unrolled on the board by then, between the bowls, with a pebble on each corner. There were four rings on it. The fourth lay at the very edge of the skin, where the carter had never had a wheel; and through the middles of the other three a line was ruled in pencil, very fine, which ran on through the fourth and off the hide.

She laid the Ternhall brass along that line, on the three centres that she had paced, and took her hands away.

"Yours," she said. "From the other end."

He had the strip from Denvash in his coat. It was cold from the back room, and it took the firelight all along one edge. He set it down on the skin beyond hers, with its end on the ring by the marsh, and slid it up until the two lay in one length; and he bent, and looked for daylight.

There was none. The edge lay on the dot at the hall, and on the dot at the Stair, where hers met it; he moved it along, and it lay on the dot at the Fallow. It touched the three for him exactly as hers had touched them for her.

*The sites were on an alignment. Not random.*

He did not say it. It was Karis who spoke, with her finger on the place where the pencil left the skin.

"Someone made this pattern."

She had not written it. She said it once, to the board, in the small exact voice; and nobody at that fire answered her. Cael counted four seconds by the tick of the wood. Lira put a stick on. Brom was looking at the sixth bowl.

"I have ten things that aren't a Path," Cael said, eventually, to nobody in particular, "no tier, no rank, no file I can see — and five people, one of them on a route, who've walked into the quiet three times and come out." He turned it over once and let it stand. "And a line on a map that somebody drew before there was a registry to draw one."

Lira, one-armed, feeding the fire: "Then we follow it."

Brom: "After the thaw."

Karis, writing: "Noted."

Down the fire, the man who used to file reports about him watched the snow on the ferry landing with evident professional approval and said nothing, which — from him — was home.

Nothing out here had read him. At three floors older than any registry, every Path in the party had stopped at a line and his had not; a fourth had been paced from outside and not crossed. Nobody alive — least of all him — knew why. Someone had made the pattern. That was as far as anyone could say.

## Source-reuse gate tightened to 8 words (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

The 10-word gate let distinctive source phrasing through in 6–9-word fragments (the first
movement's opening line, for example, echoed the current edition's). Measured at 8 words, movements
written after authors began self-checking run 1–6 shared runs per 10k; the worst ran ~30. From now
on `ed.sh overlap` and every editorial review use runs of **8 or more** words. Every listed run that
BOOK_MAP does not protect is rewritten — re-composed, not a synonym swapped. Names, Path names,
ranks and plain facts of canon will sometimes share a short run; re-compose the sentence around them.
Movements closed under the 10-word gate get one book-level 8-word sweep at book completion.

## Draft from your own event list, not from the source page (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

Book 3 Movement 6's review found the retold scenes in ch42–44 to be close paraphrase: 23–32% of
sentences followed a source sentence word for word in order, with words varied enough to pass the
8-word gate. Everything the author invented was clean. The cause is drafting with the source open.

1. Read the source chapters the packet names, once, for what happens and who these people are.

3. Draft from that list and the book map. Do not reopen the source chapters while drafting.
   Choose your own entry point into each scene and your own order of beats; the source's scene
   shape is not canon, only its events are.
4. Only protected wording (BOOK_MAP) is copied, exactly, from the map — not from the source page.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.

  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the

  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”

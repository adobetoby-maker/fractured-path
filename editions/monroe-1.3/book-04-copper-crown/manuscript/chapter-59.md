# Chapter 59 — The Posting-House

The unit's last session came round on the next Third-day: sixth bell, the long room that had been a map store. Six of the nine came.

Examinations had claimed the other three. One of the third-years had left a note on the slate's ledge in a large apologetic hand, and Seln read it, folded it once, and put it in his pocket without remark. Then he chalked up the evening's work.

It was positional work, the same kind the unit had done all term: three short exercises, each a room drawn on a sheet with its doors and windows and one lamp, and a person to be placed in it. *Here is somebody who does not wish to be noticed. Where does he stand, and why does the room let him?* They worked in silence. The light went orange in the three tall windows and then grey.

Seln walked the table. Behind a Stone second-year he paused, and laid one finger on her sheet next to the drawn lamp.

"He's standing in its light," he said. "Move him to its shadow side."

Nine words. The girl went red to the ears and moved her figure. Further down, a Blade second-year had put his man beside the door, and Seln stopped there too.

"Everybody looks at a door when it opens. Not beside it."

Nine again. Cael counted them because he could not help counting things, and then wished he had not, because counting was attention and attention in that room had always had to be spent with care.

Gwen had placed her figure behind a curtain. Seln looked at it for a breath, and said nothing, and walked on. Gwen took this as the highest praise she had ever received and sat glowing for the rest of the exercise.

Then the closing exercise. Seln wrote it on the slate in one line and stepped back from it.

*Pick a room and describe where its attention goes.*

Cael chose the records hall's stone hall at the second bell, because he knew it best, and he could see the whole of it with his eyes shut: the counter and its chained pen, the door from the covered walk and the light that came through it, the three places a man could stand and be furniture. He could have written a page that would have been the best thing handed in that evening, and he knew it as he picked up the pencil.

He wrote a page that was about a fifth worse.

He left out the light from the covered-walk door, and the way the counter clerk's head turned toward the stair before anybody had come down it. He left in what a careful second-year would have seen. It was a good page. It was not his page. Signed, it went into the pile with the rest, and he kept his eyes off the man who collected it.

Seln gathered the sheets and knocked them square on the table, and marked the wing's ledger twice, and spoke to the room in the same flat voice he had used every Third-day since the first one.

"The unit runs again next term. Sign at the residence board when term opens. Anyone going on into assessment work will find the second harder and less interesting, which is the correct order."

Gwen caught Cael up at the door with her green scarf half wound. "Are you signing for the second one? I am. My aunt says the second of anything is where you find out if you actually like it." She looked at him with frank concern. "You didn't do very well tonight, I don't think. I saw your page. You left out the door."

"I was tired."

"I thought so. I'll draw you an eye," said Gwen kindly, and did, on the back of his hand, with her pencil, before he could stop her. Then she went off down the passage toward the Current hall, talking to a second-year about her aunt.

He went out with the others without a glance back into the room, and did not stop at the counter on his way past it. He walked out under the arch and along the covered walk at the pace of somebody going to supper. He was halfway down it before he had to stop and put his hand flat against one of the pillars, and stand there for a little while like a man with a stone in his boot.

The pillar was cold, and the little pencil eye on the back of his hand looked up at him.

In the Log that night he kept it as brief as he could make himself.

*Last session of the unit. Every Third-day since the week of the records hall. Position, taught at eight feet by a Shadow practitioner, off a syllabus with Gault's initials on it and an attendance book bound for Bracken's shelves, and taught precisely as nine ordinary second-years would have been taught it. Anything else would have shown.*

*I've never been handed anything bigger, by anyone, and there isn't a page anywhere that says so. There won't be. If there were, it would finish him.*

*Filed under debts, no current mechanism of payment. Second entry. Same file. Same nothing.*

---

Eight days before that last session, on the evening of the twenty-fourth of Reaping, the delegation was still on the road home. Where the registry road left the river and began to climb into the hills, there was a posting-house with a slate roof and a yard behind it, and the delegation's carriages pulled into the yard on the second evening out from Halcenvane, on the hour the clerks had set for them on the coast. The clerks who built a delegation's road knew their work. They put a spare hour into every stage for the things that go wrong, and on this road nothing had.

Three rooms had been kept upstairs for the senior officers. The counsel looked into all three before she chose. The Archmarshal, Vastin, who had led the inspection at Halcenvane, tried a single door, the furthest at the back. A table stood under its window. That settled it. Neither of the others had a table.

A narrow bed under the slope of the roof; a washstand with a cracked jug; one chair; and the table, scrubbed nearly white, standing a little out of true, so that a pencil set down on it began after a while to travel toward the wall. A folded card, pushed under the short leg, cured it. From the window he could see the length of the yard: the stable lamps coming on one after another, a boy sluicing the cobbles, an ostler walking a lame grey up and down to watch which foot it favoured.

He went down to supper first, because the counsel expected it and because a man who eats alone in his room on an inspection road is a man whom clerks discuss.

They had the long table nearest the hearth. The counsel talked, as she had talked the whole road from the coast, about inns: this one's mutton, which was good, and its beds, which had been aired. Then she told of a magistrate she had once watched eat a whole game pie at this very table so that he would not have to answer a question put to him between courses. The records officer handled his fork like an instrument under calibration. The assessor ate a little bread and went up before the pudding. Vastin asked the counsel what the question had been, which pleased her a great deal, and listened to the answer, and did not say one word about Halcenvane.

Work was not for public rooms; it never had been, with him. Whatever is said across a posting-house table is said, in a month, by the man who sat at the next one. And talk was bad for a thought that had not set. A man said a thing aloud and heard how well it sounded, and from then on he was defending the sound.

At the stair's foot he wished the counsel goodnight. Upstairs, the candle gave its flame to the lamp, and his coat went on the nail behind the door.

He laid the brass watch on the table, and beside it, in a row, four pencils of four colours, though he did not expect to use them. Then the file in its three tapes: grey round the record, red round what had been tendered in it, string round the rest. Last of all, from the inside pocket of the coat on the nail, the small calf-bound notebook.

That he put face down under the watch, and left there.

Because he wanted the notebook, it would wait. Whatever was in it had been written by a man who had been in the room, in the minute, with the thing still ringing; and a man who reads his own minute-notes before he reads the record will read the whole record through them. He would come to the notebook last, when the record had had its chance to disagree with it.

Below the window the ostler had decided about the grey and taken it in. The yard was empty under its lamps. Somewhere past the stables a dog began to bark and a woman's voice told it, without heat, to stop, and it stopped.

He untied the grey tape.

---

Topmost lay the counsel's findings. She had finished them on the road and given them to him at the last change of horses with the air of a woman handing over a cake she knew had risen. They said the clause stood and had never been repealed; that the enrollment conformed to its words; that the house's documentary practice was complete, the highest word the form allowed; and that the fire-watch's oil book merited a clerical remark, as, the counsel had told him with satisfaction, it had merited one the last time.

They were correct, and he read them once and set them to one side. They answered every question the notice had asked, and none of the questions he had brought up the bluff in his own head. He had said as much to the boy's face, across a whitewashed table, and meant it.

Beneath them, in the wing clerk's hand, lay the evaluation record; that he read entire.

He read it from the first line, where the clerk had set down the room and the hour and every name at the table and along the wall, to the last, where the clerk had pressed the stamp and written *stamped* beside it as though the stamp might not be believed. Every trial. Every figure. Every interval asked for and the time it was asked. The fault, the void, the reserve frame, the re-run, the cold board. The note, as Gault had read it into the record.

He had sat through every minute of that morning on the fourth chair along the east wall. He read it anyway, every line, for this reason: in forty years he had never once found the two to be identical. It was in the gap between them that he had learned most of what he knew.

There was a gap tonight, as there always was.

The record had the interval. *At the enrollee's request: fatigue affecting response quality.* The clerk had set the minute beside it, and then, in a smaller hand, squeezed in under the line, three words of his own: *before instruments indicated*. No one had asked him to. He had seen that the boy asked before any needle had a reason to move, and had decided that a record which left it out would be a less true record. Vastin made a mark against the clerk in his head, the kind of mark he made perhaps four times a year.

The record had the Mire instructor's words about the second notch, set down as she had demanded, those words and only those. It did not have her face, which had gone the white of a page while she wrenched at the release.

But the shoes were missing.

It had the chalk tray. The clerk at the tray's far end was there, since he had entered himself. But no clerk alive writes his own feet into a ledger, and so the brown shoes standing a pace and a half past the place where a heel would have landed were not anywhere in it. Vastin had seen them. He had seen the boy's answer too, when Gault asked him why. The record had the answer word for word: *Because the clerk was standing where I'd have landed.* Read without the shoes it sounded like a pleasant thing a boy had said. Read with them, it was a figure.

He wrote nothing. The chair complained under him as he leaned back, and he let it.

He let himself go back to the fourth chair for a moment, and to the second notch.

He had been looking at the boy, not the frame, when it went. That had been luck and nothing else; at that moment he had been reading the set of the boy's shoulders, the way a man reads a hand of cards he has not been dealt. The order of it, therefore, he had seen. The hips had dropped for the burst. The held breath had started; there was a small hitch at the throat that went with it every time. And then, before any sound from the rack, before the drag and the late clack that had turned every other head in the room, the whole of that answer had been stopped where it stood and folded back into a quarter of itself.

The sound had come after. He was as sure of that as he was of anything he had seen in a measured room. He had heard the catch of the notch while he was already watching the boy go short.

When the weight struck, he had looked once at the frame, to see where it had come down, as everyone in the room had. After that every head had swung back to the boy for a second look. He had not needed one, and had taken care not to take one, because a second look in a room like that is a question asked with the eyes, and he had no question about it that looking could answer. He had sat on through the quarter of an hour, and taken out the notebook, and written.

Even then, with the frame still ringing in his ears and the porters' boots in the passage, he had known that what he was writing was too large. He had written it anyway. That was what the notebook was for.

The pencil on the table had begun its slow journey toward the wall. He watched it go as far as the folded card and stop.

He turned the watch face up, and slid the notebook free.

---

It was small, its calf gone soft at the corners from years of coat pockets, a third of its leaves used and the rest blank. His coat had carried it to the last inspection; it would carry it to the next. Nothing in it ever went into a file. Proof was no part of its purpose. He kept it so that he could set a thought down while it was warm and come back to it cold, and see whether it had survived the cooling.

Two pages of it were Halcenvane.

He turned first to the second, the one from the twentieth, because that was the one the evening had been for.

He had set it down in the quarter of an hour the porters took to fetch the reserve frame from the far store, with the notebook open on his knee and the boy at the west rail with his weight off one leg. He had written fast. Then at the very end, when the wing was emptying and he was on his feet with the chair pushed back, he had taken the book out again and drawn a single line through three of the words. One stroke, so that the words could still be read under it. A man who blacks out his own error so that nobody can see what it was has told a lie about his own mind, and Vastin did not tell those on paper if he could avoid it.

He had felt the boy's eyes on him for that stroke, from across the room. He had not looked back. The stroke was not for the boy.

Then, that night, on the guest floor, with the counsel's voice going on through the wall about the state of her collars, he had written the line beneath.

Under the lamp it all read as it had read for four days.

*Frame fault, trial five. Correction preceded the audible release. ~~Capability exceeds classification.~~*

*Correction was sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration. Note the sizing. Not the speed.*

The struck words had been a verdict. They might even be the right verdict; turning them over now, cold, he thought the odds were better than even. But he had set them down within minutes of the frame failing, before the re-run, before he had watched the boy go back onto the reserve frame with a leg that had just paid for a floor and tell the panel, unasked, which of his runs were the hip's and not his. A finding written that fast is a stake driven before the survey. Every measurement taken afterward gets read as a line toward the stake. The surveyor thinks he is checking his work, and he is only admiring it.

He had learned that once in a cellar office, at twenty-three, from a man whose name he could now barely bring to mind. He had said it since to a good many young officers and let them take it for wisdom. It was not wisdom. It was a rail you put up where you know you are going to fall, and on the twentieth, two hours into a long sitting, with a handsome sentence on the page in his own hand, he had needed the rail as badly as any of them.

So he had struck the handsome sentence, and kept what was under it. The line beneath was a lesser claim and a sturdier one, and he would have traded ten handsome verdicts for it.

*Sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration.*

He had been watching people meet the thing they did not expect for the whole of his working life. A Stone officer in a collapsing gallery who threw up a wall so thick he brought down the roof he was holding off. A Current girl at a qualifying sitting who answered a stumble by pouring out twice her measured strength and burned her own hands on it. A magistrate's guard on a quay at night who met a stranger's sudden movement with a blow that would have stopped a horse, and the stranger only reaching for his papers. Every tier, every service, every age. They gave it more. Surprise came at them like a wall, and they hit the wall harder. It was the steadiest thing he knew about people under strain, steadier than anything in any manual, and he had never set it down anywhere, because something that every man does tells you nothing about any one man.

All morning on the twentieth the boy had shown a burst of one size, again and again, on the board and in the combined trial, to within the width of a brass mark. Then the frame had failed, and the weight had come early and high into a square with no room left, and the boy had not given it more. He had given it a quarter. He had shut his own technique off halfway through paying for it, and put the rest into his own hip, so as not to come down on a man's shoes.

Timing was a thing he could account for, if he allowed the boy the read along the skin that stood in his record and in the Greyvane sittings. Timing could be trained.

The size was the thing.

Sixteen years old. Mid-payment. Under every instrument and every pair of eyes that would ever sit in judgment on his life. And met with a hazard nobody had rehearsed him for, he had answered it *smaller*.

Vastin turned back a page, to the nineteenth.

*The Copper champion is Iron-grade. The bracket is the error. Recommend the advancement evaluation proceed unimpeded.*

That one he had written at the eighth bell in the Crown yard, as the tiers emptied, and had never needed to touch. He had had it by the fourth exchange, watching a Wind fighter on a hip that had no business holding her take an Iron Skin fighter apart with nothing but distance, in a bracket built by people who had imagined neither of them. One day someone at his own height would be asked whether the house's filing for her was sound. He had answered already. There was nothing else worth an Archmarshal's doing at a bracket final. The answer would stand.

The notebook went down by the file. The pencils stayed in their row.

---

He went on through the rest of the file at the pace of the lamp.

The Greyvane transcript, the sittings that touched the provision: a third reading, because the road up had given him two. The exhibits in their red tape. The season's standings under the registrar's seal. Then the inky clerk's ruled column from the twelve-by-fourteen room, the whole interview on one sheet: the minutes counted off down the margin, eleven questions down the middle, and near the foot a question going the other way.

He stopped at that one, as he had known he would.

*What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?*

People put questions to him across tables as a matter of course. Most of them came wrapped round a want: a softer word in a finding, a delay, a name to blame, a door left open. When had anyone last asked him a question with nothing wrapped round it? He tried to remember, and could not: a person who simply wanted to know, who had seen that knowing would cost some of his own half-hour, and had paid. He had answered the boy truly. He was glad, in a mild way he did not examine, that the question had been put to a man who would.

Then the other question. The one he had not asked.

The boy kept records. That fact sat in every monitoring filing on the file and in the Greyvane transcript besides, and every officer who had ever read the folder had passed over it as the dullest line in it. Vastin had asked what the records were for and been told, and had believed what he was told: *So I can't lie to myself later.* The next question he had let lie. It was the obvious next question. Withrow's counsel had drawn breath to hear it. Gault, at the boy's elbow, had gone as still as a post. Any capable officer would have put it, and any poor one would have put it badly, and he had looked at it for less than a second and not put it.

It had not been mercy.

The mandate on that bluff had been the record against the practitioner, and a private notebook was not in the record. An officer who reaches past his mandate because the reach happens to be open to him has stopped evaluating. He has begun to collect. And a collector at Vastin's height, carrying Vastin's authority, is the worst thing the Compact is able to make.

He had seen one made, when he was young. A senior man of great gifts, who had read other men's letters because he could, and kept what he found, and used it by degrees, a little and then more, until half a district's officers did their work looking over their shoulders at him. Bringing him down had taken four officers, a magistrate and the best part of ten years, and had used up two careers that deserved better. Vastin had been thirty-four on the night it ended. He had stood in a corridor afterward with rain on the window at the end of it and made himself a promise that he would not grow into the thing he had spent those years taking to pieces.

He had kept his hands at his sides, and he would go on keeping them there. On the day the question became properly his, if such a day came, he would ask it in a room with the door shut and tell the boy first. Not before.

It was not this day. It might never be.

The wing's six filings were not in the folder. He had read them on his first morning on the bluff, the thirteenth, before the first bell, and put them where they belonged in his own reckoning, a good way under the record: a capable officer's honest account of the chair he had been given and the list he had been handed. There had been nothing in them he needed twice. He had tied the pink tape again knot over knot, as it had come, and set the case by the door for the clerk to carry home to the wing, and he did not think of it now.

Last, before the note, he went through the enrollment file one more time, with his finger running down Bracken's numbered index.

He had handled more enrollment files than he could count, and this one was the best-made of any that had crossed his table in years. Each sheet carried one proof and said so in one line, and nothing argued, and nothing was ornamented. The delegation's counsel, who had wanted very badly to find a weak sheet, had found the load-bearing one in under a minute and a half on her first attempt and had looked, for a moment, as though she might clap.

Good work should be told it is good by somebody who knows the difference, while the man who did it is still in his chair. So he drew a sheet of the house's paper from the table drawer and wrote to the registrar at Halcenvane, privately, four sentences long. What the file had been tested for, he left unsaid.

*Registrar. Your enrollment file on the assay provision is the best-made I have handled in some years. Each sheet proves one thing and says which, and my own counsel, who wished to find a weak one, found the load-bearing sheet on her first attempt, which is the only real test of an index. I thought you should hear it from somebody who has no reason to say it.*

He signed it with his name entire, and set it aside for the morning post going back down the river.

---

One sheet was left, and of everything in the folder it was the one that would last.

A finding is read once, by the people it concerns, and filed. A file note goes on being read, by every officer who ever lifts the folder down, for as long as the folder exists. Whoever came to this folder after him would come to it through his note, and would read the rest in its light, whether they meant to or not. He had spent his life refusing to let other men's digests do that to him. He had no wish to do it to anyone else.

The date went onto a clean sheet. Nothing went under it, for a while.

Instead he took a scrap of the house's paper, the back of a bill, and wrote on it the first thing an officer in his place would write.

*No adverse finding.*

He looked at it. It was true. It was also a door shut in the face of whoever might need it open in a year, and it said nothing that the findings had not already said. He folded the scrap in half.

On the other half went the second thing.

*Subject warrants attention.*

The second got shorter shrift. Within a month it would put a warden on the bluff road. And in a boy who had already sat through one adjudication, it would bring on the very behaviour that turns a monitoring file into a stage play, because a boy who knows why he is watched begins to perform for the watcher, and nothing true has ever been got out of a performance. He tore it across. The halves lay one on the other by the lamp.

The third he never put to paper.

It was the kind reading, and half the continent had reached it already. A small house in the hills had staked a year on it, and he did not think less of them. It said the file was a mistake. Some clerk at some station had made one wrong mark at a boy's Kindling, and everything since, two academies and a hearing and a provision and a stamp, had been the long labour of picking out one bad stitch. It was comfortable because it asked nobody to change their mind about anything: the machine was sound, it had caught its sleeve once on one boy, and decent people had spent a year working the sleeve free.

He did not believe it.

It was not the boy's gifts. The registry swallowed a few remarkable children every year without so much as a hiccup; finding them a shelf was what it had been built for. What decided him was an absence: no carelessness anywhere. A slip leaves fingermarks. He had hunted this file for them for eleven days and four nights and found not one.

Nobody had decided anything about the boy when the designation went onto his sheet. The Kindling had entered it, in the first minutes of the file's life. It had stood ever since. Three houses, a public adjudication, and a registrar's index that left the other side almost nothing to say: it had come through all of them. And through all of it nobody, at any height, had put forward another classification, or amended the entry by a word, or written so much as a sheet suggesting it be looked at again.

A slipped hand gets corrected. This has never been touched.

So the record was not broken. It was doing something.

He did not write that. He had no evidence for it. It lay a very long way outside his mandate. And an officer of his grade who puts a guess into a file has arranged for some junior man, three years on, to act on the guess without its history and without its doubt.

He could set down the finding his own eyes had earned, and stop at the edge of it.

The line went down in his plain upright clerk's hand, the hand he had written in since his cuffs were inky, and under it went his name: all of it, as on everything he signed. He had never yet found a reason good enough for an officer to hide his name on a paper that might ruin someone.

*Classification error is unlikely. Continue observation.*

He laid down the pen.

He did not underline anything. He read it once, and it still meant what he meant, and he knew no better proof for a sentence than that. The tapes went back round the folder, grey, red and string. He put the two halves of the torn scrap into the lamp's chimney, one after the other, and watched them go. Then he turned the lamp down to nothing and stayed at the dark window over the empty yard, his left hand, which had begun to dislike evenings as well as mornings, lying open on his knee.

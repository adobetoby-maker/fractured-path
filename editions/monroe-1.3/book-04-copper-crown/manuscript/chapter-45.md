# Chapter 45 — The Panel's Order

On the thirteenth of Reaping, before he lit the lamp, Cael did nothing at all, and he did it on purpose.

He lay on his back in the dark with the blanket to his chin and set the stake, and only when it was settled did he let the rest of him wake up and start thinking about the day.

The ache came up behind his eyes as he sat, the dull one, the rent. It was cheap in the mornings. By evening it would have corners.

He lit the lamp low and opened the notebook at the delegation's pages.

There were eleven names on the manifest and he had pages on nine of them. The library had given him three of the four escort riders out of a dozen years of attendance lists, the counsel out of her published findings, and the three clerks out of a registry yearbook that nobody had borrowed in a decade. Havel had a page with a question of Lira's at the foot of it. Ilsev had the thickest page and the least use, because six findings told him what she would do with a document and nothing at all about what she did with a morning. The fourth rider was too new to be anywhere. And the eleventh name had one line on its page, and under the line, in his own hand, a single word: *Lira*.

He read the eleven through once and closed the book. Then he opened the plan instead, at the page with two words ruled off across its head, written on the eve of the twelfth in a hand that had not been quite steady: *Hold. First.*

None of it was the first task any more. That had changed in a corridor two days ago, with Brom standing square across the flagstones like a post. Reading the delegation was the second task. The first was to stay as thick as an ordinary boy for every hour of every day they were on the bluff, and the first task did not care how interesting the second one got.

Lira came down to breakfast without the stick.

She came down the residence stair one step at a time, with her hand on the rail and her face arranged to suggest that this was simply how she had always preferred to come down stairs, and that anybody who thought otherwise was welcome to say so. Her four days were over. The safety instructor had said four days with nothing on it, and four days had ended at midnight, and Lira had always kept to the letter of an order she disliked.

Brom half rose from the bench. She looked at him, and he sat down again and became very interested in his porridge.

"Well?" said Karis.

"It holds me up." Lira lowered herself onto the bench beside Cael with the leading leg out straight, and set her bowl in front of her, and did not look at anybody. "It isn't happy about it. It holds me up."

"Six days," said Brom, to his spoon.

"Six days," said Lira. "Nobody talks about hips."

In six days she would walk out onto the oak for the Copper final, Copper Rank Two on her certificate and Iron-equivalent by every honest ledger on the bluff, on a leg the safety instructor had not cleared, against the one fighter in the bracket who knew it best.

Karis had the grey notebook open beside her bread, at a page Cael knew. Down its left side ran a column of small ruled boxes, one for every night between the semifinals and the final, and every box above this morning's line had a neat cross in it. It was the second round of Rooke's nineteen exercises, the ones Brom did by lamplight in the corner of the common room. All season he had done them four a week, but on the night of his semifinal Rooke had put him on one a night until the final, and Karis had ruled a fresh page to match. The boxes ran out to the eighteenth, the night before the final, and she had told nobody but Cael why she stopped there.

"You didn't miss one," she said to Brom.

"I never miss one."

"I know. I'm writing it down so that you'll know I know." She inked a cross into last night's box and blotted it with the side of her hand. "They signed for the chests, by the way. Yesterday. Bracken's clerk made every one of them sign before he'd let a box through the door. The counsel signed with a face like a cat in a bath."

"Good," said Lira.

"I thought you'd like that."

"What are you doing today?" Brom asked Lira. He asked it carefully.

"Lectures. Then I'm going to walk the length of the Crown yard and back, twice, slowly, with nobody watching." She ate a spoonful of porridge. "Then I'm going to sit down and decide whether I liked it."

"I could walk with you."

"You could not. You're the other half of the final." She pointed her spoon at him. "If you walk beside me you'll watch how I put the foot down, because you can't help it, and then you'll know, and then I'll know you know, and we'll be playing chess again by supper."

Brom thought about it, and nodded slowly, and went back to his bowl.

"I'll walk with her," said Karis. "I don't know anything about feet."

"That," said Lira, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me in four days."

Cael ate and listened and minded his edges. Across the hall a table of Mire second-years were telling each other in low voices what the Archmarshal had eaten at supper, which none of them could possibly know. At the next table a Blade fourth-year was explaining to a first-year, at length and with diagrams drawn in spilt water, how an inspection worked, and Cael listened to that for a while because the fourth-year was wrong in a very confident and orderly way. Then he stopped listening, because the stopping was also practice.

"You've got your morning face on," said Lira, without turning her head.

"I've got a page with one line on it."

"You've got a page with my name on it." She broke her bread in two. "Eat. If you go thin at breakfast I'll know, and I'm sitting on the side with the bad leg, so I'll have to tread on you with the good one, and it'll hurt us both."

Cael ate. In seven days it would be his own turn on the oak, with an Archmarshal at the rail, and if the five came out no bigger than his baseline, the one true sentence anybody had ever written about him would stop being true.

---

Through the wall of the guest floor, the counsel was talking to her clerk while she dressed.

Vastin had heard her through walls for four nights of inns, and he no longer heard the words. He heard the shape. It went on low and level, about the water in the jug and the state of a collar and something a magistrate had once said to her on a stair. Then it stopped in the middle, and there was a silence about as long as a page, and then it went on again from the exact syllable where it had broken off. She had been handed something to read. He had noticed the habit on the second day of the road and had not written it down, because he did not take notes on colleagues. He only noticed that he had noticed, and let it go.

He sat at a scrubbed table under the window with his coat on, because the guest floor was cold before the fires were lit, and his watch lay face-up beside his hand.

It was a plain brass watch, older than the clerk who had brought up his water. Every Seventh-day for longer than he cared to count, wherever he happened to be, he had set it against the best clock in the building and written the difference on a card in its lid, and so he knew that it gained in the cold, and by how much. A thing that measured anything had to be checked, now and then, against some other thing that measured. Otherwise it was only a thing with a number on its face.

On the table in front of the watch lay the wing's filings.

They had come across the evening before, under the assessment wing's own seal, in answer to a requisition the counsel had written on the road in the ordinary form. The wing had answered it in the ordinary time. Pink tape. A covering index on top, in a hand that was not the hand of the filings under it. Six filings, with an appendix behind each, covering one term of embedded coverage on an enrollee who held his place under a provision.

He had put them at the bottom of everything, on purpose. Everything else was read now. So he cut the tape at the knot, where it could be tied again exactly as it had been, and began.

Nine minutes, by the watch. He went down every line and did not lift his pencil once. At the end he squared the sheets and laid the index back on top. Then he sat with his hands flat on either side of the stack and looked out of the window at a wet roof, on which a gull was standing on one leg and disapproving of the morning.

Complete in its form. Correct in every appendix. One term of a capable officer's work.

He had made a great deal of such work himself, in his time. For years he had been the man at the edge of the room with a board on his knee, writing down what could be seen from the chair he had been given. He had been good at it. He had been told so, honestly, and the work had been honest too. It was out of those years, long after, that he had made a sentence for himself. He had said it aloud perhaps half a dozen times since, always to somebody young, and had never written it anywhere.

*Observer product is testimony about the observer.*

He did not mean it unkindly. He meant it as a sum. Any filing carried three things inside it, and only one of them was the thing it said it was about. It carried what a person had noticed. It also carried the chair that person had been put in, and the list of what they had been told to look for, and nobody in the whole history of the service had ever found a way to wash those two out of the ink afterward. You could not ask a filing to see round its own chair. You could lean on it a little. You could not lay a floor across it and stand.

These six told him, plainly and well, where a good officer had been asked to sit for a term, and what he had been asked to watch for from there. That was a true thing to know. It was a thing about the chair.

He set it in its place, a long way under the record and a little way under the exhibits, and turned the watch face-down.

There was a knock, and the counsel put her head round the door with her collar still undone.

"Archmarshal. Will you sit with us today?"

"Not today."

"The provision's paper is up this morning. The registrar's boxes." Her eyes went to the tied stack by his hand and came away again without a question. "I'd value the company. I'm told their index is better than ours, and I'd like a witness when I admit it."

"The first three days are yours," said Vastin. "If I sit at your table, your clerks will write down what I look at instead of what you ask. I'll come on the fifteenth."

She considered that, and him, with her head a little on one side.

"That's a kindness," she said, "dressed up as a procedure."

"Most procedures are," said Vastin. "It's what they're for."

She laughed once, a short surprised sound, and went away down the passage talking. Through the wall he heard her take up her sentence to the clerk at the exact word where she had left it.

---

The record he had been through four times on the road, in the oldest of the three carriages.

He had taken that one because nobody else had asked for it. It rode hard, and its door had a device painted out so long ago that the paint had worn thin over it, and nobody wanted to be seen getting down from it. That suited him. A carriage nobody wanted was a carriage nobody came to talk in. For four days the road's grit had ticked against the half-closed shutter. The papers had ridden on the bench opposite him, and somewhere ahead the counsel's voice had gone on and on, faint, like a stream on the other side of a wall.

At the coast he had tied the papers into three bundles, each in a different tape, so that his hand would always come to them in the same order without his having to think. Grey tape round the record itself. Red round everything tendered in it. Plain string round what other officers had written about the boy over two years.

The order was the panel's. It was the order the panel had been given the matter in, and so it cost the most to read, and he had paid that price on every file for most of his life.

He read the grey bundle first and all of it, from the top: the magistrate's opening words, which the clerk had asked to have spoken twice because he had dropped his pen; the roll of the room; every dull procedural quarrel, including a long one about whether an academy's day-book was a paper of the academy or of the man who kept it. Then the red bundle, in the order the papers had been handed up, which was never the order anyone would have chosen afterward. Fourteen demonstration sittings from a single semester, sheet on sheet. A framework of movement that the record called Wind-adjacent, measured on a stone floor. A read along the skin, logged trial by trial. And at the very back, late and thin, one sheet about a spark. Last, the string bundle. He had untied that one once.

The red bundle he had read the most slowly of the three. One semester of a boy's sittings, fourteen of them, signed in turn by two officers of the academy, and the numbers in them agreed with one another. They agreed from sitting to sitting and from week to week, a good deal more closely than numbers usually agree when the person producing them is fifteen and growing out of his boots between one sitting and the next. He had made no mark about that, because agreement was not a question. It was simply a thing that was so. But he had read those sheets twice where he read most things once, and he had noticed himself doing it.

People liked to believe that order did not matter. It mattered more than almost anything. A digest was always laid out for its own ending, so that the reader arrived at the ending feeling he had walked there himself. And once a man knows where a road comes out, every bend of it looks like a turn toward that place. He had watched able men read that way all his working life. He had once done it himself. That was the reason for the tape.

His marks were in the margins of all four passes, one colour of pencil to each, and every mark was a question.

It was the only part of his method that had ever embarrassed him a little. In the registry's lower vault there was a long drawer of his old working copies, kept because a rule somewhere said they must be. On a wet afternoon once he had reckoned, idly, that the drawer held about two thousand of his margins. He did not believe a clerk could go through it from end to end and come away with six conclusions.

Three of the margins in this record carried the rest.

The first stood beside the respondent's opening. A boy of fifteen, with nobody beside him at the table, had begun by agreeing with the challenge. He had handed it, flatly and before anyone had pressed him, the one thing the whole room had come there to watch him fight over, and then he had calmly built his entire argument on the far side of the gift.

*Why concede the category?*

The second stood beside the stretch where the boy had counted the schedule's places out loud for the panel, five of them and then a sixth bound in at the back, and had treated the count as a wall. He had understood that a list which says *these and no others* shuts out its own author as firmly as anybody else. Men with long registry careers forgot that every year.

*Who taught him the schedule's structure?*

And the third stood at the foot of the record's first sheet, beside two dates the Compact's own clerk had entered there: the day the challenge was filed, and the day the first sitting was called. He had drawn two lines under it.

*Verify: three weeks' preparation — from what baseline?*

He never underlined to make a thing louder. Two lines meant a note to the man who would read this copy next, who was always himself. It said: *everything else will rest its weight here, so watch yourself, because the cheap answer will look very good.*

---

Three weeks was in the record, and no one had argued with it. Both dates were in the Compact's own hand.

So there were two ways to read it. Either the three weeks were wrong in the record, which happened, quietly and for years. Or something in the two years before them had made a boy who could do that much with three weeks. And nowhere in the file, in two years of district filings or in any page of the warden's long reports, did a single sentence say what. There was a great deal about what the boy had done. There was nothing about what had made him able to do it.

There was the gap. He had come on it in the first pass, somewhere on the coast road with a gull's cry outside the shutter. The next three passes had not closed it by a hair. It was the most interesting gap that had crossed his table in a long while. He meant that in the plainest way a craftsman can mean it, the way a joiner is interested in a joint that ought to have failed and has not.

He had no theory about it. He took some care not to grow one.

He had been taught that care at twenty-three, at a scarred table in a cellar office that smelled of lamp soot and wet wool coats. The man across the table had spoken to him perhaps twenty times in two years. Once, though, he had leaned his elbows on the scars and said a sentence that had outlasted everything else about him: his face, his voice, and very nearly his name.

*An evaluation that begins from its conclusion is a report about the evaluator.*

Vastin had heard it and copied it into the back of a book and gone on not understanding it for four years. He understood it in a bad month, sitting on the end of a narrow bed in a posting-house with his boots still on. A finding of his had come apart that afternoon at its first review, and he had sat there and traced it back. He had built it step by step from a suspicion he had liked holding, and every step had looked to him like evidence, because he had been walking toward the place where it came out the whole time and had never once turned round to look. The finding had not lived. His career had come within a sheet of paper of going down with it.

He had read files in the panel's order ever since, and grown no theories he could help.

His left hand had begun to complain on cold mornings, a stiffness across the knuckles that eased by the second bell. It was complaining now. For the last two winters it had wanted the pen held a little further back, and he had let it have its way and said nothing. A man who argues with his own hand writes worse, and he still had a great deal to write.

Below the window, across the wet quadrangle, a boy was standing on an upturned bucket under an arch. He was writing on a slate that hung on the wall there, a figure at a time, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth. Then he got down and looked at what he had written from the cobbles, and got up again, and changed one figure. Vastin watched him do it twice. He had no idea what the slate was for. Whatever it was, the boy had decided it ought to be right, though there was nobody anywhere in the quadrangle to check it.

He tied the wing's filings in their pink tape again, knot over knot, as they had come, and set them by the door for the clerk.

The notice that had sent him here was a true notice. It named an academy that had done an unusual thing lawfully, and an inspection of the ordinary kind, and nothing else. He had filed no question. He knew perfectly well that not one person on this hill would believe it. Years ago he had given up trying to close the space between his work and the work people imagined for him. It was a useful space. It showed you what each of them feared he had come for. But what it showed was theirs, not his, and he left it to them.

He would walk the building. He would watch its rooms being used. Rooms told you what a house had decided to be, if you let them talk long enough.

This house had answered the notice by changing nothing, and it had not troubled to hide that it was doing so. He had seen it in the courtyard yesterday. There was a gutter mended badly three times and left that way. The cobbles had not been swept for visitors. And the registrar had told the first man through his door where the file stood and how many boxes it filled, instead of how hard it would be to find. A house that changed nothing for an inspector was either very sure of itself or very well advised. Either was worth knowing. Neither was a finding.

A bell began somewhere below, the first of the day. He laid the grey bundle on top of the others, the right way up, and went down.

---

An inspection, it turned out, was mostly doors.

He had read the schedule a dozen times, and nothing in it had warned him how small the whole business would look from the floor. The hill did not stop for eleven people. It made room for them, and everyone stepped round them, and by noon nobody quite saw them any more.

But three doors that had stood open all season were shut now, from the second bell until the fourth. One was the records hall's back room, where Bracken had built the file. One was the long upper room over the lecture range, where the faculty argued on Fifth-days. The third was the wing's document store behind Gault's office. Cael had never before seen it closed. He had always known it by the instrument woman's oil can, standing on its sill.

And the covered walk filled with boxes.

They were black, with brass hasps, and they went across in the morning and came back at the fourth bell. A young clerk carried them at a fast walk in front of his chest, breathing through his mouth. A second clerk walked beside him with a list. The third came along behind, at his own pace, and never carried anything.

He was the eldest of the three, a grey man with a careful face, and his left knee was bad. Cael saw it on the walk's two shallow steps, which the man took sideways, one at a time, the way somebody does who has worked out to the farthing what a step will charge him. He wrote with his left hand as well, on a board pressed flat against his coat. He was the one who said which box went. The young man only carried what he was told.

So whatever crossed the walk at the second bell was what the sittings would read by the third. Whatever came back at the fourth was finished with.

Cael did not have to do anything to learn it. He only had to stand at the walk's rail on his way to the morning lecture, as he had stood there every morning of the term, and see what was in the young clerk's arms as the young clerk went hurrying past. At the second bell it was two boxes that wore the registrar's index labels. At the fourth those two came back, and one went over the other way, labelled in the wing's hand.

*They're on the provision's paper today,* he wrote at noon, on the clerks' page. *The wing is tomorrow. The old man's knee tells you half a day early. Nobody has to say a word.*

The counsel he had in four minutes, on the same walk, before the first box had come back.

She came along it with Havel a half-pace behind her, and she was talking. She was always talking, low and pleasant, to whoever was nearest, about anything at all. Then the young clerk came up beside her with a single sheet. She took it and stopped dead in the middle of the flagstones, so that the crowd in the walk had to part round her, and read it to the bottom without a sound, while a first-year with an armful of books stood stranded behind her and did not dare go round. She handed it back. She walked on.

And she went on talking from the very word where she had stopped.

Not the start of the sentence. Not some new sentence. The word. Cael had heard the first half of it from the rail before the sheet came. He heard the second half after it went, fitted on as neatly as if nothing at all had come between.

He took that to carrel eleven at noon, where Karis was eating an apple over a sheet of the archive's lending rules. Bracken had forbidden her the records hall until the fifteenth, in writing, when she was to sit at the table for the enrollment basis and not one day before. She was being very good about it, in a way that made her bite apples hard.

"Her talking and her reading keep to different rooms in her head," he said. "She can argue with you and read your file at the same time, and she won't lose her place in either."

Karis put the apple down.

"That's the one who's going to take the boxes apart."

"Yes."

She looked at the lending rules for a while without reading them.

"Good," she said at last. "I want her good. If she's good and she can't get in, then it holds. If she's bad and she can't get in, it only means she was bad." She picked up the apple again. "I'd rather lose to somebody good than win against somebody stupid. You can't keep a win you got off somebody stupid. It doesn't stay won."

"Bracken says the file has to work on strangers."

"Bracken's right. Bracken's nearly always right. It's very tiring." She bit the apple. "Which boxes?"

He told her the labels. She shut her eyes and said the index numbers back to him in order, and what each box held, and what in each of them a good counsel would go for first. She was right about all of it. He could see her being right.

"I can't go in till the fifteenth," she said, with her eyes still shut.

"I know."

"I built it, and I have to wait outside like a parcel." She opened her eyes. "That's the whole point of it, of course. I understand that. I'd just like it noted that I mind."

"Noted," said Cael, and she threw the apple core at him, and missed on purpose.

She turned her pen.

"And the eleventh?"

"Nothing. I haven't tried."

"Good." She said it at once. "Don't. Not from across a courtyard, not on your own. You read him with everything on the tier yesterday, and Lira says you stayed, and I believe her. But I'd rather you didn't find out how many times you can do that."

"I'm not going to look for him."

"I know you're not going to *look*," said Karis. "I'm asking you not to go and stand where he'll be."

---

Ilsev crossed the walk a little before the third bell, carrying her own case.

It was the plain brown one with the strap. She went down the exact middle of the flagstones at a pace that never altered, and her eyes did not leave the door at the far end. She did not look at the rail, or the clock over the arch, or the knot of first-years who had found an urgent reason to be at the north end of the walk at that hour. She reached the door, went through it, and was gone.

He had watched every step and got nothing that surprised him, and that was the finding. That night he wrote it at the foot of her page, under six findings' worth of notes, and boxed it.

*Exactly the person the papers describe. She reads the text, goes where the text goes, and wants nothing out of it. Of the eleven, she's the only one who looks in the flesh just as she looks on paper. Nobody else on the manifest does. It's the rarest thing they brought.*

The rest of the morning he spent being ordinary at the rate of every other morning.

He sat his lectures in his usual seat. He did his supervised hour in his usual hall. At the end of the morning he gave his coursework to the Ash instructor, on the day it was due. He had thought about giving it in a day early, to be safe, and had seen the trap in the thought before it was finished. Early was a change. A change was a performance. And Withrow had stood in front of the whole school and told it that a performance was the one thing an inspection could really see.

The Ash instructor took his pages without looking at him and laid them on her pile.

"On the day," she said.

"Yes, Instructor."

"Hm." She squared the pile with two taps. "Half the second year gave theirs in yesterday. In their best hands." She looked at the top sheet, which was not his, with deep suspicion. "I shall be marking handwriting for a week."

He went out with the hold steady and the ache behind his eyes just starting to grow its corners.

From the walk's rail on the way back he could see down into the Crown yard. Two figures were crossing its long floor from end to end, very slowly, with nobody else on the boards and nobody on the tiers. The shorter one was talking with both hands, about something that was plainly not feet. The taller one walked with the leading leg set down a little too carefully each time, like a person putting down a full cup. At the north end they turned, and stood a moment, and started back.

He did not read the hip. He could have. He could have told Lira, from the rail, how many days it was from sound and where on the stride it caught. He kept his eyes on Karis's hands instead, which were drawing something in the air that might have been an index, and he let the two of them walk the length of the yard twice, as Lira had said, with nobody watching.

Six days, he thought. Ten days old by the time she fights on it.

Nine pages, one line, and one name under it. The eleventh he left alone. To try it he would have to stand near the man, and nothing on the bluff that morning had given him an honest reason to.

The schedule on the board would give him one tomorrow: the fourteenth, hall three, the fourth bell. It was his own ordinary Sixth-day hour. The board in the wing had told him so a week ago, and that same evening he had made up his mind to change nothing about it at all.

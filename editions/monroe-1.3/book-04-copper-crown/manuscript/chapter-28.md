# Chapter 28 — Exception

Of his four hands, the fourth was the one Seln used least and trusted most.

The round clerk's script belonged to the copying table, and everybody in the wing had seen it. The quick cramped one was for his own notes, and nobody had. The third, a merchant's sloping hand with a flourish on its capitals, was for wrappers addressed to a cloth house up the coast that had never sold a yard of cloth. The fourth was upright and plain and a little old-fashioned, the hand of a man who had been taught to write by somebody strict, and it was for the product alone. He took it out that night a little after the ninth bell, in his room on the staff corridor, with the shutter fastened and the lamp turned down to a bead.

On the table in front of him lay what the afternoon had left. The warden's draft, which he had copied himself at the wing's long table not two hours since, in the round hand, every word of it, because the wing copied everything. The duty clerk's entry, four lines long: *Hall three, N. bay, 6th bell. Apparatus failure, resistance post. No evaluable content. Warden attending.* And the thing that was not on the table, because it was in him: eleven paces of open boards, and what had happened at the far end of them.

He laid out a sheet and wrote the heading and the date and the number of his brief. Then, before he wrote a word of the body, he did what he always did before a report that mattered. He called up the auditor.

The auditor was himself, at twenty-two. Two years of his life he had spent at a long table in a records house up the coast, with a red pencil and a stack of other officers' product, reading for the places where it lied. He had been very good at it. He had been, he thought now, rather a cold young man, and quite right about most things, and he kept that young man in the back of his head the way some people keep a dead teacher, to be consulted. So he sat the young man down at the table, in his mind, and gave him the boy's baseline to hold in his left hand, and waited for the report to be put into his right.

He knew what the report had to carry. He had known it since the forearm came up.

He had stood with the dial sheets against his chest and watched a post lock at the seventh setting and send the third-year's whole delivery back off the pad. He had watched a boy who had been six feet away arrive between the third-year and the pad in less time than the delivery took to come back. Seln's whole craft was being where nobody looked for him. In fifteen years he had never seen anybody get anywhere that fast, at that size, in that direction, toward the danger and not away. And then the boy's arm had taken the return the way a stone quay takes a barge coming in too fast: it had not given, and it had not broken, and the force had gone on down through the boy into the boards. Two things, at the least. The file had a name for neither.

His brief had categories, as every brief did. The second of them was the one the whole posting existed to fill, if it ever needed filling: *observed capability materially in excess of the subject's documented baseline.* Seln had filed under it eleven times in fifteen years. Eleven times he had been right. Eleven people had gone, afterward, into files further up the coast that he had never been shown and had never asked to see.

He dipped his pen and wrote.

*Hall three (east), north bay, sixth bell, Third-day. Apparatus failure during a supervised floor hour: the retaining pin of a resistance post sheared under load at the seventh setting, locking the core during a Force Path third-year's fourth declaration of his third set and returning the delivery off the pad. Subject was at the bay's edge on an adjoining footwork drill, signed for by the Ash Path instructor, and intervened. The third-year was not struck. No injury requiring the infirmary. The academy's floor warden attended within the quarter-hour, withdrew all four posts on the bluff from use, and has recommended inspection and replacement of every retaining pin in the academy's apparatus; her draft and the wing's log are enclosed. Response consistent with documented baseline.*

He blotted it, and handed it across the table to the young man with the red pencil.

---

The young man read it the way he had read everything, with the baseline in his left hand, looking first for a quarrel.

That was always the first thing: whether two papers about the same man disagreed. Seln watched him look. Gault's office had filed the baseline in the boy's first weeks, and it had been copied into the product soon after. It gave a practitioner of non-standard architecture who executed consistently. It gave a displacement framework, measured to a working ceiling on sprung timber. And it gave a plate trial at the frame's heaviest weight, in which the enrollee had taken the blow on a braced arm and kept his feet. So the young man found, on record, a boy who could move suddenly and could take a blow on his arm. In the report he found a boy who had moved suddenly and taken a blow on his arm, and a closing sentence saying the two agreed. Nothing quarrelled. The red pencil did not move.

Next he weighed it. He had a habit, Seln remembered, of lifting a report in his palm before he read it closely, as though he could feel its heft through the paper. He had learned in his first month that frightened officers wrote thin. A man with something to hide wanted to give the machine as little as he could, and so on the days that most needed a full account he sent up three lines and a signature. Seln had caught two men that way himself, at that long table, by nothing more than the thinness of what they sent on a busy day. So this report was not thin. Apparatus, cause, setting, the drill, the instructor's signature, the outcome, the warden, the posts, the pins, the enclosures. The young man hefted it in his imagined hand and set it down satisfied. It weighed what a day like that ought to weigh.

Last, and slowest, he read for warmth.

That was the hardest test, and the one Seln had been proudest of passing other men's product through, long ago. A man's attention could not be hidden, because it was not a thing he chose; it pooled of its own accord wherever he was interested, and the sentences there grew longer and fuller and a little warm, like a hearthstone. Find where a report grew warm and you knew where its writer's mind had been. Find a cold place where warmth should have been, and you had found the hole.

This report was warm about the pin.

It lingered on the shear and the setting, the core and the collar, the warden coming within the quarter-hour, every post on the bluff withdrawn, every pin in the academy to be inspected and replaced. It was warm about the institution. The young man would have nodded at that. Of course the officer was interested in the institution. It was what a man in an assessment wing was best placed to see. And an academy finding a flaw in nine years of its own apparatus was exactly the weather the analysts liked best.

And it was true. That was the whole of the art, and the young man with the red pencil had never understood it, because he had only ever been on the reading side of the table. A gap in a report did not survive because it was well hidden. It survived because every word around it was real. Seln was interested in the pin. On the walk back from the hall it had filled his head. A tired pin in a nine-year collar, found in a term when an inspection might come at any time, with the posts chained and the warden's draft already on its way to faculty, was the best weather he had recorded since the posting began.

The young man finished, and put the report down, and found nothing. He had been very good, and he found nothing, because there was nothing on the page to find.

Seln sat looking at it after the young man had gone.

The boy's crossing was not in it. The boy's arm was not in it, except as *intervened*. No sentence formed about it. He saw it the way a man coming into his own room in the dark knows, without lighting a lamp, that one chair has been moved a hand's breadth from where it stood. The room was a different shape. He stood in the shape for a while and looked at it.

He found it would bear weight.

He folded the sheet, and sealed it, and wrote the wrapper in the merchant's sloping hand.

---

He walked it down, as he always did. The academy's own bag went to Ostrand twice a day for nothing, and that was precisely why nothing of his ever went in it.

Out by the wing's side door, with his own key. Along the bottom of the north quadrangle in the dark. A nod to the night porter at the lodge, returned without the porter's eyes leaving his newspaper. Then the four long bends of the bluff road, down toward the lamps of the river.

At the foot of the last bend, by the toll board, two grey coats stamped under their lamp. He let the Path run on that stretch from long habit and felt their attention lift toward him and touch him and slide off like water off oilcloth. Neither of them would ever know that the one paper in a month worth carrying had gone by them in a satchel at arm's length.

Halfway along the river street it began to rain. It was a fine cold rain that did not so much fall as gather on him, on his shoulders and the brim of his hat and the backs of his hands. It was still gathering when he paid the courier's clerk by the fish steps from his own purse and watched the packet go into the canvas sack. It was still gathering an hour and ten minutes after he had left, on the last bend going up. He had made that walk five times this term. He had never minded it once. He did not mind it now.

He let himself into the wing with the duty key a little past the eleventh bell. There was a week's ledger of floor hours waiting, to be added up and set against the instructors' sheets before morning.

He added it up. Four long columns. He got it right.

He got such sums right four times in five, and took care never to be right oftener, because a man who never slipped at figures was a man people eventually began to watch. Last week, in front of the desk clerk, he had carried a ten into the wrong column and been put right very kindly, and had thanked her, and coloured a little. Tonight there was nobody in front of whom to be wrong, so he was not wrong.

Then he pulled out the cabinet drawer where the term's carbons were kept. It held a carbon of everything that had gone down the coast since the term began: calendar extracts, floor issues, the list of the boy's associates, the first product with its line about a private notation habit, and each month after it. The drawer ran out on its runners without a whisper, because he had waxed them his first week.

The whole wing was silent. Far below the bluff, the river was going about its business.

He filed the report. Then he sat for a while with the file drawer open, not filing anything else.

---

Cael told Lira and Brom that night in Brom's room with the door shut. He sat on the end of the bed; Brom sat on the floor against the wall with his knees up; Lira stood at the window.

He told them all of it, plainly, in order: the grind that did not come, the read on time and the wrong shape, the Wind sent forward, Reydan's fragment on contact, the floor giving a third of it back, the landing beat in front of the jammed post. Then Jask, and the true short answer. Then the drawing, and the nine dots, and the ninth.

Nobody said anything for a while when he had finished.

"Your arm," said Lira at last, without turning round from the window.

"It hums."

"Hums."

"Like a bell after somebody's stopped ringing it. It hasn't stopped." He moved the fingers of his left hand to show her they moved. "It's not the ordinary ache. I don't know what it is yet. I've written it down."

Lira turned round then and looked at the arm, and then at him, for a long time, and he watched her decide not to say any of the first four things she wanted to say.

"Jask's walking about," she said instead.

"Jask's walking about."

"Good." She sat down on the window ledge with her bad leg out. "Then it was right. Whatever comes next."

Brom had his chin on his knees. "What comes next?"

"Something goes up the coast. It has to." Cael told them the rest of the page: another officer, perhaps; more coats at the road; a letter to Withrow. "It isn't him being wicked. It's what he was sent for. Lira, I don't think it's your terms. He hasn't touched my paper wrong. He'll write it down right."

"I know what my terms were," said Lira. "I said *wrong*. I'm not a child." She looked out of the window at the dark where the ferry landing was. "That's what makes it worse. Every time this man has a chance to be easy to hate, he does his work properly instead."

"So we wait," said Brom.

"We get ready," said Cael. "And then we wait."

Karis, when he found her in carrel eleven the next morning, did not wait for him to finish. By noon she had a box. It was a plain pasteboard document box from the stationer's in Ostrand. Into it, in order, went every sheet the provision had ever produced about him: the enrollment record, Gault's baseline note, every drill sheet he had built and every countersignature on each, every floor issue, wrong and revised, every supervised hour since his first week on the bluff. Each was copied in her small hand where the original could not leave the wing, and each copy was dated and initialled and cross-referenced to the original's place in the wing's own files.

"If somebody asks," she said, tying the box's tape in a flat bow, "we hand them this, and it's already in order, and it's already boring. Nobody has ever been made to read a box like this and come out of it angry. They come out of it tired."

"And if nobody asks?"

"Then I've a very tidy box," said Karis, "and I'll sleep better for it, which is worth a morning."

The incident report itself came up through the wing three days later. The floor warden's draft had grown to four pages by then, and the wing copied it for the faculty and for the file. Cael, who read everything on the bluff with his name in it, asked at the counter for the copy he was entitled to, and read it standing at the end of the passage.

It named the pin, the collar, the setting, the nine years, the inspections passed. It recommended, in its last paragraph, a schedule for replacing every retaining pin in every apparatus on the bluff, in language so dry and so exact that he copied two of its sentences into the observation notebook as a model of how such things should be written.

He was in it once. Page two, in the warden's account of the afternoon:

*Enrollee (assay provision) present at bay edge; moved to intervene; received the release on a guard arm; no injury reported. Third-year not struck.*

He read it three times. Every word of it was true, and none of it weighed anything. *Moved to intervene.* Six feet in a blink, forward into the hazard, and the hall's own record had two words for it, and both of them were right.

Nobody asked him a single question. Not the warden, not the wing, not Gault, not the Ash instructor, whose supplementary note said only that the enrollee had acted promptly and correctly, which was true. Of all of it, that was what he could not put down. He had spent the whole of his life being asked. On the one afternoon he had done a thing worth asking about, the bluff had written it down in eleven words and turned the page.

---

He watched the coats.

On the first evening after the pin, at the change of the fourth hour, he sat on the coping of the wall behind the second quadrangle with Lira. Together they watched the relief walk up the river street to the ferry landing: two grey coats going, two coming, on the bell. The same as the night before. The same as every night since the bridge.

On the second day the change at the road's foot was four minutes late. He wrote that down and then crossed it out, because one of the relieving pair had stopped on the way to buy a hot pie from a barrow and could be seen eating it.

On the third day there were two and two, on the bell.

On the morning of the fourth day he woke, and lay still before getting up, and took stock of himself as he did before anything that mattered, and found the hum in his forearm gone. It had not faded. It had simply stopped, sometime in the night, the way a sound stops that has been going on so long you have forgotten to hear it, and the arm under it was only an arm again, a little stiff, a little bruised along the outer bone where the sheet had struck. He wrote: *Compression: the hum, gone on the fourth morning. Arm sound.* Then he went out to watch the coats.

Two and two, on the bell.

"Nothing," said Lira on the coping beside him, that evening.

"Nothing yet."

"You said four days."

"I said a change would show in four days." He watched the relief nod to the two it was relieving, polite as shopkeepers. "I didn't say no change in four days meant nothing went up. A report goes up the coast by courier. Somebody has to read it. Somebody has to decide what it means. Somebody has to decide what to do about what it means." He shook his head. "Four days was the soonest it could show. Not the latest."

"So we don't know."

"We don't know." He wrote the date and *two and two, on the bell*, and closed the notebook. "I'll know more in a week. Or I'll know that I still don't know, which is also something."

Lira looked at him sideways.

"You're very calm about it," she said.

"I'm not calm. I've only got one instrument, and it's slow. Shouting at it won't make it quicker."

On the fifth day the minutes of the faculty's session went up on the board by the theater door, as they always did, and Cael read them because he read everything. Under *apparatus*, Rooke had moved that the floor warden's schedule for replacing every pin on the bluff be adopted in full and at once. The bursar had proposed phasing it over a year. Rooke's ground was minuted in his own words: *the next pin to fail will not wait for the bursar's year*. The motion was carried. Under it, in Bracken's own hand, was a note that the registrar's office would find the money from the reserve within a fortnight and would account for it at the next session.

Jask's story, meanwhile, had grown in the refectory queue exactly as Cael had known it would. By the sixth day the assay boy had crossed the whole hall. By the seventh he had caught the post as it fell, though it had not fallen, and somebody on the lecture stair was telling it with a third-year flung through the air. Brom reported each version at supper with enormous solemnity, like a man reading out the weather from a far country.

"Today you lifted the post over your head," he said on the seventh evening. "With one arm. The bad one."

"Did I put it down again?"

"Nobody knows. The story ends before that."

---

The fifteenth session was the eighth day after the pin, and the whole bluff knew what it was a week before it came.

Lira sent her challenge up to the top line on the morning the cards were made, in her own hand, on the clerk's slip: her name, the arrow, and the name with the brass stud beside it. She did it before breakfast and told nobody, and by the time the three of them came into the refectory, half the room had already read the board.

On paper it was a challenge upward of a single place, the only kind there was, from the second line to the first. Underneath that it was the seeding of the final, since whoever held the top line at the season's close fought her semifinal on the earlier day and came to the final rested. And underneath that, it was a thing Cael found he could only say plainly in the observation notebook, on the night before, at the foot of three weeks of pages headed *Fiske*.

*Tomorrow is the first time all season either of them meets somebody the ladder didn't hand her. Everyone else Fiske has fought this year, the bracket built for her. Everyone Lira's fought, the bracket built for somebody else. Tomorrow neither of them is what the paper says, and both of them know it, and they're the only two people on the sheet who do.*

Lira found him in the north hall that last evening, after his hour, while he was rolling the cords. She had been running the sequence at half pace on the stone, the short fourth after the half-step, over and over, and she stopped and stood with her towel and asked him one thing.

"How long does she collect?"

"A minute and a half," said Cael. "The same as the first day. Near enough every time. Both her bad openings fall inside it."

"Every time?"

"Four bouts since you sent it. Four pages. A minute and a half, and then the doors start closing." He looked at her. "Why?"

Lira did not answer. She folded the towel in half and in half again, precisely, and hung it over her shoulder, and he saw her lips move once, as Karis's moved before a clause, on a number.

"Go to bed," she said. "You look worse than me."

He had watched Fiske fight four times since Lira's decision, as Lira had wanted him to. Four pages. On every one of them the same thing: a minute and a half of collecting, two bad openings, and then the floor closing behind her opponents like doors in a long corridor, one after another, until there was nowhere left to stand. Fiske never struck anybody. She put her force into the boards where they were about to be, and they arrived on it, and stumbled, and found her hand on them.

On the evening before the bout, crossing the covered walk, Lira and Fiske passed each other under the north stair, in front of the board where both their names stood one above the other.

Cael was ten paces behind and saw it. Neither of them stopped walking.

"Fifteenth," said Fiske.

"Fifteenth," said Lira.

That was the whole of it. Fiske went on toward the halls with her hands at the small of her back, and Lira went on toward the residence with her weight on the ball of her right foot, and neither looked round.

The next afternoon the yard was full to the rail.

Cael had never seen it full. He had seen it crowded, for Nyle and for the Shield fifth-year, with people standing at the backs of the tiers. This was something else. Every tier on all four sides was packed from the lowest step to the rail at the top, and people sat on the steps of the north stair and stood in the archways and leaned out of the windows of the lecture range opposite. The faculty box on the north side had every seat taken and three people standing at its back. The standings clerk, who sat at a small table by the long board for every session of every season, was standing up.

Withrow sat in the west gallery, upright and white-haired, with two people from Ostrand on either side of her whose coats Cael priced, without wanting to, at about a year of residence fees apiece.

He counted, because he could not help counting crowds, the Silver-sheet enrollees he could see on the tiers. Eleven. There had not been more than two at any Copper bout all season.

He did not look for the man with the forms. Twenty-six days now he had kept his eyes off the man. But the brass hood of the lamp hung where it always hung, at the edge of his eye. In its curve the upper east tier lay in afternoon shade, and in the shade, third in a knot of six, a small figure stood with something pale against its chest.

He looked away, at the floor, where the porter was chalking the ring for the last time, slowly, with the face of a man who knew that he was being watched by more people than had ever watched him do anything.

Lira was on the bench at the floor's edge with her towel round her neck and her eyes shut. Brom sat beside Cael on the east tier, very upright, as he sat in church. Karis was on Cael's other side with the grey notebook open on her knee at a fresh page, and she had written the date at the top of it and nothing else, and her pencil was not moving.

Fiske came down the north steps into the sunk floor without hurry, and went to her chalk, and looked at the door frame before she looked at the door.

The adjudicating instructor stood at the table and read the terms in a voice that carried to the rail. A challenge upon the holder's line. Four exchanges, each on the table's glass, each to the cleaner touch or called even. The bout to the practitioner holding more touches at the close of the fourth. A drawn bout to leave the holder where she stood.

The glass was turned. The sand began to run.

"Begin," said the table.

# Chapter 57 — Seals

On the twenty-second of Reaping the residence ran out of ice before the second bell.

Brom went down to the kitchen gate for more, limping, with an empty sack over his shoulder and his strapped hand held away from his body. He came back with the sack full and a heel of yesterday's bread, which the man at the kitchen gate had given him for nothing, because the man at the kitchen gate had seen the final from the top of the north stair and had decided, apparently, that the losing finalist should not have to pay for bread for the rest of his life.

"He says I'm owed," said Brom, dividing the ice into three bags on the common-room table. "I said I lost. He said that's what he meant."

He gave the first bag to Lira, who was on the settle with the bad hip up on two cushions and her face turned to the wall in protest at the whole arrangement. He gave the second to Cael, who had the good chair because he had asked for it. He put the third on his own knee and sat down on the floor by the stove with a grunt.

Karis looked at the three of them and wrote something in the grey notebook.

"Don't," said Lira, to the wall.

"It's a date. I'm writing the date." Karis blotted it. "Third morning of ice."

Cael's hip had stiffened in the night into a single line, crest to inner knee, that did not hurt so much as disagree with everything he asked of it. Two more mornings of it, by his own reckoning. His shoulder was easier. The ache in it had lost its edges and gone dull, like a coin carried a long time in a pocket. Brom's knee had swollen round the strapping, and his right hand still lay on his thigh as if he had been told to keep it there.

So they sat. The house was quiet in a way it had not been for eleven days. Out on the quadrangle there were footsteps going places at an ordinary pace. Nobody in the corridor was talking low.

He had carried one sentence down the records-hall stair the afternoon before, and he still had it. He had not written it. He had meant to, and then he had sat at the window with the pen and found that he did not trust it yet. A sentence made in the first hour after a thing was usually a sentence about how the hour had felt. He wanted to know whether this one would still be standing in a day.

It was still standing. He let it stand a while longer.

At the third bell a boy from the registrar's office came up the stair with a half-sheet for Cael, and Cael took it down to the records hall himself, slowly, with the hip complaining on every step.

Bracken had the enrollment book open on the counter. It was the great ruled volume the house kept its roll in, and the page lay open at Cael's own line. Under the entry of the first week and the baseline of the second month, Bracken had already written the new one, dated and initialled, in the small upright hand.

*Semester evaluation, the twentieth of Reaping. Requirement satisfied. Standing renewed. Panel of record: Gault (presiding), with two. Observed under the charter by the inspecting delegation.*

"I wanted you to see it go in," said Bracken. "Not because it's interesting. It's the dullest line I'll write all month." He turned the book a quarter round so that the line faced Cael. "But it's the line every other line on this page has been waiting for. A provision only lives as long as somebody keeps writing it down."

Cael read it twice. Then he read the line above it, the baseline from his first month, and the line above that, the enrollment itself, with the folder marked in pencil *94 min.* lying beside the book where Bracken had clearly laid it on purpose.

"Thank you," he said, and then, before Bracken could open his mouth, "I know. Correct filing."

"Correct filing," said Bracken, with something that was nearly approval, and shut the book.

---

The delegation's counsel had finished her work on the twenty-second, and at the eighth bell Withrow's counsel gave the house what she expected the finding to say.

She did it in the records hall, at the long table, to nine people. Withrow sat halfway down one side as she always did. Bracken was beside her, and his inky-knuckled clerk sat at the corner with a pen he had been told not to use. Gault had come, and Rooke had come and was standing, and the Ash instructor sat at the far end because she had signed the evaluation and liked to know what became of her signatures. Karis was there because her name stood at the head of the provision's file. Cael was there because the provision was his.

The counsel stood with her hands on her leather case and spoke in the voice she kept for notices.

"Favourable in substance. Neutral in tone."

Nobody moved.

"That is the whole of it, and the rest is detail," she said. "There will be one clerical remark. There is always one, because a delegation that finds nothing at all looks to its own masters as though it had not been looking. Last time it was the fire-watch's oil book. I would wager a month's fee it's the oil book again." She opened the case, took out nothing, and shut it. "The enrollment basis will be found sound. The clause unrepealed, the enrollment conforming to its language. The documentary practice will be called complete, which is the highest word the form allows. It will not say we did well. A finding cannot say that. It will say there was nothing to find."

"After eleven days," said Gault.

"After eleven days, with an Archmarshal at the head of the manifest." The counsel looked at Withrow. "If I were writing for the newsletters I would call that a triumph."

"You aren't," said Withrow.

"No. And you've told me four times in twenty years that you can't abide adjectives, so I struck it before I came in." Her voice did not change at all. "I mention that I struck it so that it's in the room."

Withrow took her spectacles off and folded them.

"I can't abide them on paper," she said. "On paper they rot. Somebody reads them in ten years and wonders what you were frightened of." She put the spectacles in her pocket. "In this room you may say what you like. Say it again."

The counsel considered the ceiling for a moment.

"A triumph," she said, perfectly flat, as if it were the name of a ship.

Rooke made a sound by the fireplace that might, in another man, have been a laugh. Bracken's clerk wrote something after all and was looked at, and stopped.

Cael sat at the table's foot and watched Withrow. She had not smiled. She had sat through eleven days without once raising her voice or her pace, and she was not going to start now that the coaches were being greased. But her hands, which had lain flat on the table through every sitting, had turned over. They lay palm up on the wood, open and loose, like the hands of somebody who has set down a weight at the end of a long carry and has not yet thought to pick anything else up.

He wrote that in the notebook afterward, on the page headed *Withrow*, in three lines. It was the only thing he had ever seen her do that she had not decided to do.

---

Brom told them about Rooke at supper, and he told it word for word.

Brom did not often do that. He remembered exact words the way other people remembered faces, but he kept the gift in a drawer and took it out perhaps twice a year, when he thought a sentence had been made with care and ought not to be worn down by handling. He took it out now with his bowl in front of him and his strapped hand flat beside the bowl.

"The cohort's board, by the covered walk's end. Third bell, near enough. I was reading the cohort standings." He set down his spoon. "He came along the walk. He didn't stop to talk to me, he stopped to look at the board. Then he said it to the board."

"Said what?" asked Lira.

Brom said it in Rooke's voice, which was a dry voice that did not lift at the end of anything.

"'Your cohort standing holds. So, apparently, does your friend's paperwork. Noted.'"

There was a pause.

"And?" said Lira.

"And he went."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Lira put her spoon down. "That's nothing. A man's been calling the provision a mistake since the first week, in a faculty session, with the clerk writing it down, and he says *noted* to a board on his way past?"

"It isn't nothing." Brom turned his bowl a quarter turn on the table, the way he squared a guard. "He minuted that he was against it. He said so where it would be written down. Every faculty session since, it's been in the book that Rooke said the provision would cost the house." He looked at his hand. "He's never going to say he was wrong. He doesn't say things like that. What he does is change what he does, and then make sure it's been seen." He lifted the hand an inch and let it down. "He adjusted, and he made sure somebody heard him do it."

"You," said Karis.

"Me. Because I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell it straight." Brom picked up his spoon again. "That's his apology. He sent it by carter."

Lira looked at him for some time.

"You like him," she said.

"I don't like him. He's made me worse for a whole season on purpose." Brom ate a spoonful of stew. "Then he made me better, on the day it counted, and I lost by one beat anyway. I don't like him. I'd go through a wall for him."

Cael thought of the north arch on the day the notice came, and Rooke standing under it with the traffic of the bell going round him. He had corrected himself to Cael there, once, out loud and unasked. This was the same man doing the same thing at a greater distance, so that nobody could call it a kindness.

*He does it with documents*, Cael thought. *Even when there's no document. He talks so that it'll be in somebody's book.*

He wrote it in his own book that night, under Rooke's name, with the date.

---

The delegation left on the twenty-third, in light rain, in the same three carriages it had come in.

Cael watched from the upper tier of the Crown yard. He had watched them come in from the same stone at the east end, and it seemed to him that a person who had stood in the open to see a thing arrive ought not to watch it leave from somewhere drier. The tier was empty at that hour, and lawful. The rain came across it in thin grey slants off the river and beaded on the shoulders of his coat.

Below him the courtyard filled slowly. Porters brought the first of the iron-banded chests out of the records hall on a handbarrow. The escort walked the horses up and down to keep them warm. The delegation's counsel came out talking, with her clerk two steps behind her holding an oilcloth over a box, and got into the first carriage still talking. The three clerks followed in their order, the young one carrying, the old one with his bad knee deciding what went where.

The Archmarshal had gone to his carriage before the rain began. Cael had not seen him go. He found he did not mind.

Assessor Ilsev came down the records-hall steps last of the manifest, with her own case in her own hand. Every porter on the bluff had offered to carry it at one time or another, and none had ever been allowed.

At the foot of the steps she stopped. She stood about five yards from the bottom of the tier stair, and she lifted her head and looked straight up at him through the rain.

He went down. Staying where he was would have been a kind of hiding, and he had done enough of that to know the difference.

"Enrollee."

"Assessor."

"I'm going to tell you one thing," she said. "It isn't kind, and you shouldn't take it so." She set the case on the bottom step, out of the wet. "The sitting on the fifteenth found that the clause stands, and that your enrollment fits its words. That is a finding about a sentence. Some people wrote that sentence a long time ago, for reasons of their own, and none of them ever heard of you. Had they written it differently, the finding would have gone the other way, and you would be exactly the same person you are this morning."

"I know."

"I think you do, which is why I'm saying it to you and not to someone who'd thank me." She took up the case again. "Most people in your place want the text on their side because they think it means something about them. It doesn't. It is simply on your side, which is worth an enormous amount and is not the same as being loved by anybody."

The rain went on falling between them. Something at the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly, and was put away.

"Keep the two apart," said Ilsev. "People who mix them up are dreadful in a hearing room. And they lose."

She walked to the second carriage without looking back. Cael stood at the foot of the tier stair with the rain on his face and found that he was not cold at all.

---

Havel checked the seals.

He did it under the records-hall porch, out of the rain, with the list on a board against his forearm and the chests set down in a row in front of him on the flags. There were four. They had come up the bluff road eleven days ago under four seals apiece, and they would go down it under four new ones, pressed that morning at the delegation's working table in the counsel's wax and the senior seat's. His list had sixteen lines, one for each seal. Beside each line was the impression's number and the hand that had pressed it.

He went down the list with his pencil. Chest one, the lid's left, a clean impression, the number legible. Tick. The lid's right. Tick. The hasp. Tick.

It did not matter that he had watched every one of them pressed. He had watched, and he had written the numbers as they were pressed, and now he was checking the wax against what he had written. Nobody would ever check after him. The chests would go into the registry's vault at the end of the road, and a vault clerk would sign for four chests, not sixteen seals. The check was his and nobody else's, and so he did it slowly.

Somebody had fixed a long run of canvas along the porch roof to keep the rain off the chests. It made the light inside the porch brown and close, so that the courtyard beyond it looked very bright, though it was not bright at all.

He knew where the boy was.

He had known on the first morning. He had worked it out on the road from the plan of the yard, and he had been right, because he had seen the shape on the upper tier at the edge of his eye as the carriage came in and had not turned his head. He had not turned his head since. Not on the covered walk on the thirteenth, with the two streams jammed against the dry wall. Not at the final, from the second row of the north tier. Not at the long table on the fifteenth, nor across the brass of the wing on the twentieth. Eleven days.

That was correct. The card in his pocket said that an officer of an inspecting delegation would not acknowledge a party under review beyond ordinary courtesy. A records officer checking seals under a porch had no business with the upper tier of a training yard, courteous or otherwise. So it was correct, and he did it.

It was not why he did it.

Chest two. The lid's left. The wax had run a little at one edge, and he bent close to read the number through it, and it was the right number. Tick.

The reason he would have given, had anybody asked, was the one he had given himself at the gate. A look from a man in his coat was a thing the boy would have to carry. He would have to write it down somewhere and decide what it had meant, and then decide again in a month when he knew more. Havel had watched the boy's face at the pump step in Ardenmere, two years ago, and he had watched it at the end of a long hearing table at Greyvane. He had seen what it cost the boy to be looked at by men in that coat. A records officer could not do much about that. He could decline to add to it.

That was true, and it was not the whole of it either.

The rest was the pear. He had never been able to put the pear into any sentence that made sense, and he did not try now. A boy on a pump step had held out a pear in a twist of paper to the officer questioning him, and the officer had wanted it and had not taken it. Ever since, Havel had felt that he had been offered something once that he had not been entitled to refuse. A man who has refused a thing like that does not go looking up at tiers afterward. He does not get a second offer, and he ought not to look as though he were hoping for one.

He went down the list. Chest three. Chest four. Sixteen ticks.

At the foot of the steps the assessor had stopped. Havel saw it without looking, the way he saw everything he did not look at: a grey shape halting where grey shapes walked on. Then another shape came down from the tier to meet her. Their voices did not carry under the canvas. He bent to the fourth chest's hasp, though he had already ticked it, and read its number again.

She had a referral in her case. He knew, because he had logged it. It had gone up a level inside the first recess on the fifteenth, three sheets cross-referenced in his own hand, and it would go into the chain at the end of this road. Somewhere above them both a desk would get it, and the desk would either answer or not. He thought it would not. Nothing had ever come back with a name on it.

There were four lines in his notebook now. He would not write a fifth on this hill. There was nothing to put in it that the fourth had not already said.

The porters lifted the first chest onto the boot of the second carriage. Havel walked beside it with the list and watched them strap it, and initialled the list at the foot, and gave the list to the young clerk to carry, because the young clerk carried. Then he got in.

He sat where he had sat coming up: in the second carriage, facing backward, with the counsel's knees an inch from his own. The counsel had begun to talk about the inn at the turn of the river road, and whether its beds had been aired since the last delegation, which had been hers. Ilsev got in last, set her case on her knees and shut her eyes.

The carriage moved. Facing backward, Havel had the whole bluff in his window as they went down it: the gate, the wall, the long grey range with its tiers open to the rain. It drew away from him a bend at a time.

He looked at the list in his lap. It was all ticked. He went down it once more anyway, from the top, line by line, until the road had turned enough that the bluff was behind the shoulder of the hill and there was nothing in the window but rain and the river and the ferry landing, where two men in road-grey coats stood under a lamp exactly as they had stood eleven days ago.

Correct and careful, he thought. They were not the same thing, and on most days he managed both.

---

The carriages were gone inside a quarter of an hour. Cael stayed on the tier a while longer in the rain.

Havel had come out of the records hall with the chests and stood under the porch checking seals, and he had not looked up. Not once. Cael had counted the days on his fingers in the empty yard: eleven days, from the first bend of the bluff road to the last bend going down, and in all of them not once.

The first week he had filed it as caution. A man in that coat had every reason to be careful about where his eyes went. In the second week he had stopped being sure. Caution looked at a thing and then away. This was a man who had decided where the thing was and arranged his whole head around not looking there.

He did not have a word for it. He did not try to make one. Some things went into the notebook better without a name, because a name was a guess, and he had made enough guesses about men in grey coats to last him.

That night he sat at the window with the new pen and the Log, and the first sentence took him three tries. That had almost never happened. The first try was too clever. The second explained itself before it had said anything. The third was simply the fact.

*He never asked what I am.*

*Everybody asks. It's been the first question in every room for two years. Some ask it straight and some ask it by waiting for an answer, and Gault asks it by refusing to ask it and telling you why. Half this bluff has answered it among themselves without me, in more versions than I've bothered to count.*

*He had eleven questions written on the road. That wasn't one of them. He asked whether the record and the practitioner match.*

*I've been carrying that since the east room. Here is where it lands, and I don't like where it lands.*

*You ask what a thing is when you don't know. You check whether the record matches when you think you know already, and you want to see whether the file's been kept properly.*

*So somewhere above all this, what I am isn't an open question. It's been settled by somebody, written down, and filed at a height where Karis and I couldn't find a printed word of it in a library the size of a hill. The question was never me. The question is what to do about the thing they've settled. That isn't his sum. He told me so, and I believe him.*

*Plainly, then, so I stop dressing it:*

*The system isn't confused about me.*

*Confused systems ask. This one checks.*

*For two years I've believed I was a gap. A thing that fell between the lines because somebody drew the lines carelessly. I argued that in a hearing room, and it was true, and it held, and I'd argue it again tomorrow.*

*But nobody sends their best man to look at a gap. A gap doesn't get a designation on a file that nobody will put a name to. A gap doesn't get an Archmarshal who walks right up to the edge of the biggest question in the room and leaves it alone because it isn't his to ask.*

*Two things I don't know. What it thinks it knows about me, and why it built itself to know it.*

*Not this year's. This year's was answered on the twentieth with a stamp.*

*That one's next.*

He read it through once and did not change a word, and blotted it, and went to bed with the ice on his hip.

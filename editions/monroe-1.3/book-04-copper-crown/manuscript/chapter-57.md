# Chapter 57 — Seals

On the twenty-second of Reaping the residence ran out of ice before the second bell.

Brom went down to the kitchen gate for more, limping on the knee the final had left him, with an empty sack over his shoulder and his strapped hand held away from his body. He came back with the sack full and a heel of yesterday's bread, which the man at the kitchen gate had given him for nothing. The man at the kitchen gate had seen the final from the top of the north stair and had decided, apparently, that the losing finalist should not have to pay for bread for the rest of his life.

"He says I'm owed," said Brom, dividing the ice into three bags on the common-room table. "I said I lost. He said that's what he meant."

He gave the first bag to Lira, who was on the settle with the bad hip up on two cushions and her face turned to the wall in protest at the whole arrangement. Cael got the second; he had the good chair, having asked for it. The third went on his own knee as he lowered himself, grunting, to the floor by the stove.

Karis surveyed the wreckage, and something went into the grey notebook.

"Don't," said Lira, to the wall.

"It's a date. I'm writing the date." Karis blotted it. "Third morning of ice."

Cael's hip had stiffened in the night into a single line, crest to inner knee, that did not hurt so much as disagree with everything he asked of it. It would want two more mornings, by his reckoning, and the shoulder was easier already, the ache in it gone dull and edgeless like a coin carried a long time in a pocket. Brom's knee had swollen round the strapping, and his right hand still lay on his thigh where somebody had told him to keep it.

Karis had her marbled book open too, the one with the watchers in it, and she turned it so that Cael could see the last page without having to get up.

Two at the ferry landing. Two at the bluff road's foot. The change on the bell, every four hours, and a small tick for every change she had seen with her own eyes from the library's top window. There was a line for every day since the delegation came up the hill, and every line was the same as the one above it.

"Not one late," she said. "Not one extra. An Archmarshal in the building for eleven days, and the men at the bottom of the hill didn't so much as change their boots."

"They wouldn't," said Lira, without turning her head. "Nobody told them anything was happening."

"That's my point." Karis shut the book. "Nobody told them. Whatever is paying for those coats doesn't talk to whatever sent him. Or they talk, and there's nothing to tell." She put the book in her satchel. "It goes in as a question. Not as an answer."

"You write everything down as a question."

"I file things under what they are."

So they sat, and the house was quiet in a way it had not been since the carriages came: footsteps out on the quadrangle going places at an ordinary pace, and nobody in the corridor talking low.

One sentence had come down the records-hall stair with him the afternoon before, and it was still with him. He had meant to write it; he had even sat down to it at the window with the pen; and he had found he did not trust it yet. A sentence made in the first hour after a thing was usually a sentence about how the hour had felt. He wanted to know whether this one would still be standing in a day.

It was still standing, so he let it stand a while longer.

At the third bell a boy from the registrar's office came up the stair with a half-sheet for Cael, and Cael took it down to the records hall himself, slowly, with the hip complaining on every step.

Bracken had the enrollment book open on the counter, the great ruled volume the house kept its roll in, at the page that held Cael's own line. Under the entry of the first week and the baseline of the second month, Bracken had already written the new one, dated and initialled, in the small upright hand.

*Semester evaluation, the twentieth of Reaping. Requirement satisfied. Standing renewed. Panel of record: Gault (presiding), with two. Inspecting delegation present, under the charter.*

"I wanted you here when it went in," said Bracken. "Not because it's interesting. It's the dullest line I'll write all month." With one finger he swung the book round on the counter until the new line lay square to Cael's eye. "But it's the line every other line on this page has been waiting for. A provision only lives as long as somebody keeps writing it down."

Cael read it twice. Then he read the line above it, the baseline from his second month, and the line above that, the enrollment itself, with the folder marked in pencil *Ninety-four minutes.* lying beside the book where Bracken had clearly laid it on purpose.

"Thank you," he said, and then, before Bracken could open his mouth, "I know. Correct filing."

"Correct filing," said Bracken, with something that was nearly approval, and shut the book.

---

The delegation's counsel had finished her work on the twenty-second, and at the eighth bell Withrow's counsel gave the house what she expected the finding to say.

She did it in the records hall, at the long table, to nine people. Withrow sat halfway down one side as she always did, with Bracken next to her and, at the corner, his inky-knuckled clerk holding a pen he had been forbidden to use. Gault had come, and Rooke had come and was standing, and the Ash instructor sat at the far end because she had signed the evaluation and liked to know what became of her signatures. Karis had a chair because the provision's file began with her name. Cael was there because the provision was his.

The counsel stood with her hands on her leather case and spoke in the voice she kept for notices.

"Favourable in substance. Neutral in tone."

Nobody moved.

"Those six words are the whole of it," she said. "The rest is detail. There will be one clerical remark. There is always one, because a delegation that finds nothing at all looks to its own masters as though it had not been looking. Last time it was the fire-watch's oil book. I would wager a month's fee it's the oil book again." Her fingers worked the clasp of the case open, and shut it again on nothing. "The enrollment basis will be found sound. Alive, the clause; fitted to it, the enrollment. The documentary practice will be called complete, which is the highest word the form allows. It will not say we did well. A finding cannot say that. Its whole message will be that nobody found anything."

"After all that," said Gault.

"After eleven days of being looked at by an Archmarshal." The counsel looked at Withrow. "If I were writing for the newsletters I would call that a triumph."

"You aren't," said Withrow.

"No. And you've told me more than once that you dislike adjectives, so I struck it before I came in." Her voice did not change at all. "I mention that I struck it so that it's in the room."

Withrow took her spectacles off and folded them.

"I dislike them on paper," she said. "On paper they rot. Somebody reads them in ten years and wonders what you were frightened of." She put the spectacles in her pocket. "Here you may say what you please. Say it again."

The counsel weighed this with her eyes on the beams.

"A triumph," she said, perfectly flat, as if it were the name of a ship.

Rooke made a sound by the fireplace that might, in another man, have been a laugh. Bracken's clerk wrote something after all and was looked at, and stopped.

Cael sat at the table's foot and watched Withrow. She had not smiled; she had sat through the whole inspection without once raising her voice or her pace, and she was not going to start now that the coaches were being greased. Through every sitting her hands had lain flat on the table, and now they had turned over. They lay palm up on the wood, open and loose, like the hands of somebody who has set down a weight at the end of a long carry and has not yet thought to pick anything else up.

He wrote that in the notebook afterward, on the page headed *Withrow*, in three lines. Of everything he had watched her do since the first week, it was the one thing she had not chosen.

---

Brom told them about Rooke at supper, and he told it word for word.

Brom did not often do that. He remembered exact words the way other people remembered faces, but he kept the gift in a drawer and took it out perhaps twice a year, when he thought a sentence had been made with care and ought not to be worn down by handling. He took it out now with his bowl in front of him and his strapped hand flat beside the bowl.

"The cohort's board, by the covered walk's end. Third bell, near enough. I was reading the cohort standings." He set down his spoon. "He came along the walk. He didn't stop to talk to me, he stopped to look at the board. Then he said it to the board."

"Said what?" asked Lira.

Brom said it in Rooke's voice, which was a dry voice that did not lift at the end of anything.

"'Your cohort standing holds. So, apparently, does your friend's paperwork. Noted.'"

There was a pause.

"And?" said Lira.

"And he went."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Lira put her spoon down. "That's nothing. A man's been calling the provision a mistake since the first week, in a faculty session, with the clerk writing it down, and he says *noted* to a board on his way past?"

"It isn't nothing." Brom turned his bowl a quarter turn on the table, the way he squared a guard. "He minuted that he was against it. He said so where it would be written down. Every faculty session since, it's been in the book that Rooke said the provision would cost the house." He looked at his hand. "Wrong is a word he won't say, not about himself. What he does is change what he does, and then make sure it's been seen." The hand rose an inch from the wood and settled again. "He adjusted, and he made sure somebody heard him do it."

"You," said Karis.

"Me. Because I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell it straight." Brom picked up his spoon again. "That's his apology. He sent it by carter."

Lira looked at him for some time.

"You like him," she said.

"I don't like him. He's made me worse for a whole season on purpose." Brom ate a spoonful of stew. "Then he made me better, on the day it counted, and I lost by one beat anyway. I don't like him. I'd go through a wall for him."

Cael thought of the north arch on the day the notice came, and Rooke standing under it with the traffic of the bell going round him. He had corrected himself to Cael there, once, out loud and unasked. This was the same man doing the same thing at a greater distance, so that nobody could call it a kindness.

*He does it with documents*, Cael thought. *Even when there's no document. He talks so that it'll be in somebody's book.*

He wrote it in his own book that night, under Rooke's name, with the date.

---

By the first bell of the twenty-third it was raining, not hard, a fine grey rain that came up the river in no hurry and settled on everything. The three carriages stood in the courtyard below the records hall with their shutters up and the horses steaming.

Cael stood high on the Crown yard's top tier. From the same east-end stone he had watched the delegation's arrival, and some time in the night he had decided to watch its going from there too. A person who had seen a thing arrive from out in the open had no business watching it leave from under a roof. The tier was empty and lawful, and the wet came across it off the river in slants and beaded on his sleeves.

Below him the leaving went forward in its order. Porters wheeled the iron-banded chests out of the records hall on a handbarrow and set them down under the porch, where somebody had rigged a run of canvas to keep them dry. The escort walked the horses up and down the cobbles. The delegation's counsel came out talking, with her clerk behind her holding an oilcloth over a box, and climbed into the second carriage without pausing in her sentence. The three clerks came after her in their order: the young one carrying, the list clerk walking beside him, the old one with the bad knee pointing at things.

Withrow stood on the records-hall steps in her plain coat with Bracken a pace behind her. She shook the counsel's hand at the carriage door and said something short, and Bracken handed up a receipt for the house's copies, which the counsel signed on her knee.

The Archmarshal had gone to his carriage before the rain started, and Cael had not seen him go, and found to his surprise that he did not mind.

Last of the manifest came Assessor Ilsev, carrying her own case down the steps herself. Porters had offered to carry it every day of the visit and been refused every time, so politely that two of them had started offering just to hear how she would do it.

At the last step she halted and lifted her face to the tier, rain and all.

Cael went down, because staying up there would have been a kind of hiding, and he had done enough of that in his life to know it when he saw it.

She waited for him at the foot of the steps, the case held in front of her in both hands.

"Enrollee."

"Assessor."

"You were at the table on the fifteenth," said Ilsev. "So you heard what was found. Tell me what it was."

He thought about it first, because she was not a woman who asked a question to fill a silence.

"That the clause was never struck out," he said. "That my enrollment matches what it says."

"Yes. Now tell me what it was *about*."

"The clause."

"The clause." She nodded. He had passed a small test, and she had not expected him to fail it. "Not you. A handful of people wrote that sentence long ago, for their own reasons, about practitioners none of them had met. They never heard of you and could not have. If they had written one word differently, the finding on the fifteenth would have gone the other way. And you would be standing in this rain just as you are now, no better and no worse." She shifted the case to one hand. "I'm saying this because it isn't kind. Don't take it for kindness."

"I won't."

"Good. People like you nearly always hope that a text which favours them is passing judgment on them. It isn't. It is simply on your side, which is worth an enormous amount and is not the same as being loved by anybody."

The rain fell between them. At one corner of her mouth something moved, very slightly, and was put away again before it could become anything.

"Keep those two apart all your life," said Ilsev. "I've sat across from people who couldn't. They were dreadful company at a table. They also lost."

She turned and walked to the second carriage and got in, and did not look back. Cael stayed at the foot of the tier stair with the rain running out of his hair, and felt no cold at all.

He had a page on her, the thickest in the book and, until now, the least use. He knew already what he would write on it tonight. *She told me the truth about the law and called it unkind, so that I would know it was true.*

---

Havel checked the seals.

He did it under the records-hall porch, out of the rain, with the list on a board against his forearm and the chests set down in a row in front of him on the flags. There were four of them, and they had come up the bluff road under four seals apiece, and they would go down it under four new ones, pressed that morning at the delegation's working table in the counsel's wax and the senior seat's. His list had sixteen lines, one for each seal, and beside each line the impression's number and the hand that had pressed it.

Pencil in hand, he worked down the list. Chest one, the lid's left, a clean impression, the number legible. Tick. The lid's right. Tick. The hasp. Tick.

It did not matter that he had watched every one of them pressed. He had watched, and he had written the numbers as they were pressed, and now he was checking the wax against what he had written. Nobody would ever check after him: the chests would go into the registry's vault at the end of the road, and a vault clerk would sign for four chests, not sixteen seals. The check was his and nobody else's, and so he did it slowly.

Somebody had fixed a long run of canvas along the porch roof to keep the rain off the chests. It made the light inside the porch brown and close, so that the courtyard beyond it looked very bright, though it was not bright at all.

He knew where the boy was.

He had known on the first morning, because he had worked it out on the road from the plan of the yard, and he had been right, because he had seen the shape on the upper tier at the edge of his eye as the carriage came in and had not turned his head. He had not turned his head since. Not on the covered walk on the thirteenth, with the two streams jammed against the dry wall. Not at the final, from the second row of the north tier. Not at the long table on the fifteenth, nor across the brass of the wing on the twentieth. Eleven days.

That was correct, since the card in his pocket said that an officer of an inspecting delegation would not acknowledge a party under review beyond ordinary courtesy. A records officer checking seals under a porch had no business with the upper tier of a training yard, courteous or otherwise. Correct, then; and so he did it.

It was not why he did it.

Chest two. The lid's left. The wax had run a little at one edge, and he bent close to read the number through it, and it was the right number. Tick.

Asked, he would have offered the reason he had given himself at the gate. A look from a man in his coat was a thing the boy would have to carry. He would have to write it down somewhere and decide what it had meant, and then decide again in a month when he knew more. Havel had watched the boy's face at the pump step in Ardenmere, two years ago, and he had watched it at the end of a long hearing table at Greyvane. He had seen what it cost the boy to be looked at by men in that coat. A records officer could not do much about that, but he could decline to add to it.

True. But not the whole of it.

The rest was the pear. He had never been able to put the pear into any sentence that made sense, and he did not try now. A boy on a pump step had held out a pear in a twist of paper to the officer questioning him, and the officer had wanted it and had not taken it. Ever since, Havel had felt that he had been offered something once that he had not been entitled to refuse. A man who has refused a thing like that does not go looking up at tiers afterward. He does not get a second offer, and he ought not to look as though he were hoping for one.

He went down the list. Chest three. Chest four. Sixteen ticks.

The registrar came out under the canvas while he was initialling the foot of the sheet. The house kept a log of its own of what went out of its doors, and somebody had to sign the house's side of it, and Bracken was not a man who sent a clerk to sign a thing he could sign himself.

"Sixteen?" said Bracken.

"Sixteen."

Bracken checked for himself. He went along the row and looked at each seal for about as long as it takes to read a number, and wrote a figure in his own small book, and then held the book out for Havel's initials beside his. Havel gave them, and neither man remarked on what the other had just done; it would have been like remarking that the other had a nose.

Havel had sat at the foot of the long table on the fifteenth while that man's index was turned over by the best counsel the delegation had, sheet by sheet, for most of a day, and had watched it find every paper she asked for inside a minute and a half. He had never been in a position to tell a registrar that his file was good, and he was not in one now. He could hand back a book with his initials in the right column and his eyes on the right line, and so he did that.

"Safe road," said Bracken, which was ordinary courtesy, and went back in.

At the foot of the steps the assessor had stopped. Havel saw it without looking, the way he saw everything he did not look at: a grey shape halting where grey shapes walked on. Then another shape came down from the tier to meet her. Their voices did not carry under the canvas. He bent to the fourth chest's hasp, though he had already ticked it, and read its number again.

She had a referral in her case; he knew, because he had logged it. It had gone up a level inside the first recess on the fifteenth, three sheets cross-referenced in his own hand, and it would go into the chain at the end of this road. Somewhere above them both a desk would get it, and the desk would either answer or not. He thought it would not. Nothing had ever come back with a name on it.

There were four lines in his notebook now, and a fifth would not be written on this hill, because there was nothing to put in it that the fourth had not already said.

The porters lifted the first chest onto the boot of the second carriage. Havel walked beside it with the list and watched them strap it, and initialled the list at the foot, and gave the list to the young clerk to carry, because the young clerk carried. Then he got in.

He sat where he had sat coming up: in the second carriage, facing backward, with the counsel's knees an inch from his own. The counsel had begun to talk about the inn at the turn of the river road, and whether its beds had been aired since the last delegation, which had been hers. Ilsev was already in her corner with her case on her knees and her eyes shut.

The carriage moved, and facing backward, Havel had the whole bluff in his window as they went down it: the gate, the wall, the long grey range with its tiers open to the rain. It drew away from him a bend at a time.

His eyes went down to the list in his lap, where every line had its tick. He went down it once more anyway, from the top, line by line, until the road had turned enough that the bluff was behind the shoulder of the hill and there was nothing in the window but rain and the river and the ferry landing, where two men in road-grey coats stood under a lamp just as they had stood on the morning he arrived.

Across from him the counsel broke off her sentence about the inn.

"You were a long while over those seals," she said. "Was anything wrong with them?"

"No."

"Then why so long?"

Havel thought about how to answer that within his instructions, and found that the true answer was inside them after all.

"Nobody checks them after me," he said.

The counsel looked at him with her head on one side, the way she looked at a clause she had not expected to like. Then she took up her sentence about the inn again from the exact word where she had left it.

Correct and careful, he thought. They were not the same thing, and on most days he managed both.

---

In a quarter of an hour the carriages were off the bluff, and the road below the gate was empty except for the rain. Cael lingered.

Havel had come out of the records hall with the chests and stood under the canvas with his list, ticking. He had not raised his eyes. Cael had seen him not raise them. A man checking wax at a porch has a dozen small reasons to lift his head, a horse moving or a voice or the rain changing, and Havel had lifted it for none of them.

Eleven days. On the first morning Cael had put it down as caution, because anyone in that coat had reasons to be careful where his eyes went. In the second week he had stopped being sure. Caution looks at a thing and then away. This was a man who had worked out where the thing was and then built his whole day round not looking there, as carefully as another man would have built it round looking.

Somewhere a word for it existed, and Cael let it be. A name was a guess, and he had made enough guesses about men in grey coats to last him a long while. He wrote it down without one.

Lira came down the residence passage after supper without the stick. Her four days had ended that afternoon, and she was walking the length of the passage and back as the safety instructor had told her to, slowly, with one hand trailing along the wall and her face daring anybody to comment.

At his open door she paused, eyes on the ice on his hip.

"Three days for you," she said.

"Ends tonight."

"Four for me. Ended at the fourth bell." Behind her the passage was long; ahead of her, longer. She weighed both. "We're a fine pair. Brom says he's going to buy a barrow and wheel the pair of us to lectures and charge the first-years to watch."

"He won't."

"He might. He's been free with his money since the man at the gate stopped charging him for bread." She pushed off from the doorframe. "Write your thing. You've had it in your mouth all day like a stone."

She went on down the passage, one hand on the wall, counting her steps under her breath, and he listened to her turn at the far end and come back past his door without stopping.

That night at the window the first sentence would not sit for Hesk's pen.

Try one was too clever; he struck it. He wrote it again and it spent half its length explaining itself before it said anything, and he struck that too. The third time he stopped trying to say what it meant and wrote down only what had happened.

*He never asked what I am.*

*Count the rooms. Coss asked it with his whole coat. Quenna asked once, never again, and taught me anyway. Gault won't ask it, says so to my face, and keeps saying so. Ephram turned it into a promise to wait, out loud, on the yard. This bluff has answered it among itself at supper more ways than I've bothered to write down.*

*The Archmarshal had eleven questions and didn't ask it once. His business was the fit between paper and person.*

*I've turned that over since the east room. Here's what it comes to.*

*If you don't know what a thing is, you ask. If you think you already know, you don't ask. You go and look at the paper, and then at the thing, and see whether whoever kept the paper kept it properly. That's what he did. He came to audit a file. The file was about me; that's all I was in it.*

*So somebody, somewhere, has already written down an answer to what I am, too high for Karis and me to reach with a whole library to stand on. Whatever it says, it was settled before he came up the hill. His work was checking. Doing something about it belongs to other men. He told me so across the table, and I believe him. He'd no reason to lie to me and every habit against it.*

*Bare, then, with nothing on it:*

*The system isn't confused about me.*

*Confused systems ask. This one checks.*

*For two years the word I've had for myself is gap: the thing that fell between badly drawn lines. I argued it at Greyvane. I'd argue it tomorrow and win again, because it's true.*

*But look at what a gap gets. Nothing. Nobody watches a gap. Nobody writes a designation for one and files it where no name comes back. Nobody sends the best evaluator they own to sit four feet from a gap and then leave its biggest question alone on purpose.*

*Two lines for the list, then. First: what that paper says, and who decided it. Second: why anybody would build a whole apparatus that knows a thing like that before the boy has done anything at all.*

*Not this year's. This year's question was whether I could hold. I held on the twentieth. A clerk stamped it.*

*Those are next.*

He read it through and changed nothing, then blotted it and shut the book, and sat for a while with his hand flat on the cover and the rain going on outside. The ice went to bed with him, and he slept better than he had any right to.

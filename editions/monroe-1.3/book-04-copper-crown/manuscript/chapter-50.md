# Chapter 50 — A Working Method

Bracken was in the back room when Cael got there, which he had not expected, with his coat off and his waistcoat unbuttoned and a founding-era volume open across his forearm like a waiter's napkin.

Karis was at the long press against the far wall. She had her sleeves pushed above her elbows and ink to the second joint of every finger, and she was sliding volumes into their places one at a time, reading each pencilled shelf-mark on the inside board before she let go of it. She did not look round when the door opened, and she did not have a pen in her hand, and Cael realised that he could not remember the last time he had seen her without one in the whole of the term.

"Enrollee," said Bracken. "Good. Take the low shelves. I can't bend the way I could, and Miss Karis won't let me near the high ones in case I put something back in the wrong century."

So Cael took the low shelves, and for a while the three of them worked without talking, in the smell of old paper and lamp oil and the beeswax Bracken rubbed into his box corners. It was the kind of quiet Cael had grown used to in carrels and archives over the past year, where nobody needed to say anything because everybody was doing what the others would have done.

Bracken broke it, which surprised him.

"I'll tell you a thing I didn't know until today," said the registrar. He was holding a volume up to the lamp to read its board. "Twelve years I've had this office, and I've always believed the best day a file can have is the day you close it. You tie the last box. You write the count on the lid. It's done, and it's right, and you go home." He slid the volume into its place and squared it with one finger. "It isn't. The best day a file can have is the day somebody who wishes it ill opens every box, with a good mind and a whole morning, and can't find anything wrong."

"She said your index was good," said Cael.

"She did." Bracken did not smile, quite. "I've had my work praised by people who liked me. It's pleasant. I'd never before had it praised by somebody who'd come all the way up the river hoping to dislike it." He reached for the next volume. "It's a different thing altogether. I find I'm too old to know what to do with it."

He worked a while longer, and then he took the last empty box under his arm, looked once along the press with the air of a man counting soldiers, and went to the door.

"Lock up behind you, Miss Karis. The key's on the nail. Not later than the eighth bell, please, or the porter will think we've been robbed and wake the whole range."

They heard his steps go away down the records hall, slow and even, and the outer door close.

Karis slid a volume home and did not let go of it at once.

"Did you see my writing?" she said, without turning round.

"From the wall. At the transitional article."

"I thought you might have." She took her hand off the spine. "I could feel it go. Like a table tipping. I tried to straighten it and it wouldn't straighten, so I left it." She turned round at last. "It isn't a finding. I want you to know that before I say anything. Nothing I'm about to say would stand up in that hall."

"All right," said Cael.

On the long table lay the one book that was not a founding volume. It was the directive, all forty-one pages of it, the copy Bracken's clerk had made and a librarian had sealed, and down its fore-edge stood a row of small green paper tabs.

Karis opened it without sitting down.

"You know the green ones," she said. "Bracken put them in. One for each place in the whole directive where it takes an old word out of the law and puts a new word in." She ran her finger down the four of them, just above the paper. "And this one," she turned to a page with a white slip pinned to it and a thread of red through the slip, "is the transitional article. Havel read it out this morning. It's what won."

She laid one forefinger on the last green tab and the other on the white slip, and held them there, as people hold two fingers on a map to measure a road.

"Count the articles between," she said.

He leaned over and counted, and it did not take long.

"Four."

"Four." Every word came out level and even, as though somebody else had written them and she was only the one reading them out. "One directive. One drafting. As near as anybody could ever say, one table of men with the same ink. On this side of my finger is the article that tells every academy how to take enrollment by demonstration and change it into something else. It doesn't end the practice; it walks everybody out of it. And on that side of my finger is the last of the articles that take old words away."

Cael said nothing, because he had seen where she was going as soon as she touched the tabs, and he wanted to hear her go there in her own way and at her own pace, without any help from him.

"You've got it in your old notebook," said Karis. "In a box you drew round it when you were younger and didn't know what it meant. I've got it twice in mine. We read all three copies aloud to each other at Greyvane, in Prynn's archive, at the reading shelf, a few days before the whole of that archive went under the Compact's seal. Do you remember the later hand? The black ink that didn't cross its sevens?"

"*Per standardization directive*," said Cael. "It didn't say which."

"It didn't say which, because nobody writing it ever thought there would be a question of which. There's only one." She tapped the green tab, very lightly, with the nail of her finger. "It's this one. So the article that told that clerk to change the word is one of these four. It's here, in this book, on this table, four articles from the article we won on this morning."

She lifted her fingers off the page and gripped the table's edge with both hands.

"*Shattered* was written in at that table, by somebody holding a pen, over an older word. And the older word meant unbound. Four articles along, somebody took the old system's only way of judging a person by what that person could actually show, instead of by which box they would go into, and turned it into a road out of itself, and put a sign up telling everybody to use the road." Her hands had tightened on the wood. "And the old way works. That's what I keep coming back to. It isn't a museum piece. It's working tonight, in this house, on the record, with seals on it. I sat at that table from the second bell to the seventh and watched it bear weight."

*Shattered* was the word in brackets on Cael's own registry sheet, beside his number, in the Denvash station's hand. He waited until he was sure she had finished.

"And you're not saying they're connected."

"I'm not saying anything about them at all." It came out quick and firm, like a hand coming down on a lid, and there was nothing frightened in it. "I haven't got a single sheet that joins them. I've no drafter's name for either article. There's no minute and no memorandum. There isn't one letter from a man at that table to another man at it. I've got two articles in one book with four articles between them, and that's all I've got." She looked down at the green tabs. "I know what that looks like. It looks like a pattern. And a pattern with no paper under it was the warden's whole method. I watched him build a case for two years out of things that looked alike, and I watched you beat him by reading what was actually written. I won't do it his way the same week we won by doing it ours. Two articles in one directive, four apart, is nothing to stand on. It's a coincidence with good posture."

"Then what have you got?" said Cael.

Karis did not answer at once. She reached into the satchel that hung on the back of a chair, and took out the grey notebook, and opened it, and turned it round on the table to face him.

It was a fresh page, and at the top of it, in her small upright hand, which had come back level, there was one line. Under the line the whole page was empty, to the foot.

*Why retire a working method?*

"I wrote it this afternoon," she said, "while the clerks were reading the list. It goes on a page by itself, with nothing under it. That way, whenever I open the book, I'll see it's still waiting, and I won't be tempted to fill the page with things I'd like to be true."

He read the line three times over and said nothing.

"That's all of it," said Karis. "I'm not asking who did it. I'm not asking about the word. I'm not asking any of the things I'd give a year of my life to know. I'm asking one thing. There was a way of looking at somebody the boxes couldn't hold. It worked, and it made records, and we've spent today reading them. Somebody turned it into a door and walked everybody out through the door, and nobody, anywhere in forty-one pages, wrote down a reason." She closed the notebook. "I don't know the answer. I don't know where I'd begin to look. The archive that held the other half of it is under the Compact's seal at Greyvane, and nobody alive can tell me whose hand held the black ink. I've got one line on one page."

"Do you think it's something?" he said. "Or nothing?"

Karis put the notebook back in the satchel and buckled it before she answered.

"I think I proved something today," she said. "A real thing, with seals on it, that will go on being true whether I'm here or not. I'm going to go to bed holding that. A thing I can prove is worth more to me tonight than a thing I'd only like." She looked at the press. "Three left. Then I'm going to eat something, and sleep until the final."

They put the last three volumes into the run between them, each into its pencilled place. Karis took the key from its nail and locked the door behind them and tried the handle twice, and then hung the key on her own wrist by its string, to give back to Bracken in the morning.

At the end of the records hall she stopped and turned to him. She seemed about to say something more, and then to decide against it. She only put her inky hand flat on his sleeve, the way she had laid it on the closed directive, and took it away.

"Thank you for the wall," she said. "You held it."

"Not all of it."

"Enough of it." She started off across the dark toward the residence. Over her shoulder she said, "And my foot is going to be famous," and he laughed, for the first time that day, out loud in the empty quadrangle.

---

He did not go straight up.

He stood for a while in the dark under the covered walk's north arch, where the fire-watch boy kept his slate. The boy was there already, on his upturned bucket, with the slate wiped clean and a stub of chalk in his fist. He was writing the night's first hour in large, careful, flat-footed figures. When he had written it he got down and stood back on the cobbles to look at it, and then he climbed up again and rubbed out one figure with the side of his hand and wrote it again, a little straighter.

Nobody had asked him to straighten it. Nobody would check it until noon, when the bursar's clerk came to copy it down for his quarrel with the chandler. The boy did it anyway, because it was his slate and he wanted it right.

Cael watched him do it and felt something he could not have put a name to; it was not quite comfort, but it was near it.

"Evening," said the boy, seeing him.

"Evening. That's a good four."

The boy looked at his four with suspicion, and then with pride, and nodded.

Above the quadrangle, on the guest floor of the administration range, a single window was lit. Cael looked at it for exactly as long as he looked at the clock over the arch and at the dark roof of the records hall, and then he went in.

Brom was still up when Cael came into the common room, on the floor in the corner by the one lamp, with his right wrap off and the round-two exercise for the night half done. He was doing it slowly, as Rooke had told him to, with his eyes on his own hands and his breath counted out loud under it. Karis's grey notebook lay open on the settle beside him at the page of boxes, with tonight's box still empty, waiting.

He finished the movement before he looked up. He always finished the movement.

"Sound?" said Brom.

"Sound. Ilsev stood up and said it."

Brom thought about that. Then he reached out and knocked twice with his knuckles on the floorboard beside him, softly.

"Huh," he said, which from Brom was a celebration.

"Lira told you the rest."

"Lira told me two thin and one bad, and a folder, and Karis's foot." Brom went back to his hands. "I'm not going to say anything about it tonight. Four days. I've got four days to think about nothing but the other end of a forty-foot board. On the twentieth I'll think about nothing but you." He finished the next count. "Did the Archmarshal say anything?"

"Not a word all day. He wrote five times."

"About you?"

"About the paper. How it was checked. Whether anybody could find anything in it."

Brom considered that through two more counts.

"Then he's not looking at you yet," he said.

"He's looking at what looks at me. Tiers and ranks and ladders, all of it. Whether any of it measures true."

"That's the same thing," said Brom, "only later," and he picked up the pencil from the settle and inked a neat cross into tonight's box in Karis's notebook, very carefully, as if it were somebody else's property, which it was. Then he put his wrap back on, finger by finger, and went up.

The lamp was low and the hour was late when Cael sat down at the window, and the long day's pressure still sat behind his eyes like a hand. He did not write it the way he usually wrote, but took a clean page, as Karis had, and set her line at the head of it by itself. Then he wrote under it in numbered parts, because the day had been too big to go down in one piece.

*Why retire a working method? (Karis's words. Hers to keep. Copied here so I don't lose sight of it.)*

*1. What held. Sound law, said standing up, into their own record, by the one person on their side of the table who reads to the end. A year of work. Two academies. Four grey boxes and three hundred and eleven sealed sheets. Bracken's index. Withrow's twenty years. And Karis, who spent eleven weeks of our last term at Greyvane trying to prove that nothing had happened to a clause, and proved it, and then let Bracken take every trace of her hand out of the file so that the file could stand without her. That's what today was for.*

*2. What doesn't fit. The clause I'm enrolled on and the article that changed the old word sit four apart in one directive. One drafting, as far as anybody will ever be able to say. That isn't what today was for. It's what today did to me.*

*3. What I'm not going to do. Argue from it. A warden spent two years building cases against me out of things that looked alike. A closed list and a plain reading finished him. I'm not going to start doing it his way in the very week the list held.*

He stopped there with the pen lifted. Down the bluff, the dark river kept on going. At the hour, down at the ferry landing, the two grey coats changed under their lamp on the bell, as they had changed every four hours since the bridge.

Then he wrote the fourth part, which was his own. It had been coming toward him all term, a step at a time, from somewhere a long way off, and tonight it had arrived.

*4. What I think the question is. A system that's muddled about somebody asks things. Naveth asked me things. Quenna asked me one I still owe her the answer to. Gault asks his four a session and puts every answer in his clerk's book. Muddled is honest. Muddled leaves a gap you can talk into.*

*The system over all of them doesn't ask me anything. It renames. It converts one thing into another and posts a sign. It discourages. It shuts doors behind itself. It does every bit of it in the same flat voice paper always has, a few articles apart, and nowhere does it give a reason.*

*I don't have to know who. The thing I can't leave alone is the reason it was put together like this. That could take me years.*

He read it through once. Under all of it he added two short lines that had nothing to do with any system at all.

*Tomorrow, ordinary again.*

*Four days to the final. Five to the twentieth.*

# Chapter 54 — Four Chairs

Brom's right hand was strapped to the second knuckle in clean linen, and he was holding a mug of tea in it anyway.

He sat on the residence stair two steps below the landing with his back against the plaster and his wrapped knee stuck out across the tread like a gate across a lane. A second mug stood on the step beside him, steaming. The lamp at the stair's foot had burned down to a bead.

"That one's yours," he said, without turning his head. "Before you ask, no, I didn't sleep. The knee and I had a disagreement about which way it bends."

Cael sat down a step above him and took the mug in both hands.

He had set the stake before he opened his eyes, an hour ago, in the black of the fourth hour after the last bell. That was the order of things now, and had been since the stair. Before the shutter or the cold or a single thought about the day, he reached for his own edges in the dark and found them, and fixed them where an ordinary person's would sit, and left a corner of himself standing guard there. He had tried six names for it in the working ledger and struck out all six. The least bad was a full cup carried across a crowded room. It weighed nothing. It hurt nothing. It simply could not be put down, and so it took a corner of him that would be good for nothing else all day.

Ledger day twenty-two. Thirty-five days since the stair.

"How's the corner?" said Brom.

"Fixed."

"Since when?"

"I woke at the fourth hour. Since then."

Brom drank, and thought, and worked it out loud the slow way, which was how he worked out anything that mattered.

"The sitting's three hours. Then there's the walk to the wing and the waiting outside while they set up. Then the walk back." He counted on the fingers of the hand that was not strapped. "Six. More or less."

"About that."

"You managed nine at the records table."

"Sitting down. On a hard chair against a wall, doing nothing but look like a clerk." Cael turned the mug. "And I still lost it three times. Today it's six, standing up, with Gault's instruments on me."

The mug went down beside the other one. He got himself up against the wall, the good leg first and then the other, with the face of a man lifting a cart wheel. On his feet, he considered Cael from above.

"Here's what I think, then," he said. "Today the hard part's the part nobody's measuring. Every one of them will be looking at the brass and the frame and the figures, and they'll think that's where the work is. Nobody in that room is ever going to know what the corner cost you." He reached down and knocked twice on the top of Cael's head with the knuckles of his good hand, softly, the way he knocked on a door he did not need to open. "I'll know. That's the best I've got."

"It's enough."

"It'll do," said Brom, and went up the stair a tread at a time, with the strapped hand flat against the plaster for balance, to sleep at last.

On his way out Cael crossed the common room. The crown sat dull on the long table where Brom had left it. On the mantel the slate still had Karis's six lines and her two brackets, and across the foot in her big chalk capitals, *ON THE NUMBER*. He read it twice and left it.

---

Lira was waiting at the wing gate with the safety instructor's stick.

She had the stick planted and both hands folded on its head, and she was leaning on it as if it were a fence she had stopped at to admire the view. Nobody who looked at her face would have believed the view was the reason. It was the colour of a morning with no sleep in it.

"Four days off it," said Cael. "That's what she told you."

"I'm off it. I'm on this." She lifted the stick an inch and put it down. "Day one of four. I'm spending day one here. Don't."

He did not.

They stood for a moment under the arch while two of the wing's porters went past them carrying a long box between them, and the box clinked.

"Tell me the order," said Lira.

"Corner. Then everything on top of the corner."

"And in the gaps?"

"Somebody in the room. All the way down. Not him."

"Not him," she agreed. "And if the corner goes?"

"Then I stop. I ask for an interval out loud and let the clerk write that I asked. A line in a book, not a thing they found."

"Say it if it goes."

"I'll say it."

She looked at him the way she looked at a floor before a bout, measuring how far it was across and how much it would give.

"Go on, then," she said. "Go and be measured. I'll be here when you come out." She settled more heavily on the stick. "If I fall asleep on this post, wake me gently. I've a crown to live up to."

He went in. At the wing's inner door he looked back, and she was still there under the arch, leaning, watching him all the way, and she did not wave.

---

The room reached him through his ears first.

The pendulum was going in its glass case, with its dry, patient tick. Somewhere behind the louvres of the shutter-board the drum was being wound, and it made its sound of a thumbnail drawn slowly down a comb. At the far end of the north wall the brass funnel dripped into its cup. He had stood in this room for a whole morning in his first month, and his body remembered every one of those sounds before he was through the door. For a moment it was like coming home to a house where somebody has moved the furniture.

Only four chairs had moved.

They stood in a row along the east wall, where in the first month there had been bare plaster. Bracken had set them out himself, at a guest's distance from one another, and they had people in them.

The delegation's counsel had the nearest. She was untying the ribbon of a second folder with her teeth, because both her hands were full of the first. Next to her Ilsev was sharpening a pencil with a small knife, carefully, into a fold of paper on her knee, so as to leave no shavings on the wing's floor. Beyond Ilsev, Havel was writing the date at the head of a ruled sheet, on a board across his knees, with the bound file under the board. He looked up as Cael came in. He looked for about four-tenths of a second, level and without expression, and then looked back down at his date and finished it.

Cael understood that he had just been greeted, in the only way the card allowed, and greeted properly.

The Archmarshal had the fourth chair.

He sat at his ease in his plain grey with his hands resting loose on his thighs. There was nothing in them. There was nothing on his knees, nothing at his feet, nothing anywhere near him. No folder, no case, no pencil. The little notebook from the north tier was not in sight.

Cael crossed the oak toward the crossing, and those thirty feet were where he worked it out. Everybody else on that wall had brought the tool of their trade, because people do. The counsel lived by paper, and had brought paper. Ilsev had brought a point for marking. Havel had brought the record, because keeping it was what he was for. The most senior person in the building had brought nothing. Either he meant to do nothing at all, or the instrument he worked with was already sitting in his chair.

*He'll keep it in his head*, Cael thought. *Every bit of it.*

He put the thought away where it would not trip him, stepped onto the brass crossing, set his feet, and stood.

At the long table under the south wall the panel sat as it had sat in the first month. Gault was in the middle, heavy and still, with a sheaf squared in front of him. The Ash instructor sat on his right with her book held flat against her chest. The Mire instructor sat on his left, still in her coat, with her hands buried in its pockets. A little apart, at his sloped desk, the clerk had his ledger open and his three pens in their row.

Gault did not stand. He had not stood for the baseline. Cael found that steadying beyond anything else in the room. Nothing about the place had been changed for its visitors.

"This is the wing's semester sitting for the assay-provision enrollee," said Gault, in a voice pitched for the clerk's pen. "It was put on the wing's calendar in the first week of term, and posted, before anybody here had heard of an inspection. The panel is the one you see, with the clerk keeping the book. The Compact's delegation sits under its right to observe. To observe, not to direct. I was asked to say that aloud, and I'm content to."

At the end of the near chair the counsel finished with her ribbon and inclined her head.

"Enrollee. You've stood on this oak before, and you know my three rules." Gault spoke them flatly, as he would read off a dial. "Say so when you cannot. Give me the same thing twice before you give me your best once. And you are measured against the line you drew here yourself, in your first month, and against nothing else. Not against hopes. Not against anything printed. Not against talk." He looked up. "Show me a marvel and I shall have to wonder what I missed."

"Yes, Magister."

"Before we start." His broad palm settled on the sheaf. "This wing measures what you bring it. That was true at the baseline and it's true today. Do you mean to bring anything from your record that this oak hasn't yet seen?"

"The ignition point, Magister. The one on the Greyvane record."

Gault's face did nothing at all. "Where would you like it?"

"Second, Magister. It's contact work. I want my arm fresh for it."

"Second, then. The six stand as posted otherwise, with the read carried in the third and the last." He turned his head an inch. "Clerk, enter the change." He squared the sheaf. "Begin."

---

The first instruction was the same as it had been in the first month, to the word. Cael had hoped it would be, and had not let himself hope.

"Fourth brass from the near wall. Stand on the crossing. When the board opens, be somewhere else."

The drum went round behind the louvres, picking. The pendulum ticked under it.

The sixth louvre clacked.

His knees went and his hips dropped and the oak breathed under him. Then the toll at the front, the held half-breath, when there was no air in him anywhere. Then the crossing was empty and he was most of a stride to its left, coming down. The boards took his heels and gave them back. The landing beat held him, as it always held him, for its half-breath of standing on the spot like a post somebody had driven in.

The clerk did not look up. "Three brass left. A quarter forward." He read a figure off the pendulum.

The distance was what it had been in the first month. The figure was shorter. Cael had felt it shorter in his chest.

"The same again," said Gault.

He ran it eight times. The first three were free, and he did not hide that they were free. On the fourth he let the work show, as Brom had told him to. He came off the brass with his chest going hard, and stood there and breathed with his mouth open and let all four chairs and three faculty watch him breathe.

The fifth was the best of the morning, and he knew it a whole count before the clerk spoke. The drop came cleaner. The held breath came shorter by a sliver the pendulum could find and his lungs could not. A term of four hours a week on this oak had taught his leading leg to accept a landing instead of quarrelling with it.

The sixth was its twin.

The seventh was its twin too, and he felt the first of the bill arrive as he came down: low on the leading side, a slow warmth, like a stone left in the sun.

The eighth went off line. Not far. A hand's breadth, toward a place he had not chosen, and the landing beat hung a shade too long while the pendulum swung on past it. The warmth on the leading side pulled itself together into the thread he knew, thin and bright, from the crest of the hip down the inside of the thigh to the knee. That would be with him tonight.

He stood where he had come down, breathing, and waited for the reading.

"Three and a half left. Slow."

Gault laid his pen down beside eight lines of figures and looked at them with his hands flat on either side.

"Six runs sit within a third of a brass of one another," he said. "So does the seventh, and you paid for that one in front of me. The eighth goes wide and slow." He lifted his eyes. "In your first month you told me your ceiling on this oak before the board had told me anything. Tell me again."

"Six free, Magister. The seventh I pay for tomorrow. The eighth, the day after."

"In the first month it was four."

"Four free, and the fifth on credit. It's two higher now." Cael let his breath go. "A term of four hours a week, on this floor, does that. I don't think it does more."

Gault picked the pen up and wrote for some while. Then, still writing, he gave the clerk the line for the record in his flattest voice.

"Enrollee states a working ceiling of six against four at the baseline. Seven runs within the baseline's spread, with a shorter interval. The ceiling given before the instrument gave it, as before."

He set the pen down.

"That is what the provision is for."

He said nothing else. He turned the page.

Along the east wall the counsel wrote. Havel wrote, because writing was the job he had been sent to do. Ilsev's freshly sharpened pencil had not touched her paper. Cael caught, at the very edge of his eye, that she had spent the whole trial watching the drum behind the louvres, not him.

In the fourth chair, nothing had moved.

---

Then came the first gap, and he had been waiting for it the way you wait for a step you know is loose.

Gault leaned toward the Mire instructor, and the two of them bent their heads together over the sheet and talked low, and the Ash instructor leaned in to listen. The clerk would be sent to the north wall after that, and would take his time. A minute and a half, perhaps two. Nothing for anybody on the brass to do.

Cael felt the room go slack round him, like a rope let out.

So he did what Lira had told him. He chose somebody.

He chose the clerk.

The clerk was a thin man of about thirty, younger in the face than in the hands. He had kept the book for every hour Cael had ever spent in this room, and he sat at his sloped desk now with his pen lifted, waiting to be told what to write next. Cael took him from the top, the way he had been reading people since long before there was a single Path in him to read them with.

Rhythm: slow and even. A breath and a half between every movement of the pen, never hurried, never held. Weight: settled back, but the shoulders a little forward over the desk, the way a man sits who has spent ten years over a desk and has quietly stopped noticing. Breath: through the nose, shallow, steady. Hands: the right one with the pen, the left laid flat along the ledger's edge to keep the page from lifting. And did it all agree? It did. Every layer told the same story. Here was a man who was exactly what he seemed, a clerk minding his lines, with nothing else hidden in him anywhere.

It was plain work and quite dull, and it held.

The whole time he went down through the clerk, layer by layer, the corner stayed where he had fixed it. He could feel it stay. Nothing slipped. The room had stopped asking him anything, and he had found a question of his own to ask it, and that was enough.

When Gault turned back he let the reading sink a level, as Lira had told him, and left it ticking over underneath like a clock in another room.

"The ignition point," said Gault. "Clerk, the vessel."

---

The clerk brought it from the north wall in both arms, the way you carry a thing you have been warned about.

Cael had looked at it every time he came into this room and had never once got a name for it. It was a squat copper pot on three short legs, a little bigger than a man's two fists, with a glass straw standing up out of its lid. Fine lines climbed the straw, close as the teeth of a comb. A small brass plate on its belly carried a date of calibration in paint, and the date was this month's.

The clerk set it on its legs on the oak a pace from the crossing and stepped back.

"You'll not have seen this one at work," said Gault.

"No, Magister. I've wondered about it since my second week."

"It's for heat. Water, shut in, and a glass to show the water rising. A slow thing, and an honest one. You can't argue with it and you can't hurry it." He drew a sheet toward him. "The Ember and Ash cohorts sit it each quarter. I've never yet seen it flatter a soul." He laid the sheet flat. "The Greyvane record has one exhibit for an ignition point. Contact work, one channel, written late in that house's term and written thin. You've chosen to bring it here. Hand on the copper. Make the glass climb."

Cael went down on one knee beside it and laid his right palm flat on the pot's side. The copper was cool, and faintly oily from the clerk's polishing cloth.

Of everything he owned, the Ember was the smallest. It was also the only one that had come with a signature, with Karis's signature at the foot of the page and her date in her own hand. It went one way and one distance only. Beyond that it did nothing whatever. It was not heat. He had learned that at Greyvane over two slow months and through two of Karis's words for it. First she had called it a spark. Later she had called it a letting-go.

He let go at the copper.

Under his hand the metal warmed. A hot band, two fingers broad, spread under the skin of the copper and passed inward to the water. The thread in the glass straw began to rise. It rose slowly. Slowness was the instrument's whole virtue, and Gault trusted it for exactly that. Nothing quick can fool water. The thread climbed past one line and the next, and stopped there.

"Hold it," said Gault.

"I can't hold it, Magister. It doesn't run. Each one's a single moment at the hand, finished the instant it's made. Then I start over."

"Then make it again."

He made it again.

Somewhere between that one and the next, kneeling on the oak with his palm on a copper pot in front of a row of chairs, he understood what Karis's third word was.

It was not a spark. It was not a letting-go, either, though that was what it felt like at the very end. Those were both the outside of it. What it was, was a choice of a moment. There is an instant when the unburnt turns into the burning, and what he had taken from her let him put that instant wherever he wished at the surface of his own skin. It was a decision point. Everything that came after it was the copper's business, or the water's, or the wood's. Only the moment was his. That was why it would not hold. That was why it cost him the same whatever size he asked for, and why each one had to be made new. You could not hold a decision. You could only make another one.

He made four, at gaps the clerk read off the pendulum. Each time the thread took the warmth and climbed. After the fourth it stood above the fifth line and stayed, while the water decided what it thought of what had been done to it. Gault watched the glass straw the whole while, not the hand.

"For the book," Gault said at last. "Ignition point, shown on the vessel. Four applications. The glass answered each the same, with no drift between them. No heat away from the hand. Contact only." He wrote all of it, slowly, in the order he had said it. "On the enrollee's prior record. Confirmed here, to the wing's standard."

He wrote that as well. Then, in exactly the same voice, he added one thing.

"That is a better instrument reading," he said, "than the Greyvane exhibit supports. The exhibit calls it marginal."

"That was written in my first weeks with it, Magister. Nearly a year ago now."

"Mm," said Gault, and wrote that too.

Cael took his hand away. The copper under it had gone from hot to warm and was on its way to nothing. He got up off his knee and went back to the crossing, and kept his eyes off the east wall, and knew all the same what had happened along it. He had counted rooms since Ardenmere and could no more stop than stop his heart.

The counsel's pen had run on for a long time, and she had turned her page to finish. Then she had leaned along the row and said a few words to Havel, and Havel had made a mark on his sheet. Ilsev's new point had touched her paper for the first time all morning.

They had found something: small, true, already on the record, early, and paid for out of a purse he could spare. Now, for whatever was left of the morning, everyone on that wall who had come hoping to catch him out had one catch already, folded into a pocket.

In the fourth chair, the man in grey had not written anything. He had nothing to write with.

---

Water is slow, and Gault would not go on until the glass had settled. Every minute the clerk read the straw and wrote down a figure, and between the readings there was nothing for anybody to do but wait.

Cael waited on the crossing and went back to the clerk.

The clerk held through the first reading, and the second. He held while the Ash instructor rose and went to stand over the copper herself, peering at the straw, and came back. He held through the fourth reading and the fifth, and while the Mire instructor took her hands out of her coat pockets for the first time that day and frowned at her nails.

After the sixth reading the thread stopped falling.

"A question before we go on," said Gault. "Four applications. Why four, and not one, held? You've told me it doesn't run. I'd like to know what it does instead, as nearly as you can put it."

It was a good question, and that was what was wrong with it.

To answer it honestly Cael had to go a long way in. He had to go down past the skin of his own palm, after a moment that had taken no time at all, and pull it apart in front of a panel: what it was, and why it would not stay, and where in it the choosing sat. He had understood it himself only a quarter of an hour before. Finding words for Gault's book was like trying to describe a stair you are still standing on in the dark.

He went down after the words. Somewhere down there, unattended, the corner slid.

The room's weather changed. It was the only way he had found to say it. Eight people's worth of being looked at had been pressing on him all morning, lightly, the way air presses, and now it eased by a fraction, because eight people had very slightly forgotten that a boy stood on the brass.

Two seconds. Less, perhaps. On the far side of the long table the Mire instructor's gaze slid off him and down to her own hands, the way an eye leaves an empty doorway.

He came up out of the answer faster than he had ever come up out of anything.

"Magister," he said. "I need an interval."

Gault's pen stopped. "Why?"

"Because I'm trying to answer you properly and keep the sitting going at once, and one of the two has slipped. Better a minute lost now than a poor figure on your frame."

"Which slipped?"

"The answer, Magister."

Into her book, the Ash instructor laughed, or nearly. Gault's face did not change.

"Clerk. Two minutes, asked for by the enrollee. Give the reason as fatigue affecting response quality." He turned his head a fraction. "And put down that he asked before anything on my table showed it." He looked back at Cael. "Sit down for your two minutes. You lose nothing by it. I say so because I can see you wondering."

Cael crossed to the rail along the west wall and sat on it with his palms flat on the cold wood. He spent the two minutes going after his own edges, one by one, and putting each back where an ordinary boy of sixteen would carry it.

They came back slowly, grudgingly, and took nearly all of the time. When they were back there was an ache behind his eyes of the kind the read usually sent him after a long day. It was not a good sign with an hour and a half still to run.

As far as anyone at the table or along the wall could tell, nothing had happened at all. The book would say *fatigue affecting response quality*, and that was so. It would say *at the enrollee's request*, and that was so. It would say nothing else, and that was so as well. That was the whole art of it.

He got up off the rail.

"Ready, Magister."

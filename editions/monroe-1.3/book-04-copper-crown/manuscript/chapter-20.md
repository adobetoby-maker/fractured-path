# Chapter 20 — Eleven Seconds

The brackets went up two days before the floor sheet came right, at the first bell on the seventy-seventh morning, on the long board under the Crown yard's north stair. Cael was there before the porter had finished pinning the corners, because he had not come to read the brackets. He had come to watch people read them.

The sheets themselves he could have copied in a quarter of an hour. There were three of them, one to a column, ruled in Bracken's office in a clerk's careful hand. Down each ran the names in order, a number to the left of every one, and to the right a long row of blank squares, one for every session between now and the final, waiting for chalk.

Copper ran to ninety-one names. Two hundred and nine practitioners on the bluff could have put their names to it, and a few more than half had looked at what a season cost them in hours and bruises and missed lectures, and decided not to pay. Iron ran to sixty-three of its hundred and thirty-eight. The last sheet, headed Silver, held nineteen, gathered from the Bronze and Silver tiers together, all that the top of Halcenvane had been willing to put on a wall. The thinness of that last sheet, Cael thought, was the whole reason a school like this one stayed a school like this one. By the second bell the figures were in the binder.

Then he leaned on the rail of the north stair, a little above the board, and watched.

They came in ones and twos at first, and then in a steady stream, between the bells. Every one of them did the same thing first. He saw ninety-odd people do it that morning and not one failed to: they found their own name before they looked at anything else, with a small fierce hunt of the eyes, as if somebody might have left them off. Then, having found it, they did one of three things, and the three things told him more about the bluff than a month of corridors.

Most read upward. Their eyes went from their own name to the names above it, slowly, one at a time, and their lips moved. He could see them counting: four places up, the most the rules allowed a challenge to reach, and who was standing in those four places, and how they had looked on a practice afternoon. Those had come to climb. Some read downward instead, from their own name to the names below it, and their shoulders went very slightly up as they did it. Those had come to hold what they had. Cael noted, with real interest, that nearly all of the downward readers stood in the top third of a column, and that most of them were older, fourth-years and fifth-years. Holding was a thing people learned to want late.

And a few, a very few, did neither. They read the whole column at once, top to bottom, the way you read a page and not a line. Then they turned and walked away.

In the Copper column he counted five of those all morning.

One was Lira. She came at the second bell with her hair still wet from the wash-house and read the ninety-one names in the time it would have taken him to read nine, and walked off toward the north hall without looking round, and he knew better than to follow her yet.

Another had been there before him, at the board's left end, quite still, her hands clasped at the small of her back. She went down the Copper names slowly, line by line, the way a quartermaster goes down a list of stores. Force Path. Copper Rank Eight. Fiske. Her own name stood at the very top of the column, with two seasons of results behind it in Bracken's files, and beside it, alone among the hundred and seventy-three names on all three sheets, a small brass stud pressed into the sheet like a button.

Cael had asked Bracken about that stud in his second week. Every other mark on the bluff's paper explained itself; that one said nothing, and he had not liked not knowing what it said.

"Holder," Bracken had said, without looking up from a ledger. "The name with the brass beside it holds the crown and is defending it. Fiske has had the stud two seasons. The porter puts it in himself. He's very particular about it. He polishes it."

Fiske finished reading, and stood a moment longer, and then went away along the bottom of the tiers with her hands still behind her back. She had not looked at her own name at all, Cael realized. She had found it first, as everybody did, and then never glanced at it again. She had read every other line on the sheet instead.

---

He found Lira's name where he had stopped expecting to find it, and read it three times to be sure.

*Twenty-two. Lira. Wind. Copper Rank Two. Transfer.*

Twenty-second. The clerk had told her the foot, and he had not been wrong: in the ladder book, where names went down in the order they signed, she stood on the sixty-fifth line, the last. But the ladder book was not the bracket. Twenty-second of ninety-one.

The reason was pinned beside the sheets, on a half-page in Bracken's own small upright hand, headed *Seeding*, for anybody who cared to read it. Hardly anybody did. Cael did. A bracket was drawn only from the names that had paid to enter the season; the rest of the ladder book stood aside until next year. Names that had fought last season were seeded first, in last season's closing order. Of last season's Copper names, twenty-one had paid this season. Every other name went in below those twenty-one by formal rank, and then by the order of signing.

Cael stood and did that sum twice. Nearly every name that had not fought last season was a first-year, and nearly every first-year carried a fresh Arbiter's Rank One. Lira's paper said Rank Two. The one figure on her certificate that had ever helped her had lifted her over seventy people at a stroke and set her directly beneath the last of the old names.

Her paper and her place were two different measures, and only one of them would outlast the season. Copper Rank Two was her classification, written on her certificate by an Arbiter and changed only by an advancement evaluation. Twenty-second was her seed, a standing the ladder could move every six days and would wipe clean at the season's end.

He found her an hour later in the north hall, on the stone, alone, running her evasion sequence at a walk, which was what she did when she was angry and did not want to break anything.

"Twenty-second," he said from the doorway.

"I saw." She did not stop walking. "Read it before you were awake."

"It's six. From twenty-second, if you win every bout you're allowed and every one counts, it's six to the top. Not sixteen. You counted from the ladder book. The ladder book was the wrong book."

Lira came to the end of the sequence and stood still, and looked at the floor for a moment.

"Rank Two," she said. "That's what did it, isn't it? I read the half-sheet. The new names go in by rank." She laughed, but not as if anything were funny. "Two years I've been trying to get out from under what that paper says I am. And the first time it's ever done me a single kindness, it's because it's wrong in my favour by one rank instead of five." She rolled her neck. "Six. I'll take six. I'll even say thank you to it, once, out loud, here, where nobody can hear." She did not say it. "There. Done."

"There's a draw first," said Cael. "The first three sessions are drawn, not challenged. The half-sheet says so at the bottom. Every name gets paired once against somebody from a different part of its column, so the column can have a look at itself. The results go in the record. They don't move anybody."

"I read that too." Lira picked up her towel. "I've drawn a Stone Path. Ninth. Nyle."

Cael had Nyle already. Everybody on the ladder was in the binder by now, one page each, and Nyle's was one of the fuller ones because Cael had watched him twice on practice afternoons out of pure liking. He was a third-year, Copper Rank Five, broad through the shoulders with a settled, comfortable stance, a dozen wins across two seasons in Bracken's files.

Stone was a slow Path to gather, and Nyle had learned, the way the best Stone fighters did, to make the slowness look like patience, so that an opponent waited for him when he ought to have been moving. A Stone fighter's weight could not bluff. It was the working itself, so whatever it promised, it meant, and the craft of the Path lay entirely in timing: bring it down at the instant that hurt most, and never mind who saw it coming. He breathed out through the gather, low, so that you could hear it from the tiers.

*He's good,* Cael had written the night the draw went up. *He'll be gone in under two minutes. I don't think anyone has told him.*

He did not say so to Lira. She did not need anything said to her. She had read Nyle's page over his shoulder at breakfast without asking, which she did with every page in the binder that had her name near it, and had handed it back and gone on with her bread.

---

Her bout was the season's second session, on a bright still afternoon, and the tiers were three-quarters full, which was a great crowd for the second session of anything. A good many of them, Cael guessed, had climbed the tiers for a look at the girl from the little school in the north, the one with a flag on her paper and a circuit behind her that nobody here could check.

He sat high on the east side, where he always sat. In the curve of the brass lamp, without looking for it, he saw a small figure high on the west side in a knot of five, with something pale against its chest. He put it out of his mind. He had promised the Log four days without counting, and this was the first of them.

Lira and Nyle went down into the sunk floor and to their chalk. The adjudicating instructor read the terms aloud from the table, first to three clean touches, any exchange the floor could not split called even and run again, and the second instructor stood off at the edge with his hands loose for safety. The noise of the yard did what it always did at that moment. It thinned and went away, and there was only the floor.

Nyle began his gather.

It was correct. It was what every Stone fighter on the bluff would have done against a smaller, quicker opponent: start the weight moving early, slow and heavy, and make her choose a side before he had to. Whichever side she chose, he would land on it. The yard had seen that gather beat the Copper column a dozen times.

Lira chose a side.

Cael felt it go before he saw it: a fold low in his own hips, a held half-breath closing in his own chest, three years of having her architecture in his body pulling at him from forty feet up like a hand on his sleeve. Her first burst went left, and meant it. He saw Nyle's gather begin to swing to meet it, the great honest weight leaning toward where she was going to be. She was not there by the time it leaned. The landing beat caught her in the open a pace and a half to his left, locked for its half-breath, and Nyle could not use it; his weight was still on its way to the place she had left. When the beat let go of her she stepped in under his reaching arm and laid her flat hand on his ribs.

"Touch," said the table. "Wind. One."

Three seconds, Cael thought. *One burst.* His own hip told him the price, low and plain: the half-breath at the front, the jolt at the back, the line from crest to knee that would start to write itself out tonight.

Back to chalk. Nyle had seen it. He began the gather again, but shorter, holding more weight back, so that if she went left again he could come round without leaning. It was a good answer. It was the right answer to what she had just done.

She did something else. She went straight at him, and halfway there went right, short and quick, and the right was real too, and his held-back weight was held back on the wrong side. *Two bursts.* The touch landed on his leading shoulder from behind and to the side while the landing beat still had her by the collar. She had used the beat as a place to stand still and reach from.

"Wind. Two."

Six seconds, near enough. Cael's hip had stopped being a promise and become a fact.

On the third exchange Nyle did not gather at all. He stood with his weight down and his hands up, very still, and waited for her to commit first, which was the bravest thing he did all afternoon and the only thing left to him.

She committed. She showed him left on her feet alone, a half-step with no Wind in it, and his weight leaned after it. Then the third burst took her right, and the instant its landing beat let go of her the fourth began, with no gap at all between the end of one and the start of the next, which Cael had never seen anybody do and could not have done himself. The third had put her inside his reach. The fourth was a short one, a burst no longer than a stride, that left her standing at his back on the side of his front shoulder, with every part of his stance facing the place she had been.

Then she touched the back of his front knee, just as all his weight arrived on that leg. It was hardly a strike at all. The knee simply folded, and Nyle went down heavily and well, onto his hip and shoulder as he had been taught. The safety instructor's hand was up before he landed.

"Wind," said the table. "Three. Bout."

Eleven seconds. Cael had counted them in his own breath without meaning to, from the table's first word to its last, the walks back to chalk left out. Three exchanges. Four bursts. And his own hip, forty feet up and three years away from where it had first learned this, ached with each of them in turn. It knew the whole bill: toll at the front, jolt at the back, and the hot line from crest to inner thigh that would be written out on her by suppertime and still legible at breakfast. She had known that price at breakfast. She had read Nyle's page over his shoulder and decided, between two mouthfuls of bread, what the bout was worth to her, and she had spent precisely that and kept the rest.

Then the yard did something he had not expected.

It went quiet.

He would fill two pages with it that night. Behind him the two Iron girls who had been arguing all afternoon stopped in the middle of a word. A first-year on the row below had both hands raised to clap and did not bring them together. The law lecturer who slept on the fourth tier every practice day was awake, and sitting up, with his hat in his lap. Two rows down a Copper fifth-year shut her notebook, slowly, as if somebody had told her the lesson was cancelled. For perhaps four seconds some eight hundred people on stone made no sound at all.

It was not the hush of people who have been impressed. Impressed was loud, a roar and a stamping. Cael had heard this other quiet twice before, both times in the Ironyard, both times on nights when something happened on the boards that the crowd had no place in its head to put. Every person in that yard had been handed the sheet that morning and had believed it, as you believe a printed timetable. They had trusted that ninety-one names had been put in their right order by people who knew. And now, in eleven seconds, in front of all of them, the sheet had been caught out about one name, beyond any argument, and every one of them knew it at once, and not one of them could have said what it was they knew.

It was the sound of a category failing.

Then somebody high on the north side began to clap, and the rest took it up a beat late and a little too loud, and the yard was itself again, slightly ashamed of having been anything else.

Nyle sat up on the floor, and shook his head, and laughed, and let Lira give him a hand up. By the sheet he was the ninth-best Copper practitioner on the hill, and Cael did not doubt it; he had watched him earn it. By the sheet Lira was the twenty-second. Both figures were honest. Neither of them had been watching.

Down below the long board, where the results clerk was already reaching up with his chalk, Fiske had not moved. She had not moved for the whole of it, and she did not clap now. She stood with her hands at the small of her back and looked across the floor at Lira, who was standing alone in the middle of it with one palm pressed flat to her own thigh and her face showing nothing at all. And Fiske dipped her chin, once, a very small movement, made to nobody. It was the nod you give a guest you have been laying a place for, year after year, without ever knowing whose face would be in the doorway.

Then she turned and went back to the sheet, as if to make sure nothing on it had changed. Nothing had. That, Cael thought, was rather the trouble.

---

Brom was drawn for the third session. Nothing about his bout resembled hers, and Cael would come to think he had learned more from it.

His draw was Merrick: Shield Path, Copper Rank Four, fourteenth on the sheet, a fourth-year with a long face and a careful way of putting his feet down, as if every floor he stood on might have been laid by somebody who disliked him. He was no fool. Cael had a page on him, not a long one, but every line of it said the same thing in a different way: Merrick did not do what you expected, and he did not do what he wanted; he did what you could least afford.

And the whole cohort knew about Brom's seam. That was the thing Cael kept coming back to, on the east side of the tiers with the binder on his knee. It had been a private flaw once. Wray had found it at Greyvane with her own two hands, the gap in Brom's redirect between the read and the turn, and it had cost him nothing there, because nobody at Greyvane had been quick enough to get into it. Then Ephram had found it in three sessions on the east hall's timber, and spent a fortnight showing everyone else where it was. By now half the Copper column could have drawn it on a slate.

Hitting an Iron Skin hard was a fool's game, and Merrick was no fool. He fed him instead.

On the first exchange he came in with a short, cheap strike, committed enough to look real, light enough that turning it would buy nothing, and Brom turned it, as Brom turned everything. Merrick went past him a stride off balance. Brom's open hand came down on his back.

"Touch. Iron Skin. One."

The tiers around Cael stirred, pleased. They liked Brom. Everybody liked Brom.

Cael did not stir. He had seen Merrick's face as he went back to his chalk. It was not the face of a man who had lost a touch. It was the face of a man who had paid a copper for a look at the goods.

The second exchange was the first again, twice over: two more cheap feeds, each arriving with just enough weight to make the turn the obvious answer and so little that the turn won nothing. Brom turned on both, honestly, with his whole body, as Rooke's post had not yet taught him not to. And then, on the third feed, Merrick did not feed. He waited for the turn to begin, saw Brom's weight go out to meet a strike that was not there, and stepped into the gap behind it.

His hand landed flat and clean along Brom's jaw.

"Shield. One each."

The third exchange went the same way, faster, because Merrick no longer needed the first two feeds to set it up. One cheap strike, one turn, one gap, and the second touch: harder this time, though Merrick had not meant it to be, a strike that caught Brom's head while it was still coming round from the turn. Brom's head went round further than a head goes on purpose. On the east side somebody said "Oh," quietly.

"Shield. Two to one."

One more, and the bout was Merrick's.

Brom went back to his chalk slowly. He stood on it with his hands at his sides and his head down, and for a moment Cael thought he was simply hurt and gathering himself. Then he saw Brom's lips moving, very slightly, the way they moved on the east hall's floor when he was counting, and understood that Brom was not gathering himself at all. He was thinking.

---

On the fourth exchange Brom stopped turning.

He did not announce it. Nobody but Cael, and perhaps three people in the whole yard, would have known what they were looking at. Merrick came in with his cheap feed just as before, and Brom did not turn on it. He did not turn on anything. He simply stood and took it, square, on the forearm, with Iron Skin hardening under the blow as it always had and not one ounce of his weight going out to send it anywhere.

There was a dull flat sound, like a mallet on a wet post.

It was not a touch. The table did not call it. A strike taken on a set, hardened guard was not clean; it had arrived exactly where Brom had decided it would. But it had cost him. Cael could see the cost from forty feet away. Iron Skin hardened on a fuel neither of them had ever managed to name, though they had both tried, and the fuel ran out, and it ran out faster under blow after blow with nowhere to send them. The turn had always been the thing that paid the hardening back, by passing the force along. Without the turn every strike stayed where it landed, and Brom had to hold it there himself.

Merrick fed again, puzzled, and Brom took it again. A third time, and the third was not a feed at all but a real strike, full weight, to test whether the big man really meant it. He did. He took that one square too, on the forearm and into the shoulder, and Cael saw the guard come up a fraction slower afterward than it had before. He heard Brom's breathing go short and hard and rationed, like a man climbing a long hill at the end of a long day.

Three strikes, flat, at the full price, with no return on any of them. It was the dearest way an Iron Skin practitioner could spend a minute, and Brom spent it like a man paying a debt he had decided was fair.

Merrick stepped back, and looked at him, and Cael watched him think. There was no gap any more, because there was no turn to open one. The only way left to Brom's body was through the front of it, and the front of it had become a door.

So Merrick did what a clever man does when his trick stops working. He assumed it would start working again. He came in with a feed, and then, sure that the old habit must come back if he only gave it a moment, he committed to the gap a fourth time, his whole weight going into the place where Brom's turn had always left him open.

The turn did not come. The gap did not open. Merrick, all his weight committed, arrived at a shoulder that ought by every rule he knew to be swinging out of his way, and found it standing where it was, set and hard as a gatepost.

He bounced. That was the only word for it. While he was still finding his feet Brom's open hand came down on the side of his neck.

"Iron Skin. Two each."

The fifth exchange was ugly and short. Merrick did not feed; he had nothing left to feed with. He came in carefully, at the front of the door, looking for a way through it, and Brom stood still and let him look and took one more on the forearm, flat, with his breath sawing. Then he put his hand on Merrick's breastbone the moment the strike had spent itself.

"Iron Skin. Three to two. Bout."

The yard applauded, warmly, the way it applauded things it had not followed but liked the look of. Most of it would have forgotten the bout by supper. Cael had a page and a half on his knee before Brom was off the floor, and at the top of it, underlined twice, the only three words he was sure of: *ugly, expensive, intelligent.* The yard had seen the first two. The third was the whole of it. It had been an ugly, expensive, entirely intelligent win, made by a man who had stood in the middle of a bout he was losing and put down the best thing he owned because it had become the thing they were beating him with.

Brom came up the steps out of the sunk floor slowly, carrying his left arm close, with a dark line coming up along his jaw where the second touch had caught him. He sat down on the bench at the floor's edge and put his head back against the stone and shut his eyes.

Rooke was in front of him.

Cael had not seen him come down. He had not seen him in the yard at all. He was simply there, narrow and grey in his dark coat, standing in front of the bench with his hands loose at his sides and his weight even on both feet, looking down at Brom, who opened his eyes and found him there and did not move.

Rooke looked at him for the time it takes to draw three breaths and let them go. Then he spoke, in the same flat voice he used for everything.

"Again. Slower. Watch yourself."

He turned and went back the way he must have come, up the south steps, without hurrying and without looking round.

Brom sat for a moment longer. Then he leaned forward and began patting his coat, which lay on the bench under two other people's, and went through every pocket, and finally found what he wanted in a tear in the lining: a pencil worn down to the length of a thumbnail. He unwound the wrap on his right wrist, the right one, which had nothing on it, not the left with Rooke's long sentence inked on the inside where only he would ever feel it. On the outside of the cloth, where anybody passing could have read it, he wrote four words, pressing so hard Cael could see the pencil bend.

Cael did not go down to him. He watched from the bottom of the tiers, and then went up and out and back to the quadrangle with the binder under his arm, and found that he was turning it over the whole way, and the next day, and for most of the week.

The ink had been chosen. Brom had carried that sentence about for days before he wrote it, and had put it on the inside of the cloth on purpose, where it would press against his pulse and nobody else would ever see it. It had been a keepsake.

This was nothing like that. This was four words in pencil, on the outside, written on the spot by a man with his head still ringing who did not trust himself to remember them as far as the gate.

In two years Cael had seen Brom keep every sort of thing in every sort of way, in his hands and his feet and his tally book and the long patient memory of his body. Not once, until today, had Brom needed paper to hold a thing for him.

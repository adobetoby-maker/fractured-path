# Chapter 61 — Retired

The Copper Crown had stood on the common-room table since the night of the final, among the bread and the ice bags and Karis's papers, and nobody had known what to do with it.

It was not a large thing: a plain circlet of beaten copper on a brass mount, dented once on the rim where some champion long ago had dropped it, with a plate riveted to the mount that had been riveted on twice because the first rivets had pulled. The porter polished it every term whether anybody had won it or not. Since the final it had acquired a ring of dried tea on its mount where Brom had put a cup down without looking, and a smear of chalk from Lira's thumb.

The evening of the fifth found Lira at that table, the Iron certificate folded square under her hand, studying the crown.

Karis wanted it moved. "It can't live with the bread."

"Doorstop," said Brom. He was lying on the hearthrug with his knee up, healed now except for a stiffness in the mornings that he denied. "That door's never shut properly. It'd hold it."

"No."

"Send it to the foundry. Your master would hang it on the beam."

"He'd melt it," said Lira. "He'd say it was good copper wasted on a hat."

Brom had another idea. "The wash-house post. Hang it there, and I'll hit it for luck."

"You'll dent it," said Karis.

"It's dented already." Brom pointed at the rim without lifting his head from the rug. "Some champion dropped it about the year they built the bridge. It's had worse than me."

"Wear it to lectures."

"Brom."

"I'm being useful," said Brom, to the ceiling. "Nobody else is."

Lira picked the crown up off the table in both hands. She held it a moment, not looking at it so much as weighing it, as she weighed things at the foundry, by the arms. Then she got up and crossed the room and set it on the shelf over the settle, between the good lamp and a stack of Karis's drafts that Karis had told everyone three times were superseded and must on no account be thrown away.

She stepped away to judge it from the hearth.

She said, "There. It's retired."

"That's it?" said Brom. "Just there? On the shelf?"

"Where else? It's not mine to keep in a box. I've left that bracket, and it won't see me again." She did not turn round. "But nothing else I own says it out loud, with a plate riveted on: the bracket had me wrong. You don't put a thing like that in a drawer. Prizes go to winners. That's what I got for having to."

Nobody said anything for a while. From the hearthrug Brom gave the ceiling a slow nod.

That night the Log got her words exactly as she had said them. Then Cael's pen went on past them, on its own account.

*It was the crown that made them look. Fiske told her as much at the board on the night of the seeding bout. "It's the looking that matters — it was always the looking. The crown's just how you force it." That night Lira gave me the sentences exact, and I wrote them in, and neither of us could quite take them for a plan.*

*It was a plan. It took a whole engine of parts: a season on a public board, a final in front of witnesses, a registrar willing to certify the record before the result was known, an officer of record willing to file, an assessor at his elbow to witness the filing, and a Compact evaluation seat sitting in the second row of the north tier while it all happened. Six pieces. Fiske had drawn all six for her in four sentences at a standings board. She had been two years in the same box. And she handed the plan to the one fighter she thought could use it.*

*I've had a page headed* Fiske *since early in the term. I looked at it tonight. It's all footwork: where she plants, how long she collects, which side she places her first declaration. There isn't a single line on it about her.*

*Fix that.*

---

The porter climbed his ladder at the fifth bell the next morning, the same as every morning, with his chalk in one hand and his rag in the other.

Guessing what the morning would bring, Cael had come down early to see it. His post was the foot of the north stair, hands deep in his coat. The Crown yard was nearly empty at that hour; two Mire first-years were sweeping the oak, and a Blade fourth-year was stretching against the rail, and the board stood up against the long wall with its columns of names chalked in the porter's round careful hand.

Lira's name stood at the top of the Copper column, where it had stood since the final, with the word beside it.

For a breath the porter only considered the word. Then he took a folded slip out of his apron, the registrar's office slip, and read it, though he must have read it already, and put it back in his apron. Letter by letter, six of them, the rag took the word away. Then, in its place, he chalked the new one, shorter, in the same round hand. He leaned back on the ladder to look at it, and polished it with his cuff.

Then he moved her name.

He had to. With that word beside it, the name had no place left among the Copper. He rubbed it out of the top of the column, letter by letter, and climbed down and moved the ladder along the wall, and climbed again, and chalked it at the foot of the Iron column, under Ephram and the rest, with the new word beside it. Down there, under a column of strangers, it looked small. The porter regarded it for a while with his head on one side. Then he climbed down and put the ladder over his shoulder and went away to do the Silver board, and nobody in the yard but Cael had watched him do any of it.

Beside the board, by the second bell, somebody from the registrar's office had pinned up a copy of the certificate, as the house did with every change of tier, so that anyone who wanted to know why a name had moved could read the reason.

Fiske came into the yard a little after the second bell.

She came in by the north arch with her kit over her shoulder, on her way to the session she had on the oak at that hour, and she saw the board before she was halfway across. Cael watched her see it. She stopped. She looked at the empty place at the head of the Copper column where Lira's name had been all season, and then down the wall to the foot of the Iron column, and then at the pinned copy beside the board.

She went and read it. She stood in front of it with her bag on her shoulder, her weight square on both feet, and read it from the top to the bottom, and Cael thought she read it twice.

Her own name stood near the top of the Copper column, with the same six letters beside it that had been there for two years.

She did not look round the yard. She did not say anything to the Mire first-years with their brooms, or to the fourth-year at the rail, or to anybody. She did not smile, either, and she did not do the opposite of smiling. She settled her bag on her shoulder and walked on across the yard to the oak, and set the bag down at her chalk, and began to warm up, as she did every morning, alone, on time.

Cael stayed by the stair a good while after.

He had watched people be gracious before, a good deal of it. Graciousness had a look: it was performed, a little, even when it was sincere, because it knew it was being watched. This had not been that. Here was a woman who had once said aloud how a thing would go, watching it go that way for someone else. She was right, and she had nothing whatever to gain from being right, and she had gone to her session.

He went up to the library and opened the observation notebook at the page headed *Fiske*, and turned past all the footwork to a clean leaf, and began at last to write about her.

---

Two days later, crossing the Crown yard at the slack hour of the afternoon, Cael heard his name from the oak.

The yard was nearly bare. The term was almost done, and the porters had begun taking down the season: the brackets were off the long wall, the tiers had been swept, and a man at the far end was coiling the rope that marked the floor's edge on ladder days. On the oak, alone, Ephram was running a Blade drill against nobody, slow, the same six steps over and over, and he stopped in the middle of the fourth and called across.

"Assay. A minute."

Cael went down the tier stair. He read Ephram on the way, out of a habit too old to stop: rhythm even and unhurried, weight on the balls of the feet, breath recovering from the drill in long pulls, no hurry in the shoulders. No quarrel anywhere in him. Ephram met him at the edge of the floor with the sweat still on him and his practice blade held loosely, point down.

"I've put a sheet on Rooke's desk," said Ephram. "Supervised floor time, next term, you and me. Rooke's view is that the provision covers it, as floor hours tied to your evaluations, and his signature's waiting on yours."

It was not what Cael had expected; he had expected nothing at all from Ephram until the recess was over, and then perhaps a nod on the stair.

"Why?"

"Two reasons. The first's about me, and it's the one I'd rather not say, so I'll say it first." Ephram gave the blade in his hand a sour look. "In the second month I stood up there," he tipped his head at the top of the yard, "in front of sixty people and said I'd wait for your evaluation before I made up my mind. Then on the lecture stair, with thirty more listening, I changed my terms in your favour and thought well of myself for doing it in the open. Both times I was announcing a feint. *Watch me, I'm going to judge this.* And what I'd appointed myself to judge was a closed panel that would never have let me through its door. Everybody on this hill knew that but me."

"Plenty of people make promises they can't keep."

"Not in front of sixty people. Not on purpose." Ephram's mouth twisted. "So I'm not keeping it. I'm putting it down, here, where there's nobody to see me do it but you, because you're the one it was about."

"And the second reason?"

"I paid six marks for the transcript and read every page. It's very good on law. On whether you can fight, it's silent. Nothing anybody says about you can tell me either; I've heard a dozen versions this year and I couldn't check a single one. So I'll learn it the only honest way there is." The blade's point knocked once on the oak. "Here. With Rooke at the rail. Then whatever I think, I'll have earned it."

"That's fair," said Cael.

"It's the only fair thing left." Ephram turned back toward his chalk, and then turned round again. "One more thing. Rooke's cohort works at a speed you have never been hit at. Ask your friend. A season and two losses taught him, and he's made for taking blows. Better you hear it now, with the yard empty, than find it out from me in front of Rooke."

"Understood."

"Next term," said Ephram, and went back to his six steps, slow, the interruption already put away.

Cael put it in the Log that night, under Ephram's own page and then on a fresh one.

*Told me the speed before the session, not after. That's worth more to me than whatever happens in it.*

*All season he'd carried a promise made in public, one the house's own rules forbade him ever to keep. He could have let it quietly drop. Nobody would have said a word. Instead he took it apart in front of the one person it concerned, in a yard with nobody else in it, and then went back to his drill.*

*Rooke under the north arch, on the day the notice came. Ephram on the oak this afternoon. Two people this year who changed their minds about me to my face, when standing pat would have cost them nothing.*

*I am going to be very careful about how I remember Halcenvane.*

The archive sweep ran on the tenth.

Cael saw it from the law range's third-floor landing, where he had gone on purpose. Bracken's clerks came along the carrels in the morning with a hand-cart and an accession book, three of them: the young one pushing, the one with the cold reading out numbers, the inky-knuckled one writing them down. At each carrel they stopped and opened the door and took out whatever lay inside that was not nailed down. Loose papers went into numbered folders and books into the cart. Each thing they lifted was written down and tagged. Somebody's forgotten scarf got a number, and so did a broken pen.

Carrel eleven was empty.

They opened its door and looked in, and the young clerk said, "Nothing," and the inky-knuckled one wrote *nil* against its number, and they moved on to twelve. There was a pale square on the wall at eye height where four printed sheets had once been pinned, and a ring on the desk where an inkpot had stood all term.

On the landing beside him Karis watched them go by with her key on its string round her wrist and her hands in her coat. She did not say anything until the cart had gone round the corner of the range.

"Seventeen folders from the carrels on this floor alone," she said. "One of them's a second-year's whole term of notes on irrigation law. I saw it go in. She'll come back for it after the recess and find it has a number."

"Will she get it back?"

"She'll get a copy, if she asks properly and waits." Karis turned her key over on its string. "She'll have to ask properly. She'll have to know to ask." She looked down the empty passage. "Nobody tells you. That's the thing about it. It's all written down, and nobody tells you."

Cael said nothing. Somebody had told her. Both of them knew who, and both of them stood on the landing knowing it, with the cart's wheels squeaking away round the corner of the range and a second-year's irrigation law going into the house's keeping for good. Neither of them said one word of it. That was the arrangement, and the arrangement held on a stair landing with nobody near as well as it held anywhere, because the only way to keep it was to keep it everywhere.

Bracken came up the stair behind the cart a little later, as he came behind most things his clerks did, to see them done. He stopped at carrel eleven and looked at the *nil* in the accession book over the inky clerk's shoulder, and at the pale square on the wall where the sheets had hung, and his face did nothing at all.

Then he turned to Karis.

"Your room's on the top-floor list," he said. "I've entered it in the book of named assignments. A named room doesn't lapse at the close of term; it stands through the recess and into the next, until you give the key back." He said it in the voice he used for the hours the counter was open. "You'll want to know that if you mean to leave anything up there over the recess. People ask. It's as well to know before you ask."

"Thank you," said Karis.

"Don't." He took the accession book from his clerk and ran an eye down the column. "It's filing. I've three hundred season sheets to certify before the selection file can be opened, and a recess to do them in, and every one of them will be read by strangers. Correct filing is all I have to offer anybody this side of the river." He handed the book back. "Carry on."

Karis watched him go down the stair again at his ordinary pace, and said nothing about it, then or afterward, before she went back up to the top floor and the room with her name on the door.

---

On the twelfth Withrow closed the year.

She did it in the great hall of the lecture range with the whole house in front of her: the faculty along the front benches, the cohorts behind them by Path, and the clerks and porters standing along the back wall where they always stood. The clock over the door was two minutes slow, and Cael had begun to suspect that it would stay two minutes slow until she retired, and that somebody would mend it the week after and feel very bold.

The first part of it was the ordinary business of shutting a house for the recess, and she gave it the attention of a woman counting linen. The roof over the north hall was poor and would be seen to. The wing's frame was being rebuilt, and the man who built frames had been written to, at length. The porters were thanked one by one, by name, and the porter of the Crown yard went very red. Brom shifted on the bench. Lira, beside Cael, sat with one hand inside her coat, resting flat over the pocket where the folded certificate lived, as though it might otherwise get up and leave.

Then Withrow took a single sheet from under the others and held it up, so that the hall could see it was a real paper and not a speech.

"Here is what the Compact found," said Withrow. "It was filed in the first days of this month and reached the registrar on the ninth. I will read you its four lines, because they are short, and because you earned them."

Her voice gave them no colour at all.

"*Charter compliance: full. Enrollment basis: sound. Documentary practice: complete. Remarks: one, clerical, the fire-watch oil book.*"

She lowered the sheet.

"Some of you are new, so I'll tell you what you've just heard. There is no finding above that one. A delegation does not come to praise a house; it comes to look for what is wrong, and this one looked for eleven days with an Archmarshal at its head, and the only fault it could put its name to was a book of lamp oil." Half the hall laughed, and Withrow waited for it to stop of its own accord. "I'd ask the fire-watch to keep their oil book better. I'd ask everybody else to remember what eleven days of being looked at felt like, because you'll be looked at again."

She took off her spectacles and folded them.

"Now the thing I have waited a long time to say from here. I'll say it bare, because it is a fact.

"Halcenvane Academy will contest the qualifying season. The tournament is the Continental, its next cycle opens a year from now, and this house will be in it."

The hall did what a hall does.

Spectacles in hand, Withrow let it run. She had budgeted for the noise as she budgeted for everything, and only when it had spent itself did the spectacles go back on.

"You may have three sentences of meaning from me," she said. "After that you may carry your excitement home to the residences and keep it there.

"One: the side is chosen under the tournament's own charter and by nothing else, and the registrar will pin that charter on the covered walk's board, entire, before a single name goes forward, because this house does not pick its people by gossip.

"Two: every sheet written about you this year, the ladder's, the season's, the certified results, goes into the file the selectors read, and strangers will read it, and a season chalked on a board goes into the record, whether or not you lived as though it would.

"Three: this house has not qualified in eighteen years, which matters to me only as a sum, and what I care about instead is that the Compact sat in these buildings for eleven days hoping to catch us out, and did not, and so we know now that this house can do a difficult thing correctly with people watching it.

"That is the entire qualification. Now go and finish your year."

The hall emptied in a roar that went out through both doors at once and broke up on the quadrangle into a hundred smaller roars. Cael let the crowd carry him as far as the steps and then stepped out of it against the wall, and the other three found him there, as they always found him, without looking.

"Next year," said Brom. He tried the words slowly, like a new grip on a familiar handle.

"A year from now," said Karis. "A whole qualifying season first. She was very clear about that. Four sets of sheets, probably, and every one of them read by strangers."

"I heard her." Brom looked out across the quadrangle at the Crown yard's long wall, where the porter's boards stood bare and clean now, ready for whatever came after the recess. "A season chalked on a board goes into the record. She said so. I've had a season on a board." He was quiet for a moment. "Third in the cohort. Lost the final by a beat. Some stranger reads that."

Lira shook her head. "It goes in as a final. Against the champion. Lost by one beat in the fourth exchange on a mended seam." Her palm settled over the certificate in her coat. "If I were a stranger reading it, I'd want to know who you were."

Brom looked at her for a while, and then down at his boots, and kept whatever he thought to himself.

---

Cael found Karis in the top-floor reading room that afternoon, and she was already still.

He knew it from the door. Bracken's copy of the tournament charter lay open in front of her, and the Greyvane notebooks had stood stacked by her left hand since the evening of the hand-cart. She was not reading. She had one finger on a line and was not moving at all. Not her eyes, not her breath that he could see. A year and a half had taught him the look: a page had done something to her. A word inside that first minute would cost her the thread.

He eased the door shut, took the far chair, opened the observation notebook at Fiske's page, and let her be.

After a while she took up her pen and copied something into the grey notebook, slowly, one word at a time, checking each word against the charter before she wrote the next. The open notebook came sliding down the table to him. She said nothing.

He read the copied line.

*Participating academies shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.*

He read it a second time, then looked up.

"Sixth part," said Karis. "Participation. The fourth subsection." Her voice was very even. "Read the preamble before you say anything. It's on the facing page."

He swung the charter round to face him. Most of the paragraph was dry history. Halfway down, the history woke up. Forty years ago a famous house had left two of its strongest practitioners out of a qualifying season altogether, though both were fit and both were enrolled. It had done it to keep a richer house sweet over a bargain about postings. The tournament's board had been so angry that it wrote the fourth subsection the following year, so that no house could ever again keep a person off its side for reasons of its own.

"It's a compulsion clause," he said.

"It's a compulsion clause. Written by angry people, in a hurry, about something else entirely." Her palm went flat to the wood. "Now read the line again. *Any practitioner.* Then *enrollment record*. That's all. It does not say any *classified* practitioner. Nor of any rated tier. It doesn't name the schedule. I've been through the whole sixth part twice, every subsection, looking for the place where somebody thought to add it, and nobody did."

Neither of them said the next thing. It sat on the table between them.

Cael's eyes stayed on the copied line. Later, writing it down, he set down first what he had felt first, because that was the honest order: not hope, and not excitement. It was a flat, cold sort of knowing, the kind you feel meeting somebody in a strange town whose face you have seen before. Old words, set down by the dead for one purpose, quietly doing another they had never dreamed of, with nobody left alive to ask what they had meant.

The assay clause had been that. Greyvane's schedule, with its gap, had been one too. It had the shape of every good thing that ever happened to him.

"Somebody will fight it," he said.

"Somebody will fight every word of it. By counsel. Before the tournament's board. Clause on clause. Somewhere there are clauses I haven't found that lean on this one, and at least one leans against us." Karis shut the charter, gently, the way one shuts a door on someone asleep. "And I won't touch it. Not this week, not this month. Bracken won't hear it from me, nor the chancellor, nor Lira over supper. I want to tell you why before you want to move, because you will want to, and it's cheaper to argue now than later."

"Tell me."

"A question you ask early is a question somebody else gets to answer first." She held his eyes. "Walk it down to counsel before the recess and it's out of this room. By the time we need it, some other house will have read it as well, and written its note on it. The people who've thought longest about this line will be people who did their thinking on the other side of the table."

"So it goes in the book and stays there."

"Into the book, and there it sits." She took the grey notebook back, and beside the copied line she wrote a single word, and Cael read it across the table, upside down, as he read most things.

*Interesting.*

She closed the notebook on it.

"That's all it is," she said. "Right now it's somebody else's sentence and one word of mine. Should it ever grow into something, it grows next year, out of documents, with a season's labour under it. Not a day less."

"Agreed."

"Don't agree with me. Decide."

"It's banked," said Cael. "No one touches it. You, me, anybody, until it's ready."

"Good," said Karis. The notebook went inside her coat, and she turned back to the charter's fifth part, on apparatus, which she had not yet read; her head did not come up again until the bell.

# Chapter 12 — Doctrine

Karis read the clause to him in carrel eleven with her finger under each word, the way Vell had once taught him to read a ledger, and on the fourth line she stopped.

"There," she said. "That's the part nobody tells you."

He leaned over her shoulder. It was Halcenvane's own copy of the founding clause, with the charter article that carried it set out beneath, and below that the assessment office's standing rule for applying it, in a dry modern hand. He had read all three before, on the first night, and again in Bracken's room, and again on the night the notice came. He had not read them the way Karis read things, which was as if each word had been put there by somebody who might be lying.

*The demonstration so made shall stand as the measure of record for the enrollee, and every subsequent demonstration shall be laid against it.*

"Laid against," said Karis. "Like a rule against a plank. A plank isn't measured by its length. You measure it by how far it is from the mark." She took her finger off the line. "The office doesn't ask whether you're good. Not once, anywhere in this. It asks whether you're the same as you were, and then whether you're more. That's all a semester evaluation is. The old line, and the new line, and the distance between them."

"So the first line is everything."

"The first line is the only one you get to draw yourself." She sat back in her chair. "Every one after that is drawn by the first."

He walked back to the second quadrangle in the dusk with that sentence turning in his head, and for three nights afterward he slept badly.

It was not the room he feared; he had stood before two hundred people with the Compact's warden at the other table and read a schedule back to them page by page, and no room of instruments could be worse than that. It was the arithmetic: every practitioner on every board had been set down by an Arbiter at fourteen and handed a number. He was being handed a pen, and whatever he drew with it on the day would go into a folder in Gault's cabinet and stay there. One day an inspector would take it out, with a grudge or only with a schedule, and read it on a table, or aloud in a hearing room. *Choose with that in mind,* Bracken had said, holding out the tag on its cord. Cael had nodded at the time as if he understood. He was only now beginning to.

By the third night he could see the two ways to cheat it, one low and one high, and he could see neither was right, and he could not yet see what was.

He put it to the others on the fourth evening, in the common room, because three nights alone with it had taught him he was carrying it badly.

Brom was cheerful on the floor with fresh ice on his shoulder, because Ephram had found a new hole in him that afternoon. Lira had her knees drawn up on the window seat. Karis was quarrelling in pencil with a fair copy of the clause.

He gave them the low line first, since it was the one he was ashamed of wanting, and he wanted it out on the table where they could all see it. Show less than he had, carefully, just enough to satisfy the provision; then let the rest out a little each semester and have it written down as growth. Years of easy evaluations, and room to hide in.

"It's comfortable," he said. "That's the trouble with it. Comfortable for years, and then one day not, and all at once." He held up a finger. "Three costs. One: the line's a document. It goes into the file in Gault's hand, and one day an inspector opens the file. Bracken's warned me twice, and Withrow wrote *sooner* on her sheet with her own pen. Find a feeble line in there and you've found proof the provision lets feeble people in. Somebody uses that against the provision, and I've lost the only floor I stand on."

"Two," said Karis, not looking up.

"Two, it runs out." Another finger. "What I've held back is a fixed sum. Spend it as growth, a bit each semester, and one day I'll need to grow for real and find the purse empty. The requirement never stops. Every semester, no exceptions; that's the record I signed."

"Three," said Brom, from the floor.

Cael looked at him.

"You'd have to stand there while he wrote it down," said Brom. He was mild about it, easing the ice along his shoulder. "In his own room. Him being straight with you, and you knowing what you'd left out, and him thanking you for it." He looked up. "I couldn't do that. Not to somebody being straight with me. I'd go red, like you did over the registrar."

There was a short silence.

"And it's a lie," said Cael quietly. "Written down. In the one file I went into a hearing room to get made honest."

The high line took less time, because everybody in the room already knew why it could not be done. He could not show everything. Showing everything meant Pressure, which nobody had ever seen him use, and Compression, which nobody had ever seen at all, and the Ember on a floor in front of a faculty panel, and every one of them was a question with a door behind it that he could not open, and the door behind all the doors was the thing only the four of them knew.

"Low is a lie, then," he said, "and honest can't be done. That's the choice."

"It isn't the choice," said Lira.

They all looked round. She had not moved from the window seat.

"Low or honest, you keep saying. Those aren't the two." She said it calmly, as if pointing out a step he had missed on a stair. "Three years you've kept things in your pocket on every floor we've stood on. Choosing what goes out and what stays in. I was there for all of it. I never once thought of it as lying, and neither did you."

"That was leaving things unsaid. This is writing a figure against my own name and putting my signature under it."

"It's picking from what's already been said about you." She swung her feet down off the seat. "Out loud, on paper, in public, by people who weren't you. The stationer in Ostrand will sell anybody a copy for six silver. Tell me what's in it."

He could have recited it. Fourteen of Greyvane's monthly sittings had been read into the hearing as exhibits, one after another, and every one of them, under three assessors' names, had said the same two things about him: that he could get out of the way with the Wind, and that he could feel a weight coming before it came. A court had looked at those two and filed them where anybody could find them. Nothing else about how he was made had ever been put where the world could read it, except the Ember, and that only in a single late exhibit, half a page, so thin that showing it on Gault's floor would not confirm anything. It would announce something. Of Pressure and Compression it said nothing.

"Two," he said.

"Then show them those two." Lira leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "All of them. Not most. Not the safe amount. Every bit you've got, in those two and nowhere else." She held his eyes. "A low line's a lie about you. This is the plain truth about the boy in that transcript. And when you're better in a semester's time, which you will be, because you're fifteen and it'll happen whether you want it or not, you'll be better in exactly the places they're already watching."

Cael was silent a good while.

"That's it," he said.

"I know."

"You've known for days."

"Since Tuesday." Lira stood up and stretched until her shoulders cracked. "If I'd said it on Tuesday you'd have spent four days finding what was wrong with it, and then argued it out with yourself in front of Gault, halfway through a trial." She paused at the door. "And don't go writing this down as me managing you. That's how you work. I've had three years to learn it."

---

That night it went into the binder under *method*. Lira, he thought, would accept that.

*Baseline doctrine. Two capabilities are on the public record. Those two I show at full stretch, with nothing kept back inside either. Everything the record doesn't hold stays held.*

*Not the low line. Not the whole of me either. It's as much of the public boy as there is. Sworn, and asked if it was fair to the record: yes, at once, with no pause for a clerk to time.*

*Cost: it hems me in. From now on every semester has to grow those two and only those two, or explain itself. The way through gets tighter each time.*

*In return: a path I could defend in a hearing room, and I've been inside those rooms. Narrow and defensible beats wide.*

He slept, after that, for the first time in four nights, and woke at the fifth bell with his mind quite clear and one thing still sitting in it.

*Every bit you've got, in those two.* He lay looking at the words in his head for a long while, and found he did not know how much that was. He knew his ceiling: four free bursts on timber now, and the fifth to pay. He knew the read's distance and its limits, and one new limit he had found in the low hall. But he had spent three years learning to show three-quarters of everything, on every floor, as a habit, the way a man who has been poor a long time goes on eating half his bread and saving the rest even after he has money. No panel had ever seen the whole of anything from him, and he was no longer sure he knew how to show it.

He told Karis about the Ember the next morning, in carrel eleven, because it was hers before it was his and he did not want her to hear it from the table.

"I'm leaving it out," he said. "The exhibit's thin. If I show it, it's news, not confirmation."

Karis did not look up from the page she was copying for a moment. Then she put her pen down and laid both hands flat on the desk, the way she did when she had decided something.

"Good," she said.

"You don't mind?"

"I mind a great deal, in a way that has nothing to do with whether you're right." She looked at him then, and her face was quite steady. "Of everything anybody's ever taken from me, that's the one I was glad to give. I'd like it to stay in our book a while longer before it goes into anybody else's. Gault's included." She picked up the pen again. "That's sentiment, not law. Ignore it if it ever matters. It doesn't matter yet."

He had two supervised sessions left before the day, if the day came when he thought it would. He used them both.

In the first, under a Shield instructor who crossed over at the eleventh minute, he ran the framework at its full pace against nobody, alone on the east hall's timber, and made himself go all the way through every burst instead of easing off at the end, and found he had been easing off at the end for so long it felt like falling. In the second, under a Mire Path instructor who had never supervised him and plainly resented the hour, he stood at the edge of the floor with his eyes shut while two second-years threw practice weights at the boards in front of him, and called the weight and the line of each before it landed. He was right about all of them. It was harder than it had any right to be, because he was trying, for the first time in years, to let the read be as good as it was.

The Mire instructor watched the whole of the second session with her arms folded and her mouth turned down, and crossed over, like everybody, at about the fourteenth minute. But she did one thing none of the others had done. At the end, signing his sheet, she asked the second-years which weights they had thrown, and checked his calls against their answers, one by one, with her pen. They all matched, and she let that pass without a word. She only signed, and gave him the sheet, and went out. He wrote her into the margin of the eleven with a small mark beside her name: *checks.*

The notice came on the forty-sixth morning, in his pigeonhole, two days before the day, as the first one had said it would.

*The assessment office will receive the entry demonstration of the assay-provision enrollee on the forty-eighth day, at the third bell, in the demonstration room of the assessment wing. Magister Gault presiding. The enrollee will present himself at the near door.*

He read it standing in the cloister, and found he was not afraid of it any longer. What he was instead had no name yet. It was a little like the moment on the mark before Edran, when he had found a question was steadier to stand on than an answer.

---

The night before, Lira took him out to the cracked yard behind the second quadrangle, where nobody went after dark, and made him walk the framework through once at a stroll, with no bursts in it at all.

"Not to practise," she said. "You've practised. Just so your feet know where they're going in the morning."

They walked it through together, slowly, in the starlight. The stone was uneven, like the yard at Greyvane, and she had chosen it for that. When they were done she did not go in. She sat down on the step of the wash-house with her arms round her knees, and after a moment he sat beside her.

"Fenmark," she said, out of nowhere. "When I was fourteen."

He waited. She had told him about Fenmark once, on a road, in the dark, and never again.

"The Arbiter took about a minute and a half." Her eyes were on the wall's dark bulk, not on him. "It came, and I saw it, and it looked at me, and then the station clerk wrote *Copper* on a sheet and *Rank One*, and that was me. A minute and a half. Two years now I've been trying to get somebody to look longer than that." She was quiet a moment. "That man tomorrow has a room built for nothing but looking. Eleven instruments. He's got a clerk to time your blinks."

"I know."

"Do you know what I'd give to stand in a room like that?" There was no self-pity in it; she might have been telling him the hour. "Not to win anything. Just to be on a floor before a man whose whole work is getting it right, and have him get me right, and write it down where it can't be lost."

"You'll get one."

"An Arbiter station. One day. When the queue moves." She shrugged. "That's not the same. Same minute and a half, nicer manners."

"So make him look." She turned her head at last. "Not at three-quarters. At you. Make him look the whole time, and at the end, make him write down what he actually saw." Her voice did not change, but something underneath it did. "Somebody in this family ought to get looked at properly by a room like that. It might as well be you first."

There was nothing he could have said to that which would not have been too small. He nodded.

"And sleep," said Lira, getting up. "That's an order, not advice. If you turn up in that room having lain awake all night, I'll know, and Gault will know, and the clerk will write it down."

He sat on the step a while after she had gone, in the dark, with the yard's broken stone pale under the stars, and found the thing she had said was sitting in his chest like a stone of its own, warm from the sun. *Somebody in this family.* She had never used the word before. Not on any road, not in any town. He did not think she had noticed using it now.

The wash-house door creaked behind him, and Karis came out with his coat over her arm, which he had left in the common room, and dropped it round his shoulders without asking whether he was cold.

"I can't come in tomorrow," she said, sitting down on the step where Lira had been. "Closed panel. I asked Bracken. He said I could read the note afterward, when it's filed, like everybody else, and then he looked at me as if I'd asked to borrow his spectacles."

"I know."

"I'll be in carrel eleven with the door open." She pulled her own coat tighter. "It goes on the list, whatever he writes. The one I told you to keep, of everything anybody writes on you." She glanced sideways at him. "Did you start it?"

"Last week."

"How long is it?"

"Four pages."

Karis nodded slowly, as if that were about what she had expected and somewhat worse.

"It'll be four and a line tomorrow," she said. "That's all it'll be. One line. Everybody's lived through worse lines than one." She got up. "Go to bed. Lira will have told you that already. I'm only saying it so it's from two of us."

He slept, more or less.

In the morning Brom was waiting at the bottom of the quadrangle stair with two pieces of bread wrapped in a cloth, one for each of them.

He walked Cael across the bluff in the early light without saying anything at all, which was exactly what Cael wanted. They went by the long way, round the rim of the Crown yard, where the porter was already out with his pail chalking the practice rings, and past the north hall, where somebody was running a drill alone on the big stone floor, the same somebody perhaps who had been running one there on Cael's first morning. Brom ate his bread. Cael carried his and did not eat it, and Brom noticed and said nothing about that either. The assessment wing's low wall came up ahead of them, pale and new, with its single gate standing open and nobody in it.

At the gate Brom stopped, and put one heavy hand on his shoulder, briefly, and took it away.

"Count the half-second." He turned and went back the way they had come.

---

The demonstration room was the most expensive place on the bluff, and he stopped just inside the near door for a moment longer than he had meant to, looking at it.

Weeks ago, from the far side of the low wall, he had guessed at what the place had cost from its own gate and its own wall and the begging letters Karis had pinned up in carrel eleven. The guess had been much too small.

Fifty feet by thirty, the room was floored in oak laid on bearers like the east hall's, and the boards answered his first step. Brass ran through the boards in a grid, finger-wide strips set flush a third of a pace apart, wall to wall in both directions. Nobody at the long table would need to stand to see where a foot had come down. He had grown up at Hesk's bench, watching an instrument-maker scribe a line on brass and check it three times before he cut, and he knew what it cost to set a true line over fifty feet, in any material at all. Whoever had laid this had done it by survey and not by eye. At the far end, where a careless setter's work always wandered, these ran true. The crossings were as sharp at the back wall as at his feet.

Along the north wall stood the apparatus, and it was making its small noises.

Two weeks of asking had got him the names of nine pieces out of the eleven. First a drop-frame: a trigger, a rail, a weight to run down it, and four more weights racked underneath; someone at the far end tested the pawl, and it gave one soft click. A pendulum escapement in a glass case, ticking a steady interval that he found himself breathing in time with before he noticed and stopped. Nine shutters in a board, opened in an order a drum behind them decided; the drum's ratchet made the noise of a thumbnail going slowly down the teeth of a comb. Three spring plates on braces, each with a date of calibration painted on its housing. For footprints, a shallow tray of chalk dust. And at the very end, a thing like a tall brass funnel over a cup, which dripped into the cup with a small bright sound every few seconds, and which he could not name at all.

Facing all of it across the grid, against the south wall, stood a long table with three chairs, and at its end, a little way off, a stool and a sloped desk for the clerk.

Magister Gault was already in the middle chair. He was a heavy man in his fifties, Silver-tier, with a stillness about him that Cael read at once as trained and not natural, the stillness of somebody who had decided long ago that moving was a thing to be done on purpose. He did not stand. On his right sat the Ash Path instructor from the lecture range, the fourth name on Cael's standing list, with her book against her chest. On his left sat the Mire instructor who had resented his last session, and who did not look as though she had grown to like the hour since. The clerk, a young man with a ledger and three pens laid in a row, had already written something.

"Enrollee." Gault did not offer a hand. "Sit. I will tell you how the morning goes, once. You will have it every half-year from now on, and I would rather not repeat myself."

Cael sat in the single chair that faced the table.

"There is no opponent here." Gault put his broad palm down on the forms. "You will not be scored against anybody. You will do six things for me, and each of them is built to look at one part of you and nothing else. In the gaps I'll put questions to you, and they go in the book with the rest. All of it goes down in my clerk's hand: my question, your answer, and the length of the silence between."

He paused, and Cael understood that the next part was the part Gault cared about.

"Three rules of this room. The first. When you reach something you cannot do, tell me. The book will record that you could not. Nothing more, and nothing held against you; a plain fact, written down. If I have to work out for myself that you've refused, it tells me less than if you had said so." His finger moved a little on the forms. "The second. I want sameness, not your best. Six runs that agree at three-quarters tell me more than a marvel followed by five that contradict it. The third." For the first time his eyes came straight to Cael's. "This morning is your line. You will be laid against it every half-year you are here. So don't do it for me. Do it for the boy who'll stand on this oak five months from now and have to beat whatever you leave here this morning."

Of all the things Cael had readied himself for, he had not thought of this one: that the man holding the measure would name, in his first minute and as a matter of routine, the very thing about the measure that had kept Cael awake for three nights.

"Understood, Magister."

"Then we begin."

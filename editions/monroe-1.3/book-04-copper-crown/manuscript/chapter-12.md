# Chapter 12 — Doctrine

Karis read the clause to him in carrel eleven with her finger under each word, the way Vell had once taught him to read a ledger, and on the fourth line she stopped.

"There," she said. "That's the part nobody tells you."

He leaned over her shoulder. It was Halcenvane's own copy of the founding clause, with the charter article that carried it set out beneath, and below that the assessment office's standing rule for applying it, in a dry modern hand. He had read all three before. He had read them on the first night, and again in Bracken's room, and again on the night the notice came. He had not read them the way Karis read things, which was as if each word had been put there by somebody who might be lying.

*The demonstration so made shall stand as the measure of record for the enrollee, and every subsequent demonstration shall be laid against it.*

"Laid against," said Karis. "Like a rule against a plank. You don't measure a plank by how long it is. You measure it by how far it is from the mark." She took her finger off the line. "The office doesn't ask whether you're good. Not once, anywhere in this. It asks whether you're the same as you were, and then whether you're more. That's all a semester evaluation is. The old line, and the new line, and the distance between them."

"So the first line is everything."

"The first line is the only one you get to draw yourself." She sat back in her chair. "Every one after that is drawn by the first."

He walked back to the second quadrangle in the dusk with that sentence going round in his head like a cart wheel, and that night he did not sleep, and the night after he slept very little.

It was not fear of the room. He had stood in worse rooms. He had stood in a hall in front of two hundred people with the Compact's warden at the other table, and read the schedule back to them from its first page to its last, and that had been worse than any room with instruments in it could be. This was something else. It was the plain arithmetic of the clause, and the arithmetic would not let him alone.

Whatever he showed on the day would be the line. Every half-year after it, for as long as he was on the bluff, somebody would lay a new line beside it and measure the gap. And he could choose, now, before the day came, where the first line went. Nobody else on the bluff had ever been given that choice about themselves. Every practitioner on every board had been set down by an Arbiter at fourteen and handed a number they had no say in. He was being handed a pen.

He kept thinking of the file, the actual file, a folder in Gault's cabinet with a sheet in it. He could see it quite clearly in the dark: a heavy man's handwriting, a date, a few lines. He could see it being taken out in five years by somebody he had never met, an inspector with a grudge or only with a schedule, and laid on a table, and read. Bracken had told him on the first day that somebody would come up the road one day to read his record with a grudge. Withrow had written *sooner* on her sheet. Whatever went into that folder on the day would be read by them. It would be read by every evaluator who came after Gault. It would be read, if things went the way things had a habit of going for him, in a hearing room, aloud.

*Choose with that in mind,* Bracken had said, holding out the tag on its cord. He had nodded, at the time, as if he had understood. He had not understood. He was beginning to.

On the third night he lay looking at the ceiling and worked out, for the first time with complete clarity, the two ways it could be made to cheat.

He could set the line low. He could walk into Gault's room and show less than he had, on purpose, carefully, enough to satisfy the provision and no more. Then each semester he could show a little more of what he had always had, and it would be written down as growth, and nobody would ever know the difference, because the instruments only measured the gap. It would buy him years of easy evaluations. It would buy him room to hide in.

Or he could set it high. He could show everything. Then there would be no room at all, and every semester would have to find real growth on demand, in a body that was fifteen and could not be hurried, and the first time it failed to grow on schedule the gap would read as a fall.

He lay there between the two, and neither of them was right, and he could not see where the right one was.

---

He put it to the others on the fourth evening, in the common room, because he had been carrying it alone long enough to know he was carrying it badly.

Brom was on the floor with his back against the wall and the ice on his shoulder again; Ephram had found something new that afternoon and Brom was in high spirits about it. Lira was on the window seat with her knees drawn up. Karis had the clause copied out on the table, with three notes already in the margin in her small pencil.

He laid out the low line first, because it was the one he was ashamed of being tempted by, and he wanted it on the table where they could all look at it.

"It's very comfortable," he said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's comfortable for years, and then it isn't, and when it stops being comfortable it stops all at once." He held up a finger. "It costs three ways. First, the line's a document. Gault writes it, it goes in the file, and one day an inspector reads it. Bracken's told me so twice. Withrow told me with her own pen, on her own sheet: *sooner*. If an inspector opens the file and finds a weak line, that's evidence the provision let in a weak practitioner. That's an argument against the provision. And the provision is the only floor I've got."

"Second," said Karis, not looking up.

"Second, it runs out." He put up another finger. "I've only got so much held back. If I spend it a little each semester as growth, I'll have nothing left on the day I really need to grow and can't. And the requirement doesn't stop. Every semester. Without exception. It says so on the record I signed."

"Third," said Brom, from the floor.

Cael looked at him.

"You'd have to stand there while he wrote it down," said Brom. He said it quite mildly, shifting the ice. "In his own room. Him being straight with you, and you knowing what you'd left out, and him thanking you for it." He looked up. "I couldn't do that. Not to somebody being straight with me. I'd go red, like you did over the registrar."

There was a short silence.

"Third," said Cael quietly, "it's a lie. In writing. On a paper I fought a hearing to make true."

He laid out the high line after that, and it was shorter, because everybody in the room already knew why it was impossible. He could not show everything. Showing everything meant Pressure, which nobody had ever seen him use, and Compression, which nobody had ever seen at all, and the Ember on a floor in front of a faculty panel, and every one of them was a question with a door behind it that he could not open, and the door behind all the doors was the thing only the four of them knew.

"So it's low, or it's honest," he said. "And honest isn't possible, and low is a lie."

"It isn't," said Lira.

They all looked round. She had not moved from the window seat.

"You keep saying low or honest. Those aren't the two." She said it calmly, as if she were pointing out a step he had missed on a stair. "You've been banking for three years, Cael. Holding things back. Choosing what to show and when. Nobody's ever called it lying, and I've watched you do every bit of it."

"Banking's choosing not to show something. This is choosing what a number says I am."

"No. This is choosing which of the things that are *already on the record* you show them." She swung her feet down off the seat. "There's a transcript. It's public. Anybody between here and the coast can send for it at six silver marks. What does it say you can do?"

He knew without thinking. He had read that transcript more times than anybody alive except perhaps Withrow.

Greyvane's monthly demonstrations had gone into the hearing record as exhibits, fourteen sessions of them, and across all fourteen they carried two things: the Wind framework, and the Iron read. Those two were in an adjudicated record with three assessors' names under them. They were, quite literally, the only two things about his architecture that the world had any right to know. The Ember was in it too, but in one exhibit, late and thin, half a page; showing it on Gault's floor would not be confirmation of anything the record held. It would be news. And the rest was not there at all.

"Two," he said.

"Two." Lira leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "At the top of what you can really do with them. Nothing shaved off. Not three-quarters. Not *enough*. Everything you've got, inside those two." She held his eyes. "That's not a low line. That's a true line, for the boy in the transcript. And next semester, when you've grown, you'll have grown in the two they already know about, and it'll be real, because you're fifteen and you'll be better in five months whether you plan it or not."

Cael looked at the clause on the table for a long time.

"That's it," he said.

"I know."

"You've known for days."

"Since Tuesday." Lira stood up and stretched until her shoulders cracked. "But you had to get there on your own feet or you'd have argued with it in Gault's room, halfway through a trial, and that would have been a disaster." She paused at the door. "And that's not me managing you, before you go and write it down as that. It's just how you work. I've had three years of it."

---

He wrote it down that night as method, not management, which he felt was fair to both of them.

*Baseline doctrine. Show what the record already carries, at genuine top quality, with nothing held back inside those two. Hold everything the record doesn't carry.*

*It isn't the low line. It isn't the whole truth either. It's the honest maximum of the public version of me. If anybody ever asks me under oath whether the baseline was a fair statement of what the record showed, the answer is yes, and I won't need to stop and think before I give it.*

*Price: it narrows me. Every evaluation from now on has to show growth in two things only, or show a reason why not. That's a narrow road, and it'll get narrower every half-year.*

*Gain: it's a road I can walk into a hearing room and defend.*

*I've been in one. That counts for more than the road being wide.*

He slept, after that, for the first time in four nights, and woke at the fifth bell with his mind quite clear and one thing still sitting in it.

*At the top of what you can really do.* He was not sure he knew what that was. He knew his ceiling: four free bursts on timber now, and the fifth to pay. He knew the read's distance and its limits, and one new limit he had found in the low hall. But he had spent three years learning to show three-quarters of everything, on every floor, as a habit, the way a man who has been poor a long time goes on eating half his bread and saving the rest even after he has money. He had never once in front of a panel shown the whole of anything. He did not know if he still could.

He told Karis about the Ember the next morning, in carrel eleven, because it was hers before it was his and he did not want her to hear it from the table.

"I'm leaving it out," he said. "The exhibit's thin. If I show it, it's news, not confirmation."

Karis did not look up from the page she was copying for a moment. Then she put her pen down and laid both hands flat on the desk, the way she did when she had decided something.

"Good," she said.

"You don't mind?"

"I mind a great deal, in a way that has nothing to do with whether you're right." She looked at him then, and her face was quite steady. "It was the first thing anybody ever took from me that I wanted taken. I'd like it to stay in our book a while longer before it goes into anybody else's. Gault's included." She picked up the pen again. "That's sentiment, not law. Ignore it if it ever matters. It doesn't matter yet."

He had two supervised sessions left before the day, if the day came when he thought it would. He used them both.

In the first, under a Shield instructor who crossed over at the eleventh minute, he ran the framework at its full pace against nobody, alone on the east hall's timber, and made himself go all the way through every burst instead of easing off at the end, and found he had been easing off at the end for so long that it felt like falling. In the second, under a Mire Path instructor who had never supervised him and plainly resented the hour, he stood at the edge of the floor with his eyes shut while two second-years threw practice weights at the boards in front of him, and called the weight and the line of each before it landed. He was right about all of them. It was harder than it had any right to be, because he was trying, for the first time in years, to let the read be as good as it was.

The Mire instructor watched the whole of the second session with her arms folded and her mouth turned down, and crossed over, like everybody, at about the fourteenth minute. But she did one thing none of the others had done. At the end, signing his sheet, she asked the second-years which weights they had thrown, and checked his calls against their answers, one by one, with her pen. She did not say anything when they all matched. She only signed, and gave him the sheet, and went out. He wrote her into the margin of the eleven with a small mark beside her name: *checks.*

The notice came on the forty-sixth morning, in his pigeonhole, two days before the day, as the first one had said it would.

*The assessment office will receive the entry demonstration of the assay-provision enrollee on the forty-eighth day, at the third bell, in the demonstration room of the assessment wing. Magister Gault presiding. The enrollee will present himself at the near door.*

He read it standing in the cloister, and found he was not afraid of it any longer. He was something else, something he did not have a word for yet. It was a little like the moment on the mark before Edran, when he had found that a question was steadier to stand on than an answer.

---

The night before, Lira took him out to the cracked yard behind the second quadrangle, where nobody went after dark, and made him walk the framework through once at a stroll, with no bursts in it at all.

"Not to practise," she said. "You've practised. Just so your feet know where they're going in the morning."

They walked it through together, slowly, in the starlight. The stone was uneven, like the yard at Greyvane, and she had chosen it for that. When they were done she did not go in. She sat down on the step of the wash-house with her arms round her knees, and after a moment he sat beside her.

"Fenmark," she said, out of nowhere. "When I was fourteen."

He waited. She had told him about Fenmark once, on a road, in the dark, and never again.

"The Arbiter took about a minute and a half." Lira was looking at the dark shape of the wall, not at him. "It came, and I saw it, and it looked at me, and then the station clerk wrote *Copper* on a sheet and *Rank One*, and that was me. A minute and a half. I've been trying for two years to make somebody look at me for longer than that." She was quiet a moment. "That man tomorrow has a room built for nothing but looking. Eleven instruments. A clerk who writes down how long you take to blink."

"I know."

"Do you know what I'd give to stand in a room like that?" She said it without any self-pity at all, as plainly as she might have given the time. "Not to win anything. Just to stand on a floor in front of a man whose whole work is getting it right, and have him get me right, and write it down where it can't be lost."

"You'll get one."

"An Arbiter station. One day. When the queue moves." She shrugged. "That's not the same. That's the same minute and a half again, only with better manners."

"So make him look." She turned her head at last. "Not at three-quarters. At you. Make him look the whole time, and at the end, make him write down what he actually saw." Her voice did not change, but something underneath it did. "Somebody in this family ought to get looked at properly by a room like that. It might as well be you first."

He did not trust himself to answer that. He nodded.

"And sleep," said Lira, getting up. "That's an order, not advice. If you turn up in that room having lain awake all night, I'll know, and Gault will know, and the clerk will write it down."

He sat on the step a while after she had gone, in the dark, with the yard's broken stone pale under the stars, and found that the thing she had said was sitting in his chest like a stone of its own, warm from the sun. *Somebody in this family.* She had never used the word before. Not on any road, not in any town. He did not think she had noticed using it now.

The wash-house door creaked behind him, and Karis came out with his coat over her arm, which he had left in the common room, and dropped it round his shoulders without asking whether he was cold.

"I can't come in tomorrow," she said, sitting down on the step where Lira had been. "Closed panel. I asked Bracken. He said I could read the note afterward, when it's filed, like everybody else, and then he looked at me as if I'd asked to borrow his spectacles."

"I know."

"I'll be in carrel eleven with the door open." She pulled her own coat tighter. "Whatever he writes, I'll put it on the list. The one I told you to keep, of everything anybody writes on you." She glanced sideways at him. "Did you start it?"

"Last week."

"How long is it?"

"Four pages."

Karis nodded slowly, as if that were about what she had expected and somewhat worse.

"It'll be four and a line tomorrow," she said. "That's all it'll be. One line. Everybody's lived through worse lines than one." She got up. "Go to bed. Lira will have told you that already. I'm only saying it so that it's from two of us."

He slept, more or less.

In the morning Brom was waiting at the bottom of the quadrangle stair with two pieces of bread wrapped in a cloth, one for each of them.

He walked Cael across the bluff in the early light without saying anything at all, which was exactly what Cael wanted. They went by the long way, round the rim of the Crown yard, where the porter was already out with his pail chalking the practice rings, and past the north hall, where somebody was running a drill alone on the big stone floor, the same somebody perhaps who had been running one there on Cael's first morning. Brom ate his bread. Cael carried his and did not eat it, and Brom noticed and said nothing about that either. The assessment wing's low wall came up ahead of them, pale and new, with its single gate standing open and nobody in it.

At the gate Brom stopped, and put one heavy hand on his shoulder, briefly, and took it away.

"Count the half-second," he said, and went.

---

The demonstration room was the most expensive place on the bluff, and he stopped just inside the near door for a moment longer than he had meant to, looking at it.

He had worked the money out from outside, weeks ago, from the separate wall and the separate gate and Karis's appeal letters. He had not been ready for what the money had bought.

The room was fifty feet long and thirty wide and its floor was oak, sprung like the east hall's on bearers, so that he felt it answer under his first step. Let into the oak, flush with its surface, ran strips of brass no wider than a finger, every third of a pace, both ways, the whole length and breadth of the room, so that the floor was a grid anybody at the long table could read a position off without rising. He had grown up at Hesk's bench, watching an instrument-maker scribe a line on brass and check it three times before he cut, and he knew what it cost to set a true line over fifty feet, in any material at all. Whoever had laid this had done it by survey and not by eye. The lines did not drift at the far end, where a lazy man's always drifted. The crossings were as sharp at the back wall as at his feet.

Along the north wall stood the apparatus, and it was making its small noises.

He had spent two weeks finding out what he could about it, and could now name nine of the eleven. A drop-frame, a weight on a rail released by a trigger, with four weights racked beneath it; its pawl clicked once, softly, as somebody at the end of the room checked it. A pendulum escapement in a glass case, ticking a steady interval that he found himself breathing in time with before he noticed and stopped. A shutter-board of nine louvres with a drum behind it that chose their order, and the drum's ratchet made a sound like a fingernail drawn slowly down a comb. Three spring plates on braces, each with a date of calibration painted on its housing. A shallow tray of fine chalk dust for taking the print of a foot. And at the very end, a thing like a tall brass funnel over a cup, which dripped into the cup with a small bright sound every few seconds, and which he could not name at all.

On the south side, a long table, three chairs behind it, and a fourth set apart at the end for the clerk.

Magister Gault was already in the middle chair. He was a heavy man in his fifties, Silver-tier, with a stillness about him that Cael read at once as trained and not natural, the stillness of somebody who had decided long ago that moving was a thing to be done on purpose. He did not stand. On his right sat the Ash Path instructor from the lecture range, the fourth name on Cael's standing list, with her book against her chest. On his left sat the Mire instructor who had resented his last session, and who did not look as though she had grown to like the hour since. The clerk, a young man with a ledger and three pens laid in a row, had already written something.

"Enrollee." Gault did not offer a hand. "Sit, please. Before we begin you will hear how this is done, because you will be doing it every semester and I would prefer to say it once."

Cael sat in the single chair set facing the table.

"Nobody in this room will fight you." Gault laid one broad hand flat on a stack of forms. "Nothing you do will be scored against anybody. This is a measure, not a bout. Six trials, each built to isolate one thing. Between them I will ask you questions, and the questions are part of the record too. The clerk writes everything. What I ask, what you answer, and how long you take to begin."

He paused, and Cael understood that the next part was the part Gault cared about.

"Three points of procedure. First. If at any moment you cannot do a thing, say so. It will be written that you could not, and it will count against you in no way except that it will be true. I would rather hear a refusal from you than work one out for myself." He moved one finger on the forms. "Second. I am measuring consistency. Not your best. I would rather have six trials that agree with each other at three-quarters than one remarkable trial and five that argue with it. Third." He looked at Cael directly for the first time. "What you do today is the line for as long as you are here. Do not do it for me. Do it for whoever you will be in five months, who has to stand on this floor and be better than whatever you put down this morning."

Cael had prepared for a great many things. He had not prepared for the man holding the instrument to say the thing about the instrument that he himself had lain awake three nights over, and say it plainly, in the first minute, as procedure.

"Understood, Magister."

"Then we begin."

# Chapter 22 — Minuted

Karis wrote notes, and everybody on the bluff who had dealings with her learned it inside a fortnight; most of them learned to be grateful for it.

They came under the door folded in three, in a small upright hand, with the date at the top left and a subject line under it, ruled once. *Prynn's index, shelf-marks 40–60: two wrong.* *Your Lira page: she leads with the left foot on stairs. Did you know?* *Brom has my good pencil. Please recover.*

Cael kept them in a biscuit tin under his bed, the lid held down with a strap because the hinge had gone. By the sixth week of the season there were thirty-four, and he had read every one of them at least twice. They were the only letters anyone had ever sent him that said exactly what they meant and then stopped.

So when she came up the long reading room under the law range on a wet Fourth-day afternoon, in person, with her ledger hugged to her front and both arms crossed over it like a child guarding a slate, he put his pen down before she had reached his table.

The rain had settled in at noon and showed no sign of leaving. It slid down the tall windows in a grey sheet that shook whenever the wind came off the river, and the light in the room had gone the colour of weak tea. Two law students were asleep over a single commentary at the far end, heads nearly touching. The lamp-boy had given up on his round and was sitting by the stove with his knees drawn up.

Cael had Lira's page open in front of him, the one he rewrote every few days now: the names that stood within four places of tenth on the Copper sheet, and what each had shown on the practice rings, and what each would do when a Wind fighter came at them off the chalk.

"No note," he said.

"No note." Karis did not sit down. "I'd like a formal session. Tonight, the common room, the ninth bell. All four of us, two hours, the door shut. I'll keep a minute and everybody will sign it."

"About him."

"About what I've been writing down about him." She moved the ledger a little higher on her chest. "I want you to hear it from me, in order, with the evidence first. Before you've had time to hear it from yourself. You're quicker than I am at some things. I'd rather you weren't quicker at this."

He looked at her properly: there was ink on the heel of her right hand, and a small crease between her brows that had not been there at breakfast. Her mouth had the set it took over a clause she had decided to read aloud although she disliked every word of it.

"You've been over it more than once," he said.

"Six times." Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "When a result annoys me, I go back to the beginning and walk through it again. If it's still on its feet after the sixth walk, I lose the right to be annoyed. I have to sign it."

"And it's still standing."

"Ninth bell." She turned to go, and then turned back, and her voice dropped so the sleeping law students would not have heard it even awake. "I stopped, Cael. In the middle of it, for six days, I stopped adding to it. I could see where the pieces were going, and I put the pencil down so they wouldn't get there. I've never done that in my life. Not once. Not over anything." She held his eyes. "Put that in the minute too. Under my own name."

She went away down the long room between the empty tables, quick and narrow, and the lamp-boy lifted his head as she passed and put it down again.

Cael sat and listened to the rain.

The binder lay shut by his elbow, and he left it shut. He knew what was there, and he knew what was not there, and the second was the trouble. Somewhere toward the back of the study there was a shape he had been stepping round for a fortnight, the way a man steps round a loose board in his own hall, so often and so smoothly that after a while he forgets the board is loose and only remembers that he always walks a little to the left there.

Karis had stopped for six days and come to confess it in a reading room. He had stopped for fourteen and called it method.

He picked up his pen and finished Lira's page. He did it slowly and with great care, every name and every habit, because he suspected it was the last ordinary piece of work he was going to do that day.

---

Brom came back from the cohort's evening hour at the eighth bell, wet to the knees and moving like a man who has been carried somewhere in a sack and set down again.

Six weeks now Brom had belonged, every evening, to what the residence called Rooke's taking-apart, though nobody could say who had first called it that. It was the hour after supper when the cohort stood round the east hall's walls and Rooke sent them in one at a time to find whatever Brom had not yet stopped doing. They always found it, because Rooke chose who went in, and he chose by what each of them had seen the night before. Then Rooke would stop the floor and have Brom do the thing again, slower, so that fourteen people could watch the exact moment it went wrong. It was, Brom said, like being a lesson somebody else was learning, and having to stand very still while they took notes on you.

"Merrick had me four times tonight," he said, lowering himself into the big chair by the common room fire with the care of a man setting down a crate of eggs. "Four. And Tarn twice, with short ones, which I didn't take, so Tarn's cross with me. Merrick's started saying sorry before he does it."

"Is he sorry?" said Lira.

"Not even slightly. It's just good manners." Brom worked one boot off with the toe of the other and set it on the fender, and then the second beside it, and stretched his stocking feet toward the coals with a groan of real feeling. "Rooke says I'm worse than I was in the first week. He says it as if it's a compliment."

"It is one," said Karis. "From him."

"I know it is. That's what's so upsetting."

Lira had not taken the window seat. She was on the hearth rug with her back against the settle and her knees up, close enough to the fire that the heat lay along her left side, which she only did when the hip had been asked for something. She had climbed again at the seventh session, from fourteenth to tenth in a little over two minutes, and come off the floor with her face showing nothing and her left hand pressed flat to her thigh.

Four bouts and none lost, the first of them her opening bout, drawn by lot, which had moved nobody. The yard had begun to argue about her in the refectory queue, over the bread, in the hushed and slightly offended tone people use for a thing they cannot fit anywhere, and Cael had written that down as a measurement in its own right.

"What's in the ledger?" Lira said to him, low, as he came past the settle.

"I don't know."

She looked up at him from the rug for a moment, and then away, and he knew she had heard the lie in it and had decided to let it pass. He did not know what was in the ledger. He knew exactly what was in it. Both were true, and only one of them was honest.

Karis had the table. She had laid out her ledger and, beside it, a second notebook Cael had never seen, thin and grey-covered, and three pencils in a row with their points all lying the same way. When Cael came in last and pushed the door to, she looked at the door, and then at him, and he went back and turned the key.

"Thank you," said Karis. "I'm going to be very formal. I'm sorry. I can't do it any other way."

"Be as formal as you like," said Brom, with his eyes shut. "It's restful. It's like church, with sums."

Karis wrote the day and the bell at the head of a clean page, and the four names under them, and under the names: *Business: one research finding, and the question that follows it.* She looked at the line as if weighing whether the word *question* was strong enough, and decided it was, and looked up.

"I'll give you the finding," she said. "Then I'll stop. After that it isn't research any more, and I won't pretend I know more about the rest than any of you. I don't. I've tried."

She set out the rules of the minute before anything else, as she had set them out at Greyvane on the first evening of the whitewashed room: every speaker named, and words written as spoken, not as the writer would have liked them. Nothing struck out; a correction went in beside the thing corrected, with the hour. And at the end every person in the room signed, under whatever had been said, whether they agreed with it or not, so that afterward nobody could say they had not been there.

"Even Brom," she said.

"Even me," Brom agreed, from the depths of the chair. "I'll sign anything. I signed for a horse once. Never saw the horse."

"That," said Karis, "is exactly why the minute has rules."

---

She began, as she always did, a long way back from where she meant to arrive.

"Greyvane," she said. "The whitewashed room, with the slate on the nail outside it. You all remember. Brom, you were witness twice and ate an apple through both."

"It was a long session."

"It was a quarter of an hour." A strip of blue paper marked her place. She opened to it and then let the page lie. "Twenty-two of them, in the end. Every one a Path declaration at demonstration intensity, every one with my consent said out loud and the hour written beside it. Nine paces, five, three. A quarter-hour, three hours. Cael reaching every way he could think of: attention, invitation, irritation, once." She glanced at Lira. "You took his pen for that one."

"I remember," said Lira. "It didn't work."

"Nothing worked. Twenty-two nulls. On the last day I ruled a line across the page, the way you rule off a column before you add it, and under the line I wrote that we were missing a condition. I was right about that. I hadn't the faintest idea what the condition was."

"And then the yard," said Brom.

"And then the yard." Karis put one fingertip on the blue strip. "Three exchanges under standings rules, three chairs at the end of the floor, three hundred people on the benches, and me across the chalk from him. Trying to win. And on the third exchange it completed, and the notice said something none of his notices had ever said before."

"*Acquisition: directed,*" said Cael.

"Yes." She did not look at him. "So now I had something better than a hunch. I had two rooms to set side by side. The whitewashed room had everything the yard had, except one thing. Same girl, same Path, same boy who'd spent six weeks learning every point I laid. In the whitewashed room I had built it so that nothing could matter. If I'd laid a point badly it went in a ledger as a fact. In the yard there was a result, and I wanted it, and I was working with everything I had to take it away from him."

Brom had opened his eyes.

"That's the condition," said Karis. "I spent a month afterward getting it wrong, so I'll say it carefully. It isn't danger. People hear *stakes* and they think of a fist, or a blade, or somebody bleeding on chalk. I didn't hurt him in that yard; I don't think I could have hurt him if I'd tried. What I did was mean it. I set the whole of myself against him for something real, and he had spent six weeks learning what the whole of me looked like when I worked."

"So it isn't about hitting," said Brom slowly.

"It was never about hitting. Long, close watching first. Then earnest engagement against him: another person working at full stretch, on purpose, for a thing they want, with him as the thing in the way." Karis looked round at the three of them. "In the whitewashed room I was a demonstration. In the yard I was an opponent. Same Path, same girl. Twenty-two nulls on one side of that difference and a notice on the other."

She let that sit.

"That's the model," she said. "Two conditions. I've kept it since Greyvane, in this ledger, and I've tested every new thing I've learned about him against it, the way you'd test a key in a lock you weren't sure of."

"And now you've got a new thing," said Lira.

"For forty days Cael has been studying a man in the assessment wing. He asked me to hold the second pencil, because he said a thing watched by one pair of eyes is a hobby and not a study, and he was right. So I've held it. Every page." Karis drew the thin grey notebook a finger's width nearer to her. "Tonight I'm going to put that study beside the model, one column at a time, and show you what I see. Stop me if a number's wrong. Don't stop me at the end."

Cael felt the room go still round him. He found that he had folded his hands on the table, and he left them folded.

---

"First column," said Karis. "How long."

She drew a line down the middle of a fresh page and wrote *Greyvane* on the left and *here* on the right.

"Six weeks of study, at Greyvane, by appointment. Hours I chose, in rooms I chose. Here." She tapped the right-hand side. "He has been on this hill every hour Cael has been awake on it since the forty-sixth day. Sixty-seven days. Cael has known what he is for forty. Nobody books it. It doesn't stop for meals or sleep or rain. It is the weather on this hill now."

She made a mark.

"Second column. How near." She wrote it. "In the whitewashed room I stood nine paces off, then five, then three, and three was close enough to frighten me a little. In this notebook I have ten occasions when this man has stood inside two paces of Cael. Six of them across the office counter, with a form between them and a date being argued over. Two in the wing passage. One on the lecture stair, the same step, passing. One at the residence board." She looked up. "Once he stood three places behind you in the board queue for nearly a quarter of an hour. You told me you could hear him turning his notices over."

"I could," said Cael.

"Ten times inside two paces," said Brom to the ceiling. "Merrick was nearer than that to me four times tonight, and he only wanted the touch."

"Merrick isn't the comparison," said Karis, still making her mark. "I am. That's the point of the column."

"Third column. How deep." She turned the ledger round on the table and pushed it across to him. He looked down at his own work in her handwriting: the stair tally and its control column, the fifth map with its brown dots laid over the black, the toll at the covered-walk rail, the curve that bent at four hours, the facing page headed *cannot reach*.

"I want this in the minute in plain words. I have never read anything like this about any Path. Not in the founding-era surveys. Not in anything my instructors kept. Not in anything Quenna ever showed me, and she showed me most things in the end. You have written down how a Bronze-tier Shadow Path pays for its declaration, at what rate, on what curve through a day, and three of the places where it can't reach. From outside. In forty days. Off a man who has never once let you see him use it." She took the ledger back. "When I did the same to you, it took me the better part of a year, and I had your permission in writing."

On the rug, Lira lifted her chin from her knees. "That's three," she said. "There's a fourth."

"There's a fourth." Karis closed the ledger and kept her hand flat on it. "The fourth is the one I went over six times. It's the one I stopped on."

She was silent for a moment, and her lips moved once without sound. Cael had seen her do it in the Greyvane hearing, in the moment before she read a clause into the record: a last silent rehearsal of words she had already fixed at her own table, so that nobody in the room could jostle a better or a worse one out of her.

"Edran wanted a bout," she said. "I wanted a match. Neither of us wanted anything worse than an afternoon. This man doesn't want an afternoon. He has been sent to settle what Cael is, for people who have spent two years trying to find a reason to take the provision away, and everything he does, every form and every post he leans on and every hour of that curve, is aimed at that. The enrollment. The assay provision. The standing. The paper this whole school has signed its name to. And he is doing it with everything he has. He's the best I have ever seen at anything, and he gets up every morning and spends all of it against Cael." She lifted her hand from the ledger. "Nothing on this hill is more in earnest than that. Nothing in Cael's life is."

Nobody said anything. Somewhere above the window the rain had found a gap in the leading, and a drop came down the inside of the frame and fell onto the sill, and after a while another, slow and regular, like a clock that had grown bored with keeping time.

Karis opened the grey notebook, where one short paragraph stood on the first page and every page after it was white.

She did not read it. She said it.

"The conditions are met. If you directed it, I believe it would complete." Her hands closed the notebook on its single paragraph and stayed there, flat on the grey cover. "Which is why we have to talk about whether you may."

---

He had known it was coming since the reading room. He had known, if he was honest, for longer than that. It made no difference at all.

There had been a loose sheet at the back of the Shadow section a fortnight ago with a single word on it, *conditions*, and a line after the word that went nowhere. He had looked at it for some time one night. Then he had told himself the curve was more urgent, and gone on with the curve, and some days later he had moved the sheet into the third column, among the dead ideas, where it sat between the clock map and the list of the man's acquaintances. Nothing had killed it; he had simply put it there.

The third column was the oldest rule in the binder, older than Ardenmere. When the evidence killed an idea, the idea went in there with its date, so that it could not come back at two in the morning wearing a new coat. That was all the column was for. It was not a cupboard to keep an idea in while the evidence was still busy proving it, and for a fortnight he had kept one there like that, with the door shut.

It was the worst misuse of his own method he had ever caught himself in. And he had not even caught himself. Karis had done it for him, because Karis did not own any such cupboard; she wrote down what she found whether she liked it or not, and then went back six times to make sure.

That was the error, and underneath the error was the want, and the want was worse, because there was nothing wrong with it except that it was his.

A sixth fragment. Not another way to move, or brace, or feel weight on the air a pace and a half out. He had five of those already, and he could have priced any of them to the half-breath in his sleep. This would be a fragment of a Path he had no shape for anywhere in him: the toll on every passing eye, the thing that let a man stand in a crowded yard and not be what the yard was looking at.

He thought, of all things, of the two girls in Current smocks on the lecture stair, who had nudged each other every morning for five weeks as he came down, as regular as the bell. He had grown fond of them. He had put them in the margin of the tally, and their nudging was the warmest thing on the page, and it was still a record of two people deciding to look at him.

Since his Kindling he had not had one day without somebody writing him down. Assessors, a panel, an Arbiter with a classification, a warden with a challenge in his pocket, and a semester evaluation waiting at the end of this one; grey coats at every gate and ferry on two roads; and now a whole hill that had bought his transcript at six silver marks a copy and read it before he walked through the gate. He had grown so used to it that he only noticed it now in the way a man notices his own coat when somebody else asks him to take it off.

He sat with his hands folded on the table and let the want go through him from end to end, clean and entire, and he did not let it reach his face.

"You want it," said Lira.

She said it to the fire, without lifting her head.

"Yes," said Cael.

"Good." Now she looked round at him. "Say that again in an hour, out loud, when we're all being clever. I want to hear whether it's still the same size."

Brom laughed, one short breath through the nose, and stopped, and Karis had already written it. Cael watched the words go down in her small hand, *L.: "You want it." C.: "Yes."*, with no line beneath either of them.

"That's in the minute now," said Karis. "For good. I'm sorry."

"Don't be." He found that his hands had come unfolded on their own and were lying open on the wood. "It was the easy part. It was only true. Everything we say from here on is going to be harder to get right."

Lira watched him a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, and turned back to the fire, and Cael understood that she had not asked the question for his sake, or for the minute's. She had asked it so that later, when they were all being clever, there would be one plain thing on the page that nobody could argue themselves round.

Karis turned the minute to a fresh side and wrote a single heading across the top of it, and underlined it, which she almost never did. *Whether.* Then she set the pencil down and folded her hands on the grey notebook and looked at the three of them in turn, at Brom in his stockings and Lira on the rug and Cael at the table with his palms open on the wood.

"I'll go first," she said. "It's only fair. I'm the one who built the model, and I know exactly what's in it, and I have argued with myself about the next part for six days and lost every time." She drew a breath. "So you'll have to argue with me instead. I'd be grateful. I can't seem to do it alone."

The drip went on falling on the sill. Brom reached out without getting up, picked his boots off the fender, and set them down on the hearthstone farther from the fire, as though he expected to be there a long while and did not want them to scorch.

# Chapter 31 — Both

Lira locked the door and put the key in her pocket, which was how every one of these evenings began now, and sat on the arm of the settle with her bad leg along the cushions.

Cael read them the counter.

He read it from the observation notebook, word for word, because he had written it word for word on the walk back from the wing before it could soften in his head. The sheet and the chained pen. *Your training requirement will take coursework.* Gault, the unit, the back half of term, the room in the wing, Rooke's boards. *Who takes it? I do.* The file sentence. His own three words. The board, and everybody's names on it, his among them. He read the half-second at the door as well, because leaving it out would have been the first lie of the evening. Then he turned the page and read them the other entry, the one about his feet in hall three, and what he wanted, in his own hand.

Nobody said anything until he had closed the notebook.

"No," said Lira. "Don't go in that room. Not for anything."

She said it to the cold fireplace, with her arms folded, and then shut her mouth on it and looked as if she would very much have liked that to be the end of the evening.

It was not. They all knew it was not. Since the Fiske bout Lira had taken up a habit none of them had seen in her before: when she had said a thing hard, she turned straight round and argued the other side of it, as hard as she could, to see what was left standing. She said it was what Fiske had done to her in the second exchange, and that it had been the most useful thing anybody had done to her all season.

So after a moment she unfolded her arms.

"Right. The other side." She looked at the ceiling as if the case were written on it. "He's offering it in public. On a board. With Gault's name on the syllabus and every second-year who wants to be an assessor in the same room. You can't be quietly anything in a room full of second-years. If he wanted you on your own, that's the last place he'd put you." She stopped. "And you'd learn something. From the only man on the bluff who could teach it. That's not nothing. I'd have walked a long way at your age for a teacher like that." She looked down at Cael. "I still think no. I just can't make it as big as I'd like it to be."

"That's honest," said Karis.

"It's irritating," said Lira. "Your turn."

Karis had her hands flat on the table, one each side of the grey notebook, the way she held them when she was keeping still on purpose.

"I'm going to give you my position," she said, "and then I'm going to tell you why you shouldn't trust it, because I don't, and I'd rather say so here than at the fourth bell with nobody to say it to."

"Go on," said Cael.

"Position. It breaks nothing in the minute. You didn't arrange it. You didn't go near him. The offer came over a counter, by the route any clerk would use, and the right it rests on is printed in your own file. Every rule you wrote is standing." She paused. "Now the reason I distrust it. I want it. Not for you. For me. I've wanted to sit in a room with a working Shadow practitioner at a table's length since the first page you wrote about him, and I want it the way I want a missing volume, which is badly and for my own sake. And I caught myself, about halfway through you reading it out, building the case for yes. I'd got two good reasons laid out before I noticed the want they were standing on." She turned one hand over. "So whatever I say tonight, mark it down. Mark it down a long way."

"How far?" said Brom, from the floor.

"Further than you'd think. I'll tell you if I see myself doing it again."

Brom was sitting under the window with his back to the plaster, as he always sat, with Rooke's sheet open on his knee and Karis's twelve ticks along the top of it in pencil. He was running his thumb down the fold in it, slowly, as if he were trying to smooth out a crease that had been put there with an iron.

"He's not recruiting you," he said.

"You don't know that," said Lira.

"No. I don't." Brom looked up. "But if that's recruiting, it's the worst I've ever seen. My grandmother hired people all her life. Clerks, factors, yard men. You make it easy for them. You make it quiet. You make it feel like their idea." He tapped the sheet. "He's done the opposite of every one of those. You have to put your own name on a board in the residence passage, where three hundred people walk past it twice a day. You have to sit in a room with eight others who'll talk about it at supper. If I wanted somebody close to me without a fuss, the last thing on earth I'd do is make him sign for it in public. If he wanted you, he'd have left you an easy way to refuse. He's left you a hard way to accept."

Nobody had anything to add to that. Lira looked at Brom for a long moment with an expression of mild betrayal, and then at the fireplace again.

"I hate it when he does that," she said.

Brom only nodded, comfortably, and went on smoothing the crease.

---

Cael waited until the barge on the river had finished its long call and the answer had come back from the bend.

"The course isn't what I keep coming back to," he said.

They waited.

"The coats haven't changed."

He laid Karis's marbled log open on the table between the four of them and turned it so that it faced the room. Eleven days since the pin, in her small hand and his scrawl and once in Lira's square capitals. Every change at the ferry landing and every change at the foot of the bluff road, the bell, the faces where faces could be told apart, a tick for on time.

Eleven rows of ticks.

"Here's what I know about the Compact," he said. "It answers paper with people. That's all it has ever had to spend. Somebody writes something interesting at the bottom of the coast, and it goes up, and somebody reads it, and what comes back down is coats. More of them, or different ones, or closer. I've seen it every time anything about me has gone up." He touched the first row. "The registry's district office is half a morning down the river. If an exception report had gone up from this bluff with the afternoon of the pin in it, something would have come back down by now. Not a letter. A man."

"So nothing went up," said Lira.

"Something went up. It must have. Sending nothing for a fortnight would leave a hole in his product that a clerk could see from the door, and he doesn't make holes clerks can see." Cael looked at the column. "But the afternoon wasn't in it. Not the part that matters. He watched me stand in front of a locked post and take an Iron-tier return on my arm and send it into the floor. And eleven days later there are the same two men at the landing that there were the morning before. One of them still has his stiff knee."

Karis had stopped writing. She sat looking at the column of ticks with her pencil held a little above the page.

"That's one observation," she said.

"I know."

"It fits your reading. It fits others as well, and I'm going to write them down so that in a few months, when we know, we can look back and see what we believed and what was so." She drew a line under the date and wrote a figure one. "One. It's lying in a tray somewhere, under other trays. Clerks have trays; so do the people above them. Two. It went up and somebody decided to change nothing while an inspection might be coming, because moving coats is the kind of thing an inspection notices. Three. It went up whole, and he's been told to stand where he is and keep watching." She wrote a four, and stopped, and then wrote beside it. "Four. It went up, and it was written so that a reader who wasn't in that hall would find nothing in it to act on. All true. Nothing to see."

Cael looked at the fourth line for a while.

"That's mine," he said. "That's the same as mine."

"It isn't. Yours says he left it out. Mine says he could have put it all in and still made it say nothing. Those are different men." She set the pencil down. "I'm not arguing with you. I'm putting the other roads on the page. That's all."

"I know."

Karis looked at the pencil in her hand as though it belonged to someone else. "Now the part that isn't work. Once only. Afterwards you'll all have the decency to forget it."

"Say it," said Brom.

"An instrument doesn't change sides. It changes readings." She said it the way she must have said it to herself a thousand times, flatly, as a thing she had built her working life on. "I told you that before the season. I meant it as a warning, and I've believed it since I was old enough to hold a pencil." She lined the notebook up with the table's edge, as though it had moved. "Eleven days isn't a finding. I know that better than anybody in this room. But it's the first thing I've ever written down that won't go into that sentence, however I turn it, and I dislike that so much that I'd rather tell you now than wait until I've found a way to make it fit."

The room was very quiet. Down on the river a barge called once, long, and nobody answered it.

"Thank you," said Cael.

"Don't thank me. I've told you it's unprofessional." But the corner of her mouth had gone tight in the way it did when she was pleased with something and refused to show it. "Write it down. Somewhere I don't have to look at it."

Lira had listened to all of it with her chin on her fist. Now she reached over and turned the marbled log round to face her, and ran her finger down the eleven rows the way she ran it down the standings, looking for the line that did not belong.

"So which one do you believe?"

"None of them, yet." Cael tapped the column. "I believe the ticks. The ticks I watched happen."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that I didn't make up." He shrugged. "Ask me again when something changes down there. If it ever does."

"I'd like to meet the man with the knee," said Brom from the floor. "I feel I know him now. Eleven days. I could pick his walk out of a crowd at the fair." He thought about it. "I'd buy him a pie. He looks like a man who's never once been bought a pie."

"You can't buy a Compact watcher a pie," said Karis.

"Why not?"

Karis opened her mouth, and closed it again, and looked at the column of ticks, and for once in Cael's experience could not find the clause.

---

"Then there's the part nobody wants to say," said Lira, "so I'll say it."

She swung her leg down off the settle and sat forward.

"If you sit in that room, you sit eight feet from him once a week. Earnest, in his own work, close. That's your rule two. You wrote it. If it's ever going to happen, that room is where it's likeliest." She looked at Cael and did not look away. "So I want it written now what happens after. Not on the night. Now. While nothing's wrong with you and you can still think."

"It mightn't happen," said Brom.

"Then we'll have wasted a sheet of Karis's paper, and she'll forgive us."

"I won't," said Karis, already tearing one out.

So they wrote it, the four of them, at the common room table, in the last hour before the residence lamps went down. Cael held the pen and the others argued over his shoulder, and it took them most of the hour to make it as short as it was, and when it was done it was the dullest thing any of them had ever signed. Karis said that was how you could tell it was good.

*One. If it happens: eight days of nothing the wing can measure. No floor time hard enough to make a note, no demonstration, no supervised intensity. Ask Gault's office to defer my training for fatigue. That's true, and they give it to anybody who asks.*

*Two. The three of you are told before, which is now, and within the hour after, whatever the hour is.*

*Three. Karis takes measurements this week, while nothing is wrong, so that there is a before to set the after against. Last time we had only the after.*

*Four. If any of the five slips past the line we draw tonight, everything halts — deferral, unit, the lot — and I say it out loud to Karis and Lira, in plain words. I don't write it in the Log and sit waiting for someone to ask.*

"What's tolerable?" said Brom.

They argued that for a quarter of an hour.

Lira wanted nothing tolerable at all: one wrong thing, anywhere, and stop. Karis said that would stop him on the first morning, since last time had begun with two misfires before breakfast, and that a rule nobody could keep was only a way of feeling safe. Brom wanted to know what counted as wrong, and whether a slow burst was wrong or only tired, and whether the read going a beat late counted, since it went a beat late every time Cael had been counting anything. Cael wanted the line drawn where last time had ended, and said so, and Lira said that was exactly the kind of line a person drew when he meant to walk right up to it.

"Within the hour," said Brom at one point, reading part two upside down. "Whatever the hour is. What if it's the fourth hour of the night?"

"Then you get woken."

"Wake Karis first," said Brom. "She's nicer about it."

"I'm not," said Karis, without looking up. "I'm only quieter."

In the end Karis wrote it in under the fourth part in her own hand: *Tolerable: one misfire in any session, self-correcting. Anything more, or anything that does not correct, or anything in the read: stop.*

Cael signed it. Then he held the pen out and the others signed under him, Karis neatly, Brom slowly and with his tongue between his teeth, Lira in her square capitals that took up two lines.

"Read four," said Lira.

He read four.

"Again."

He read it again, aloud, to the end, *I don't write it in the Log and sit waiting for someone to ask*, and she listened to the whole of it with her eyes shut.

"Right," she said, and stood, in two stages, and unlocked the door. "That one I'll hold you to. The others are Karis's." She went out. They heard her uneven step go along the passage and her door shut.

"She means that," said Brom.

"I know she does."

"No," said Brom. "I mean she's going to lie awake tonight saying it to herself. That's what it costs her, you going near him. She'd rather you'd never seen him." He folded Rooke's sheet along its crease and put it inside his coat. "She signed anyway. Write that down too, somewhere."

---

He opened the Log at his window at the ninth bell, with the shutter back and the river loud in the dark below, and the fresh-signed sheet laid in the back board beside Karis's minute.

He wrote the standing lines first, as he always did. Five confirmed. The forearm's amendment to the recovery column. Then, for a while, he sat with the pen lifted and did nothing, because he knew what the next entry was going to be and was not sure he would like it.

It came out whole. That almost never happened.

*He watched me stop Iron-tier force with a Copper baseline and his desk stayed quiet. Eleven days of the same two coats at the landing say so. This morning he offered, at a counter, to teach me his own Path across a table. Either it's the best trap the Compact ever built, or the man they sent to watch me has started watching them. Karis says instruments don't change sides. Karis has never met this instrument.*

He read it back and did not change it. Then he wrote the working out underneath, because a conclusion with no working under it was only a thing you had decided to feel.

*The case for a trap. The door into his range is one he cut himself, and nobody knows what eight feet of him is worth better than he does. A room where my watching is the thing being measured, with him as the measure, is the best design anyone could ask for. It costs the Compact nothing. On paper it's a teaching assistant running coursework.*

*Against: eleven days, the same two coats. Brom's point, which I haven't been able to break: every single thing the man has ever let me have, I've had to claim in my own hand, in public. And the way it came. He spoke to the floor sheet, not to me: a single sentence, in the dullest hour, in words the desk clerk could have repeated at supper and bored her whole table. A trap wants to be walked into. This was built so that I could turn it down, and neither of us would ever have been in a conversation.*

*What I think, put here so I can be shown wrong later where everybody can see: for thirteen weeks he has been careful around me. This morning, I think, he was careful on my behalf. Careful* for *me. I haven't one piece of evidence for that preposition, and it's the only part of today I'm sure of.*

*What I'll do: sign the board. Sit in the room. Learn the Path, if he teaches it.*

*What I won't do is let one morning move my reading of him. He still draws the Compact's pay. In thirteen weeks he hasn't said a word to me that wasn't about a form. A man who says nothing can begin saying something on any day he likes. I'd be a fool to build on eleven days of a ferry landing.*

*So: both. I read him as an officer, and I sit in his room anyway.*

He stopped there. Then, because it was the kind of entry that would be easy to leave unwritten until it was too late to be honest, he turned to a fresh page and wrote the last part in advance.

*If the day comes when I take something from this man, it will be a day he made possible. He'll have spent a whole term making it possible, and nobody will have asked him to, and he can never be told. That isn't a detail of the cost. It is the cost, nearly all of it. It won't feel like a cost on the night. It'll feel like something else, and I'll want to call it by a kinder name. So I'm writing it here, tonight, while I can still see it plainly. Read this first.*

He blotted the page and sat looking at it. Then he took the pencil and, at the head of the page, in small capitals, wrote *READ FIRST*.

There was one more thing, and it was not about the debt. It was about Karis's fourth road, which had been sitting in him since the common room like a stone in a shoe.

*Karis's fourth: he put the whole afternoon in and wrote it so that it says nothing. Mine: he left it out. She says those are different men, and she's right. One of them hides a thing by leaving a hole. The other hides it by filling the hole with so much truth that nobody looks down. I don't know which man he is. I only know that either one is better at paper than I am, and I've been working at paper since I could hold a pen.*

He closed the Log on that. Through the wall, along the passage, he heard Lira's floorboard go, once and then again, the long step and the careful one, as somebody walked to a window and stood there. Then nothing. He did not knock. Brom had been right about what it cost her, and the least he could do with a thing that cost her was not make her talk about it at the ninth bell.

---

The sheet went up on the residence board at the first bell next morning, in the wing's round copying hand.

*Field assessment (staff unit). Weekly, from Third-day, the sixth bell, the long room off the assessment wing. Observation; position; presence. Open to second and third years; provision enrollees admitted on the training requirement. Places: twelve. Sign below.*

Cael was there before breakfast, before he could find a reason to be there after it. The passage was empty except for a first-year reading the lost-property list with the absorption of a man reading a will. He took the pencil off its string and wrote his name on the first line, *Cael*, and under it *assay provision*, as the cards did, and put the pencil back.

By supper there were nine names.

He read them on the way in, because he read everything. Four second-years he knew by sight from the Mire and Current halls. Two third-years, one of whom had sat behind him in the drainage lecture and slept through most of it. A Stone girl from the second quadrangle whose card said *Stone, Copper* and who was said to want to be a registry assessor when she was grown, and to say so to anyone who would listen.

And on the line below his own, in a large confident hand with a flourish under it, *Gwen — Second Year — Current*, and after it, drawn very small and carefully, an eye.

"She's drawn an eye," said Brom, reading it over his shoulder with a plate in one hand.

"I can see that."

"After her name. On a sheet about watching." Brom considered it with real respect. "That's either very clever or it isn't clever at all, and I can't tell which, and I think I'm going to like her."

"You don't know her."

"I'll know her by Third-day. She'll be sitting next to you." Brom said this with the calm of a man reading the weather. "People who draw eyes on sheets always sit next to the person they've decided is interesting. It's a law."

"Whose law?"

"Mine. I've just made it." He went in to supper.

Lira read the sheet on her way past with a bowl of something in each hand. She read it the way she read the standings, from the bottom up, and stopped at the top.

"Third-day," she said. "The sixth bell."

"Yes."

"I'll be in hall one at the sixth bell. On the stone, at a third." She did not look at him; she looked at the sheet. "I'll know where you are."

"You always know where I am."

"I know where you said you'd be. That's different." She shifted one bowl so she could hold it against her hip. "Eat something. You've been looking at that board as if it owes you money." And she went in.

Karis was waiting for Cael in the second-quadrangle wash-house afterward with the grey notebook, a lamp, a wooden rule a foot long, and an expression of great purpose. Brom's sandbags hung from their beam in the corner where they had hung since the third week, three of them in a row, light, middle and heavy, with the knotted cord and the peg.

"Part three," she said. "Measurements. While nothing is wrong. Sit."

He sat on the upturned tub. She held the rule upright by its top end, with its foot between his open finger and thumb, and let go without warning. He caught it. She looked at where his fingers had closed and wrote down a number. She did it eleven more times, and wrote eleven more numbers.

"What does it tell you?"

"Nothing yet. It tells me what you are on a First-day evening, after supper, tired a normal amount. Next week it might tell me something." She made him count his breath for a minute by the wash-house's clock, at rest and then after twenty steps up and down the yard stair, and wrote that. She made him take the middle bag on the forearm, the way he did in the mornings, and asked how many he would get to before it went, and wrote down his guess before he began, and then wrote down the true count beside it, which was eleven, and which was the same as his guess. "Good. You're honest about that one, at least."

"I'm honest about all of them."

"You round," said Karis. "Up, when it's the Wind, and down when it's anything that hurts. You've done it since Greyvane. I've got it in a column." She closed the notebook. "Tomorrow, the read at the post with Brom. The day after, the Wind on the stone, with Lira, at a third. And then I'll have a before."

"And Ember?"

"Ember stays off the floor. We agreed that. I'll take it from your own account, and I'll trust it, because it's mine and I'll know if you've rounded it." She picked up the lamp. "Go to bed. You look like somebody who's been carrying a sentence about all day."

"I have."

"I know. I read it on your face at supper." She paused in the wash-house door with the lamp held up and the light running along the three hanging sandbags. "It's a good unit, Cael, whatever else it is. Observation, position, presence. Somebody ought to teach it properly, and nobody on this bluff ever has." She considered. "Mark that down a long way too."

# Chapter 6 — Four Hundred and Twelve

On the first full morning he was out of the quadrangle before the fifth bell had finished, with the observation notebook buttoned inside his coat and a single question to carry, as he carried one into every new place, because a question kept his eyes honest.

The question was not *what is this place*. Karis already had that out of the prospectus, and she would have the rest of it out of the library inside a week. His was older and sharper, and he had brought it down from Greyvane in the same pocket as the notebook. *Why did they take me?* Every school he had stood in told one story across a desk and another in its stone. The desk story was for visitors. The stone story was in which roof got mended first and which teacher got the good hall, in what was weighed every week and what was left to the damp. Anybody willing to walk slowly could read it.

Greyvane's stone had told him everything in three days. He allowed this place four, and he was wrong.

He walked the north edge first, where the three long halls stood with their high windows facing the morning. The largest had six floors marked out on its boards, and somebody was already on one of them at that hour, alone, running a drill he did not know. The second hall was smaller and its floors were timber, not stone, and when he stood in its doorway and shifted his weight he felt the boards answer him very slightly, a give and a return, as though the floor had a breath of its own. He wrote that down and underlined it and did not yet know why. The third hall was low and set apart behind the assessment wing. Its door was locked from the outside, with the key on a nail beside it, which was a thing he had never seen before and could not think of a reason for.

He did not try the key. He wrote that down too.

The assessment wing had its own wall and its own gate, and through an open door on the ground floor he counted pieces of apparatus standing along a wall under cloths. There were eleven, and he could guess the purpose of four. The library was the heavy-roofed building he had picked out from the bridge, and an accession board inside its door claimed forty-one thousand volumes in brass numerals. Karis, when he told her that night, said that accession boards counted everything a library had ever been given, including the things it had since burned for being damp, and that she would believe thirty-five.

The residence was three quadrangles, and it was the residence that gave him the first true line.

The first quadrangle was the oldest and had the best rooms, with deep windows and doors that shut properly. The third was newest and largest, built in a hurry, and its stair rang under every step. Theirs, the second, sat between. On the second morning he watched the porters carrying trunks across from the gate, and he noticed which trunks went where. It was not by tier, or not only by tier. A Copper girl whose tag carried a small painted mark went to the first quadrangle. Two Iron boys with plain tags and quiet faces went to the third. He asked the porter, idly, what the painted mark was.

"Seeded last season," said the porter. "Top four in her bracket. They get first pick of rooms."

"And the Iron boys?"

"Didn't enter," said the porter, and shrugged. "Never do, those two. Good lads."

Cael wrote that down in the notebook, and then sat on the steps of the second quadrangle for a long time looking at it.

Lira found him there a little after the second bell. She had been up before him, which was usual, and she had already walked the whole of the Crown yard's rim once, slowly, and stood a long while at the registrar's table at its side, where the ladder list lay open for names. She had not written hers.

"The clerk at intake said early," he said.

"The clerk at intake said by the fourteenth." She sat down beside him on the step and stretched her legs out and looked at the toes of her boots. "Early's for people who want to be looked at. I want to do the looking first. Every name that goes on that list before mine is somebody I can watch walk up to that table and choose to be counted." She shrugged. "I'll put mine on when I've seen enough of them. Not before."

"That's a schedule."

"It's a grudge," said Lira. "I told you. Same thing, if you keep it." She looked at the notebook on his knee. "What have you got?"

He showed her the line about the rooms. She read it twice, and then gave it back to him without saying anything. That was how he knew it was true.

*Rooms go to the ladder. Not to tier, not to need. To whoever looks as if they might bring something home for the school.*

Greyvane had sorted by need, every time. It had been the whole of what Greyvane was, and the whole of what was wrong with it, too, because need was the one thing nobody could ever pay a bill with. Halcenvane sorted by something he did not yet have a word for, something to do with how near a person stood to what the place wanted to become.

---

On the third morning, at breakfast, he found an entry in the notebook that was not true.

It was in his own hand, from the evening before, in the middle of a page of others like it: *Current Path, tall, dark braid, third year by the tag. Favours the right leg coming down stairs. Old injury, healed short.* It was a good entry, and he remembered making it: the braid, the stair outside the north hall, and the small hitch in the right leg on the second step down.

The tall girl with the dark braid came down the refectory steps past his table two at a time, light and quick, with nothing wrong with either leg at all.

He watched her all the way to the hatch and back, and watched her sit. When she got up again to fetch water he watched her go down the two steps to the pump trough, and then come back up them, and there was no hitch.

He went back through the notebook, very slowly.

He had sixty-three entries from three days, and he went through every one of them, asking of each the same question: *Did I see this, or did I fill it in?* For most he could not tell. That was the thing that turned him cold, sitting at the long refectory table with his porridge going hard in the bowl. It was not that some of the entries were wrong, since any method made mistakes. It was that he could not tell the wrong ones from the right ones by looking at them, because they were all in the same hand and the same confident sentences, with the same small details, and not one of them carried any mark of where it had come from. The limp had been real. He was nearly sure the limp had been real. It had belonged to somebody else, a boy perhaps, somewhere on the same stair, and at some point between the stair and the notebook his mind had taken the limp off the boy and put it on the girl. It had done it so smoothly that he had never felt the seam.

He went to the north hall after breakfast to find out where the seam was.

A Force Path cohort was on the largest floor, twenty-two students and four instructors, running a rotation he had not learned. He sat on the gallery bench and did the thing he had been doing for three days without thinking about it. He ran the gaze across the floor, all four layers at once, rhythm and weight and breath and position, building each student as a live thing in his head and keeping them all moving.

Then he did what he should have done on the first day. He chose one student, a stocky boy at the north end, and shut his eyes, and said under his breath what the boy was doing. He opened them and checked, and he was right. He did it with two students, then four, then six, and he was right every time.

At nine he was right about eight of them.

At eleven he was right about seven, and wrong about the other four in ways that sounded exactly like being right. He had a girl stepping off on her left foot who was stepping off on her right. He had a boy holding his breath at the top of a push who was breathing out. He had an instructor standing at the west end who was standing at the east. The wrong answers did not come to him blurred or doubtful; they came complete, with all their details, as clear as the true ones. When he passed a dozen, the gaze did not lose its grip and go vague but went on handing him pictures, as sure and detailed as ever, with nothing behind them.

He sat on the bench with his hands between his knees and made himself put a price on it, honestly, there and then, before he could start to make it smaller.

*North hall, third day. I wasn't tired. I'd slept and eaten. This is a limit, not a lapse. Past nine people, somewhere short of twelve, the gaze stops seeing and starts supplying. It doesn't fade to warn me. It keeps its sure voice. It hands me a whole person with every detail in place, and some of the details were never there. I've been wrong that way for three days and never once felt it, because at Greyvane nine was a crowded floor.*

*Bill: three days of pages. Mending them won't do. I can't tell the lines I saw from the lines the gaze made up, so every line goes.*

He took the notebook back to his room at noon and cut the three days' pages out along the binding with Karis's penknife, borrowed. He folded them small and took them down to the stove in the quadrangle's common room and fed them in, one at a time, and watched each one go brown at the corners and curl and catch. Sixty-three entries. Some of them had been the best observation he had ever done, and he did not know which.

It took most of the afternoon to stop feeling sick about it.

---

He rebuilt it that evening in carrel eleven, because Karis had a door that shut and a lamp that did not smoke, and because he did not want to do it alone.

He told her all of it first, and she listened without interrupting, with her chin on her hand, and was quiet for a while when he had finished.

"You're trying to count every sheep on the hill," she said.

"What?"

"There's a way they survey a hill farm for the tithe. They don't count every sheep. Nobody could. They count the folds. They take one fold in every so many and count every animal in it properly, and they write down which fold and which day. For the rest they write how many folds, and roughly how big, and leave it." She turned her pen in her fingers. "The tithe man isn't lying about the sheep he didn't count. He's writing down that he didn't count them, and how he guessed. It's the same number of sheep either way. It's a different kind of honest."

He turned it over while the lamp burned down a finger's width. Then he took a clean page and built it.

It came in three tiers. He named them as he wrote them, because a thing he could not name he could not keep.

*Standing.* These were the people he would carry at full depth all the time, kept live and updated every day. Eight of them at the most, and never nine, because nine was where the failing started and he meant to keep one place clear between himself and the edge.

*Sampled.* These were people he would look at properly, but only at set times, one session in four, with the date written beside every entry, so that he would always know how old a thing was before he leaned on it.

*Structural.* This was everybody else, and he would not hold them as people at all, but the way the tithe man held the folds he never opened: as a cohort and a drill and a habit, as how a group moved and what its instructors kept correcting, as where its middle sat. He would write a whole hall down in four lines and know he was doing it.

His pen did not want to write the third tier, because to put it down was to admit, on paper, that the hill held four hundred people he had already chosen not to see properly. He made the pen do it. Then he boxed the reason, so that he would have to look at it every time he opened the page.

*This tier is here because the other way invents people. Better a fold I know I never opened than a sheep I wrote down that was never in it. The tithe man isn't lazy. He's telling you which numbers he counted.*

"Who goes in the eight?" said Karis.

He had been afraid she would ask that, and had not let himself think about it yet, and now that she had asked it he saw why.

At Greyvane there had been no choosing, because nine people had been the most on any floor and he had held every one. Here he would have to choose, and choosing meant putting down on paper, privately, his own ranking of who on this bluff was going to matter, which was the same as deciding which nine-tenths of the people around him would be weather.

"It's a ladder," he said slowly. "It's my own private ladder. I'm going to rank people by whether they'll matter, before I know them, and then I'll only look properly at the ones I've already decided matter."

"Yes," said Karis. "Everybody does that. You're only the first person I've met who's going to write the list down."

He wrote three names and stopped. *Bracken.* *The Blade instructor, Rooke, whom Brom stands in front of on the ninth.* *The porter, who knows where everybody sleeps and why.* He left five lines empty under them.

*Eight is a ceiling, not a target. Leave room. Whoever earns a line, earns it on the page, with a date.*

The five blank lines sat under the three names, and they made him less uneasy than he would have expected. A list with room in it was a list that could still be wrong out loud.

Karis leaned over and read the three names upside down.

"The porter," she said.

"He knows where everybody sleeps, and why. He knows which trunks go to which quadrangle before the trunks do." Cael considered it. "He might be the best-informed man on this bluff, and nobody's ever going to ask him anything."

"You're going to ask him things."

"I'm going to listen to him tell me things he thinks are dull. It's not the same." He capped his pen. "People tell you the most when they think it doesn't matter."

Karis looked at him for a moment with an odd expression, half amused and half something else.

"You know that's what you do too," she said. "Every time you write in that book in front of us. You think it's the dull parts we don't notice."

That one he could not answer. He put the notebook away, and she let him, and they sat a while longer in the carrel with the lamp between them and the door shut on the whole of the law range, and neither of them minded the quiet.

---

He kept the Crown yard back until the sixth morning on purpose, because he wanted the whole bluff understood first, so that when he finally stood in the middle he would know what it was the middle of.

He went at dawn, when it was empty, and he did not go to the seats. He went down onto the floor.

It was dressed stone, sixty feet by forty, laid so close that he could not get a fingernail into the joints. And it was sunk. The whole floor lay four feet below the ground around it, with three short flights of steps going down into it. The tiered seats did not rise from the floor's edge the way the benches at Greyvane had risen, but from the ground's edge, from above. He stood in the middle of the stone floor and turned slowly round, and on three sides the seats went up and back from a point above his head, row on row, pale and empty, and every one of them was looking down at him.

That had been meant. Somebody had dug a hole and put the fighting at the bottom of it, so that a thousand people could sit above and look down on two. He stood at the bottom of it in the grey light and felt it on the back of his neck, the way he had felt the gaze of a full gallery at Greyvane, though there was nobody here at all.

He counted the seats, which took a while, and made it a little over eleven hundred, more people than Halcenvane had enrolled; so the yard was not for the students alone, but for the town, and for visitors, and for whoever the academy wanted to show something to.

The fourth side of the yard, the open side, had a raised stand for faculty, a long table with a registrar's chair, and four boards painted black and mounted on the wall behind them.

He was reading the boards when the porter came down the steps with a pail of chalk and a rag and said, "You'll get shouted at."

"There's no bout on."

"There'll be one at the fourth bell. You'll get shouted at in advance." The porter was a heavy, slow-moving man with a groundskeeper's arms and a habit of speaking to the ground in front of him rather than to anybody's face. He set the pail down and began wiping last season's headers off the first board with long even strokes. "You're the assay one."

"Yes."

"Thought so. You read things." He wiped. "Go on, then. Read 'em. I'll tell you what you get wrong."

He read them and wrote them down as he went, in the shorthand he kept for things he would need again.

*Boards, four. One: the year's afternoons, both terms, with the windows for challenges marked in red between. Two: Copper. Three: Iron. Last season's order still up on both and fading, each line a name, a rank, fights, wins, a place. Four: headed Silver. Half the length of the others.*

"Silver's short," said Cael.

"Silver's everybody above Iron." The porter did not stop wiping. "Bronze and Silver both. Fifty-one Bronze on the hill this year and fourteen Silver. Not enough of either for a bracket of its own, so they all go in one, and it takes the name of the top of it."

"Three brackets for five tiers."

"Five tiers if you count the Gold, and she doesn't count." The porter jerked his head toward the north halls. "One Gold on the bluff. She's not on any board. Finished her coursework two years back and stayed on, under some fellowship the chancellor's office drew up. Doesn't fight on the ladder. Nobody within two tiers of her to fight." He started on the Copper board. "Two hundred and nine Copper. Hundred and thirty-eight Iron. The fifty-one and the fourteen. That's your whole school."

He added it while the porter was still talking. The Copper and the Iron together came to three hundred and forty-seven, and the sixty-five on the short board brought it to four hundred and twelve.

Until that moment he had not known how many people the bluff held. Standing there he saw he still did not. What the porter had given him was a tally of papers. The Gold fellow was not in it, because the brackets had run out of room above her. And he was not in it either. The brackets had never had a column for him at all. There were two people on this bluff that the boards could not describe, one at the top and one off the side, and neither of them appeared on any wall.

*The bracket lines look like they tell you who's good,* he wrote, on the steps afterward. *What they tell you is how many there are. Where the count runs thin, the school gives up dividing and calls whatever's left Silver.*

"How does it work?" he asked the porter. "The ladder."

The porter told him, wiping, as a man tells a thing he has told so often he could tell it asleep. Cael put it straight into shorthand.

*Ladder. Four fights a session afternoon; the assessment office pairs them. In between, you may call out anybody above you in your own column, four names up at most. Beat him and his place is yours, and everybody between slips one. Lose, and the board stays as it was.*

"Nothing?"

"Nothing on the board. Costs you plenty in your face." The porter shook out his rag. "But sit idle four sessions running and you slide a place on your own. So you can't sit on a good standing and hope. You've got to keep putting your name up. That's what drives the whole thing. Not the winning. The having to keep at it."

*Sit out four sessions running and you slip one anyway. Year's end: the best four in each column are seeded to the semifinals, which run in the second term's closing weeks. The final comes on the year's last day of competition. Faculty sit as the panel. The registrar keeps the record.*

"And the winner?" said Cael.

The porter stopped wiping.

"The winner gets the crown," he said. "There's three. Copper, Iron, Silver. They live in a case in the chancellor's hall and I polish them, so I know." He started wiping again, slower. "The Copper's the eldest by a long way. They've been riveting new brass on under the old for a hundred and thirty-one years, to make room for the names. Two years now it's been Fiske's. Force Path, Copper Rank Eight. Put her in the Iron column tomorrow and she'd walk off with that one as well."

"Then why isn't she in it?"

"Her certificate says Copper." The porter wrote *COPPER* in big square capitals across the top of the clean board. "The board doesn't care what you can do, lad. It reads what's on your certificate and puts you where that says. It's not unkind. It's a board."

---

He took Brom down to Ostrand on the ninth evening, because Brom needed new wraps before his first cohort hour. He had been assessed that morning in the east hall and placed in the Blade instructor's cohort, and the first hour was set for the following week. About the assessment he had said only that the cohort was fast, and Cael had decided not to ask him for more.

They crossed the bridge on foot, and Brom paid, and said very little on the way over. He walked a little faster than usual, and twice Cael caught him turning his right hand over and looking at the back of it, the way he looked at a joint after a fall to see whether it had swollen, though he had not fallen and nothing on him was swollen. Cael watched him do it the second time and did not ask. Brom had a face on him that was not ready yet, and Brom always came out with things in his own time and never before.

On the river street, in the long light, they walked past what Cael had come down to see. Every second shop near the bridge foot sold the same things, wraps and soft-soled shoes and quilted jackets hung in the windows like washing, and nobody kept a row of shops like that alive on passing trade: somebody up the hill was buying all of it, every season, by the cartload. Along the river road the lodging houses had boards out by their doors, freshly painted over older paint that said the same: *Term lets. Rooms by the term. Academy families welcome.*

"The whole town leans uphill," said Brom, reading the boards.

"A hundred and forty years of it."

They went into the third outfitter's, because Brom liked the look of the woman behind the counter, who measured his hands without being asked and told him he had been buying his wraps a finger too short for years. Brom accepted this calmly, as he accepted most truths about himself. While she cut, Cael leaned on the counter and asked her, idly, how much the ladder paid.

She looked at him over her shears as if he had asked her the price of the river.

"Pays?" she said. "Nobody's paid on that hill. It's an academy."

Brom stopped turning the cut cloth over in his hands.

They walked back to the bridge in silence. Halfway across, at the crown of the middle span, Brom stopped at the parapet where Lira had stopped on the first day, and looked up at the bluff.

"At the Ironyard a man fought for eight marks," said Brom slowly. "Nine a month in rent, eight a bout. You could see why he got up off the floor. He'd eat if he did." He turned the new wraps over. "Nobody up there's going hungry. So what are they fighting for?"

Cael had been working at it since the outfitter's counter, and he had it.

"Standings. That's the currency."

Brom looked at him.

"It changes into everything else," said Cael. "Rooms. You saw the trunks. The instructor's eye, who gets a word after the drill and who doesn't. Which cohort you're put in, and who you train beside. And Karis says the standings feed something bigger, something the academy wants more than any of that." He looked up at the dark hollow of the Crown yard, just visible over the wall at the top of the bluff. "Nobody hands you a purse at the end, because the prize is how they treat you for as long as you're here. Every bout is paid for in where they let you stand next year."

Brom was quiet for a long time.

"I liked the eight marks better," he said at last. "You knew what you'd got."

Two nights later, on the eleventh day, Cael wrote it down at the desk by his window, with the second quadrangle dark below and the lamps of Ostrand across the river.

*Eleven days. Why they took me: not finished. But I know what they spend on, and that's half of it.*

*They spend on the yard, and the boards, and the rooms that go to whoever's seeded. They spend on a walled wing full of instruments under cloths. Nobody's paid in coin here. They're paid in standing, and standing pays in everything.*

*A circuit that pays coin has to tell you the price before the bout. You know what you're fighting for, and so does everybody watching. A ladder that pays in standing never has to say. It just moves you up or down a wall, and lets you work out afterward what it cost.*

*I'm not on the wall. The Gold fellow isn't either. Two of us outside the count, for opposite reasons.*

The last line looked back at him. He added one more under it, smaller.

*Brom: Rooke took him into the Blade cohort on the ninth. First hour next week. He's gone quiet the way he goes quiet before a weight he hasn't lifted yet. Leave it for him.*

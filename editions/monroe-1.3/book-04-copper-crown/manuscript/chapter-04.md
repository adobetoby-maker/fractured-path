# Chapter 4 — Soon

Quenna found him at the pump on the Thursday of week twenty-one, at the end of the afternoon, when the yard was emptying and the light had gone long and yellow across the stones.

His hip had given him back on the fourth morning, as he had written that it would. He had kept Lira's three days to the hour. He had not taken a stair two at a time, though he had wanted to on the second evening, badly, and Lira had been on the landing above with her arms folded as if she had known. Now he was standing at the pump with the handle under one hand, not pumping, only feeling the iron cold under his palm and the old ease back in his leading side. He was thinking about nothing in particular, which was rare enough that he noticed it.

She came across the yard from the old grey building with her hands in her coat and stood beside him. She did not look at him. She looked across the yard toward the road-house end of the academy, where the stone was oldest.

"Told you it would be soon."

He waited for the rest.

There was no rest. She stood a moment longer with her hands in her coat, and then she took one hand out and laid two fingers on his sleeve, briefly, just above the wrist. Then she put the hand back in her pocket and went away across the yard toward the old building, not quickly, and went in.

He stood at the pump with the handle under his palm and did not move for a long time.

He knew what she had not said. He knew it as well as if she had sat down on the edge of the trough and said all of it in her driest voice, one sentence after another, the way she read a record into the roll. She had made a bet once, sitting down on a bench beside a boy nobody could classify, with her name in her pocket. It had been the largest bet of her life, and she had lost a good deal on it before it paid. She had stood behind him through a winter of sittings and a hearing, in front of a magistrate and the Compact's own officer, in a chair one row back at his own request. And now a house four times the size of this one was taking him on a documented basis, in law. For two years Quenna had looked for exactly that, a ground for him to stand on that was not borrowed, and had not found it. It had been sitting the whole time in Prynn's fifth case, waiting for Karis and her ruler.

She had not said any of that. She had not said that she was proud of him, or that she would miss him, or that she wished it had been her. He knew all three were true. He knew she would never say a word of any of them, not to him, not to anybody, because Quenna did not put things into words that she could not afterward stand behind on a record. And she could not stand behind *I wish it had been me*. It was too much like asking for something.

So she had said one sentence instead, the one that held all of it and gave none of it away. Standing at the pump, he weighed the sentence she had spent against the paragraph she had kept back, and knew which of the two had come dearer.

He did not go after her. He knew that going after her would make her say something, and then the sentence would be smaller.

He thought instead of a question she had asked him months ago, in the week after the match with Karis. She had asked it not for the file but for herself, and he had written it in the binder the same night because he had not known the answer. Whether he could *not* do it. Whether, in front of people who wished him harm, with everything lined up the way it had lined up once in a formal yard, he could hold still and keep his hands to himself.

He still did not know. He had not been tested. She had not asked again, and he did not think she would, and he was not going to go across the yard and offer her an answer he did not have.

He let go of the pump handle. The iron had warmed under his hand. He wiped his palm on his coat and went in to supper.

---

Naveth's last notice went up on the board in the main hall on the Monday of week twenty-two, below the posting for the end-of-term sittings and above a sheet about the kitchen rota.

It was one line long, in the clerk's small even hand. It said that the enrollment of the candidate on the observer track concluded at the end of the present term by transfer to a chartered institution, at the candidate's request, as entered by the provost. It cited nothing. It explained nothing. Karis read it three times and pronounced it perfect.

"Not one interesting word," she said. "He's done it again. That paper is going to sit in a drawer for a hundred years without anybody ever wanting to read it."

"Is that good?"

"It's the best thing that can happen to a paper." She copied it into her narrow brown notebook, all of it, the date included. "The interesting ones get read by people with pens."

The last days went quickly and slowly at once.

He wrote to Hesk on the Tuesday night. It was a short letter, shorter than the last, because he had learned that Hesk read the short ones more than once. He said where they were going, and why, and that there was a registrar at the other end who had asked how to spell Hesk's name and had got it in capitals. He said the four of them were well. He did not say anything about the bout. He thought about it, and found that what he wanted to write was *I won by one exchange and maybe none*, and he could not see a way to put that to Hesk that would not sound like asking to be told it was all right. He left it out. Hesk would hear it from somebody, or he would not, and either way it would keep.

He had not heard from Vell.

The letter had gone down the hill at the end of the winter term, in his own hand, with the ruling copied out under it word for word. It had been five months. He had stopped listening for the coach somewhere around week six, and had started again around week fifteen, and had made himself stop a second time. Vell had never in her life answered anything quickly. She read a letter the way she read a ledger, every line in order, and then she put it somewhere and let it settle before she touched it again. He knew that. It did not make the silence lighter. It only told him what shape it was.

Wray found him on the defensive floor on the Wednesday morning, where he had gone out of habit to run the framework one last time on her stone.

She did not come over at once. She stood by the rail with her slate under her arm and watched him run it, four passes, at panel speed. She broke the count on the third pass, as she always had, and said nothing about the pivot, because there was nothing to say. When he finished she came down off the rail and stood a pace away from him with her hands behind her back.

"Your ceiling," she said. "Three free on stone. You told my sittings that, in those words, every time I asked."

"Yes, Instructor."

"You measured it on my stone." Wray looked down at the floor between them, old grey flags worn smooth in the middle by forty years of feet. "My stone is the hardest floor in the district. I chose it because it doesn't lie to anybody. But it isn't the only floor in the world, and a number you measured on one floor is a fact about that floor as much as it's a fact about you." She looked up. "Where you're going they'll have boards, and halls built for the purpose, and floors I've never stood on. Measure again. Don't carry my number into their hall and call it yours."

He thought about it. He thought about the line from crest to knee after the fourth burst on Wray's swept boards, and how it had come early, and how he had not known whether it would.

"I'll measure again," he said.

"Good." She turned to go, and then turned back. "And find somebody there who'll break your count on the third pass. Everybody gets lazy on the third pass. You more than most, because you're good at the first two."

She went back up the rail and out by the side door, and that was all the goodbye there was. He stood on her stone a while longer and found that it had been exactly enough.

He packed on the Wednesday. It did not take long, because there was not much. Two shirts and his good coat. The wraps. The observation notebook, with Greyvane's section full and closed and a blank page already headed for the next place. Hesk's letter, in the inside pocket where Brom's page had once been. The binder last of all, a third full now, with Lira's slip in the back pocket behind Karis's consent and the term's count on the last written page.

Karis packed in an hour, and most of the hour was books. She had a coat with a deep inside pocket that buttoned, and she had worn it every day since the autumn. On the Wednesday night, while Cael watched from the door of her room, she checked the pocket the way he checked the knots on his wraps, without seeming to think about it. She put two fingers in, felt the folded paper there, took them out, and did up the button. She did not look at him. He did not ask. There was a copy of something in that pocket that both of them had written out by hand from a register that had not come back up the hill, and neither of them had understood it, and they had agreed without saying so that it would stay buttoned until they did.

"Ready?" said Karis.

"Nearly."

"Liar," said Karis, quite kindly, and put out her lamp.

---

Prynn came to the gate on the Thursday morning of week twenty-two.

Nobody had ever seen Prynn at the gate. Cael was not sure anybody had seen her farther from her own door than the long table at the end of her own room, and he had sat at that table for the best part of a year. She came down the cart road from the old building in the grey light before the first bell, in a black coat that looked older than the academy, walking slowly and without a stick. She had something under her arm.

The four of them were at the gate with their bags. The gate clerk was in the lodge with her list, and the cart that would take their bags down to the stage-house was standing in the road with the carter on the box, and two of the five watchers were where they always were at that hour, one at the gate post and the one who only worked mornings by the wall of the stable. Lira saw Prynn first. She stopped talking in the middle of a sentence and put her hand on Cael's arm.

Prynn did not greet any of them. She came up to Cael and held out what she had under her arm.

It was a sheaf of paper, thick as a thumb, sewn along one edge with linen thread and bound between two boards covered in grey cloth. He took it. It was heavier than it looked. He opened it at the first page and saw her hand, small and upright and perfectly even, in a black ink that had not faded at all.

It was an index. It listed the citations of registry law in Prynn's archive, every one she held, and each entry carried three cross-references. One was by subject, one by date of enactment, and one by the clause each entry amended or was amended by. Every entry ended with the name of the compilation the text could be found in and the shelf-mark where that compilation actually stood, in this archive, on this hill. He turned the pages. There were more than fifty of them. Some of the entries had small marks beside them, a tick or a cross or a short line.

"The ticks are sound," said Prynn. "The crosses are struck. The lines are the ones nobody has looked at in so long that I couldn't tell you, and neither could anybody else, and you'll want to find out before you lean on them." She looked at the sheaf in his hands and not at him. "The next place you go will have a bigger archive than mine and a worse one. Big archives always are. They take on young people to catalogue and then they make the good ones into keepers of keys, and the cataloguing stops, and nobody notices for twenty years. You'll find what you need in a place like that only if you know what it's called before you go in. This is what it's called."

"Prynn," he said. "This must have taken—"

"It took the winter." She did not let him finish. "I don't sleep well in the winter. It was something to do."

He did not believe her for one instant, and she knew he did not, and she did not care.

"Thank you," he said.

"Thank me by opening it." She had already put her back to him. She had gone three steps when she stopped, without turning round, and spoke over her shoulder in the same dry level voice she used for shelf-marks.

"The shelf gap is still there. It'll be there when you're forty."

Then she walked on up the cart road, slowly, without a stick, and did not look back.

He stood at the gate with the sheaf in his hands and watched her go. He had thought, for the whole of the term, that the space on the third shelf of the second-to-last case was a thing Prynn could not bear to close. He saw now that he had had it the wrong way round. She was not failing to close it. She was keeping it open. She would go on keeping it open every morning she unlocked her door, for the rest of her life, as the one entry in her archive that could not be answered with another entry. It would wait there for whoever came back to ask about it. She had just told him who she expected that to be, and roughly when.

Karis had come up at his shoulder and was looking down at the open sheaf with an expression he had last seen on her face in the intake room at Greyvane, when she first heard the word *archive*.

"Shelf-marks," she said, under her breath. "She's given you the shelf-marks. Every one." She did not reach for it. She kept her hands at her sides, very deliberately. "Do you know what that's worth? In a strange archive, the first month goes on finding where things are. She's given you the first month."

"You can borrow it."

"I'll copy it," said Karis. "On the road. Every evening. You can read it while I copy." She looked up the cart road after Prynn's black coat. "I'm not borrowing the only one of those that exists. She'd know. From four days off, she'd know."

Brom had picked up all four bags while they were talking and was standing by the cart holding them, two in each hand, as if they weighed nothing, waiting for somebody to tell him where to put them.

He put the index in his bag, on top of the binder, and buttoned the flap.

"Well," said Lira, very quietly, beside him.

"Yes," he said.

The gate clerk leaned out of the lodge window with her list. "Four of you going out," she said. "I've had you down as going out since the second bell yesterday. Are you going out, or are you going to stand in my gateway all morning admiring the archivist?"

They went out.

---

Four days of road lay between Greyvane and the river, and on every mile of it somebody in grey kept them company.

Cael counted them from the first morning, because counting was what he did with his hands when there was nothing else for them, and because a thing counted was a thing he could stop being afraid of. He did not have to look hard. Nobody was hiding.

*Day one. Two. Both mounted, both in grey, a quarter-mile back on the road from the foot of the hill, keeping the same distance whether we walk or stop. Changed at the second waystation for two more, also grey, also mounted. Neither pair spoke to the other. They nodded.*

It was good walking country after the first morning. The cart road off the hill joined the high road at the stage-house, and the high road ran south and east along the top of a long ridge with farms falling away on both sides, sheepfolds and stone walls and the brown of turned fields. The four of them walked it in the order they always walked a road without ever having agreed to: Brom in front because his legs were longest, Karis beside Cael in the middle because she liked to talk while she walked, and Lira a few paces back. She said it was so that she could see everything. Cael thought it was so that she could see him.

They let the cart go on ahead with the bags and walked behind it. Nobody suggested riding. They had walked into Greyvane, and it seemed right to walk out.

Karis walked beside him with her narrow brown notebook open in one hand and a pencil in the other. She wrote while she walked, which Cael had never been able to do without falling over. At the noon stop they laid the two notebooks side by side on the wall of a sheepfold and compared, line against line, as they had compared the consolidations.

"Same," said Karis. "Except you've got the second pair changing horses and I haven't."

"You were looking at the first pair."

"I was looking at the first pair because the first pair was looking at us." She made a small correction in her own book. "Yours is better. I'll take it."

*Day two. Three. The two mounted from yesterday's change, and one on the coach with us from the second stage, a grey coat with a satchel on his knees. He took the seat with the widest view of the four of us, and kept it. He read a book. He didn't turn a page in an hour.*

That afternoon, rattling along in the coach with the grey coat across from them pretending to read, Brom leaned across and spoke to Cael very low.

"He's not even trying."

"No."

"Last year they tried." Brom looked out of the window at the two riders keeping their quarter-mile. "Last year it was one man at the edge of a crowd who you couldn't be sure of. Now it's three, and one of them is sitting on my bag." He considered this. "Why'd they stop trying?"

Cael had been thinking about it since the foot of the hill, and he gave Brom the answer he had come to.

"If a man hides while he watches you, he wants to learn something," he said. "He wants you to forget he's there, so that you'll do whatever you'd have done anyway, and he can write it down. A watcher who doesn't hide wants the opposite." He kept his voice under the noise of the wheels. "He wants you to know. He wants it to change what you do. He's not collecting anything. He's trying to make something happen in you."

Brom thought about that for most of a mile.

The coach rocked down off the ridge into a valley of orchards, and the grey coat across from them turned a page at last, and Lira, who had been asleep against the window, opened one eye and looked at him and closed it again.

"Is it working?"

"I don't know yet," said Cael honestly. "Ask me at the other end."

---

*Day three. Two. New faces, both mounted, took over at the river stage before we were up. One of them has a horse with a white sock on the near hind. The coach man from yesterday got off at the river stage and didn't get back on.*

*Day four. Two. One is the white sock from day three. The other is the coach man from day two, riding now, on a brown horse that isn't his. He doesn't look at us. He looked at us for a whole day yesterday from three feet away and now he doesn't.*

The fourth night they slept a half-day short of the river, at an inn with a low room under the eaves, and there he laid the observation notebook flat under the lamp. He added it up the way he would have added up a column in Vell's ledger.

*Four days. Distinct faces: seven. Distinct changes of watch: five. Never fewer than two on us at any time. Three at junctions and at the inn stops, where the roads split and they couldn't be sure which way we'd take. Nobody spoke to us. Nobody needed to.*

*What this costs them: two men and a horse-change every eighty miles, for as long as I'm worth watching. That isn't a whim. It's a budget. Somebody signs for it, and keeps on signing.*

*Their side of it: I know they're there, and knowing is supposed to bend me. My side: seven faces, five rotations, a white sock on the near hind, and a fair idea of what the Compact thinks I'm worth per mile.*

He sat back and looked at it.

Brom had come in behind him with two mugs of something hot from the kitchen. He set one down at Cael's elbow, read the page over his shoulder without asking, and sat down on the end of the bed, which complained.

"Does it bother you?" said Brom.

"Less than it should." Cael turned the mug on the table and thought about it again, because Brom's questions deserved a second try. "They're in plain sight. I've got every one of them on a page, and a fair idea of the bill, and I could tell you now where they'll stand at breakfast. That's not what frightens me." He looked at the page. "What would frighten me is a morning with nobody on the road behind us, and no reason I could find for it."

Brom drank some of his mug.

"That'd mean they stopped."

"Or that they got better."

Brom looked at him over the rim of the mug for a while. Then he nodded slowly, once, as if he had been handed a weight and found it heavier than it looked, and drank the rest without saying anything.

Karis put her head round the door a little later with her narrow brown notebook in her hand.

"Five changes," she said. "I make it five. You?"

"Five. Seven faces."

"Seven." She came in and sat on the other end of the bed from Brom, who made room. "Then here's what I keep coming back to. They aren't hiding. That's a decision. And they aren't stopping, either, not even now that the ruling's public and there's nothing left to find. That's a second decision, and it costs money every month, and somebody has to put their name to it every month." She closed the notebook on her pencil. "The first one I understand. That second one has a name signed under it somewhere, every month. I'd like to read it."

"So would I."

"Then that's two of us who'll be counting at the other end." She got up. "Goodnight. Don't sit up writing."

She went out. Lira was asleep already in the next room, and now Karis was writing by her own lamp next door; he could hear the pencil through the wall. Cael turned back a few pages in the binder to the last entry he had made at Greyvane. It was the term's count, written at the long table on the last night, before the gate and Prynn and the index. Under it there was half a page left. He looked at it for a while, and then wrote two lines at the foot of it, in the square hand.

*Greyvane. Twenty-two weeks. A word found and not killed. A bout won by one exchange, perhaps none. A sentence from Quenna and a book from Prynn, and both of them meant more than they said.*

*Done. Tomorrow, the river.*

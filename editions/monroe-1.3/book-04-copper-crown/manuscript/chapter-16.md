# Chapter 16 — Aim

The east hall was cold at the second bell, with nobody in it.

Cael had never seen it empty. In his mind it was always full: Rooke at the west end not moving, fourteen of the cohort along the walls, Brom in the middle of the boards being found out by four people at once. Without them it was a long pale room with the light coming in low through the south windows and lying across the oak in bars, and the floor seemed larger, the way a floor does when there is nothing on it to give it a size. When he sat down on the bench inside the near door the boards gave their faint answer even to that, a breath down and a breath back, as if the hall had noticed him and gone back to sleep.

His supervised session was at the third bell. He was forty minutes early, and he had told nobody he would be. That was the whole of the test.

He had worked it out on the walk back from the counter the day before, while his face was still warm from the floor trade. Anything written went through the office. Anything that went through the office reached the copying table. So the next test could not be written, or asked for, or filed, or entered. It had to be a thing he simply did, on an ordinary morning, with no paper behind it at all. And the only thing he could do without paper was turn up.

He set the binder on the bench beside him, shut, and did not touch it. A boy reading notes looks as if he is waiting for something. A boy sitting on a bench in a cold hall before his session looks like a boy who came too early, which happens to everybody, and which is very dull.

Above him and to his right, the gallery ran the length of the north wall, a narrow timber walk at the height of a man's head, with its rail and its bench and the near-ladder of a stair coming down at the east end. Cael knew it well. He had sat on it for a week of afternoons watching Ephram take Brom apart.

He had also learned, in the last few days, a thing about it he had not known then. The gallery did not end at the west wall. A low door there let onto the upper passage that ran across to the north hall, and staff who had business between the two halls used the gallery as a short cut to save themselves a stair. He had six of them in the shorthand, crossing at one bell or another. One of the six was the man from the copying table, who crossed at about this hour on three mornings in five, on his way from the wing to the north hall's records cupboard with an armful of something.

On the mornings when Cael trained here, the man came along the gallery and did not cross. He stopped at the turned post at the west end, in among whoever had come up to watch the assay enrollee, and stayed there until the bell.

There were always people to stay among. Cael had never once trained in the east hall without a gallery; he was the thing on the bluff people climbed stairs to look at.

Today there was nobody to climb for. The floor below the gallery was empty, except for a boy on a bench by the door, and the boy was not due for forty minutes.

The light moved a hand's width across the boards. Somewhere outside a porter was rolling a barrel over cobbles, a long hollow grumble that rose and fell and stopped. The cold came up off the floor into his feet. He sat with his hands loose between his knees and thought about nothing in particular, on purpose, the way a man stands at a river trying not to think about the fish.

At about the twenty-third minute the low door at the west end of the gallery opened.

Cael did not look up. He had placed himself so he would not need to. Across the hall, the near door's glass, propped open against the wall to air the room, held the gallery in it like a picture in a dark frame, small and a little bent. He could see the whole walk in it without lifting his head from the floor in front of his boots.

A man came out onto the gallery with an armful of forms.

He came along it at a clerk's pace, neither quick nor slow. He passed the turned post, where he had stood on every one of Cael's mornings in this hall for nine days, and he did not slow at it. He did not glance at it. Most of all, and this was the thing that went through Cael like cold water, he did not look down. Below him, alone in an empty hall at an hour when nothing was scheduled, sat the most stared-at student on the bluff. Every porter and lecturer and first-year who had ever crossed that gallery would have glanced down at him from pure habit, the way anybody glances at the one lit window in a dark street.

The man looked at his forms and went on. He reached the stair, went down it with one hand on the rail, and out through the door to the yard.

Cael sat on the bench for the rest of the forty minutes without moving, because if he had moved he would have taken out the binder, and if he had taken out the binder his hands would have shown what he felt.

When the hall began to fill for the third bell, and the supervisor had signed, and the first of the gallery's watchers had begun to clatter up the stair, Cael finally opened the binder on his knee, as if checking the hour. He wrote four words in the margin, small, and closed it again.

*He needs a crowd.*

---

He turned the four words over all through the session, under everything else.

If the man needed a crowd, then the thing the man did had conditions. It worked in some places and not in others. It could be made to fail, and the man knew when it would fail, well enough to walk away from a perfect view rather than test it. A thing with conditions was a thing being operated. A clerk who happened not to look at people needed nothing. Nobody needs a crowd to read his forms in.

So the man was not incidentally anywhere. He was at work, all day, every day, and his work was standing.

It was not, Cael discovered within a quarter of an hour, a thing he could turn over under a supervised session and still train well.

The supervisor that morning was the Shield instructor from the north hall, the one who wrote letters on his knee for the first stretch of every session and then folded them and sat on them. He had a dry, tired voice and a very good eye once it opened. He had set a drill Cael had done before: two second-years, one at each end of the floor, sending practice weights across the boards on short cords at no particular rhythm. Cael stood in the middle, calling the line of each and stepping off it, without the framework, on his feet alone. It was a read drill. It asked nothing of the Wind. He had done it eleven times in his first month and been clean every time.

On the fourth weight he called it late.

Not wrong; late. The press came along his forearm as it always did, the small push on the air before the weight, telling him how heavy and which way, but it arrived as if it had been sent by a slower post. He stepped off the line a hair after he should have, and the cord's weight brushed the back of his calf going past.

"Again," said the Shield man, not looking up from his letter.

On the seventh, the same. On the ninth, the same, worse. Each time the read came in, it came in true, and each time it came after he needed it.

He tried to cheat it, which was a mistake, and knew it was a mistake while he did it. If the read was slow, he thought, he could start moving on the first faint touch of it instead of waiting for the whole press, and make up the time that way. On the tenth weight he went early, on half a signal, and the half had been wrong: the second-year had checked her throw at the last instant, the way a good feeder does to keep a drill honest. The weight came in a foot lower than its first push had promised.

It took him across the shin. It did not hurt much. It told him a great deal. A slow instrument was a nuisance. A slow instrument he had started guessing ahead of was a danger, because a guess felt exactly like a reading from the inside, right up until it was wrong.

He stood in the middle of the floor while the second-years wound in their cords and made himself take stock of his own body as he would have taken stock of an opponent's. He was not tired. He had slept. His hip was quiet; he had not touched the framework. Nothing hurt. The Iron-adjacent fragment had never been fast; it was a pace and a half and no more, cheap and quiet and never anything you could hurry. But it had always been prompt. A pace and a half had always been time enough. This morning it was arriving with half that time already spent, as if something between his skin and his attention had begun to keep it waiting at a door.

He did not know what. He knew better than to guess on one morning.

At the thirteenth minute the Shield man folded his letter, sat on it, and watched the rest of the drill with both eyes. He did not say anything about the late calls. At the end, signing the sheet, he looked at Cael for a moment over the pen as if about to ask, and then did not ask, as none of them ever asked, and handed the sheet back.

Up on the gallery, as the session broke, Cael let himself look up once, the way everybody looked up at a gallery when they came off a floor, at nothing and everyone. Six people were coming down the stair. Four more were still at the rail. At the west end, by the turned post, in the middle of a knot of three second-years arguing about the drill, stood a man with an armful of forms, reading the top one.

Cael looked away at once, and went out, and did not look back.

That evening he wrote the morning down in two places. Under the shorthand he wrote the gallery, the door, the twenty-third minute, the clerk's pace, the four words. In the Power Log, where he kept his own architecture and nothing else, he wrote the drill.

*Iron-adjacent fragment, east hall, Shield supervisor, cord drill. Three late in twelve. Not wrong. Late, about a beat. No fatigue I can find, no hip, no injury. Cause unknown. One instance; not yet a fact. Watch it.*

He did not put the two entries side by side. It did not occur to him to, then.

---

The Crown yard ran practice on the following afternoon, the sixty-ninth day, and Cael went down to the lower rail on the east side early, which was where he always went, and stood with his back to the upper tiers.

He had chosen the spot a month ago for its view of the rings and nothing else. It was only in the last week that he had noticed what stood at the north end of the same rail.

The yard had a lamp for evening sessions, a tall brass thing on a post at the north end of the floor, the height of two men. Its hood was polished every week by the porter, who was proud of it. By day its great curved belly gave back the whole yard in a smear of warm brown: the floor, the rings, and above them the tiers, rising in bent bands like the inside of a bowl. From where Cael stood, if he turned his head a little toward the rings, the lamp hung at the edge of his eye, and in its curve, very small and stretched sideways, sat the high west tiers.

He could watch them without facing them.

He had found it by accident three days before and had felt, at the time, a shameful little leap of pleasure, as if he had found a coin in a coat he had stopped wearing. Now he set his weight against the rail, folded his arms on it, and looked at the rings.

The yard on a practice afternoon had its own smell, of chalk and of the oil the porter rubbed into the rail, and its own noise, which was not the roar of a ladder session but a low steady murmur, like a market an hour before closing. People came to practice to learn, or to be seen learning, or to sit in the sun on stone with a friend and argue about a fight neither of them was in. Two Iron girls behind him were quarrelling in whispers about whether a Mire practitioner could stick a Wind fighter's feet before she left the ground. A lecturer from the law range was asleep on the fourth tier with his hat over his face.

The first ring had a Copper practice bout on it, a Force fourth-year against a Current girl, first to three clean touches. The second had two Stone boys doing a slow, careful drill that nobody was watching. The third was empty, waiting for its pair. The tiers were about a quarter full, which was ordinary for a practice afternoon. Notebooks were out along the second and third tiers, as they always were.

In the brass, high on the west side, in a knot of five people, a small brown figure stood with something pale held against its chest.

Cael watched the Force fourth-year.

The Force boy gathered and threw, and the throw went off the floor like a door slammed in a stairwell, a flat crack of air that turned every head in the yard. The Current girl had been there. She was not there now; she had gone along the line of the force on its own current, the way that Path went, and come off it two paces to the side with her hand reaching. The whole yard looked round at it, as a yard will at a door banging.

In the curve of the lamp, the small figure did not turn.

Cael felt it the way he had felt the empty gallery: low in the stomach, cold.

The bout went on. The Current girl took the first touch, and the Force boy the second, and the third exchange ran long, round and round the ring with the force cracking off the boards three times and the girl riding the edge of it. On the third crack the girl misjudged her current and the force took her square. She went down on her back on the floor with a sound that carried right across the yard and up into the lecture range, the flat whack of a body arriving all at once. Somebody on the second tier said "Oh!" aloud. The instructor at the table stood up. The girl lay a moment looking at the sky, then lifted one hand to show she was whole, and the yard let out its breath and laughed with relief.

The small figure in the brass had not moved. Not at the crack. Not at the fall. Not at the laugh. For ten minutes, while the only interesting thing in the whole bowl of the yard was happening on its floor, the man high on the west side had not once looked down at it, nor at the rail where Cael leaned, three paces from the edge of the first ring.

Cael watched the girl get up and shake her arm out and walk back to her chalk. He made himself watch her until the bout was done. Then he turned and went up the east stair and out, because it was the hour he always left on practice afternoons, and leaving at any other would have been a sentence of its own.

---

Two days on, he tried the lamp a second time, from the far side of the yard.

He needed another angle, because one reading off one lamp from one rail was only one reading, and because he wanted to know whether the man would sit differently against a different Cael. So on the seventy-first day he did not go to the lower east rail. He went to the south steps, where the porter kept his chalk and his pail, and sat on the third step to unlace and lace his boot, slowly, because a broken lace is the one thing every person in the world will believe without question. From the south steps the same lamp was nearer, and the curve turned the west tiers the other way, so they lay along the bottom of the brass instead of the top, but they were there.

Middle of a knot of four, high on the west side. Pale forms against the chest.

He laced his boot. He unlaced it and laced it again. He took out his binder and wrote the practice pairings in it, all three rings, with the exact care of a boy whose friend was on the ladder and who wanted the record, which was true. Eleven minutes. Two touches on the second ring, one fall, a Mire practitioner making the boards so sticky under a Blade boy's feet that the Blade boy laughed out loud and appealed to the table. Through all of it, in the bottom of the brass, the figure stood among its four and looked at the floor's far end, or at the tiers opposite, or at its forms, at everywhere in the whole bowl of the yard except the steps where Cael sat.

He was lacing the boot for the third time when the porter came down the steps behind him with his pail, and stopped, and stood looking where Cael's eye had last been.

Cael's heart did something unpleasant.

"Fine bit of brass, that," said the porter.

"It is," said Cael, carefully.

"Forty years old. Older than the tiers on that side; they rebuilt them round it." The porter set his pail down on the step, plainly settling in. "Every Fourth-day I'm up a ladder with a rag and the red paste. First-years laugh. They think it's for show." He nodded at the lamp with deep satisfaction. "It's for the evening sessions. Light off that hood goes right down to the far ring. Dull brass, you'd have half a floor in the dark. You keep a thing bright, it gives back what you put in it."

"I can see that," said Cael, and found, to his own surprise, that it was perfectly true and that he could say it with a straight face.

The porter picked up his pail. "You'll want a new lace," he said. "That one's done for. There's a man in Ostrand by the fish steps does them in waxed linen, two for a copper." And he went off along the bottom tier to chalk the third ring, and never looked at the lamp again, and had never in his life, Cael was nearly sure, considered what else the bright brass gave back to anybody who sat on the south steps fiddling with a boot.

That night, at the desk by the window, he did not write the shorthand first. He did not write anything for a long time. He sat with the binder open at a clean page and the pen in his hand and the river sliding by in the dark under the bluff. The sentence came to him from wherever such sentences waited, and came out whole, on the first try, without one word crossed through, which almost never happened.

*Nobody avoids me that consistently by accident. Avoidance at that precision is aim.*

He looked at it.

Then, because he did not trust any sentence that came that easily, he wrote underneath it everything that would have to be true for it to be wrong. The man might be shy. A shy man on that gallery would have looked down, once, from pure curiosity, and then gone red. The man might be uninterested. An uninterested man at a practice afternoon would still have turned at a crack loud enough to stop conversations in the lecture range. The man might simply be a clerk with a clerk's habits. A clerk's habits did not stand him on the single best view of Cael's hour nineteen times in nineteen, and take him past that view the one time it was empty.

He read the list twice. Every line on it was weaker than the sentence above it.

He let the sentence stand.

Then he sat a while longer with the lamp, and found that something in him had gone quiet and cold, and he made himself look at what it was, the way he would have made himself look at a bruise.

It was not fear, exactly. He had been watched for two years and had grown used to it, as a man grows used to rain. But the coats at the ferry landing had never aimed at anything. They stood where they were put and looked at what came by. This was different.

Somebody had sat down, somewhere, and thought carefully about Cael, about the shape of his days and the places he would be and the angles from which those places could be seen. Then that somebody had put a skilled man into those angles, and kept him there, every day, without once letting him look. It was the difference between rain and an archer. He had known, in a general way, that the Compact cared what he was. He had not known until tonight that it cared enough to be precise.

He wrote one more line, small, at the very bottom of the page, and did not underline it.

*Somebody thinks I'm worth aiming at. Note it. Don't be flattered by it.*

---

On the next evening, the seventy-second, he went out to the wall behind the second quadrangle with the binder under his arm, because it was the place he thought best, and found Lira already on the coping with her knees drawn up and her eyes on the far bank.

"Twenty past," she said, without turning. "Two going down, two coming up. On the bell, or near it. They've kept the bell every day since the late one."

He leaned on the stone beside her. Across the water, under their lamp at the foot of the ferry landing, the two grey coats stood as plainly as a pair of gateposts, and two more were walking up the river street to relieve them. They did not hide; they had never once tried. They stood where they could be seen and changed where the change could be watched, every four hours, and had done so since the bridge, and at Greyvane before that, and on every mile of road between.

He found he was looking at them as if they were new.

"Do you remember Havel?" he said.

Lira did turn her head at that. "The assessor. At Ardenmere. With the coat that was too good for him."

"What did you notice first about him?"

She thought, which she did properly when asked properly. "His pencil. He wrote in that little book where you could see him do it. And he'd look at you, and then look away at the street as if the street had called his name." She frowned at the water. "Like a boy sent to fetch something out of a room he's not sure he's allowed in. He kept checking the door."

"He was checking the door," said Cael. "That's what it was. He spent half of every look on us and the other half on whether he ought to be looking. I thought he was clumsy. He wasn't clumsy. He was split down the middle."

"And them?" Lira tipped her chin at the landing.

"They're the opposite. They've no doubt at all. Somebody pays for them to stand there and be seen, and they stand there and are seen, and they never once wonder whether they should." He watched the relief reach the lamp and the four of them nod to one another, as polite as shopkeepers. "But the being seen is the whole of what they do, so everything they see, I've already picked out for them. You can't watch a person who can see you at it. All you can do is take down what he lets you have. Two years of their reports, and I could have dictated every line."

"So Havel was frightened, and those are bored," said Lira. "And?"

"And there's a third kind."

She waited. Below them a barge went downstream with its sweeps shipped, and its master blew his horn, the long note and the two short, and somewhere under the bluff another horn answered.

"Tomorrow," said Cael. "With the others. I want to give it to all of you at once."

Lira looked at him, and then away at the coats again, and said nothing, which meant yes.

He opened the binder on the coping in the last of the light and wrote what he had been carrying since the lamp.

*Havel watched me and watched himself watching, both at once, and it leaked out of him at every seam. The coats watch me so that I will watch them; their whole craft is being looked at. The third does not watch me at all. He watches whatever I happen to be standing in, a hall, a yard, a stair, and lets me be the reason it is worth his while. I have been looking for his eyes. His eyes were never the instrument. His feet are.*

He read it, and then wrote the next part, because he had promised the binder years ago that true lines went in whether he liked them or not.

*For the record: he is better at this than I am. I've been at it nine days with a pencil and a lamp. He has been at it every hour he has spent on this hill, and for years before that, I'd guess, and in all that time I have found him put a foot wrong exactly once, on a morning I built for the purpose.*

"You've got that face again," said Lira. "Only worse."

"It isn't a number any more." He closed the binder. "Can you get Brom off the post tomorrow? And Karis out of carrel eleven? Common room, after supper, door shut."

"Sixth-day. After supper." She swung her feet down off the coping. "Is it bad?"

He weighed it, and she let him, the way she always let him.

"It's the best piece of luck I've had since the bridge," he said. "And you're going to hate it."

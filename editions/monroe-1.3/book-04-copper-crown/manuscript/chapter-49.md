# Chapter 49 — Live on an Active File

Ilsev had asked for the routing file before the sitting began, and Havel brought it to her at the recess, as she had known he would, without being reminded.

The afternoon would come to the ongoing requirement. The ongoing requirement touched the monitoring of the enrollee, and the monitoring lived in the routing file. She did not ask questions about a thing she had not read. That was the whole of her method, and in thirty years nobody had ever found it necessary to make it more complicated.

"Assessor," said Havel, and laid the file at her elbow, square to the table's edge, and went back to the foot.

The hall was emptying round her. The counsel had gone out talking, with two of the clerks trailing behind her, and the academy's side had risen and drifted toward the windows to stretch. Ilsev stayed where she was. She untied the file's tape and turned back the cover.

The wing's compilation was not there. It had gone back to the wing the evening before, receipted, which she had noted with approval when she saw it in the log; a file should be in its own house when it was not being read. What remained was the routing history, in a long narrow ledger, every request and transmittal that had ever touched the subject's file, each in its own line. Behind the ledger was the file's correspondence, which was where a records office put whatever came back.

She read the routing history first, because it came first. It held nothing she did not already know. She had read most of it before, at a long table in a cold hill-town, a year and a different academy ago.

Then the correspondence. It was thin. Most of it was the ordinary paper of a file in motion: requests, acknowledgements, a transfer notice. At the very back, entered by the records officer at the eighth bell of the evening before, in his dry careful hand, *correspondence in, delegation's courier, up*, lay a single folded sheet with the registry's stamp on it.

She knew what it was before she unfolded it. She unfolded it anyway, and read it, because knowing what a thing was had never once in her life been the same as having read it.

*Return, from the chain of review, to the senior evaluation seat, on referral of the matter at Greyvane. Designation: Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

*Determination: no originating authority of record. Designation predates file creation. No action available at this clearance.*

She read it once, and laid it flat on the table, and put her two hands flat on the wood on either side of it. Other people, she had noticed over a long career, did a great many things with their hands at such moments. They rubbed their eyes, or reached for a cup, or tapped a pen. She had never seen that any of it helped.

---

She had seen the first two sentences before.

They had come to her at Greyvane, on a Saturday, after the fifth bell, on a sheet laid square on the corner of the long table by the same careful hand that had laid this one. *No originating authority of record; designation predates file creation.* That had been the answer to her query, the plain question she had put at the lowest temperature the form allowed: who had entered this mark, and on what authority? She had read that answer twice. Then she had done the correct thing. She had written a referral, attached the return to it, marked it as an administrative irregularity, and sent it up the chain on the Monday morning courier, to whoever sat above the office that had answered. Somebody higher would know. Somebody higher always knew. That was what higher was for.

That had been eleven months ago.

And here was higher. Higher had read her referral, and the return attached to it, and had sent back the same two sentences, word for word, with a third added on the end, like a door being closed quietly by somebody who does not want to be heard closing it.

She read the sheet again, the way she read everything: the sentence that did the work first, then the sentences round it, then the working sentence again, against them.

*No originating authority of record.*

A designation was not weather. It did not fall on a file. Somebody issued it, and issuing was an act, and every act in the registry had a hand behind it that could be named, because that was the purpose of the registry. That was the entire reason it existed: so that anyone with standing could ask *who did this?* and be told. It was not a belief she held about the registry. It was the floor she had stood on for thirty years, under every finding she had ever signed.

*Designation predates file creation.*

That was the one she could not get past. She had now read it, between the two sheets, six times, and she still could not get past it.

No mark is older than the paper it sits on. If the designation was older than the file, then one of two things was true. Perhaps the file had been made again at some point and the mark copied across from an earlier one. Then the earlier file was the real record, and her query ought to have gone to wherever that file was kept, and somebody had sent it to the wrong door twice. Or else the sentence meant exactly what it said, and there had been a value in that field before there was a field.

She took her working sheet from her case and wrote on it, in her own small hand:

*Either this is a routing failure, or this is not an answer. Either one goes up. Neither is mine to settle.*

She looked at that for some time.

There was a next thought, and she could feel the shape of it waiting just past the edge of the page, the way you can feel a step in the dark before your foot finds it. She did not take the step. She knew she was not taking it, and she was easy in the knowing. She had watched able people, all her working life, ruin sound findings by going one clause further than their evidence went. A mark in a registry with no author anyone could name was a thing that existed. Things like that were either mistakes or somebody's discipline. She had no ground at all, not one line of paper, on which to say which this was. A woman who wrote down a guess about that had stopped being an assessor. She had become a letter-writer, and a letter-writer had no business at this table.

---

*No action available at this clearance.*

She read that sentence last, and it was the one that decided what she would do.

It was a well-made sentence. She could admire it as a piece of work while it closed a door on her. It was polite. It was final. It cost nothing to accept, and it had been built to be accepted. Most people who received it would read it, and nod, and put it in the file, and go out to stretch at the window with the others. They would be quite right to, by every rule she knew. It did not ask anything of them. That was what it was for.

There were exactly two things a senior evaluator could do with a returned referral that had not been answered. She could take the return as her answer, note it in the file, and go on with her afternoon. Or she could file again.

She took a referral form from her case.

The hall had nearly emptied. Somewhere behind her a clerk was stacking chairs that did not need stacking. One of the registrar's young men came along the table with a tray of tea for whoever wanted it, and set a cup at her elbow without a word, and went away again. She did not look at it.

She filled in the form in order, line by line, because the order was there to protect the reader. First the date and reference of her referral from Greyvane. Then the date and reference of the return now in front of her. Then the return's working language, copied out in full, every word of it, not summed up. A query that has been summed up can be answered by answering the summary, and she had seen whole matters lost that way. And then, in the box at the foot marked *grounds for escalation*, one sentence, which she wrote without stopping, because she had known what it would be since the moment she read the third line of the return.

*A determination that no originating authority exists is not a determination as to the designation's validity, and the designation remains live on an active file.*

She read it over once. It said what it needed to say, and nothing more, and it reached no further than the paper in front of her.

She marked the form for the level above the one that had returned it. She signed it. She dated it. She checked the date against the return's date and against her own Greyvane reference, because a referral with a wrong date was a referral that could be sent back for its date. Then she stood, carried it down the length of the table, and laid it in the shallow wooden tray at the foot, where the records officer would collect it. She came back to her chair.

It had taken her eleven minutes. She knew because she had looked at the clock over the door when she took out the form, out of long habit, and she looked again now.

The tea at her elbow had gone cold. She drank it anyway, all of it, in four swallows, and set the cup down on its saucer without a sound.

She did not think about the referral again for the rest of the afternoon. That was not indifference. It was the other half of the same discipline. A thing correctly filed was a thing you no longer had to carry. That was the whole reason forms had been invented. A woman who could not set a filed matter down would sooner or later file badly, just to make the carrying stop.

The counsel came back in, talking. Ilsev turned to the next page of the routing history and read it from the top.

---

Havel collected the tray when the recess ended, while the room was still finding its chairs.

There were two sheets in it, and he knew both. He logged them into the routing history in the ordinary way, at his place at the foot of the table, with the brass glass on its side. It took him four minutes, as such things did when they were done properly. *Return, in*: he had entered that the night before, but he entered its passage across the table now, from the file to the senior seat and back, because it had moved and a thing that moves gets a line. *Referral, out, escalated one level.* He gave the referral a reference to the return, and the return a reference to the Greyvane referral, and the Greyvane referral, already in the ledger, a reference forward to them both. Three sheets, each pointing at the other two, so that anybody who picked up any one of them would be led to the rest.

He initialled the lines and squared the ledger and turned the brass glass, and was ready when the counsel began.

He had read both sheets while he logged them. He always read what he logged. An officer who carried paper without reading it was only a pair of hands, and Havel had decided long ago that if he was going to be a pair of hands for the service, he would at least be a pair of hands that knew what it was holding.

So he had read the return the night before, when the courier came up the bluff in the last of the light and he signed for the satchel at the guest floor's door. He had known then what it would do to her. He had not laid it on her corner of the table, as he had laid the first one at Greyvane. He had put it in the file, in its place at the back of the correspondence, where she would come to it in her own order. She would want to meet it that way. He did not know how he knew that, but he was sure of it.

At Greyvane, when the first return came, he had watched her read the designation a third time after she had already referred it. She had looked at it the way a carter looks at a wheel that has come off on a straight dry road. That had been almost the only sign he had ever seen her give that a thing had reached her.

Today she had not read it a third time. She had read it, and filed again, inside the recess, and drunk her cold tea. And Havel, who had watched her from the foot of the table with his head down over his ledger, found that the filing had moved him more than the third reading had. The third reading had only been a person, puzzled. The filing was a person deciding to go on being puzzled, out loud, on the record, at the next level up.

Up the table, the counsel had begun on the ongoing requirement. Havel wrote it all down, exactly, and nothing else.

At the delegation's side, past the counsel, the Archmarshal's pen lifted from the folder while Havel was entering the referral, and made a mark, and was laid down again. Havel did not see it. He was looking at his ledger.

---

The second recess came at the fourth bell. The counsel wanted her own boxes from the guest floor, and the hall broke up again. Havel had to fetch a new roll for the recording case, because the morning had eaten the old one, and the spare rolls were in the delegation's working room up the stair.

The working room was empty. It was a narrow room with one window over the quadrangle, a table, five hard chairs, and the delegation's chests along the wall. He found the roll in the second chest, and fitted it into the case, and checked the spindles. Then, instead of going straight back down, he sat on one of the hard chairs with the case on his knees and took the notebook out of his inside pocket.

He did not often do that in a room that was not his own. But the stair was long, and the room was empty, and the counsel would want a quarter of an hour at least with her boxes.

The notebook had a cardboard cover gone soft at the corners. Ninety pages. Four of them were used. The rest were blank, and most of them, he supposed, always would be.

He read the three entries first, slowly, in order, the way he had read them on the night of Greyvane. A market, and a marker that matched nothing on the subject's file, and the word *Noted*. A manual opened to the section nobody opened, a query drafted twice and sent, and four lines back the same afternoon from the officer whose name was on the file: *Asked. Told to stop. Stopped.* And then Greyvane: the designation in the routing papers, the senior seat's query, the first return, the referral up, and the one line at the end that he had allowed himself because it was the only new thing in it. *Not only me this time.*

Then he dated the fourth, the day and the month and the year, and wrote it in the same dry hand as the others. The sameness of the hand was the point. Anybody who ever opened the notebook was to find a record, not a man with a theory.

*Halcenvane, the delegation. The senior seat's referral from Greyvane returned from the chain above, eleven months on. Return reads: no originating authority of record; designation predates file creation; no action available at this clearance. The same first two lines as at Greyvane, from one level higher, and a third. She filed again inside the recess, one level up again, grounds set down in a single sentence, return's words copied whole. Both logged in my hand. Cross-referenced each to each.*

He read it back. It was true, and it said no more than he knew.

He sat looking at it for a while. Below the window somebody was crossing the quadrangle with a bucket. Two carriers, he thought, now. Two of them, each holding one end of the same question, and neither of them cleared to be told the answer.

Then he did a thing he had not done in four entries. He broke his own rule about the notebook, which was that it held nothing but what had happened. He decided, sitting there with the roll in the case on his knees, that he could afford to break it once.

*Two years.*

*Two of us now.*

*Nothing has ever come back with a name on it.*

He looked at the three short lines. They were not a fact, not the way the lines above them were. They were the only thing in the notebook that a stranger reading it would know for certain had been written by a person.

He closed it, and put it back in his inside pocket, and went down the long stair with the case held flat against his side, and was in his chair at the foot of the table with the glass turned before the counsel came back with her boxes. There had been a sitting after the recess every working day of his life, and there would be one after this recess too, and the only thing he had ever been able to do about any of the rest of it was keep the count, and keep the work.

---

The afternoon came to the ongoing requirement, and Ilsev did the work.

Cael had read about her in six published findings and had built her page out of them, line by line. He had never once watched her do it in a room. She did not do it the way he had pictured. She did not press, and she did not argue, and her questions went in directions he could not have guessed from his chair.

"Registrar," said Ilsev, "what does the clause require of the institution? Not of the practitioner. Of the house."

Bracken told her. She listened to all of it without a word.

"Thank you. Would you read the clause's working sentence aloud, please, exactly as it stands?"

Bracken read it.

"And again," said Ilsev, "but this time beginning from the subordinate clause, and taking the main clause after it."

Bracken looked at her for a moment. Then he did it. He read the same words in a different order, and nothing in them had changed, not a letter, and the hall heard something new. The first time, the sentence had been about a practitioner who must be evaluated. The second time, it was about an institution that must evaluate him, and keep the record of it, every term, without exception. It was the same law. It was a different weight on the same scale. Cael, on his hard chair, felt the room tilt toward the academy's side by about a finger's width.

"Thank you," said Ilsev. "Magister Gault."

Gault had sat through the whole morning at the door end of the table without a single sheet in front of him. He lifted his head.

"The semester evaluation your wing gives," said Ilsev. "Is it the evaluation this clause describes? Or is it an evaluation the academy designed for itself, and named after the clause?"

Gault answered her in one paragraph. He did it without warmth and without the smallest pause, and it was the longest speech Cael had heard him make in a whole term.

"The clause asks for evaluation by demonstration, before a panel of the house, against a standard the house sets and records. My wing set the standard at this enrollee's baseline, in his first month. We published it on the board the same week. The apparatus has been calibrated on its own posted schedule since, by a keeper who initials a card for it, and the card is on my wall. On the twentieth we will measure him against that standard, in the order the standard lays down, whether or not anyone from this delegation chooses to be in the room. The clause doesn't hand me a standard. It tells me to set one, to apply it, and to write it down."

He stopped, as if he had come to the end of a line in a ledger. Then he said one more thing, in exactly the same voice.

"I have. I do. I will."

Across the table, the Archmarshal's pen lifted from his folder and marked, and was laid down.

"Thank you," said Ilsev, and wrote for a while on her working sheet.

Then she turned to the delegation's counsel, and the room stirred a little, because nobody had expected her to turn that way.

"Counsel. Is the delegation contending that this enrollment is unlawful? Or that it is irregular? They are different findings, and they carry different consequences, and I would like to know which of the two I am being asked to consider."

The counsel was quiet for a moment. Cael watched her weight, and it did not come forward onto her forearms. It stayed where it was.

"Irregular," she said.

"Then I have what I need," said Ilsev.

---

She closed her case a little after the sixth bell and stood to give the finding. She did not need to stand. Nobody else had stood to speak all afternoon. Cael thought that, like Bracken in the morning, she did it because the day deserved it.

He had nothing in his hands but the floor sheet he had collected from the wing's counter the evening before. So he turned it over on his knee and wrote down every word of what she said on its back, in pencil, as fast as he could make the letters.

"The clause is unrepealed. It appears in two independently certified copies of the founding schedule, is incorporated by the receiving institution's charter under a standard article, and is not among the eleven clauses the standardization directive repealed by name in a schedule its drafters had open in front of them. The directive's transitional article presupposes enrollments made under it. The enrollment before us conforms to the clause's language in its basis, its ongoing requirement, and its recording. Whatever else the delegation reports, the basis is sound law."

She sat down.

There was no sound in the hall for a moment, and then only the ordinary sounds: a chair, a pen laid down, someone at the far end breathing out. It was a records sitting, and records sittings did not cheer. Bracken made one small note in the margin of his index and underlined it once. Withrow did not move at all. And at the end of the academy's side, a few feet in front of Cael's chair, Karis had gone perfectly still, with her pen lifted off the page. She was sitting the way a person sits who has carried a heavy thing in her arms for a year and has just been told, by the one person whose word counted, that she may set it down.

Cael looked across the table at the Archmarshal.

The pen lay crosswise on the closed folder, exactly where it had lain since Gault's answer. It did not lift. It had not lifted when the counsel's best argument came down on the table like a weight, or when Withrow answered it, or when the second certified copy came out of the box. It did not lift now, when a senior evaluator of the Compact stood up and decided, on the record, that the enrollment of the boy on the hard chair was sound law.

He turned the floor sheet over again, to the front, where the sixteenth's hour was written in the desk clerk's hand, and began, in the margin, very small, to make a list.

---

He made it as the clerks began to read the day's exhibit list into the record. That was the last long gap of the sitting: a clerk at each end of the table taking turns with numbers and titles, a hundred and some of them, in flat voices, while everybody else waited for it to be over.

*One. When the registrar said how the copies were compared: a clerk who'd read neither.*

*Two. When the counsel said the index was good.*

*Three. When a clerk had sixty-two on the table in a breath.*

*Four. When Havel logged the tray after the first recess.*

*Five. When Gault said: write it down.*

*Not once when anybody scored. Not when the counsel argued. Not when Withrow answered. Not at the second copy. Not at the finding.*

He looked at the list. Then he wrote under it the thing the list said, and it was not a thing he enjoyed writing.

*He isn't keeping score of the argument. Nine hours on whether my enrollment is lawful, and he's made five marks, and every one is about whether this house's paper can be trusted. How the copies were checked. Whether the index works. Whether a clerk can find a sheet. Whether the records officer logs what he's handed. Whether the wing writes down what it does. Same question he asked Gault about the posts. Same question he asked the table in the yard. Third day running, a new angle each time.*

*He isn't judging the clause. He's finding out whether a number that comes out of Halcenvane means anything.*

*He is calibrating the instrument. I am the reading.*

It was while he was looking at that last line, with the clerks' voices going on and on at either end of the table, that he went.

Two of Bracken's clerks had been sent off at the second recess with boxes and had not come back, so the chairs on either side of him stood empty. He was looking at the floor sheet. Then, with no seam at all, he was looking at the floor sheet and the clerk at the far end was reading a different number. A good many numbers had gone by. He could not have said how many. He had not felt anything go.

And on the empty chair right beside him there was a folder.

It had not been there before. The inky-knuckled clerk, two chairs along, had set it down while Cael was gone. He had set it on the empty chair right next to Cael, not on the empty chair on his own side, which was nearer his hand. He had reached across a boy to put a folder down on the seat beside him, the way you reach across an empty bench.

Nobody looked. Nobody had noticed anything at all. Cael sat with his heart going hard and the pressure coming up behind both eyes like water filling a lock, and he thought, very clearly, the words arriving one by one: *that is a man putting something down in an empty place.*

Then something touched his ankle.

It was Karis's foot. She had not turned round. She had not stopped writing. She had slid her own foot back under her chair, as far back as it would reach, until the side of her shoe found the bone of his ankle and rested there. It was perhaps three-quarters of an inch of contact. It lasted about as long as a breath.

It brought him back to his own edges faster than anything he had ever written down in the working ledger.

He set the stake again and held it, hard, for the rest of the reading of the list. The clerk retrieved his folder some time later without the least surprise, as if it were quite ordinary to find it on that chair, and went back to his place. The sitting ended at the seventh bell. People stood and stretched and talked, and the counsel began, almost at once, to argue happily with Withrow's counsel about the age of a binding.

Cael sat on in his chair with the floor sheet on his knee and wrote two more lines at the foot of the margin, small, in a hand worse than any he had written all term.

*Nine hours. Two thin, one bad. The bad one in a gap, with my eyes on my own paper. Brom found it on a floor and I put it in the wrong kind of room. The twentieth is three hours, and the panel talks between the trials. That's where I'll go.*

*No answer for it yet. Karis's foot isn't an answer. It's a friend. That's worth more, and it works less often.*

He looked up. Karis had turned round in her chair at last, and was looking at him over its back, and did not say anything, and did not need to.

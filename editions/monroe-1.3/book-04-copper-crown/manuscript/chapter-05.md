# Chapter 5 — The First True Sentence

The toll board at the near end of the bridge had nine kinds of traveller painted on it, and Cael was still reading the sixth when Brom put eight coppers on the ledge.

*Persons on foot, two coppers. Persons with a pack beast, five. Handcart, laden, four. Handcart, empty, two.* Then a line for drovers, charged by the head, and a line for wagons, charged by the axle. Below those were three he had to read twice. *Persons bearing goods for the customs house, no charge on the house's tally. Persons of the river guilds, by their lodge's arrangement. Academy enrollees in the first week of any term, on presentation of the gate list, one copper.* The last had been painted in later than the rest, in a whiter white, and its price had been painted over at least twice. Every line on that board, Cael thought, was the healed-over place where somebody had won an argument with the toll keeper's grandfather.

"One each for enrollees," said Karis. "It's on the board."

"With the list." Brom collected four wooden tallies from the toll keeper and handed them round. "The list is at the top of that hill. We're at the bottom of it."

"I could have paid my own."

"Next time," said Brom, and walked on.

There was never a next time. Somewhere between Ardenmere and here it had become simply one of the facts of travelling with Brom, like his long stride and the way he ducked under doorframes he would have cleared. About once a week there was a toll or a ferry or a loaf at a stall, and Brom's hand was on the ledge first, and nobody was allowed to mention it afterward. Cael put his tally in his pocket and followed him out onto the bridge.

It was old stone and very long: five spans on four piers, each pier with a pointed cutwater shouldering into the current. Halfway across, at the crown of the middle span, Lira stopped at the parapet without saying why. The other three stopped with her, because that was what they did.

Below them the Ost went by wide and slow and brown. A barge was working up against it, sitting low with sacks under a tarred cloth, two men leaning on the long sweeps and a boy at the tiller, the three of them bent as though they were shoving the whole river uphill. Another came down the other way easily, sweeps shipped, and its master stood on the cabin roof and put a horn to his mouth and blew three short notes. From somewhere on the near bank a second horn answered with two.

"What's that?" said Lira.

"He's taking the middle arch," said Karis, "and somebody on the bank is telling him it's clear. I think. Give me a week and I'll have every call they've got."

Along the near bank the town went on as far as he could see each way, warehouses shouldering down to the water. He counted six long sheds with their doors open on the river and a seventh going up in raw yellow timber. A public wharf had a wooden crane on it with four men walking its wheel, and below that a second, smaller wharf stood behind a fence with a company's mark on its gate. Between the bridge foot and the public wharf, across the only street that ran down to the water, a building of good grey stone sat with its own steps and its own dock and a flag over its door. Nothing could come off a boat and into Ostrand, Cael saw, without passing under that flag.

"Customs," said Karis, watching where he looked. "Above the bridge the river's too shallow for anything big. So everything coming down out of the hills gets broken out of carts here and loaded onto boats, and everything coming up gets the reverse. Whoever stands at the place where one kind of carrying turns into the other takes a share of both." She leaned her back on the parapet and recited it as if she were reading it off a page in her head. "About twenty thousand people. Fourteen guild lodges. And two registry offices on Weighbridge Street, a hundred yards apart from each other. One is a district office and one is a stations office."

"Why have two?" said Brom.

"I don't know yet." Karis said it the way she said things she meant to fix. "But Lira's flag is going into a registry somewhere, and when it comes out it'll come out of one of those two doors. I want to know which door before it does."

Lira did not look round, but Cael saw her shoulders change.

He was looking past all of it, across the water, at the far bluff.

Halcenvane stood along its top. The first thing he noticed was that nothing in it had been inherited. Every school he had ever seen had been made out of something older: a road-house, a granary, a lord's barn with a floor laid in it. This one had been built to be what it was, all at once, by somebody who had drawn it first. The roofs all ran at the same pitch. The stone was the same pale stone from end to end. Along the bluff's north lip ran three long halls with their windows set high under the eaves, all on the side the sun came up. One heavy-roofed building had walls thick enough to carry a great weight of something, and he thought it must be books. A separate block sat behind a low wall of its own. And in the very centre, where everything else on the bluff seemed to lean toward it, there was a great hollow ringed on three sides with tiered stone seats.

He found he could not take his eyes off the hollow. Whatever it was for, the whole place had been arranged around it.

He had been keeping the count of days since the gate at Greyvane, every evening, in the margin of the observation notebook. It was five months and nine days, tonight, since a magistrate had stood up in a crowded hall and read him a ruling. He was fifteen years old. He had added the months up twice on the road against Hesk's last letter, because he no longer trusted any number that only he was keeping.

At the near end of the bridge, by the toll house, the two grey coats who had ridden the last stage behind them had got down from their horses and were not crossing. One handed his reins to the other and walked off along the bank toward the ferry landing.

"Changing at the river," said Lira, very low.

"Yes."

"I'll have them all by the end of the week," said Lira. "Come on. I want to be at that gate before somebody decides to shut it."

---

The road up the far side climbed the bluff in two long switchbacks, and it had passing bays.

Cael noticed them at the first bend, because passing bays were a thing nobody bothered with on a road meant for people walking. These were wide enough to pull a loaded wagon into while another went past it, paved at the edge, with a stone post at each end for the wheels to keep off. The road had a gutter on its uphill side and a culvert at each turn. Somebody had spent money on this road, and kept on spending it, which meant that the money went up and down this hill every week in wagons and somebody needed it to arrive.

At the foot of the road, where it left the river street, two men in grey coats were sitting on a low wall outside a chandler's. They were not the two from the bridge. They did not look up as the four of them turned onto the hill, and Cael added them to the count and did not look back.

"Two at the ferry," said Lira, behind him. "Two here."

"Changing every four hours, if it's the same as the road."

"We'll see." Lira hitched her pack higher. "I've got all season."

Halfway up they stepped into a passing bay to let a wagon come down, four casks roped in its bed and the brake shoe screaming on the wheel. The carter lifted two fingers off the reins at them and went past. Brom lifted two fingers back. When Cael raised his eyebrows at him, Brom shrugged.

"Carters," he said. "You always answer a carter. They remember who didn't, and they remember it on narrow roads."

The wall at the top came up no higher than Cael's chest, pale and new and clean-jointed. Nobody could have defended it, and nobody had meant to. It only marked a line on the ground and said *here*. The gate stood open with both leaves folded back. Beside it was a lodge with a glass window, and in the window was a man with a ledger, and in front of the window was a queue: three carts, a woman with two boys and a trunk, a porter with a handbarrow, and a young man in a good coat arguing about something with nobody in particular.

The man in the lodge was dealing with all of it at once and not enjoying any of it. They joined the end of the queue and waited for a quarter of an hour, and when they reached the window he did not look up.

"Names."

"Cael. Lira. Brom. Karis Dellenmoor."

He found the page without hunting for it. He read it off fast and flat, in the voice of a man who had read sixty names since the first bell and could hear the next cart's wheels already.

"Dellenmoor. Standard. Research standing pending; the library committee writes to you. Brom. Standard. Ninth day, first bell, east hall; the Blade cohort wants a look at you. Lira. Transfer, re-certification. Copper bracket. You register for the ladder by the fourteenth or you start the season on the bottom rung and climb from there." He licked a finger and turned the page. "Cael. Assay-provision enrollment. Straight to the registrar. Assessment wing, ground floor, the door with the brass plate. He's cleared the whole morning, which he does not do, so go now and look at the view later."

Then he did look up, once, at all four of them, with the face of a man who had a cart horse breathing on the back of his neck.

"Rooms, second quadrangle. The porter's got your keys. The assessment wing has a wall of its own; you'll know it. Don't cross the Crown yard when there's a bout on; they'll shout at you, and I'll be glad they did." He was already reaching for the next carter's slip. "Welcome to Halcenvane. You there, with the casks."

They went through the gate.

Cael had braced himself for it since the bridge. The only other gate in his life that had kept a list with his name on it had nearly stopped him dead on its threshold with something too large to have a name. He waited for that to happen again, and it did not. He walked through between the folded leaves and out the other side, and the only thing he felt was a kind of quiet click, like a latch going home in a door that had always been meant to close. Four people, four lines in a stranger's book, and four roads already decided in ink by people who had never seen their faces. Nobody at this gate had needed to argue for any of it. The arguing had been done somewhere else, months ago, on paper, and here was only the result.

It was very restful. He was not at all sure that it ought to be.

---

There were twelve desks in the intake hall and four of them were working.

The other eight stood under linen dust sheets down the far end of the room, in two rows, square and pale, like a second set of applicants who had come early and been told to wait. Cael stood in the line for the fourth desk and looked at them for a long time. The hall ran a hundred feet under a coffered ceiling, lit down its whole west wall by tall windows, on an oak floor laid for this room and no other. Somebody had sized it for an academy three times the one that was using it this morning. Somebody had looked at Halcenvane as it was, and built a room for Halcenvane as it meant to become, and paid for it, and then sat down to wait for the school to grow into its own furniture.

Greyvane had built for the people it had. He had loved Greyvane for that. He did not know yet what to feel about a place that built for people it had not met.

He counted arrivals, because it was something to do with his eyes. In the hour and a half he sat waiting, the double doors let in thirty-one people. His whole first year at Greyvane, all told, had come to forty-three.

Brom was done before Cael had counted ten. He came back across the oak with a tag on a cord round his neck, turning it in his fingers and looking injured.

"Nine minutes," he said. "I timed it by the bell. She looked at my papers the way my aunt looks at eggs. Held each one up, decided it wasn't cracked, put it in the basket." He sat on the bench beside Cael. "I've been the easy one twice now. You'd think I'd get used to it."

"East hall on the ninth," said Cael.

"Blade cohort. Everybody I've asked says the same thing about it. Go and stand in front of that lot for an hour, they say, if you'd like to learn what slow means." Brom tucked the tag inside his shirt. "So that's my ninth. A lesson in slow. I can't wait."

Karis came back from her desk with three forms and a letter, and her face held the particular delight she kept for documents that had clauses.

"There's a schedule of terms for the registry-law holdings," she said. "It's eleven clauses long. The ninth says what happens when the library committee and a reader disagree about access, and it gives the reader a right of appeal to the chancellor in writing." She sat down on Cael's other side and laid the forms on her knees as if they might take fright. "Greyvane had Prynn. Prynn was worth more than eleven clauses and a chancellor. But if Prynn said no, there was nowhere to go but the stove."

Lira was at the third desk for most of an hour.

Cael could not hear all of it from the bench, but he could see it, and he could read Lira standing at an official table with her arms folded better than he could read most people's faces. Her clerk was a grey-haired woman with spectacles on a cord. Lira's file had come up from Greyvane ahead of her: the old Fenmark record, the acknowledgment of standing she had signed before any of them knew her, and, pinned at the back, the sheet Greyvane had given her. It was the flag asking the registry to look at her tier again, under Greyvane's seal, the provost's whole two terms of watching folded into one line.

The clerk read the flag through. She gave it a single nod, wrote two words beside it, and set it on a pile by itself.

"That goes on," Cael heard her say. "To the registry's review queue. It isn't ours to decide. It's theirs."

Lira said something he could not hear.

"It moves," said the clerk. "Not fast. I've sent three on from this desk since the spring before last and had three answers back, so it isn't a hole in the floor. Until it comes back, you're placed by your formal standing." She turned a page. "Copper."

He watched Lira's jaw set.

They went on for a while longer, the clerk explaining and Lira not arguing, which was worse. *Only an Arbiter evaluation can change the tier*, the clerk said at one point, clearly enough for Cael to hear it across the room, and *I don't like the pace of it any more than you do, dear*, and *register early; the people who matter read the boards.* And then Lira unfolded her arms, put both hands flat on the edge of the desk, and said something that made the clerk look up at her properly for the first time.

The clerk thought about it. Then she dipped her pen and wrote a line at the foot of Lira's sheet, and turned it round so Lira could read it, and Lira read it and nodded, and signed under it.

She came back across the hall with a face like a shut door.

"What did you ask her for?" said Karis.

"The date it goes out," said Lira. "The day the flag leaves this building for the registry. I made her write it on my sheet, with her initials." She sat down hard on the bench. "If I'm going to wait for a queue, I'm going to know what day I started waiting."

Then the clerk at the fourth desk called Cael's name.

---

Registrar Bracken had a pen behind his ear and another in his hand and four stacks of paper on a desk with nothing else on it.

The stacks stood a hand apart, squared to the edge, each with a narrow slip on top and a number on the slip in a small upright hand. Cael knew that hand from twenty-two letters. Bracken himself was a spare man in a grey coat, greying at the temples, with a clerk's stoop he corrected whenever he caught himself in it, so every few minutes he seemed to grow an inch. He did not offer his hand. He pointed at the chair.

"You've read the letters," he said. "All of them?"

"All of them."

"Then we start at the fourth stack and work backward." Bracken sat. "Everybody wants to start at the fourth stack. It's the one with your name on it. So we'll look at it first, and you will not sign it, and then we'll go back through the other three until you understand why it stands up. Then you'll sign it. Not before."

He turned the fourth stack round and slid its top sheet across the desk. Cael began to reach for it, and Bracken put one finger on the corner.

"Read it," he said. "Don't touch the pen."

*Enrollment record. Halcenvane Academy, chartered.*

*Category: assay-provision practitioner: evaluation by demonstration per charter clause, standing basis.*

*Entry requirement: satisfied by demonstration before the assessment office, this term.*

*Ongoing requirement: full evaluation by demonstration before the panel of the assessment office, each semester, without exception. Compact observation rights attach.*

He read it once. Bracken took it back and set it face down on the desk.

"Now," said Bracken, "why should anybody believe a word of it?"

In the first stack lay the clause, copied out of three founding-era compilations, every copy certified, every one tagged with where its book stood and which clerk had sworn to it. Bracken read out each mark. Cael, because the index was in his bag, took it out and found each one in Prynn's small upright hand and read her cross-reference back. Two of the compilations were ones Karis had worked from. The third Greyvane had never owned.

On the second certified copy, Cael stopped.

"The date on the clerk's certificate," he said. "It's a year after the date in the compilation's own colophon. But the shelf-mark is for the first printing."

Bracken took the sheet back and looked at it for a long moment. Then he took the pen from behind his ear and made a small mark in the margin.

"The copying clerk's slip," he said. "He took the shelf-mark off the first printing and the certificate off the second, because they stand side by side. The text's the same in both. I checked that myself. But the sheet says two things at once, and anybody who wanted to could pull on that thread." He set the sheet aside on a pile of its own. "It'll be redone this week. That's what I asked you here for. Thank you."

Bracken called the second stack the negative case. In it were the three lists of repeals, and the consolidations, and a table ruled in his own hand, list against list and page against page, with the clause missing from every column. Under the table he had written two sentences. The first said what the absence showed. The second said, just as plainly, what it did not show, and why nobody should read it as showing that.

"Most people would leave the second sentence out," said Bracken. "It looks like weakness. It's the strongest line in the file." He tapped it. "Never let a document," he said, "claim more than it proves. If it claims one line more, that one line is where it gets attacked. Then people stop reading, and the whole file goes into the fire with its true lines and its false one together."

"Karis says nearly that."

"She's seventeen, your Dellenmoor. I asked her outright, after my forty-one came back answered better than I'd asked them. I didn't believe the first reply." Bracken squared the second stack. "Third."

The third stack was the hearing.

It held the whole transcript, both sittings, every exhibit, the deposition and the ruling, bound in grey boards and tabbed down the fore-edge in four colours. It was the copy of somebody who meant to work from it, not keep it nice. On top lay the ruling, with a pencil line under two of its sentences.

"Your floor and your difficulty, both in one binding," said Bracken. "Your hearing settled that what people do and what is written are two different things, and the written one wins. That's your floor. This clause is written, and all that ever happened to it was people stopping. And it puts your name at the top of a public record that anybody between here and the coast can send for at six silver marks. That's permanent. Both halves of it are permanent." He looked at Cael over the boards. "You knew that when you argued it, I suppose."

"Yes."

Bracken studied him for a moment longer than he needed to.

"Yes," he said. "I think you probably did. Fourth again."

He turned the enrollment record face up and slid it back.

Cael read it a second time, and then a third, and knew he was doing it, and knew why. He had done it once before. That had been at a narrow table at Greyvane, with Quenna on one side of him and a cold cup at his elbow, over a form that said *unclassified observer*. He had read it three times then not to understand it, but to make himself agree to sign a thing that was not him. The form had held. Every word on it had been true. Not one of them had been about him, and everybody at that table had known it.

This one was about him. It did not say what he could do. It was careful not to, and he could see now that the not-saying was what made it strong. It said only how he and the academy stood to each other. Here was a person whose capability no registry line could give, so it would be shown, in a room, before instruments, every semester, for as long as he was here. Nothing in it was hopeful. Nothing in it was kind. Every word of it was so.

"Well?" said Bracken.

"It's the first one that's true."

Bracken was quiet for the space of a breath. Then he picked up the pen and laid it beside the sheet.

"Two signatures," he said. "The first is for the standing. The second is for the obligation, and I want it on its own line, in its own stroke. A man who signs both with one movement can say later he only read the half he liked." He watched Cael's hand. "Somebody will come up that road one day to read this with a grudge. Inside two years, I'd guess. Keep that in the same pocket as the rest."

Cael signed twice, in the square hand he kept for the binder. He watched the second signature go dull as the ink dried. Something rose in his chest and his eyes stung, all at once and without asking him. He turned his head and looked very hard at the window, which showed a slice of pale wall and a gutter.

Bracken had got up and gone into the cabinet room behind the desk. Cael heard a drawer open and stay open for a long time.

When Bracken came back he had a tag on a cord and a folder, and he wrote on the folder's cover before he put it away: the date, and under it, *94 min.*

"I'd allowed two hours," he said, as if he were confessing to a fault. "The assessment office will set the day for your entry demonstration. Magister Gault sits that panel. His face gives nothing away. That isn't a judgment of you; it's his face. Whatever you show him that day is your baseline, and the baseline doesn't move. Every semester's evaluation is measured against it, as long as you're here." He held out the tag. "Choose with that in mind."

Cael took it. At the door he turned.

"Twenty-two letters," he said. "Why so many?"

Bracken had already sat down to the next thing. He did not look up at once.

"Twelve years I've kept these rolls," he said. "In twelve years, every record I've filed has been true because nobody ever had cause to look at it closely. Yours had to be true *because* I did the work properly. One day somebody will be paid to hunt for the flaw in it." He looked up. "Do you know how long I've waited for a file like that?"

---

Lira was on the wall before any of them, alone, with her elbows on the coping and the river below.

It ran along the edge of the bluff behind the second quadrangle, chest-high, the same pale stone as everything else, and beyond it the ground fell away in rough terraces to the water. Ostrand lay across the river with the late light going gold along its roofs. Down on the wharves somebody was lighting lamps, one after another, and the lamps ran out along the waterfront like a line of stitches.

Cael came and leaned beside her and did not say anything. After a while she spoke without turning her head.

"Did it say what you are?"

"It said how I stand." He thought about it. "Which is nearer than anything's ever said before."

"Good," said Lira. She meant it. He could hear that she meant it, and he could hear the other thing underneath, and she did not hide either.

Brom and Karis came across the grass from the refectory with half a loaf each and a twist of salt and a pot of something that might have been butter. Brom put the pot on the wall. Karis took a key on a string out of her collar and laid it beside the pot as if it were the more important of the two, which to her it was.

"Carrel eleven," she said. "Third floor of the law range. A door that shuts. A key. If I leave a book open on the desk tonight, it'll be open at the same page in the morning, with nobody's elbow in it."

"Is eleven a good number?" said Brom.

"It's a number." Karis put the key back inside her collar. "Greyvane had a table. I loved that table. But you can't lock a table."

Lira tore off a piece of Brom's bread without asking.

"Copper bracket," she said, to the river.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to.

"Two terms of putting Iron-equivalents on the floor. An academy's seal on a flag. A queue that's answered that woman three times since the spring before last." She ate the bread. "And they read the number on my certificate, and they stop reading. They always have."

"The flag transfers," said Brom, carefully.

"The flag transfers into a queue." Lira turned round and put her back to the wall. "I'm not sulking. Somebody write that down, because I'll want it later. I got through a gate today Greyvane couldn't have opened for me in ten years of trying, and I know who paid for it. But I'm going to be extremely tiresome about that bracket for one season. Then I'm going to take its crown off it. And then I'm going to stand in front of that queue with the crown in one hand and the date the clerk wrote in the other, and we'll see how fast it moves."

Brom considered this with great seriousness.

"So it's a plan," he said.

"That's a grudge with a schedule." Lira took the last of Brom's bread. "Same thing, if you keep the schedule."

Cael took out the binder. He set it open on the coping next to the butter, and nobody looked away, and nobody looked over his shoulder either. That was how it had been at this end of every day for a long time now.

He wrote it plainly.

*Enrolled.*

*Second time the word's been used about me. First time it's been accurate.*

He sat looking at it while the light went red on the river. Then:

*Karis found it and tried for eleven weeks to kill it, and it wouldn't die. Bracken checked it from the other end because he wanted to, not because anyone asked. A counsel I'll never meet put a seal on it, and Naveth read the seal twice and was glad for me in a way that cost him. Every form anybody ever got me through before was true in its words and false about me. This one is true about me. It took a lot of people to make one sentence.*

*I intend to be worth the sentence.*

He left a line and went on, because an entry that only counted what he had got was half an entry.

*What it costs: a panel every semester, a room, a full evaluation each time, and a chair in that room for the Compact whenever it wants one. Whatever I show Gault first becomes the baseline, and the baseline doesn't move. For as long as I'm here. Nobody has ever told me a price in advance before, in writing, and let me read it twice before I signed.*

*I'd have paid more.*

He closed the binder. Down on the water the last lamp on the last wharf came alight. Lira bumped her shoulder against his, once, the old knock, and stayed leaning there. Brom passed the butter. Karis, beside them, was already writing her own day into her own book, small and fast. In the middle of the bluff behind them, in the hollow of stone seats he had seen from the bridge, nobody had lit a lamp at all.

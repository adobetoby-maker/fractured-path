# Chapter 53 — Calibration

The residence stair had twenty steps, and Lira took them like a siege.

Right hand on the rail, left arm across Cael's shoulders. She would put the bad leg down on a tread and let it bear for about as long as it takes to say *one, two*, and then the leg asked to come up again, and she let it. Then the good leg did the climbing. Then they waited. Then they did it again.

"Talk," she said on the sixth step. "Say anything. It's worse when it's quiet."

"The practitioner," said Cael. "In the yard, while I was in Gault's office. What did she say?"

"That it's a deep strain along the top of the bone, and nothing's torn. Four days off it. Ice tonight, heat in the morning, and a stick if I've any sense." Lira breathed through her teeth and came up a step. "Then she gave me a long look and said, *On purpose?*"

"What did you tell her?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"She asked if I'd done it twice." Another step. "I said once. I told her the other three were accidents."

Below them on the stair Brom made a noise. It might have been a laugh. It might have been his knee, which had a bag of ice bound round it and plenty to say about stairs. He was carrying the Copper Crown dangling from two fingers at his side, the way a man carries a hat he has taken off indoors, because Lira had both hands busy and somebody had to.

Karis brought up the rear, loaded like a carter: two loaves; a stone jar the kitchen had pressed on her, since the kitchen had watched the final too; Bracken's copies; and a flat grey folder with Cael's name on the tab. She had collected that from the wing's counter on the way past, because the counter had been closing and she disliked leaving paper where it might be mislaid.

It took them a little over twenty minutes. At the top, Cael got the common-room door open with his foot and steered Lira to the good chair by the stove, the one with arms. She sat in it without arguing. That frightened him more than anything the practitioner had said.

For a while nobody did anything useful. Lira sat with her eyes shut until the colour came back into her face. Brom put the crown on the long table and stood frowning at it. Then he let himself down onto the boards by the stove, iced knee straight out. Karis lit the good lamp and filled the kettle.

"Table," said Lira, without opening her eyes.

"In the morning," said Cael.

"The morning's for the thing itself. Table now."

"You can't even stand up."

"I don't need to stand up. I need to sit here and tell you what you look like." She opened her eyes. "It's the only use I'm going to be tonight, and I'd like to be some use. If you argue with me I'll lose my temper, and that hurts as well."

Brom looked at the ceiling. Karis was already pulling the long table round toward the stove so that Lira would not have to turn her head.

---

They rebuilt the wing out of the furniture.

It was Brom's idea, and he ran it from the floor like a foreman. The long table, pushed against the settle, became the panel's bench. The slate Karis had used for the three columns stood on the mantel, propped against the clock, and would be the wall where the order was posted. Two chairs set back to back marked the rail of the frame. The hearthrug was the oak. A crack in the floorboards ran almost exactly where the brass crossing would be, and Brom pointed at it with great satisfaction and called it the fourth brass from the near wall.

"And I'm the frame," he said.

"You can't get up," said Karis.

"A frame doesn't get up. A frame sits where it's put and drops weights on people." He settled his back against the wall beside the stove. "I'm better at that than anything in this house."

Before Karis could begin, he held up one big hand.

"I'll say mine first," he said. "Because if I wait, she'll have built six sheets of sums and it'll sound like I'm arguing with them, and I'm not. It's a different thing."

Karis set down her chalk.

"Rooke stands on the gallery in the mornings," said Brom. "He doesn't watch our hands much. He watches how we carry ourselves between strikes. One day in the second week he stopped the whole hour and pointed at a Shield boy, and said, *That one's being careful.* And he was right. Everybody looked, and everybody could see it, plain as a hat. The boy wasn't doing anything wrong. He just looked like a man carrying a full bowl across a room."

He shifted the knee.

"You're going to walk into Gault's room tomorrow and be careful for three hours. You've never once not been careful, the whole time I've known you. I doubt you've any idea how it shows from across a room. It looks like that. So don't hide the cost. Let it cost you. Breathe hard after the board. After the plate, shake the arm out. Make a show of it. Let your fourth run be a fourth run, a bit worse than the first, the way fourth runs are. Then gather yourself for the fifth, and let the gathering be seen. Anybody who's ever trained anybody knows what a body that's working looks like. Give them that, and they'll stop looking for the other thing."

Cael found his ears had gone hot.

"What does the other thing look like?"

Brom said, "Like somebody with something in his pocket."

---

Karis took up the chalk again and drew six lines down the slate.

"The wing posts its order for a semester sitting," she said. "It's pinned beside the Magister's door, under the calibration card. I'm fairly sure I'm the only person who has ever read it from top to bottom, including the Magister." She wrote as she spoke. "One, the board. Two, the frame, read only, your back to the rail. Three, read and move together. Four, the plate. The last two are the first two over again, with an hour of oak in your legs. That's what you did at the baseline. This time the read stops being a trial by itself and gets folded into the third and the cold runs. And the fifth slot is new. The heaviest weight goes on the rail, they bring your square in close, and they set a tray of chalk dust beside it. The clerk reads your feet from the dust, not from your account of them."

"The weight I took on the plate," said Cael.

"That one. At the baseline it was too heavy and too close to get out from under, so they made you stand and take it. Now they want to know if you can leave."

Beside each line, in small figures, she wrote what the baseline had given. Four free on the board, the fifth billed, the sixth wide and slow. Eleven calls in twelve off the frame. Seven clear in eight on the combined, at weight three. The plate held low, four times, *and not eight*. Then she drew two chalk brackets down the right side of the slate, one above the other, with a gap between them.

"This is the part I've been doing for a fortnight," she said. "The floor and the roof. Between them is what a term of honest work ought to buy somebody like you, from that baseline, with nothing strange happening. Under the floor, the provision's failed in its own plain words. You were enrolled to be measured by what you can show, and you showed nothing. Over the roof—" she tapped the upper bracket "—you stop being a reading and start being a question. The room stops measuring and starts wondering. That's worse."

"What's between?"

"On the board, five free, or six. On the combined, the wing numbers its weights one to five, light to heavy. Weight four, and clear at least five times in six. Or weight three, clear every time." She put the chalk down. "That's the gap. I made it wide on purpose, and then I made it wider, and then I gave the sums to Bracken without saying what they were for, and he worked them through and handed them back the same."

"It doesn't look wide."

"It isn't the width that's hard. The hard part is doing it on every instrument at once, for a man who's told you to your face he'd sooner have six runs that agree than one wonder and five that don't, while you carry something nobody in the room knows about." She looked at him. "For three hours."

From the good chair Lira said, "He knows how long it is, Karis."

"I'm saying it so he hears it in somebody else's voice."

---

Karis decided to rehearse the plate first, out of order, because it was the one line on the slate that was not allowed to move.

There was no plate in the common room. She gave him the residence's book of hall rules instead, which was bound in boards nearly an inch thick, and told him to hold it out at shoulder height on his right arm, braced the plain way. Brom hit it from the floor. It was a short, heavy, sitting blow, and it went through the book and up the arm and into the shoulder, and stopped there.

"Your account," said Karis, in the flat voice she had heard Gault use.

"Four I can stand. Not eight. The shoulder keeps it."

"Again."

Four more. The book jumped the same distance every time. His shoulder made the same complaints in the same order. She had him do a third set and a fourth, and they came out like printed pages, one exactly the twin of the next, and she put the chalk down.

"Now give me a scared one," she said. "The one you'd give a room full of people who scare you."

It took him three goes to make it look real, and he did not enjoy discovering that. In the end he let the arm fold a little more on every blow, and after the third he stepped back off the hearthrug and said he could not take another.

Karis stood looking at the slate for a long while with her arms folded.

"I hadn't seen that coming," she said at last. "It would have had you, too. Everything you've planned for a fortnight has been about the roof. Not going over it. Not becoming a question." She tapped the lower bracket with the chalk. "Look where you just went. Under. That plate line is in Gault's own log, in his own hand, with *four, not eight* written beside it because you said it before he asked. Anybody in that room can lay tomorrow's line on top of it. Go in frightened, clamp down on everything by feel, and you won't just scrape the floor. You'll go through it. And then the record says a boy got worse over a whole supervised term, while the Compact sat and watched it happen."

Nobody said anything.

"So it isn't *hold back*," Karis said. "It never was. It's *land on the number*. The plate doesn't move at all, not by a graduation. Everything else moves exactly as far as a term would move it. That's far harder than holding back. It's also the actual job."

She wrote a line across the foot of the slate, under both brackets, in letters bigger than the rest: *ON THE NUMBER.*

---

The combined trial went the other way.

Brom threw from the wall, a straight sitting blow at whatever instant he liked, and Cael stood on the crack in the boards with his back half turned and had to feel it coming and be gone. That was as near as anything in the house came to a weight falling down a rail.

The first run was clean. Cael felt it arrive along the outsides of his arms, a pace and a half out, turned his shoulder out of its road, and was clear. Karis timed it on the wash-house glass she had borrowed and wrote the gap. She asked her question. He answered.

The second was clean.

The third was clean and quick and smooth as oil, and Cael felt it go right, and had begun to be pleased, when Karis said, "Stop."

"That was the best one."

"That was a five," said Karis.

For a moment the word meant nothing to him.

"We haven't a frame," she said. "Nobody's invented a scale for a punch." "I've a glass and my eyes and Brom. All three of them say that run was quicker than your first by a gap I can see without instruments. You went off at an angle I've never seen you use near anybody from the wing. If Brom were a weight on a rail, that wasn't a four. That was the heaviest thing they've got."

"Brom?"

Brom rubbed his fist. "You weren't reading me at the skin," he said slowly. "You were reading me further in. Like at the post, before the delegation came."

Cael gripped the top rail of the nearest chair.

He understood it about twenty seconds before he could say it, and the twenty seconds were the worst of the evening. Then he said it, because things of that kind were exactly what this room was for.

"It's open," he said. "The read. It's open all the way down. Since the delegation came I've kept it shallow. The hold and the reach drink from one cup, and the cup's small. The thin layer's cheap. It reads a room. It can't read a fist. This afternoon in the yard I took my hand off it, because nobody was looking at me and I wanted to see the two of you properly. The hold was never in danger; there was too much to watch. But the read came all the way up while I was watching, and it's been up ever since, and I didn't notice."

Karis had gone very still. "And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I hold from waking. The read drops to its stub. So that run can't happen." He was glad his voice stayed even. "Whatever I put on Gault's oak in the third trial, I'll be putting there with my best instrument turned nearly off. He'll write it down. It'll be honest. It'll be lower than what I can do."

"Inside the gap?"

He worked it through. When the sum came out, it was the best news of the hour.

"Weight four. Five clear in six. With the read on its stub." He let his breath go. "Inside. Right on the floor of it."

"Then that's where you put it," said Karis, and wrote it on the slate beside the third line, and said nothing comforting at all. It was a great deal better than comfort.

The fifth trial they did not try. Nothing in the house could stand in for the heaviest weight on a thin margin, and there was no baseline line to land on, because the baseline had never run it. Karis would not rehearse what she could not measure. She wrote that on the slate as well, so as not to pretend.

*5. No rehearsal. Nothing to land on. Do it honestly and let it fall where it falls.*

Underneath it she added a line, and read it out as she wrote.

*Hold going past thin: stop the trial. Say it aloud. An interval you ask for goes in the book as a line. A drift goes in as evidence.*

"You're allowed to stop?" said Brom. "In the middle of a thing?"

"Gault said it on the first morning. It's in my notebook." Cael found the words where he had kept them. "*When you reach something you cannot do, tell me. The book will record that you could not. Nothing more, and nothing held against you; a plain fact, written down. If I have to work out for myself that you've refused, it tells me less than if you had said so.*" He shrugged. "That's been folded up in my head since the first morning. I've never spent it."

---

In the second hour Karis began to run the gaps, and the gaps were where it came apart.

She ran them as a panel would. After each trial she turned half away with Brom, put her head close to his over the sheet, and talked low for a minute and a half while Cael stood on the crack in the floor and waited. Tomorrow there would be a dozen such pauses, perhaps more.

Partway through the second hour he felt himself slip.

It was not much. It was the feeling he had come to know since the stair: the hand on the rope sliding an inch without being told to, while his mind was looking somewhere else. He caught it and pulled it back, and was rather pleased that he had caught it.

"There," said Lira.

He looked round. Lira sat sideways in the good chair with the bad hip up on a cushion and a cloth of ice across it, and in the polished arm of the chair, under her right thumb, there was a short row of marks. She had made them with her thumbnail in the wax. He counted them from where he stood. Eleven.

"That's how many?" he said.

"That's how many I've seen tonight. You caught four of them. I watched your face when you did." She ran her thumb along the marks. "Nine of them were nothing much. Two or three seconds, and back. Nobody else in the world would have seen them. I'm not being clever; I've been looking at you for three years, and it's the only reason I can. But eleven."

"Where?"

"That's the thing." She lifted her thumb. "Not once while Brom was hitting you. Not once while Karis was asking you something. Every one of them was while she was turned away and you were standing on that crack waiting for her to finish. Every single one. In the gaps."

Karis was chalking *11/11* on the slate before Lira had finished. "Eleven of eleven," she said, half to herself. "That isn't luck. That's how it works."

Cael didn't move. Pieces were joining.

"Brom found it weeks ago," he said slowly. "In hall three. I wrote it down, but I wrote it the wrong way round. I thought the danger was being busy, all of me at once. It isn't. Busy is the safest thing there is. It never goes while I'm working. Not on a drill, not at the rail, not in four minutes of watching you two without blinking. It slips when the room wants nothing from me. When nobody's pulling, the hand slides."

"Then don't stand there with nothing being asked," said Lira.

Everybody turned to her.

"You keep treating it like a thing to grit your teeth at," she said. "You've got a whole page of ways to keep it still, and every one of them is a way of doing something, and the gap gives you nothing to do. So give yourself something. When Gault turns his back tomorrow, choose somebody in that room. Anybody. Then read them. The whole way down. How they breathe, where their weight sits, what their hands do, whether it all agrees. One person. And when the panel turns back, don't stop dead. Let it drop underneath and keep running."

"The read's going to be on its stub. I won't have—"

"I don't mean the read." Her voice sharpened. "I mean *you*. The gaze. The notebook. The watching. The habit you made yourself, back when there wasn't a single Path in you. That isn't a fragment, Cael. Nobody gave you that. It's the one thing in your whole ledger nobody else had first. It costs the hold nothing. And it'll keep you out at your own edges for all three hours. That's the whole trouble."

The room was quiet.

Cael thought of a line in the working ledger from the first week after the stair. The hiding half came unasked, it said, and went out like a pinched wick the moment he truly looked. He had filed that as the fragment's flaw. It was a cure. The gaze and the hiding could not run in him at the same time. Whichever he ran, the other stopped.

Karis said, "Yes. That works," and looked faintly annoyed with herself.

Lira shrugged with one shoulder. "I've had nothing else to do but look."

Her eyes stayed on him. Her voice dropped. This, he understood, was the reason for the twenty steps.

"There's another half to it. You won't like it as much." She shifted the ice. "Tomorrow's about being measured right, and everything tonight's been about that, and I've helped with it, and it's right. But you're a fighter. When they put the instruments on you, there has to be a you under them. Clamp it all down, give Gault a careful boy sitting neat on Karis's floor, and yes, you'll pass. And you'll come out a little bit less. Then you'll do it next half-year, and the half-year after, and every time a little less."

"You don't know that."

"I know a clerk at Fenmark who never looked up from his page." She said it without any heat. "He didn't mean me harm. He meant to be tidy. That's how they do it, mostly. They tidy you." She held his eyes. "So be the fighter. In a measured room. Both."

---

By the first hour after the last bell Brom had gone up, one tread at a time, with the ice in his hand. Lira was asleep sideways in the good chair with her cheek on the wing of it, which she would deny in the morning. Karis was kneeling at the stove with the poker, banking it down for the night, and the slate on the mantel still had the six lines and the two brackets and *ON THE NUMBER* across the foot.

Cael opened the grey folder then. He wanted Gault's marks on his coursework; they were wing marks, and might belong on Karis's slate.

The three exercises were inside, in the driest hand he owned, every word true and none of them complete. Each had a tick by its title and two figures in the margin, and the tab bore an initial. The wing marked everything so.

Inside the back board lay something he had not handed in. A slip of paper.

It was wing paper, cut small. The writing was the wing's trained hand, the one every clerk in the office was taught until it belonged to nobody. It was unsigned and undated. To anybody who had not already been turning the same question over, it would have meant nothing at all.

*Panels see what they expect prepared for. Show growth where the baseline predicts it. Surprise them exactly once, small, early — a watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.*

His breath stopped for a moment on the first reading. He read it again.

Then he learned it. It was short and plainly meant to be learned, and it took him about a minute. The back was blank. He walked to the stove, where Karis knelt with the poker, and said, "Excuse me," and she moved her hand. He opened the little door and laid the slip flat on the red coals.

It went brown at one corner, then black across the middle, and then it was gone. Four seconds, perhaps. The ash it left could not be told from the rest.

Crouched by the stove, he kept his palm on the warm iron door a while.

Whoever had written it had taken a trade it must have cost years to learn, cut a corner off it, and put it in a boy's coursework, knowing what it would mean for him if it were ever found. What could he give back for that? Only this: that the paper should be gone within a minute of being read. It was the whole of what he had.

Beside him Karis had not moved. She was looking at the stove door, with the poker across her knees.

"Was that—" she began.

She stopped. He watched it happen. The question came up into her face the way questions always did, quick and bright and certain of its right to an answer. And then something else came down over it and covered it, quietly, the way you lay a cloth over a cage.

"No," she said. "Never mind."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She gave the coals one more push and hung the poker on its hook. "Is it any good?"

"Nobody's told me anything truer about tomorrow. I'd have paid a year for it."

"Then spend it," said Karis, and went back to her fair copy at the far end of the table, and neither of them ever spoke of it again.

---

*Show growth where the baseline predicts it.* That was Karis's gap between the brackets, arrived at by another road entirely. That was what first made him trust it. Karis and whoever had cut the slip had never exchanged a word on the subject, and they had built the same rule out of opposite materials.

*Surprise them exactly once, small, early.* That was the piece missing from everything, and he could never have made it himself. He sat at the table with a clean sheet for most of twenty minutes before he saw it.

It had to be on the record already, or it was not a surprise but a confession. It had to be small. And it had to be something nobody at Halcenvane had ever seen.

Only one thing he owned met all three. The Ember. Karis's ignition point, which sat in a single thin half-page at the back of the Greyvane transcript, where anybody who paid six marks could read it and nobody ever had. No instrument in the wing had measured it. No panel on the bluff had seen it. On paper it was the least remarkable thing he owned: a point of fire at contact, gone the instant it was made.

The wing put its instruments on whatever an enrollee laid before it. The placing was his.

He looked down the table.

"Karis."

She lifted her pen.

"The Ember stays off the floor. That's what we agreed when term began. It was yours before it was mine." He kept his voice down, for the sleeper in the good chair. "I want to put it on Gault's oak tomorrow. Once, second in the order. I'm asking you."

Karis looked at him over the lamp. He saw her not ask why.

"Yes," she said. "Second. And show it properly. I've never seen anybody measure it." She went back to her copy. "Then come and tell me what it did."

In a fair hand he wrote out the new order twice. Nothing else went into the stove. Then he went up to his window.

---

*Ledger, day twenty-one.*

*Held: from waking, nineteen hours. Read: let up in the yard, on purpose, and never put back. Found out at the table. Drifts: eleven by Lira's thumbnail, four by my own count. All eleven in the gaps.*

*Given tonight: a gap between two brackets, with a registrar's sums under it. A way to look like a man working instead of a man carrying a full bowl. A thing to do with the empty minutes that costs nothing, because it's the only part of me nobody handed me. And something else, which is where it belongs, and is ash.*

*Nobody gave me a present tonight. Everybody at that table gave me a piece of tomorrow. I think that's what it is to have people in it with you, rather than only people who love you. I've got both now. I didn't, two years ago.*

The lamp went down a notch. Then the rest.

*Quenna asked me, at Greyvane, whether I could keep from using it with people watching who'd be glad to see me hurt. I told her I didn't know. Tomorrow I find out. Three hours of it, six trials, and through all of it one thing kept at nothing that would love to be let off its leash, in the most watched room I'll ever stand in.*

*Only I'm not sure the man watching would be glad to see me hurt. A week of him. He asked Gault three things, all about when the posts were last checked. In nine hours at the records table his pen moved five times, and every time it was about this house's paper, not about me. A final he sat through with his book shut, and one line at the end of it. Nothing in that is hoping I fall over. All of it wants the figure to come out true.*

*He doesn't wish me harm. He wishes me* measured.

*I've tried all evening to work out which of those is worse, and I can't. Perhaps I'll know by noon.*

*Sleep. Fight in the morning.*

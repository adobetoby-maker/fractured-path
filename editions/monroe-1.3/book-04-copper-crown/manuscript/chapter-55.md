# Chapter 55 — Renewed

The Mire instructor racked the fourth weight with her own hands and tested the pawl with her thumb, twice, listening to it.

Then she cranked the rail to a height nobody named aloud, and went back to her chair, and put her hands in her coat pockets again. The clerk drew a fresh square in chalk on the oak beneath the rail and stepped back to his desk. Nobody told Cael which rack the weight hung from. That was the point.

"Third trial," said Gault. "Feel it come, call where from, and be out of the square before it lands. Twelve."

The read was on its stub, as he had told Karis it would be. All morning he had kept it at its thinnest layer, the one that cost nothing, because the hold and the reach drank from one cup and the cup was small. At its thinnest the read would still find a falling weight a pace and a half out. It would not find it early, or clearly, or give him anything to spare.

He stood in the chalk with his back to the rail.

The pawl let go.

It came to him late and thin, like a voice heard through a wall: a pressure gathering on the outside of his left arm, high, and then the whole weight of it arriving there at once.

"High, near," he said, and was already moving. His hips went, the held breath went, and the square was empty when the weight hit the oak behind him with a flat heavy knock.

"High, near," the clerk agreed. "Clear."

He did twelve. He called each one aloud as it came and was gone before it finished arriving. The fourth he let cost him; he came out of it blowing hard and stood with his hands on his knees for a moment, and the Mire instructor watched him do it. The seventh came so thin he nearly did not feel it at all, and he called it a beat late and went a beat late, and still cleared it by the width of a boot.

The tenth he lost. The press arrived smeared, a little early and then a little more, and he had two answers and chose the wrong one. He went right when he should have gone back, and the weight came down on the oak a finger's breadth behind his heel. The knock went up through his boot.

"Low, far," said the clerk. "Called low, near. Struck."

"That's mine, Magister," said Cael. "The frame's fine. I chose."

Gault wrote. The other two came clean.

"Eleven of twelve, at the fourth weight," said Gault. "At the baseline, seven of eight, at the third." He looked at the two columns side by side for a while. "Better. Not much better. As a term should be."

Cael stood in the chalk with his chest going and felt something he had no column for, which was relief so plain it was almost funny. *On the number.* Weight four, clear eleven times in twelve, with his best instrument turned down to nearly nothing. It was inside Karis's brackets. It was sitting on the floor of them, as near as made no difference, where it was supposed to sit.

In the gap that came after, while the panel conferred, he chose the Mire instructor.

She was easier than the clerk and more interesting. Rhythm: quick and impatient, a woman who did a thing and was finished. Weight: forward, always forward, her shoulders inside her coat leaning toward the frame like a dog's toward a door it does not trust. Breath: short. Hands: back in the pockets, but the right one moving inside the cloth, working something over and over. A coin, perhaps, or a key. Whether it agreed: it did. All of her was pointed at her apparatus, and it was the apparatus she was worried about, not him. She had not forgotten a sticking notch from the first month.

The corner held the whole time. He hardly had to think about it.

---

The fourth trial was the one where nothing was allowed to happen.

The Mire instructor racked the fifth weight, the heaviest. The clerk rubbed out the chalk square and drew it again, one pace from the rail. Then he fetched the plate on its brace and handed it over.

"You can't get out of this," said Gault. "Take it."

Feryn's fragment stirred in his right arm, and reached.

He had carried it for three years, since a bout he had lost honestly, and it had always lived close under the skin. It did not keep a blow; it gathered one, and turned it, and sent it somewhere else. It was made for exactly the thing in his hands. If anybody had been marking answers right and wrong, it was the right answer, and the little dial in the plate's housing would have swung round to a figure no one in this room had ever seen it show.

It was not on this wing's record.

It was written down elsewhere. Vell's copy of the Ardenmere circuit record had the bouts where he had used it in public, years ago, before he knew enough to hold it back. But Gault's book did not have it. Gault's book had a plate held low, four times, with the shoulder taking all of it, and a boy saying so before he was asked. To bring the other thing out now would be a second surprise, on the third instrument, in a place the baseline had never pointed at. A slip of wing paper had told him how many surprises a sitting could afford.

So he let the reaching go, as he had let it go on more floors than he could count, and his arm was only his arm.

He took the weight the plain way. Feet wide; the plate held out on an arm loaded from the shoulder; everything driven down through the legs into the oak. The pawl let go and the weight came down the rail and met the plate, and the shock went up the bones of his forearm and stopped in the shoulder joint, all of it, like a nail into a beam. Behind the plate the spring hit the end of its travel with a dull clack.

"Held," said the clerk. "Low."

Four times. By the fourth his right arm had no feeling from the wrist to halfway up to the elbow, and a hard bright ache had settled deep in the shoulder exactly where it had settled in the first month. It would stay there until the week's end, as it had then.

"Your account," said Gault.

"Four, Magister. The same as in my first month. A fifth would be the shoulder's business, not mine." He opened and shut his right hand where the whole row could see it, and shook the arm out from the shoulder, and let it hang. "It doesn't go anywhere. The joint keeps it."

Every word of it was true. All the words together were a coat cut to look like the whole man, with a good deal of the man left at home, and he knew it while he spoke. He kept his face still.

Gault laid two sheets side by side: the first month's plate line and this morning's. He looked from one to the other for some time. Line for line the two sheets matched. Not a graduation lay between them.

"Fourth trial. No change from the baseline," he said, for the book. "Held low at both sittings. Four at both sittings. Nothing in the method moved."

He put his pen down, which he did not usually do between trials.

"I'll say the useful thing about that before somebody else in this building says the other one," he said, to the room at large. "A man who comes in after a single term better at every instrument I own is either a wonder, or a man whose evaluator ought to go and check his instruments. I check mine on Fourth-days, and they were checked yesterday. This wing measures six things. Five of them have moved. One hasn't. That," said Gault, "is what a real semester looks like."

He picked the pen up again.

"Anything to add, Enrollee?"

In his right arm sat a thing that could have met that weight twice as often and handed half of every blow on to the oak. He had taken it from a fighter who had beaten him fairly, and it was written down in a circuit record far from here, and not in this one. The man across the table had just defended him for something that was not quite true. Twice now in one term Cael had given him something true with a piece cut out of it, in his own room, for his own book.

"No, Magister."

*The same bill as last time*, he thought. *And I still haven't a column to put it in.*

But the line had not dropped. That was the thing Karis had found on the hearthrug, and it was the thing that mattered. He had not gone under the floor. He had landed on the number, to the graduation, and no one could lay the two sheets together and find a boy who had got worse.

---

The fifth trial was new, and so it was the one he had feared most, and he had feared the wrong part of it.

"The heaviest weight," said Gault. "The square brought in. Leave before it lands."

In the first month this weight had been too near and too heavy to escape, and they had made him stand under it with the plate. Now the clerk chalked a square only a pace and a half out from the rail. Along its landing side he laid the long shallow tray of chalk dust and drew a ruler across it to smooth it flat. The wing read that tray standing, at once, before the enrollee stepped out of it, so that nobody's memory could tidy up what the dust said. So the clerk took his place at the tray's far end with the ledger open along his forearm, near enough to read a heel.

The Mire instructor tried the release before she racked the weight. She did it four times, slowly, with her head turned to listen, as she had in the first month when she had found the second notch catching. It let go clean four times. She grunted, hung the fifth weight, and went back to her chair.

The drum went round.

Afterward Cael could put the next second in order, a piece at a time, and he did, that night, on paper. There were four pieces.

The first was a sound. The pawl went, and under it, a hair before it should have, came another sound: a small dry catch and drag, like a key that will not quite turn in a cold lock, and then a release. The second notch. It had caught on the way through, and held, and then let go all at once, high, off the rack above the one the clerk had set.

The second was his forearms. Even on its stub the read still did the one thing it had ever done. It put weight and direction on his skin a fraction before they arrived anywhere else. What it put there now was all wrong. Too early. Too heavy. Too much of it, from too high, into a square that had no room to spare in it.

The third was the answer. It was already in his legs. He had given it eleven times that morning and it had been clean every time: the burst, out and sideways and gone. His hips had begun to drop for it before he had thought anything at all.

The fourth was a pair of shoes.

They were the clerk's, brown, at the far end of the chalk tray, a pace and a half from where a burst would set him down. And a burst did not set you down anywhere you could steer. It ended in the landing beat, half a breath of being bolted to the spot. Whatever he came down on, he would come down on, and stand there, unable to turn aside, until the beat let go of him.

He did not finish the burst.

He had begun it; he could not have helped that. The hips had gone and the held breath had started, and the door the Wind came through was open. He shut it. He shut it in the middle, with the breath half paid, which was a thing he had tried and failed to do in every practice hour for three years. He did not do it well now. He went out of the square perhaps a quarter of the way he had gone all morning, crabwise, short of the tray. The rest of the burst he simply refused. All the force that should have carried him out across the oak went down through his leading hip instead, at once, from the crest of the bone to the inside of the knee, and stopped there.

Behind his shoulder the weight struck the boards. The whole floor jumped under his feet.

Then the room made the noise rooms make, which is mostly chairs.

The Mire instructor reached the frame while it was still ringing. She took hold of the release and worked it four times with her own hands, hard. On the second pull it caught and dragged. On the fourth it went straight through without catching at all. She stepped back from it. Her face had gone white, and her voice, when it came, was perfectly level.

"The second notch has failed," she said. "It isn't sticking. It's gone. It let go early, off the upper rack. Put that in the book in those words, please, and no others."

"As she said it," said Gault. "The fifth trial is void. Strike the readings, clerk."

"Struck."

Gault had not moved from his chair. His hands lay flat on his papers.

"Enrollee. Are you hurt?"

Cael's hip had just paid the day's bill and the next day's too, all in one coin. By evening it would show whatever he claimed. He told the truth.

"Not injured, Magister. The hip took what the floor should have had. It'll tell me about it for three days."

"Because you didn't complete the burst."

"Yes, Magister."

"Why didn't you?"

He had spent a whole term arranging his life so that he could answer a question like that one honestly when it came. Here it was, asked as mildly as Gault asked for a figure, with the Compact's four chairs along the wall and Havel's pen moving on every syllable.

"Because the clerk was standing where I'd have landed."

Nobody spoke.

At the far end of the tray the clerk raised his head from the ledger. He looked at the smooth dust, at the place a heel would have come down. He looked at his own brown shoes, which stood just beyond it. Then he lowered his head very carefully and looked at his page.

Gault wrote for some while before he spoke.

"For the book. The frame failed at its second notch. It let go early, and from the rack above. Trial five, void. The enrollee broke off a burst already begun and took the load in his own body, rather than come down on ground with a man on it. No one told him to. No injury." He put the pen down. "This frame is not to be used in my wing again until it has been taken apart on a bench. Set the reserve. We'll run the fifth when it stands."

---

The porters needed eleven minutes to fetch the reserve frame from the far store and bolt it up.

Cael spent them on the rail by the west wall with his palms on the wood and his weight off the bad leg. The hip was past aching. It was simply loud. The pressure behind his eyes from the hour-and-a-half mark had come all the way in and taken a chair.

And he had gone thin.

He had not felt it go. He felt it only on the way back, with a lurch like a missed stair. For two or three breaths after the weight hit the oak, while the frame rang and chairs scraped and the Mire instructor tore at the release, his corner had simply not been there. Every eye in the room had been on the frame. Nobody had been looking at the boy at all.

That was luck. He knew it, and he would write it down as luck. He had no column for luck and did not intend to start one.

He kept his face off the east wall. But the oldest instrument he owned had started counting without being asked, the one he had built in the first weeks of term on the stairs of this very building, a tally of glances. It counted now.

Everybody had looked at the frame first. That was only natural.

Then everybody had looked back at him.

Gault: twice, short and level. He had a line to write and needed to see what he was writing about. The Mire instructor: four times, her eyes going from the boy to the frame and back each time, like a woman adding a column. The Ash instructor: twice, over the top of her book. The counsel: three times, and long, the way counsel look at an incident, to fix it before it moves. Havel: twice, the second time quicker than the first. Ilsev: to the tray of dust, to the clerk's shoes, to Cael, and to the dust again.

The fourth chair: once. At the frame, with everybody else.

After that, nothing. The man in grey had gone on looking at the room at his own steady pace, the grid and the wall and the window, as he had looked at it for two hours. His weight had not shifted in the chair. His hands had not moved on his thighs. His eyes did not come back to the rail. Not once, in all eleven minutes.

Cael sat on the rail with his hip roaring and understood it. It was the coldest understanding he had come to all year.

You look twice at a thing you didn't expect. That isn't politeness, or temper, or training. It's what eyes are for. The first look tells you that something has happened, and the second tells you what. Seven people in this room had needed the second look.

Not the fourth chair. Whatever had happened on the oak, he had taken all of it in the first look, at speed, and weighed it, and put it away. There was nothing more in this room he could get by looking at Cael again.

At the end of the row, while the porters were still tightening the reserve frame's last bolt, the man reached into his coat and took out the small notebook from the north tier. He opened it on his knee and wrote something short. The book shut and vanished into the grey coat.

Cael's tally gave it a mark of its own. *One.*

---

On the reserve frame they ran the fifth trial nine times, and Cael cleared eight.

The first six were sound. The seventh came a shade late off the leading leg, because the leading leg was the one that had paid. The eighth was later still, but it cleared. On the ninth the weight struck the edge of the chalk square while his back heel was still lifting out of it.

He spoke before anybody could ask him.

"The last two are the hip, Magister. The read gave me them in time. The leg didn't take them in time."

Gault looked at the tray, where the last two heel-prints lay a little deeper in the dust, and a little shorter. "The dust agrees," he said. "Logged."

The sixth trial was the cold run: the board, and the read off the reserve frame, at the end of the morning, to find what three hours had cost. The board came back a palm short of his best, with every landing beat a hair longer, and he did not hide the hip in any of them. Even so, it gave up less than the cold runs had given up in the first month. The read called twelve of twelve. It had never lost a thing in its life. Of all he owned it cost least and spoke softest, which was both its virtue and its fence.

Then Gault bent over his papers.

The pendulum ticked. The funnel dripped into its cup. Eight people waited, and no one spoke, and Cael stood on the brass with nothing in the world left to do but keep his corner where it was. It was four and a half minutes by the clerk's count.

Gault lifted the top sheet. The charter wanted the note read where the enrollee could hear it.

"Semester evaluation of the assay-provision enrollee. Measured against his own baseline, drawn here in his first month.

"Growth against baseline: confirmed across all demonstrated capabilities. Ceiling on the board raised from four to six, which the enrollee gave before the board gave it, now as then. The combined trial bettered, at the heavier weight. The ignition point shown and met to this wing's standard, against a thin earlier exhibit. One measure unchanged, the plate, and so entered. Trial five void for a fault in the apparatus, and run again on the reserve.

"Consistency: within tolerance at every trial.

"Assay-provision requirement: satisfied.

"Enrollment standing: renewed."

The Ash instructor signed. The Mire instructor signed, and then wrote four lines more beneath her name about the second notch and read them out in a voice which Cael thought would, before the month was out, cost some supplier a contract. The clerk pressed the stamp down.

Dull. Exact. Stamped.

He stood in the middle of the oak with a hip shouting and a shoulder aching and a head full of hot sand, in front of the most senior eyes the Compact had ever sent anywhere near him, and heard a house say the plainest sentence it had about him, for the second time in a term.

*Enrollment standing: renewed.*

No sentence anybody had said of him was odder. None was duller. Both were still true. It had only been said, this time, with every instrument the system owned pointed at the saying.

The east wall emptied. The counsel's chair scraped first, and she went out tying her ribbons and already talking. Havel tucked his board under his arm, with the log sheet squared on the file beneath it, and followed her with his eyes on the floor. Ilsev picked up her case and stopped for a breath or two beside the copper pot, where its thread of colour had sunk back to the first line, before she went.

The fourth chair emptied last.

The man rose out of it without using the arms. Then, standing, he took the notebook out of his coat a second time and opened it, and his pen moved once, a short stroke. From the rail, where Cael had gone back to lean, it was impossible to tell whether the stroke began a line or drew through one.

*Two*, Cael's tally said.

The man put it away. His eyes made one slow circuit of the room, grid and wall and copper and frame, with the patience he had once spent on empty tiers. Then the far door closed behind him. He had not looked at Cael.

At his desk the clerk began to wind the pendulum down.

---

Lira was asleep on the stick against the gatepost, exactly as she had threatened.

Cael woke her gently, as instructed, with one finger on her sleeve. She opened her eyes at once and looked at his face, and then at the way he was standing, with his weight off the leading leg, and she did not ask about the leg.

"Well?"

"Renewed."

She shut her eyes again, briefly. "Say all of it."

"Growth confirmed in five. Within tolerance on every trial. The requirement's met. Standing renewed." He leaned on the post beside her, because the hip asked him to. "One frame broke. One trial void. I asked for an interval at an hour and a half and they wrote it down."

"Say it if it goes," said Lira.

"I said it."

She nodded once, as if a sum had come out, and pushed herself off the post onto the stick.

They went back across the quadrangle together at the slowest pace the bluff had ever seen, two people with one good leg between them, and took the residence stair one tread at a time. Brom was waiting on the landing with a bag of ice already in his strapped hand. He held it out to Cael without a word.

Karis was in the common room with a book open on her knee at the same page it had been open at since the second bell. She shut it.

"Well?"

He told her. At *renewed* her eyes closed, briefly. From Karis that was as good as a shout.

"And the column?" she said, opening them. "You said you'd tell me."

So he told her about the copper pot, and the glass straw, and the four moments. Then he told her the word he had found for it, kneeling on the oak with his palm on the copper.

For a time Karis was silent. Then she opened the grey notebook to a page near the back, where two words already stood near the top in her hand, one under the other, *a spark* and *a letting-go*. Under them she wrote the third. She looked at it for a long time, and then she drew a short line under all three, and closed the book.

"That's the last one," she said. "I can feel that it is."

A boy from the registrar's office came up the stair before supper with a half-sheet for Cael. It was in Bracken's small upright hand.

*Records hall, the counter, at the first bell tomorrow. Bring nothing. — B.*

---

*Ledger, day twenty-two. The twentieth of Reaping.*

*Held: from the fourth hour. About six hours all told, three of them on the oak.*

*Drifts: two. (a) At the hour and a half, for two seconds or less, inside an honest answer. I went all the way down after a word and the corner slid while I was down there. New fact: answering truly costs the corner. (b) After the notch went, for two or three breaths, while every eye was on the frame. Luck. Not skill. Entered as luck.*

*Interval: one, asked for aloud. In the book as "fatigue affecting response quality." The dullest line ever written about me, and the only proof I'll ever have that I was minding something and not hiding it.*

*Paper says: five up, one level, every trial inside tolerance. Renewed. All of it true. It's the first true paper about me I've ever carried out of a room without first having to fight somebody for it. I'm not used to that yet.*

*Q.'s question, from Greyvane: could I keep it in, with people watching who'd be glad to see me hurt.*

*Answer, on the twentieth of Reaping: yes.*

*Thirty-five days since the stair, running from the moment I wake. Not a flicker in three hours, under the Compact's four best chairs. Cost: one interval, one headache. The Pressure stayed in my arm. Gault wrote "no change" about the plate and then stood up for me over it. I didn't earn that, and I can't put it right.*

*I've been keeping things back since before I had a word for it. Today it went under instruments for three hours, and it held. I'm going to stop calling it a habit.*

*The quarter-burst. Gain: no clerk under my feet; nothing off the record. Bill: one hip, in full. Three days, likely more. I shut a burst halfway through paying for it. I have never once managed that on purpose since my Kindling. I can't say how I did it, so I can't say I can do it again. One point isn't a line. Karis taught me that.*

*He didn't look twice.*

*Seven people needed a second look at me. He needed one. He had all of it the first time, weighed it in his chair, and had no more use for the rail.*

*Net: some rooms have held people who meant me harm. More have held people who meant only to enter me in a book and close it. This was the first room where somebody was done with me before I'd got off the rail. I don't know yet whether that's the safest thing that's ever happened to me, or the other thing.*

*Two lines in his book. I'll be a long time wondering.*

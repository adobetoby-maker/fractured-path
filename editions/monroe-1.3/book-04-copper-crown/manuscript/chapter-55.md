# Chapter 55 — Renewed

The Mire instructor racked the fourth weight with her own hands and tried the pawl twice with her thumb, listening to it, before she cranked the rail to a height nobody named aloud and went back to her chair and buried her hands in her coat pockets again. The clerk drew a fresh square in chalk on the oak beneath the rail and returned to his desk. Nobody told Cael which rack the weight hung from, which was the point.

"Third trial," said Gault. "Feel it come, call where from, and be out of the square before it lands. Twelve."

The read was on its stub, as he had told Karis it would be. All morning he had kept it at the thinnest layer, the one that cost nothing, because the hold and the reach drank from one cup and the cup was small. At its thinnest it would still find a falling weight a pace and a half out, but it would not find it early or clearly or leave him anything to spare.

He stood in the chalk with his back to the rail, and the pawl let go.

It came to him late and thin, like a voice through a wall: a pressure gathering high on the outside of his left arm, and then the whole weight of it arriving there at once.

"High, near," he said, and was already moving. His hips went and the held breath went, and the square was empty when the weight struck the oak behind him with a flat heavy knock.

"High, near," the clerk agreed. "Clear."

He did twelve, calling each one aloud as it came and leaving before it had finished arriving. The fourth he let cost him; he came out of it blowing and stood with his hands on his knees while the Mire instructor watched him do it. The seventh came so thin that he nearly missed it altogether, and he called it a beat late and went a beat late and still cleared it by the width of a boot.

The tenth he lost. The press reached him smeared, a little early and then a little more, so that he had two answers and picked the wrong one. He went right when he should have gone back, and the weight came down a finger's breadth behind his heel with a knock that went up through his boot.

"Low, far," said the clerk. "Called low, near. Not clear."

"That one's mine, Magister," said Cael. "The frame's sound. I chose wrong."

Gault wrote, and the last two came clean.

"Eleven of twelve at the fourth weight," said Gault, "against seven of eight at the third in your first month." For a while his eyes went between the two columns. "Better. Not much better. As a term should be."

Chest heaving in the chalk, Cael felt a thing his ledger had no column for: relief, so plain it was nearly comic. *On the number.* Weight four, clear eleven times in twelve, with his best instrument turned down to almost nothing: it was inside Karis's brackets, a call above the floor of them, which was exactly where a term should put it.

In the gap after, while the panel conferred, he chose the Mire instructor, who was easier than the clerk and more interesting. Rhythm: quick and impatient, the rhythm of a woman who did a thing and was done with it. Weight: forward, always forward, the shoulders inside her coat leaning toward the frame like a dog's toward a door it doesn't trust. Breath: short. Hands: in the pockets again, but the right one moving inside the cloth, working something over and over, a coin perhaps, or a key. Did it agree? It did. All of her was pointed at her apparatus, and it was the apparatus that worried her, not the boy; she had not forgotten a sticking notch from the first month. Throughout, the corner kept its place with hardly a thought from him.

Then came the trial in which nothing at all was allowed to happen.

The heaviest weight, the fifth, went onto the rack under the Mire instructor's hands. The clerk rubbed out the chalk square and drew it again one pace from the rail, then fetched the plate on its brace and handed it over.

"No leaving this one," said Gault. "Take it."

Feryn's fragment stirred in his right arm, and reached.

He had carried it for three years, ever since a bout he had lost honestly, and it had always lived close under his skin. It did not keep a blow. It gathered a blow, and turned it, and sent it on somewhere else. It had been made for exactly the thing in his hands. If anybody had been marking answers right and wrong, it was the right one, and the little dial in the plate's housing would have swung round to a figure that nobody in the room had ever seen it show.

It was not on this wing's record.

It was written down elsewhere. Vell's copy of the Ardenmere circuit record held the bouts in which he had used it in public, years ago, before he knew enough to keep it back. But Gault's book had no line for it. Gault's book had a plate held low four times, with the shoulder taking everything, and a boy saying so before anybody asked. To bring the other thing out now would be a second surprise, on a third instrument, in a place the baseline had never pointed at, and a slip of wing paper had told him exactly how many surprises a sitting could afford.

So he let the reaching go, as he had let it go on more floors than he could count, and his arm was only his arm.

His brace was the plain one: wide feet, the plate out on an arm loaded from the shoulder, and everything sent down through the legs. The pawl let go and the weight came down the rail into the plate, and the shock ran up the bones of his forearm and stopped in the shoulder, all of it, like a nail going into a beam. Behind the plate the spring hit the end of its travel with a dull clack.

"Held," said the clerk. "Low."

Four times. After the fourth his right forearm was numb as far as its middle. An ache like a struck bell had lodged deep in the shoulder, in the very spot the first month had chosen, and it would keep him company until the week's end as it had then.

"Your account," said Gault.

"Four, Magister, as in my first month. A fifth would be the shoulder's business, not mine." He opened and shut the hand where the whole row could see it and shook the arm out from the shoulder and let it hang. "It doesn't go anywhere. The joint keeps it."

Every word was true, and all the words together made a coat cut to look like the whole man, with a good deal of the man left at home, and he knew it while he spoke and kept his face still.

Two sheets went down side by side under Gault's hands, the first month's plate and this morning's, and his eyes went from one to the other. Line for line they matched, with not a graduation between them.

"Fourth trial," he said for the book. "Unchanged since the baseline. Low both times. Four both times. The method as it was."

The pen went down on the table, which between trials it seldom did.

"I'll say the useful thing about that before somebody else in this building says the other," he said, to the room at large. "A man who comes back after a single term better at every instrument I own is either a wonder or a man whose evaluator should go and look at his instruments, and I look at mine on Fourth-days, and they were looked at yesterday. So far this morning I've measured four things, and one of them hasn't moved. That is what a real semester looks like," said Gault.

He picked the pen up again. "Anything to add, Enrollee?"

Meanwhile his right arm held a thing that could have met the weight twice as often and handed half of every blow to the oak. He had taken it from a fighter who had beaten him fairly, and it was written down in a circuit record far from here and not in this one, and the man across the table had just stood up for him over something that was not quite true. Twice now in one term Cael had given him something true with a piece cut out of it, in his own room, for his own book.

"No, Magister."

*The same bill as last time*, he thought, *and I still haven't a column to put it in.*

But the line had not dropped. That was what Karis had found on the hearthrug, and it was what mattered. He had not gone through the floor; he had landed on the number to the graduation, and nobody could lay the two sheets together and find a boy who had got worse.

---

The fifth trial was new, so it was the one he had feared most, and he had feared the wrong part of it.

"The heaviest weight," said Gault. "The square brought in. Leave before it lands."

In the first month this weight had been too near and too heavy to escape, and they had made him stand under it with the plate. Now the clerk chalked a square only a pace and a half out from the rail, and along its landing side he laid the long shallow tray of chalk dust and drew a ruler across it to smooth it flat. The wing read that tray standing, at once, before the enrollee had stepped out of it, so that nobody's memory could tidy up what the dust said, and so the clerk took his place at the tray's far end with his ledger open along his forearm, near enough to read a heel.

Before she hung the weight, the Mire instructor tested the release. She worked it four times, slowly, with her head turned to listen, as she had in the first month when she had found the second notch catching, and four times it let go clean; she grunted, hung the fifth weight, and went back to her chair.

The drum went round.

Afterward Cael could put the next second in order, a piece at a time, and that night he did, on paper. There were four pieces, and the order of them turned out to matter.

The first was his forearms. The weight was already moving before anything in the room had made a sound. Even on its stub the read still did the one thing it had ever done, which was to lay weight and direction on his skin a fraction before they reached anything else, and what it laid there now was all wrong: too early, too heavy, too much of it from too high, into a square with no room to spare.

The second was the answer, which was already in his legs. He had given it a score of times that morning and it had been clean every time, the burst out and sideways and gone, and his hips had begun to drop for it before he had thought anything at all.

The third was a pair of shoes. They were the clerk's, brown, at the far end of the chalk tray, a pace and a half from where a burst would set him down; and a burst did not set you down anywhere you could steer. It ended in the landing beat, half a breath of being bolted to the spot, so that whatever he came down on he would come down on, and stay on, unable to turn aside, until the beat let go of him.

He did not finish the burst.

He had begun it, because he could not have helped beginning it: the hips had gone and the held breath had started and the door the Wind came through was already open. He shut it. Halfway, with the breath half paid, he shut it. Three years of practice hours had never once let him do that, and he did it badly now. He went out of the square perhaps a quarter of the way he had gone all morning, crabwise, short of the tray, and the rest of the burst he simply refused. All the force that should have carried him across the oak went down through his leading hip instead, at once, from the crest of the bone to the inside of the knee, and stopped there.

Only then came the fourth piece, which was a sound. It reached him after he had already shut the door: a small dry catch and drag, like a key that will not quite turn in a cold lock, and then the late clack of a release. The second notch had snagged on the way through and given all at once, from a rack higher than the clerk had chosen, and it had let the weight go before it made its proper noise.

Behind his shoulder the weight struck the boards, and the whole floor jumped under his feet.

Chairs scraped everywhere; most of the noise a room makes is chairs.

While the frame still rang, the Mire instructor had her hands on it, wrenching the release four times. On the second pull it caught and dragged, and on the fourth it went straight through without catching at all. She stepped back from it with her face gone white, and when her voice came it was perfectly level.

"That second notch hasn't stuck. It's failed outright. Early, and off the upper rack. I want those words in the book, please, and only those."

"As she said it," said Gault. "The fifth trial is void. Strike the readings, clerk."

"Struck."

Gault sat on with his palms flat on his papers.

"Enrollee. Are you hurt?"

His hip had settled two days' accounts in one coin. Anything he claimed would be contradicted by evening, so Cael told the truth.

"Not injured, Magister. What the floor was owed, the hip paid instead. I'll hear about it for three days."

"Because you didn't complete the burst."

"Yes, Magister."

"Why didn't you?"

He had spent a whole term arranging his life so that he could answer a question like that one honestly when it came, and here it was, asked as mildly as Gault asked for a figure, with the Compact's four chairs along the wall and Havel's pen moving on every syllable.

"Because the clerk was standing where I'd have landed."

Nobody spoke.

At the far end of the tray the clerk lifted his head from the ledger and looked at the smooth dust, at the place where a heel would have come down, and then at his own brown shoes, which stood just beyond it. Then he lowered his head very carefully and looked at his page.

Gault wrote for some while before he spoke.

"For the book. Frame fault at the second notch, releasing early and high. Trial five, void. The enrollee abandoned a burst in progress and bore the load himself, rather than land where a man was standing. No one told him to. No injury." He put the pen down. "Nobody uses this frame in my wing until it's been in pieces on a bench. Set the reserve, and we'll run the fifth when it stands."

---

The porters needed eleven minutes to fetch the reserve frame from the far store and bolt it up, and Cael spent them on the rail by the west wall with his palms on the wood and his weight off the bad leg. The hip had gone past aching into simple noise, and the pressure behind his eyes from the hour-and-a-half mark had come all the way in and taken a chair.

And he had gone thin. He had not felt it go, only come back, with a lurch like a missed stair. For two or three breaths after the weight hit the oak, while the frame rang and the chairs scraped and the Mire instructor tore at the release, his corner had simply not been there. Every face had been turned to the frame; nobody had spared the boy a glance. That was luck, and he would write it down as luck, though he had no column for luck and did not mean to start one.

He kept his face turned from the east wall, but the oldest instrument he owned had started counting without being asked. It was the tally of glances he had built in the first weeks of term on the stairs of this very building, and it counted now.

Everybody had looked at the frame first, which was only natural, and then everybody had looked back at him. Gault: twice, short and level, because he had a line to write and needed to see what he was writing about. The Mire instructor: four times, her eyes going from boy to frame and back like a woman adding up a column. The Ash instructor: twice, over the top of her book. The counsel: three times, and long, the way counsel look at an incident, to pin it before it moves. Havel: twice, the second quicker than the first. Ilsev: to the tray of dust, to the clerk's shoes, to Cael, and to the dust again.

The fourth chair: once, at the frame, with everybody else.

After that, nothing. The man in grey had gone on looking round the room at his own steady pace, grid and wall and window, as he had for two hours, without his weight shifting in the chair or his hands moving on his thighs. His eyes did not come back to the rail once in all eleven minutes.

Sitting there with his hip roaring, Cael understood. Nothing that year chilled him more.

You look twice at a thing you did not expect. That isn't manners or temper or training; it's what eyes are for. The first look tells you that something has happened and the second tells you what, and six people in that room had needed the second look. The fourth chair had not. One look had given him the whole of what happened on the oak. He had weighed it on the spot and filed it, and Cael held nothing further that another look could fetch.

At the end of the row, while the porters were still tightening the reserve frame's last bolt, the man reached into his coat and took out the small notebook from the north tier, opened it on his knee, and wrote something short. The book shut and vanished into the grey coat, and Cael's tally gave it a mark of its own. *One.*

On the reserve frame they ran the fifth trial nine times, and Cael cleared eight. The first six were sound. The seventh came a shade late off the leading leg, since the leading leg was the one that had paid; the eighth was later still but cleared; and on the ninth the weight struck the edge of the chalk square while his back heel was still lifting out of it.

He spoke before anybody could ask him.

"The last two are the hip, Magister. The read gave me them in time, and the leg didn't take them in time."

Gault looked at the tray, where the last two heel-prints lay a little deeper in the dust and a little shorter than the rest. "The dust agrees," he said. "Logged."

Last came the cold run: the board, then the read off the reserve frame, to measure what three hours had taken out of him. The board came back a palm short of his best, every landing beat a hair longer, and he did not hide the hip in any of them; even so, it gave up less than the cold runs had given up in the first month. The read called twelve of twelve. It had never lost anything in its life, and of everything he owned it cost least and spoke softest, which was both its virtue and its fence.

Gault bent to his papers. By the clerk's count it took four and a half minutes. The pendulum ticked, the funnel dripped, eight people kept silent, and Cael stood on the brass with nothing left to do in the world but mind his corner.

By charter the enrollee must hear the note, so Gault lifted the top sheet.

"Semester evaluation of the assay-provision enrollee. Laid against the line he drew on this oak when he was new.

"Growth against baseline: confirmed across all demonstrated capabilities. Ceiling on the board raised from four to six, which the enrollee gave before the board gave it, now as then. The combined trial bettered, at the heavier weight. Ignition point shown, and met to the wing's standard; the earlier exhibit thin. One measure unchanged, the plate, and so entered. Trial five void for a fault in the apparatus, and run again on the reserve.

"Consistency: within tolerance at every trial.

"Assay-provision requirement: satisfied.

"Enrollment standing: renewed."

The Ash instructor signed, and beneath her name the Mire instructor wrote four lines about the second notch, and read them aloud in a tone that, Cael guessed, some supplier would be hearing about within the month. The clerk pressed the stamp down.

Dull. Exact. Stamped.

Hip shouting, shoulder aching, head full of hot sand, before the most senior eyes the Compact had ever sent his way, he heard a house say, for the second time in one term, the plainest sentence it had about him.

*Enrollment standing: renewed.*

Odder words had never been said of him, nor duller ones, and both remained true. It had only been said this time with every instrument the system owned pointed at the saying.

The east wall emptied. The counsel's chair scraped first, and she went out re-tying her ribbons and already talking. Havel tucked his board under his arm with the log sheet squared on the file beneath it and followed her with his eyes on the floor. Ilsev picked up her case and stopped for a breath or two beside the copper pot, where its thread of colour had sunk back to the first line, before she went.

The fourth chair emptied last. The man rose from it without using its arms, and then, standing, took the notebook from his coat a second time and opened it, and his pen moved once in a short stroke. Leaning on the rail again, Cael could not make out whether that stroke started a line or cancelled one.

*Two*, said Cael's tally.

The man put it away, and his eyes made one slow circuit of the room, grid and wall and copper and frame, with the patience he had once spent on empty tiers, and then the far door closed behind him. He had not looked at Cael.

The clerk reached up and began winding down the pendulum.

Cael was at the door when the Mire instructor caught him up. She had her coat buttoned to the throat and a face that had not yet decided to forgive anybody.

"That notch," she said, without any greeting. "I found it sticking in your first month. You'll remember. It went for service the same week, with a slip in the housing, and it came back with the slip signed and the work done, and I've tried it every sitting since and it's let go clean every time." She jerked her chin back toward the frame. "It was not neglected. I want you to know that, because you're the one who'd have been under it. Something in it has worn in a way the service didn't find, and I'll know by tonight what, and so will the man who makes them."

"Thank you, Magister."

"I'm not a magister. I'm the woman who'll be taking that frame apart instead of eating supper." She looked at him a moment longer, at the way he was standing off the leg. "Ice it," she said, "and don't be brave with it tomorrow," and went off down the passage toward the store with her hands deep in her pockets.

---

Lira was asleep on the stick against the gatepost, exactly as she had threatened.

Cael woke her gently, as instructed, with a finger on her sleeve. Her eyes opened at once and went to his face, and then to the way he was standing, with his weight off the leading leg, and she did not ask about the leg.

"Well?"

"Renewed."

She shut her eyes again, briefly. "Say all of it."

"Growth confirmed in five. Within tolerance on every trial. The requirement's met. Standing renewed." He leaned on the post beside her, because the hip asked him to. "One frame broke and one trial's void, and I asked for an interval at an hour and a half, and they wrote it down."

"Say it if it goes," said Lira.

"I said it."

She nodded once, as though a sum had come out, and pushed herself off the post onto the stick.

They went back across the quadrangle at the slowest pace the bluff had ever seen, two people with two good legs between them, one each, and took the residence stair a tread at a time. Brom was waiting on the landing with a bag of ice already in his strapped hand, and he held it out to Cael without a word.

Karis was in the common room with a book open on her knee at the page it had shown since the second bell. She shut it as they came in.

"Well?"

He told her, and at *renewed* her eyes closed for a moment, which from Karis was as good as a shout.

They arranged themselves in the common room like the survivors of a coach accident. Lira had the good chair and the cushion. Brom had the floor by the stove, with his iced knee out straight and his strapped hand resting on top of it. Cael took the settle and lay along it with the ice on his hip and his right arm folded on his chest, because the shoulder did not like it anywhere else. Karis, who was the only one of them still in one piece, looked round at the three of them for a long moment.

"A champion, a finalist and a renewed enrollee," she said, and counted on her fingers. "Between the three of you, three working legs and four working arms. I'll fetch supper presently, because nobody else in this room can carry a tray."

"I can carry a tray," said Brom.

"You can't carry a tune," said Lira, with her eyes shut, and Brom considered this, and decided it was fair, and stayed where he was.

Karis turned to Cael on the settle. "And the glass? The thread in the straw. You said you'd tell me what it did."

So he told her about the copper pot and the glass straw and the four moments, and then he told her the word he had found for it, kneeling on the oak with his palm on the copper.

For a while Karis said nothing. Then she opened the grey notebook to a page near the back, where two words already stood one under the other in her hand, *a spark* and *a letting-go*, and under them she wrote the third. She looked at it for a long time before she drew a short line under all three and closed the book.

"That's the last one," she said. "I can feel that it is."

A boy from the registrar's office came up the stair before supper with a half-sheet for Cael in Bracken's small upright hand.

*Records hall, the counter, at the first bell tomorrow. Bring nothing. — B.*

---

*Ledger, day twenty-two. The twentieth of Reaping.*

*Held: from the fourth hour, about six hours all told, three of them on the oak.*

*Drifts: two. (a) At the hour and a half, for two seconds or less, inside an honest answer: I went all the way down after a word, and the corner slid while I was down there. New fact: answering truly costs the corner. (b) Straight after the notch went: two or three breaths, with the whole room staring at the frame. Luck, not skill, and entered as luck.*

*Interval: one, asked for aloud, and in the book as "fatigue affecting response quality." No duller line has ever been written about me. And it's my only evidence that I was minding something, not hiding it.*

*Paper says: five up, one level, every trial inside tolerance. Renewed. All of it true. It's the first true paper about me I've ever carried out of a room without first having to fight somebody for it, and I'm not used to that yet.*

*Q.'s question, from Greyvane: could I keep it in, with people watching who'd be glad to see me hurt.*

*Answer, on the twentieth of Reaping: yes.*

*Thirty-five days since the stair. It wakes when I do. Three hours under the Compact's four best chairs, and it stayed in. Cost: one interval and one headache. The Pressure stayed in my arm. Gault wrote "no change" about the plate and then stood up for me over it, which I didn't earn and can't put right.*

*I've been keeping things back since before I had a word for it. Today the keeping went under instruments for three hours, and it held. I'm going to stop calling it a habit.*

*The quarter-burst. Gain: the clerk keeps his feet; the record keeps its shape. Bill: every bit of one hip, three days, likely four. I shut a burst halfway through paying for it, which I have never once managed on purpose since my Kindling, and I can't say how I did it, so I can't say I can do it again. One point isn't a line. Karis taught me that.*

*He didn't look twice.*

*Six people needed a second look at me. He needed one. One look gave him the lot. He weighed it sitting down, and the rail had nothing more for him.*

*Net: I've known rooms of people who wished me hurt, and many more rooms of people who wished only to write me up and shut the book. Today, for once, the man across the room had finished with me before I'd even climbed down off the rail. Safest thing that's ever happened to me, or the reverse: I can't yet tell.*

*Two lines in his book. I'll be a long time wondering.*

# Chapter 62 — The Wall

Hesk's answer came up the bluff on the twelfth, by the evening coach, which was fast even for Hesk.

Cael had posted his own letter on the morning of the twenty-seventh of Reaping, and he had not expected a reply until after the recess began, if then. Hesk, though, wrote back the way some men catch a thrown ball: the answer was already on its way before the question landed. Cael took it from his pigeonhole in the cloister and stood there and opened it in the poor light, because he could not make himself wait until the stair.

It was a single sheet, folded twice, in the large exact hand that had grown larger every year since Cael left home. There were four lines on it.

*An Archmarshal looked at you and wrote 'keep looking.' In my day that was called respect. Don't tell him I said so. Sixteen suits you.*

He read it four times standing in the cloister, with first-years going past him in both directions on their way to supper.

Hesk could not know what any Archmarshal had written. Nobody on the bluff knew that, and nobody in Hesk's workshop at the far end of the country was going to know it either. What Hesk had was Cael's own letter: the stamp, Gault's last lines copied out, and one paragraph about a room twelve feet by fourteen and a man with eleven questions who had walked up to the edge of a twelfth and left it alone. Out of that, at a bench, with his spectacles on his forehead, Hesk had guessed what a man like that would put on paper afterward. It was a grandfather's guess. It was not evidence of anything.

None of that troubled him.

He went up to his room and put the sheet inside the notebook's front board, behind the cover, where the other one already lived: the folded note that had come in the pine case with the pen on the eleventh of Sowing. Two sheets now, in the same hand. What it cost him to put the second beside the first, he kept out of the Log. Some lines in a ledger are better left as a space.

The second letter came the next day, and it had not been looked for at all.

It was in a hand he knew from a circuit rating card at Ardenmere, small and square, a ledger-keeper's hand. The cover had been readdressed twice: once from Greyvane, where the letter had gone first and missed him, and once by somebody at the Greyvane gate who had crossed out the old line and written *Halcenvane, the bluff above Ostrand* in a careful clerkly print. Most of a year had gone by on its road to him. He carried it unopened as far as the covered walk and stood under the arches with it a while before he broke the wafer.

*Read your account three times. Filed it with your record, where it belongs. The offer of a proper session stands whenever the road runs through Ardenmere again. The ledger doesn't forget. Neither do I.*

That was all of it. Five short sentences.

Under them, instead of a signature, was Vell's mark, and beside the mark the circuit ledger's own stamp, inked and pressed. The stamp held his eye. So the letter had gone into the ledger as an item before it ever went into the post. Somewhere in Ardenmere, in a bound book on a shelf behind a counter, there was now a numbered line that recorded that Vell had written to him, and when, and what about.

He wrote it down that night.

*Vell's session. The oldest shelves of the Ardenmere archive, offered two years back, never taken, because the road took us south. Through two academies, a hearing and a year when I sent her nothing, it stayed open; now it's in ink, under her stamp, in a book that can't lose it.*

*I'll take it. Not this year.*

*And a note on myself, because I can see the shape of it now and I'd rather have it in ink. I collect open offers. Reydan's find-me-later, outside a ring two years ago. Vell's session. Edran's promise of a third bout, which Brom borrowed for Lira on the oak after the final; Edran has no notion how far his line has gone. Prynn's shelf, with the gap in it that'll still be there when I'm forty.*

*Twelve months back, that list would have gone under debts. They aren't. They're doors. Somebody stands behind each one, having chosen to keep a door unbolted against the day a boy might come back through it. For a long while, not so long ago, I could have counted those doors on no fingers at all.*

---

On the last full day of term Cael took the week's coursework down to the assessment wing for the last time.

The wing was half packed. The instrument woman had sheeted the gauges, and the frame stood bare with its weights racked and padlocked for the recess, and somebody had taken the pendulum off its hook so that it would not swing all recess for nobody. Gault's door stood open. Through it Cael could see him at his desk with a new calendar spread in front of him, ruling lines on it with a straightedge.

The desk clerk waved him at the tray. The teaching assistant got up from the copying table, took the sheet, read the head of it, and laid it in the tray face down, square to the others.

"Thank you for the form," said Cael.

Seln answered with his one sound, "Mm," and was back at the copying table with the pen in his hand before Cael had turned.

That was all. Every week it went so, by design.

On the wall beside Gault's door, where the wing pinned its notices, a new sheet had gone up that morning. It was headed *Assessment office: recess counter*, and under the heading ran the working days of the recess in a column, and beside every day in the column, in the same round copying hand, stood the same name. *S. Seln.* Not one day had been given to anybody else.

Cael read it the way he read every notice, all the way down and back up, and let nothing reach his face. A wing with no students on its floors would need its allocations made all the same, for the cohorts coming back and the sittings already booked, and somebody would have to sit at the counter through the empty weeks and make them. Nobody on the bluff would find anything to remark in it. Of all the notices on that wall it was the dullest.

Everybody he lived with would notice it before supper.

"Enrollee." Gault had come to his door with the calendar in one hand. "A moment, since you're here."

Cael went to the door.

"Next term's sittings." Gault held the calendar out for him. Every half-month had its line, and on the line for the provision's half-yearly evaluation a date had been entered in Gault's hand, and beneath it: *Panel of record: Gault, with two. Measured against: baseline (entry), and the note of the twentieth of Reaping.* "Same rules as before. Same instruments, checked on Fourth-days. The frame will have a new second notch, and the man who builds them is coming upriver in person to fit it, which I'm told he finds humiliating." The calendar went back to the desk. "I'll measure you against yourself again. That's all the provision asks, and it's all I'll do."

"Yes, Magister."

"Go and rest the hip," said Gault, and went back to his straightedge, and the conversation was over.

Cael went out past the counter. Seln was writing. He did not look up, and Cael did not look at him, and the door of the wing swung shut behind him on its weight.

He told the others at supper about the recess sheet, in one sentence, as a fact about the wing's rota. Lira put her spoon down for a moment and picked it up again. Brom looked at his bowl. Karis wrote nothing at all.

Nobody said anything about it; they had an arrangement, and they kept to it.

Later, going up the stair, Brom fell into step beside him and said, very low, without turning his head, "The whole recess."

"The whole recess."

"Somebody has to do the floor allocations," said Brom, in the voice of a man reciting a rule he had read on a board, and went on up ahead of him two steps at a time, knee and all, and that was the last any of them said about it that term.

---

On the night before the last of term, at his window, Cael uncapped Hesk's pen and closed the year's books.

He did it the way he had done it at Ardenmere and at Greyvane, as a ledger and not a letter. Every line a fact, every fact priced. It was the only way he had ever found to stop himself writing down what he wished were so. He wrote slowly, and stopped often, and twice got up and stood at the window with his hands in his pockets before he could go on.

*Six confirmed.*

*One. Wind-adjacent. From Lira, at Ardenmere, the first.*
*This year: six free on the sprung oak; the panel measured it on the twentieth. At the baseline, in the second month, it was four. The seventh is billed to the next morning, the eighth to the morning after.*
*Landing beat: the same length it has been since I first had it. Three years of trying to trim it, and nothing.*
*The quarter-burst: once, on the fifth trial, I shut a burst while it was still being paid for, and the hip took the floor's share. I don't know how I did it. One point isn't a line, so it doesn't go in the column of things I can do.*

*Two. Pressure-adjacent. From Feryn.*
*This year: held back at the plate both times. It's on the Ardenmere circuit record, not on this wing's, and to show it would have been a second surprise. Cost: one flat line on a semester note read in front of the Compact. Gault put his own name behind that flat line with a delegation listening, and never knew what was under it.*
*No column for that. Bill to the shoulder, same as ever, four blows a sitting.*

*Three. Iron-adjacent. From Brom.*
*This year: eleven clear of twelve at the fourth weight, called aloud, on its thinnest setting. Second month, third weight: seven clear of eight. Reserve frame, after the fault: eight of nine. The last two were the hip's, and I told them so unasked. Cold, at the end of three hours: twelve of twelve.*
*It tells me weight and direction, early, and nothing else. It has never lied to me. Because of it, a clerk's brown shoes never met the fifth weight. Cheapest thing I own.*

*Four. Compression-adjacent. From Reydan.*
*This year: nowhere on any record, because no record ever had it. Nine of eleven at the middle bag after a bad night, eleven of eleven rested, on three sandbags Brom sewed, in a wash-house, before the first bell. Nobody official has ever seen it.*
*It's the one thing I own whose whole use is to stop the hurt reaching somebody else. Third inventory running I've written that line. Two years now, and no official eye has seen it work.*

*Five. Ember-adjacent. From Karis, with her consent, given in writing at Greyvane and asked for again on the night before I spent it.*
*This year: four instants on the wing's copper pot, read off its glass straw in front of four Compact chairs. Gault's word: better than the Greyvane exhibit supports.*
*I said last year it would be worth more on paper than on a floor. It was. Karis has three words for it now, written one under another. She says the third is the last.*

*Six. Shadow-adjacent. Bronze. Presence-suppression; movement-masking. From a man who doesn't know, in the third hour of a bad night, on a stair.*
*Deployment: none. Held at nothing from waking, every day since. Held at nothing through three hours under the heaviest eyes I'll ever stand in front of. Never once surfaced.*
*All my year's work is in that line, and nobody will ever know the work was done.*
*Ledger: the two halves still won't sit together in my hands. To mask a moving body I have to read the room; to read the room I have to be the most awake thing in it; and being that awake is what switches the hiding off. He can do both at once. I can't yet. Brom says I'll hate it for a year and then be very good at it. I'm leaning on the first half.*
*Rent: from waking, spent or not. Drifts come in the gaps, never in the work. A whole chair-back of Lira's thumbnail marks on one bad night; two on the day of the sitting; fewer every week since. The falling count is the one line on this page that cheers me.*
*Useful where: nowhere yet. Second year of that answer begins now.*

He sat back. The window was black, and the lamp made a second room in the glass with a second boy in it, writing. Boy and reflection considered each other. Then he bent to the page again. Under the six went the entry that had stood in that spot two years running.

*Anomaly. Session nine. Tide-adjacent.*
*Tonight I tried it again, as I try it every year: can I call it back, and can I say why it came at all? I can't do either. Two years and not a Tide practitioner within a day's ride. No long exposure. Nothing at stake. Every condition this book swears by was absent. It came all the same.*
*Six and an anomaly. It isn't a seventh anything. I've kept that sum honest for two years and I'll keep it honest now. The day I count it, I've decided a thing I don't know.*
*Still open. Still real. Patience.*

He let the ink dry. Under it all went the last working line of the year.

*The system isn't confused about me. Call it the year's finding. A man asked me eleven questions in a twelve-by-fourteen room and left the twelfth where it lay, and that's how I know.*

*Here is what I hold toward the next question, all of it. A gap on Prynn's shelf where a volume never came back. One retired word, copied into two notebooks, that neither of us can read. A designation on my file that nobody I've ever asked can trace to a desk or a hand. And, somewhere over all of it, one note in one file, in a hand I'd wager signs its whole name.*

*Whatever it believes it has settled about me, and the reason it was built to settle such things. That's next year's question. I came up the bluff road with a smaller question than this. This one I came by honestly, and I've paid for all of it.*

*Close out the year.*

When the page was blotted and the book shut, he put out the lamp, and the boy in the glass went with it.

On the morning of the last day the season came off the walls.

Cael went down to the Crown yard after breakfast with the other three because Brom wanted to see his line written, and would not say so, and so all of them said they wanted to see the yard one more time before the recess and walked down with him.

A table had been carried out under the north arch and set with a green cloth, and Bracken sat at it in his gown with the season's ladder book open in front of him and a box of blank certificate sheets at his elbow. The porter stood at the long board with his ladder. He read each column aloud from the top, name and standing and record, in his round slow voice, and at the table Bracken checked every line against the book, and wrote it fair on a clean sheet, and signed the foot of the sheet and pressed the registrar's seal beside his name. Then the porter wiped that column off the board with his rag, and moved his ladder, and started on the next.

"Every one?" said Lira.

"Every one," said Karis. "They go into the selection file. Withrow said so. The registrar certifies them before they go in, so that nobody can say afterward they were copied wrong." She watched Bracken's pen. "Three hundred sheets, near enough, across the recess. He'll do them all himself."

"He could give them to a clerk."

"He could," said Karis. "He won't."

The Iron column went, and the Silver, and the single name on the Gold. Then the porter came to Rooke's cohort, which kept its own short column at the end of the Blade board, and read it down from the top. On the third line he read "Third," and Brom's name, and the record.

Brom did not move. He stood with his hands in his coat and watched Bracken write it fair, and sign it, and press the seal down beside it, and only when the porter had wiped the line off the board did he let out his breath.

"Third," he said. "It's on paper."

"It's been on paper all season," said Lira.

"Not that paper." Brom nodded at the green table. "That paper goes where people read it."

He stood a while longer watching Bracken's pen go down the Blade sheets, and Cael watched him watch. A year ago, at Greyvane, Brom would have looked at a third place in anything as a fact about other people being better. He was not looking at it like that now. He was looking at it the way he looked at a guard he had built himself and tested, and found sound, and meant to make heavier.

Rooke was at the far end of the arch with a sheaf of loose sheets under his arm. He came along the arch, stopped beside Brom, and held out one of the sheets without looking at him.

"For the recess," he said. "Nineteen. Round three. Four a week. You know the reason."

"Yes, Instructor."

Rooke nodded at the board, at the clean place where the cohort's column had been, with the look of a man who agreed with it. Then he went on toward the halls, handing out sheets to his cohort as he passed them, without breaking step.

Fiske came through the arch while they were still standing there.

She was carrying her kit, as she always was, and she stopped beside Lira at the board, where the Copper column was being read off now, from the top, her own name near the head of it with the same six letters beside it as before.

"You made them look," said Fiske.

"You told me how."

"I told you." Fiske hitched her bag. "You did it. That isn't the same." She looked at the board a moment longer, at her own line, without any expression that Cael could read. "North tier," she said, "next season. I'll be watching the Iron bracket."

She went on into the yard to her chalk, and Lira stood looking after her with her hands in her pockets.

"I'm going to write her a letter," said Lira, to nobody in particular. "Over the recess. I don't know what it'll say yet."

Nobody answered that. Cael took out the observation notebook, there at the board, and added a line to Fiske's page, which was long now and had nothing on it about footwork at all.

---

At dusk on term's last evening the four of them climbed to the wall behind the second quadrangle. They had first sat there in the third week. Below the coping the bluff fell away, and the valley lay open under a long low light.

The stone was still warm from the day. Downstream, Ostrand was lighting its lamps street by street, so that the town seemed to be drawing itself on the dusk in little points from the wharfs inward, and the bridge's long back caught the last of the sun after the water under it had gone dark. Down on the far bank the lamp at the ferry landing had just been lit, and under it two grey coats stood waiting, while two more came up the river street toward them, unhurried, to the bell.

"Four hours," said Lira.

Karis agreed: "Four hours." Into the marbled book went the term's last tick, and the book shut.

Cael sat with his back to the coping and the Log shut on his knee, and found he was looking at the three of them the way he had looked at them in the third week, from the same stone, and seeing something different.

That evening, in the third week, they had each held a road that ran away from the other three. Lira had a bracket and a queue; Brom had a cohort he would not talk about; Karis had a carrel and three floors of law beneath it; Cael had half a sheet of paper and a month. Every one of them had felt those roads pull. Tonight he could not feel anything pulling at all. Since Denvash this had never happened: not one of them had outgrown the place where they sat. Halcenvane had kept its promises to each of them, one by one, in writing: halls enough, Paths enough, a library that had swallowed Prynn's index whole and asked for more, a ladder; a season; a chancellor whose wants were pinned up where anybody could read them. A whole year, the four of them inside it, and none had found its far wall.

Lira's legs hung out over the drop. She sat with her boots in the air above a hundred feet of nothing and her hands flat on the stone either side of her, the hip quiet at last, and she was Iron-tier now, by the account of an instrument that had taken four seconds to give it. Half a mile behind her a copper crown stood retired on a shelf, between a lamp and a pile of drafts that must on no account be thrown away.

Brom sat beside her, turning a pebble over and over in the fingers of the hand that had been strapped. He was Copper on his certificate and would be until somebody filed for him. Rooke's cohort, quicker than any other on the river, had him third. One beat had cost him the final; a season and two losses had paid for mending the seam behind that beat. None of it showed on him. He was watching a barge come down toward the bend with the face of a man estimating its load.

Karis had the grey notebook open on her knee and was writing in it while the light lasted, quick and small, her head bent so close to the page that her hair hid her face.

Cael had the day in the Log already. From the end of the wall he took stock the way he always did when a thing was finished, by counting only what was true.

There was somebody else on the wall.

Sixty feet along, where the coping dipped and a man might lean his elbows on it without looking as though he had meant to stop, a figure in the plain coat of the assessment office was taking the evening air. He had come along the wall the way any of the staff walked at the close of a term, without hurry and without purpose, and he had stopped, and he stood with his hands clasped behind him and his face turned to the river. The barges seemed to interest him. He had been there a few minutes.

Nobody waved him over.

The other three, Cael saw, did not. Brom had seen the coat at the far end of the quadrangle, long before it reached the wall; his pebble had paused in his fingers for exactly one turn and then gone on. Karis's head never came up from her page; so she, Cael knew, had spotted him earliest. Lira had turned her head, once, the length of the wall, the way anyone turns at a footstep; then she had turned it back to the river and kept it there. Of the four of them, she had spent the year wanting most to haul things into the open where she could hit them, and she let this one stand where it stood.

Nobody had agreed on it. Since the hand-cart night not one word of it had been spoken. Each of the three had come to the same discipline by a separate road, and sitting there in the last of the sun Cael thought it might be the largest thing that had happened to the four of them all week. The man along the wall belonged to the evening as the river did. Everybody knew it. Nobody would ever say so.

Karis said the quiet thing first, as she always did; saying a quiet thing aloud had never once frightened her.

She said, "Next year the continent comes to measure everybody."

Lira did not even turn round.

"Good," she said.

They let that be for a while. Out on the water the barge made the bend, and somebody on its deck gave two short notes on a horn, *heard you*, to nobody any of them could see; a moment later, from the far bank, somebody answered. The sun went down behind the far hills and the river took the colour of it, first gold and then pewter, and the bluff's long shadow went out across the water toward the ferry lamp. It was the kind of light such evenings end in, at the end of such years: years that had asked more of each of them than any of them had known to expect.

Sixty feet along the coping the teaching assistant straightened, his eyes still on the water, and walked on along the wall into the dusk at the pace of a man with work waiting for him in the morning, and every working morning after it, all through the recess. Nobody watched him go.

Then Cael said, to nobody, to the valley, "I have six things that aren't a Path."

It came the way his best lines came in the Log, unplanned, at the moment they had finally got true enough to be said.

"A semester stamp that says I proved it. And four people who know what it costs."

It held when he tested it, so he left it there.

There was one thing more. He knew what kind of thing it was before he said it. He had never seen the note and he never would. No one here had read it. No one would read it to him. What he had instead was a stamp that said *renewed*; and two coats at the ferry landing changing on the bell exactly as they had changed every day since the carriages went down the hill, not one man added; and a careful man who looked once at a failing frame and not again, that whole morning, at him; and a twelfth question left lying where it lay. Out of those he had made a belief. He knew it was a belief and not a finding, and he would have written it in the Log as one. He said it anyway, the way you say a true thing you cannot prove, to the only three people in the world he would ever say it to.

He said, "And somewhere in a file, the most careful man I've ever met wrote that I'm not an error."

"You're not," said Brom.

"Never were," said Lira.

Karis's pencil was already moving in the little notebook that went everywhere with her. She did not raise her eyes. "Noted," she said.

The bluff held. The stamp was real.

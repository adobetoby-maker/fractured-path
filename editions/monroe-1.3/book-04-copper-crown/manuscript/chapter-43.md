# Chapter 43 — Nobody Talks About Hips

Cael thought about Merrick's advice all morning, and in the end he sat where he always sat.

It had taken him most of breakfast to see it properly. Merrick had stood on the gallery stair two days ago with his grave courteous face and told him that Brom looked for him on the tier before every bout, and found him, and only then began. Then Merrick had advised him to sit somewhere else, somewhere Brom would have to look for him.

It had sounded like kindness, and Cael had carried it about for two days as kindness. It was only at breakfast, watching Brom eat porridge with the steady attention of a man stoking an engine, that he turned it the other way up and saw the shape underneath. If Cael sat somewhere else, Brom would look up at the usual place before the word and not find him there, and then he would look for him. He would look for perhaps two breaths, perhaps four, and every one of those breaths would be a breath in which Brom was thinking about the tier instead of the boards. Merrick had not offered advice. Merrick had offered a feint, and offered it to the one person on the bluff who would carry it straight to Brom without knowing.

"He's very good," Cael said to Karis, on the way to the yard.

"Who is?"

"Merrick. He tried to move me."

Karis considered this. "And you're not going to be moved."

"I'm going to sit exactly where I always sit," said Cael, "and be the most boring thing on the east tier."

So he sat where he always sat, a third of the way up the east tier, with Karis on his right and the space on his left empty, because Brom was on the boards today and not beside him. Lira had refused to stay in bed. She sat on the bottom step of the tier, below them, with the leading leg straight out in front of her along the stone and a folded coat under the knee, and nobody on the bluff was foolish enough to say anything about it.

Brom came out and walked to his chalk at the south end. Before he crouched, he lifted his head and looked up at the east tier, straight at the usual place, and found Cael there in about the time it takes to blink. Something in his shoulders settled. Then he crouched and laid his wrapped right hand on the oak and stood, and was looking at Merrick, and nothing else.

Merrick, at the north chalk, saw it, and Cael watched him see it: a small grave nod, to nobody, like a man noting that a door he had tried was locked.

The semifinal ran on the ladder's terms, not the final's, since neither of them held the top line: first to three clean touches, as many exchanges as it took.

Merrick did not feed cheap strikes, and Brom had been right about that. He came off his chalk at the word with a real strike, honestly weighted, at the left shoulder, and Brom took it square on the forearm and the shoulder behind it without turning, as Rooke had built him to, and went forward through it at once, hard, driving while the struck side was still hard.

He drove into nothing. Into less than nothing. A yard in front of his chest the air went flat and bright for an instant, like a sheet of glass catching the light, and Brom's drive hit it and stopped dead. Merrick had set a pane. He had set it not between himself and Brom but in the line Brom's counter would take, a breath before the counter came, and Brom had driven his whole weight into it and bounced. In the half-beat while he was still finding his feet, Merrick's open hand came round the edge of the pane and touched him on the ribs.

"Shield," said the table. "One."

Brom went back to his chalk shaking his head.

"Oh," said Karis softly. "Oh, that's clever."

"He's learned the repair," said Cael. "He's been learning it all season, from three feet away. He knows Brom won't turn any more. So he isn't trying to make him turn. He's waiting for the drive, and putting a wall where the drive goes."

"Can Brom see the pane coming?"

"There's a tell." Cael kept his eyes on Merrick. "Every Shield fighter on the sheet has the same one. Breath, plant, pane. They take a breath and plant the back foot, and the pane comes a beat after. The Shield fifth-year's was so slow that juniors practised on him. Merrick's is fast. But it's there."

The second exchange Brom took. He absorbed Merrick's strike and drove, and drove at an angle this time, off the line, so that Merrick's pane went up a foot to the left of where he was going, and Brom went past the edge of it and touched Merrick on the hip with his whole forearm.

"Iron Skin. One each."

The third went back to Merrick, who set two panes this time, one at either angle, a narrow corridor of bright air with only the straight road open between them. Brom drove straight because straight was all there was, and Merrick was waiting at the end of the corridor with his weight already back and his hand already up, and the touch came off Brom's shoulder clean.

"Shield. Two to one."

On the tiers somebody whistled. One more and the bout was Merrick's, and the season was, for Brom, over.

Brom walked back to his chalk slowly, his eyes nowhere near the tier. He stood on the chalk for a long breath with his eyes on the floor in front of Merrick's feet, and Cael, watching him, saw him stop looking at Merrick's hands and start looking at Merrick's back foot.

At the word, Merrick came in with his strike. Brom took it on the forearm. And this time, instead of driving the moment he had taken it, he watched. He watched for no longer than a heartbeat. Merrick took his breath and planted his back foot, and in the beat between the plant and the pane, Brom went.

It was the stall. It was the old two-year stall, the gap between taking and going that every good fighter on the bluff had found in him, the gap Rooke had spent a season and nineteen exercises shrinking until Cael could barely see it from the gallery. Brom had found a use for it. He let the stall run exactly as long as Merrick's tell, and then went through the place where the pane was about to be, before it was. The pane came up a moment later, flat and bright and perfectly placed, behind him.

"Iron Skin. Two each."

The yard came up off its benches.

"He's timing it," said Karis. She had stopped writing. "He's using the stall to time the pane."

"He's using what's left of it," said Cael. "If Rooke hadn't made it short, it would be too long. If it were gone, he couldn't wait at all. It's the right size today, and it's never going to be this size again."

The fifth exchange was short and ugly.

Merrick knew now, and Merrick was clever, and he did the clever thing: he took his breath, and planted, and set no pane at all, so that when Brom went in the beat between, there would be nothing to go past and Merrick could simply meet him. But Brom had felt Merrick feel it. He did not go in the beat. He waited one beat more, standing there iron-hard with Merrick's strike still on his forearm, the stall stretched as long as he had ever let it run, until Merrick, holding a breath he had not used, had to do something with it. Merrick set the pane late.

Brom drove through the place where it should have been and touched him on the breastbone.

"Iron Skin. Three to two. Bout."

He took it in the fifth.

He walked back to his chalk with his chest going up and down like a bellows, and his forearms red from wrist to elbow, and his face entirely blank. At the chalk he turned and lifted his eyes to the usual place on the east tier. Then he looked down at Lira on the bottom step with her leg along the stone, and Cael saw something go between them that he had no column for.

Merrick crossed the boards and shook Brom's hand and said something short, and Brom said something shorter, and Merrick nodded, gravely, and went.

"What did he say?" said Karis, when Brom came up the tier at last with a towel round his neck.

"He said, *you sat in the wrong place on purpose*." Brom lowered himself onto the bench with a grunt. "I said, *no, he did*." He jerked his head at Cael. "And he said, *I know. That's what I meant.*"

"I don't understand that," said Karis.

"Neither do I," said Brom happily. "I'm going to tell Rooke. He'll understand it. He'll say *noted*."

---

The porter did not wait for the next morning. He came out of his lodge while the yard was still emptying, with a pot of white paint and a narrow brush, and climbed his little ladder in front of the long board under the north stair. There he drew the bracket's last line across the foot of the Copper column while a hundred people stood about on the boards and watched him do it.

He painted Lira's name on the left of the line and Brom's on the right. He drew a short bar between them, and climbed down, and went away with the pot without looking at anybody, as if he had done nothing more than mark a delivery of coal.

The four of them stood at the foot of the board and looked at the wet paint.

Lira had come down off the tier on a stick. The safety instructor had pressed it on her the night before, and she held it as a person holds a borrowed umbrella on a fine day, ready to hand it back the moment anyone mentioned it. Nobody mentioned it. Brom had a towel round his neck and his forearms were still red from wrist to elbow. Karis had her notebook shut and both arms wrapped round it.

"Right," said Brom, at last.

Lira leaned on the stick. "You're going to be unbearable about this."

Brom did not answer her at once. He was looking at the two names, the paint still shining on them, with an expression Cael had seen on him only once before, in an alcove at Ardenmere, the night he had found out what Cael was and had decided to stay.

"I've been trying to solve her for two years," he said, to the wet paint. "Now I get faculty supervision and a crowd."

"That," said Lira, "is exactly the unbearable part."

"That's the best part," said Brom. "That's the whole of the best part."

Karis was not looking at the bracket at all. She had turned half round and was looking up past the board, at the west gallery, where Withrow had sat that afternoon in her white hair with nobody beside her, and at the empty chairs along the gallery's front rail.

"It's the nineteenth," she said.

They turned to look at her.

"The final. It's the nineteenth. They'll have been here a week by then." She nodded at the empty chairs. "They'll be up there. Some of them. Perhaps all eleven, if they like a fight. And the chancellor won't move the date, because moving it would be doing something, and she's told five hundred people not to do anything." Karis thought about it, frowning a little, the way she thought about a clause. "She won't mind them watching, either. A full yard and a clean final in front of the Compact's own people. She knows what that's worth to a house this size. She isn't spending you two. She isn't hiding you either. I've decided I like that about her. It's honest in a way that's very hard to complain about."

Brom rolled the right shoulder that had taken every one of Merrick's strikes, and winced, and rolled it again.

"They can sit where they like," he said. "The boards are still forty feet long. She'll still be at the other end of them."

"That's almost wise," said Lira.

"I've been working on it since the fifth exchange," said Brom.

---

The two practice batons had hung on a pair of pegs beside the common room door all season. They were scarred and darkened at the grips from two years of both their hands, and everybody in the residence had learned to duck them on the way in.

That evening Lira took them down.

She did it from the settle, with the stick, hooking each one off its peg in turn and catching it as it fell, which took her three tries for the second and made Brom start up out of his chair and sit down again under her look. She laid them across her knees. Then she unwound the strip of bandage from round her own hip, which she had been told to keep on and had not, and tied the two batons together with it, tightly, in the middle, with a knot Hesk would have been ashamed of.

"There," she said. "That's us finished. Till the nineteenth."

Brom looked at the tied batons.

"Finished for tonight," he said, "or finished?"

"Finished." She turned the bundle over in her hands. "Twenty bouts in two years, you and me. Nine to me. Eleven to you."

"Ten," said Brom.

"Eleven. The one in the rain at Ardenmere counts, and you know it counts."

Brom thought about the one in the rain and conceded it with a small movement of his chin.

"Every one of those twenty," said Lira, "we won knowing everything. That's how we've always done it, you and me. Everything on the table, every time, and then we go and use it on each other. There's been nothing better in either of our lives these two years. I mean that." She tightened the knot with her thumb. "And on the nineteenth it's worth nothing. Less than nothing. You know my leg, and since yesterday you know it worse. I know where your iron gives out. If we go on, we'll spend nine days guessing what the other one's guessing about what we're guessing, and we'll walk out onto those boards playing chess." She looked up at him. "Neither of us fights chess."

Brom said nothing for a while. When he meant to agree with something, he liked to walk there on his own feet, however long the road.

"It'll make us both slower," he said.

"Yes."

"Then it's fair," said Brom.

Lira held out the tied batons, but Brom did not take them. He nodded across the room at Karis, who was at the long table with the marbled book, and Lira understood, and held them out to Karis instead.

"You keep them," said Lira. "Till the nineteenth. You keep things."

Karis took the bundle in both hands, looked at the knot with professional disapproval, and did not retie it.

"Receipt," she said, and wrote one line in the marbled book, and put the batons under her chair.

Cael had said nothing all this time; he was down by the cold grate, cross-legged, the notebook closed in his lap. Lira, who missed nothing, turned her head to him.

"You've got a face on," she said. "Out with it."

"It isn't mine to say."

"Two years you've been putting the pair of us in that book. On the nineteenth you'll be sitting up there knowing more about what's happening on those boards than anybody in the yard, the panel included." She shifted the leg on the settle. "You've earned a sentence. Say it."

He thought about which sentence, since there were a great many in the notebook, and nearly all of them were about feet.

"On the nineteenth," he said slowly, "every person up on those tiers is going to watch a Copper final, because that's what the board says it is. And it isn't one. It's two Iron-equivalent practitioners nobody has ever let fight anyone their own size, getting to do it at last, in a bracket that was never built with either of you in mind." He turned the notebook over on his knee. "I've tried to write that down all season. It always comes out sounding like a letter home. It isn't. It's just what it is."

Brom said, "Huh."

"And I can't be on that sheet," said Cael. "So I'll be on the tier. I used to mind that. I've stopped. Somebody up there ought to know what it's costing the two of you to be on those boards, and I'm the only one who does. So that's my job on the nineteenth. I'll do it properly."

Lira leaned over and jabbed him in the ribs with the end of the stick. With Lira, that meant the talk had been a good one and was now over.

"Nine days," said Lira. "Nobody talks about hips."

---

Karis stayed after the other two had gone up. She stayed at the long table with the marbled book shut and the tied batons under her chair, and when Cael got up to go she said, without looking round, "Sit down a minute. I want to ask you three things."

He sat.

She took her slate out of her satchel, the small one she used for sums she did not want to keep, and drew two lines down it with the chalk, so that it had three columns.

"What does the panel want from you on the twentieth?" she said.

"Growth."

"Why?"

"Because the provision isn't a door you walk through once." He had never put it like that before, and found that he liked it. "It's a door you have to walk through every term. Each time you have to be bigger than the time before, or it shuts. Gault took my baseline in the second month so that this term would have something to be measured against. If I stand in front of him on the twentieth exactly the size I was then, I haven't kept the terms."

Karis wrote *GROWTH* at the head of the first column.

"What does the room want?"

"The panel—"

"Not the panel. The room. Everybody in it with a pen and a reason to be there." She tapped the slate. "The gallery."

He thought about it.

"Not to be surprised," he said. "Not less from me. That's what I keep getting wrong. They don't need me to show less than I've got. They need everything I show to be the next ordinary step from something already on paper. As far as four supervised hours a week for a term would carry anybody. In the direction the supervision would push. Nothing anybody in the gallery has to find a new word for."

*CONTROL*, wrote Karis, over the second.

"And what does the record let you show?"

"Five." He said them as she wrote. "The Wind. The Pressure. The Iron read. The Compression. The Ember. Not all on the same paper, though." He made himself go through it sheet by sheet, the way she would have. "The transcript anybody can buy holds two of them, the Wind and the read, and that one thin Ember exhibit at the back that nobody ever reads. Gault's baseline holds the Wind numbers and the read, and the plate, where I held the Pressure back, so that line's flat, and a Compression line he ringed because the floor never gave him a fair test of it. And Vell copied the circuit record at Ardenmere. Every bout I fought is in it, the Pressure I used in public and the Compression that finished Reydan."

"So two are public," said Karis.

"Two are public. All five are on paper somewhere, and not one of them would be news to a panel that read everything." He looked at the slate. "And none of the sixth. Not a flicker. The sixth is new to every record there is."

She wrote *FIVE* at the head of the third column, and under it, small, *and not one more*. Then she turned the slate round on the table so that it faced him, and put the chalk down, and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "Show me where they fight."

He looked at the slate.

He had been braced for a week, he realised, for this question. He had been expecting the three to pull against each other, growth against caution, caution against honesty, and he had been bracing himself to choose between them and to dislike whatever he chose. He looked for the place where they fought, and went on looking.

There wasn't one.

"They don't," he said.

"No," said Karis. "I didn't think they did. I wanted you to find it, though, and not me. You'd only have argued with me."

He sat with the slate in front of him for a long time.

The banking doctrine had ridden in him for two years like a flinch. *Show only what the record already holds.* It had started as one, at fourteen, in a Denvash hall, and for two years it had felt like one every time he used it: shoulders up, a little ashamed, a thing done in defence. And here it was on Karis's slate in three words of chalk, and it was not a flinch at all but a plan. The panel would get a real term of growth, honestly measured, in five things that already had a trail of paper behind them somewhere, done better than anyone in that room expected him to do them. Every bit of it would be true.

"I'm not hiding anything they're entitled to," he said slowly. "And I'm not showing anything that isn't real. It's the same thing. It's the same job."

"Yes," said Karis.

"That's never happened before."

"No," said Karis, more gently. "I don't suppose it has." She wiped the slate clean with her sleeve, all three columns at once, and put it back in her satchel. "Write it down tonight. Before it starts feeling like a trick."

At the window, with Hesk's pen, he did.

*Karis's three columns: growth, control, five. Asked me where they fought. They don't. For once the hiding and the honesty don't pull apart. They're one job. It feels so good I'm suspicious of it. Writing that down too.*

Then the part that had no column.

*Quenna, at Greyvane, asked whether I could do the harder thing: whether you can* not *do it. In front of people who wish you harm. I said I didn't know. True then: one reach is a story, not a record.*

*Now I've reached twice. I own a thing that never switches off. And there's a date on a board.*

*The twentieth. She'll get her answer, though she won't be there to hear it. So will I.*

---

Hall three had frightened him to the bone once before, on the day of the pin. The next afternoon it did it again.

It was an ordinary supervised hour. The wing sent somebody to watch two of his four hours each week, because the provision said that floor time bound to an evaluation had to be witnessed, and the somebody was nearly always the same quiet woman from the wing. She kept to the north rail with a board on her knee and a pencil, and in half a year Cael had never once seen her look surprised. Brom was his partner, as he usually was on Third-days. They were running a redirect sequence at half pace round the north bay on the new pins: Brom fed, Cael took the feed and turned it, Brom fed again from the new angle, round and round, a pattern they had worked so often that it had worn a faint pale track into the timber.

It was the sort of work that wanted every part of a person. He had chosen it in the first month for that reason, because the way to learn a sequence was to give it all of you. He had not thought about that choice since.

On the fourteenth turn the sequence took the last corner of him, and he did not feel it go.

That was what frightened him afterward, more than anything. There was no lurch. There was no spike behind the eyes and no warning of any kind. There was only the sequence, which was going beautifully, better than it had gone all week. Brom's feeds were arriving where he wanted them and his own turns were coming out clean and early, and the hold, for the first time in days, was costing him nothing. He noticed that, dimly, as a pleasant thing, the way you notice a room is warm.

Then Brom stopped.

He stopped in the middle of a feed with his guard still up and his weight still set and the Iron read still running in his forearms. His eyes were open and turned toward the place where Cael stood. For a moment, a long strange moment, they were not quite on him. They were searching a little to one side of him, the way a man's eyes search a room he has walked into in the dark.

Then they found him.

"You went," said Brom, very quietly.

Cael did not move.

At the north rail the woman from the wing was writing on her board. She had been writing on it, Cael saw, with a cold that went down through him into his boots, for the whole of the last minute. Her head was bent over it, and stayed bent; she turned a page and wrote on.

Two breaths, he thought. Three at most.

They went on to the bell at the posted half pace, and on the boards neither one spoke of it. Cael fed and turned and held his edges so hard that the ache rose behind his eyes like water behind a sluice. At the bell the woman from the wing signed both their sheets in her small neat hand, without comment, and went out with her board under her arm.

In the corridor Brom stopped dead in the middle of the flagstones, square across the way, as a post stands in a yard, so that Cael would have had to walk round him.

"How far."

"Thin," said Cael. "Not gone. Thin. She had her head down."

"Down *today*." Brom's voice stayed low. Shouting would have been easier to bear. "Nine days from now you're going to be on a floor for three hours. Gault, and the panel, and an Archmarshal, and whoever else they've brought with them. And somewhere in that gallery there'll be a person whose whole trade is noticing when a thing isn't where it should be. And you've just shown me that the one thing you can't steer goes thin when all of you is busy." He did not move out of the way. "What do you call a three-hour evaluation, then?"

Cael took all of it standing, because every word was true, and in two years Brom had never once said a hard thing that did not need saying.

"All of me, busy," he said.

"For three hours."

"Yes."

"Then it isn't a drill you've got wrong." Brom stepped aside at last, so that the corridor was open again. "It's the whole thing. You've had it at the foot of the page all month. It goes at the top."

It did, and he knew it before Brom had finished. Three nights ago he had written two lines at the foot of a page with a small cross beside them: thinking about somebody else felt cheap, and perhaps that was because he wasn't paying. The sequence had felt free for the same reason. Nothing in him rang when he stopped paying. Nothing ever would. He would have to be the bell himself.

That night the plan came apart and went back together, top to bottom.

The three columns stayed, because Karis's slate had been right. But above them, where the first thing goes, he wrote a line that was not a column, and ruled it off from everything under it.

*Hold. First. Before growth, before control, before the five. Nine days of it on every floor I stand on, and then three hours of it in front of the panel. Everything else stands on this, and if this goes, everything on top of it falls.*

Then, under the line, the thing he had understood in the corridor, set down as plainly as he could manage.

*The twentieth is two jobs at once. The one they watch has to look easy. The one under it has to last the whole three hours, and nobody in the room will ever see it, and nobody will thank me for it, because nobody will know it was there.*

*It's a bout. My opponent's a stranger, and the stranger's me. And the crowd won't know there's a fight on.*

# Chapter 14 — Filed

The panel had been closed. By the fourth bell that afternoon, the whole bluff knew what had happened in it, and none of what the bluff knew was so.

He worked out how before supper, because working out how was the only part of it he could do anything with. Nobody in the room had talked. He was sure of that by the evening and surer by the end of the week; he went at it four separate ways and found no road out of the demonstration room that any word could have taken. What had happened was simpler and, he thought, rather worse. The charter required that the bare fact of every provision-status evaluation be posted for the academy to read, on the board by the theater door in the administration range, where the clerk pinned the minutes of every open session and every other act the institution did. Halcenvane did its business in public and had done for a hundred and forty-one years. So a little after the noon bell, the clerk had pinned up a slip.

*Entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee: satisfied. Baseline established and filed.*

Nine words. They said nothing at all about what he had done. And into that nothing, inside four hours, poured every one of the eleven versions of him that had been going round the bluff since the gate, until the space behind the nine words was so full that a person reading them could no longer see the wall they were pinned to.

Lira brought the first two to supper.

"You broke the drop-frame," she said, sitting down opposite him with her bowl. "That's from a Current girl in the third quadrangle. She says you put your hand on the rail and it snapped." She took a spoonful. "And you refused to demonstrate at all, and sat in a chair for an hour with your arms folded, and Gault passed you anyway because he was afraid of the Compact. That's from two Iron boys outside the north hall, and they didn't agree with each other about why he was afraid."

"Both of those?"

"Both at once. Nobody minds." She looked at his right arm, which was resting on the table because it did not like to hang. "What did you actually do to the arm?"

"Held a plate."

"That'll be the next one," said Lira. "Wait."

Brom brought the next one. He sat down beside Cael with his tray, and did not say anything at first. Then he said, very carefully, as if it might go off, "There's a fourth-year in the cohort says you did something in there that made an instructor get up and walk out of the room."

Cael put his spoon down.

"The Mire instructor got up," he said slowly. "She went to the frame to see why it had caught. She was gone for about the time it takes to say this. She came back and sat down."

"I'll tell him."

"Don't." Cael picked the spoon up again. "It'll only make it sound as if I'm trying."

Karis brought the last of them at the end of the meal, and hers was the one they were saying with the most confidence, from somebody who had it from somebody who had heard it on the library stair: that Gault had come out of the room and told his clerk it was the strangest session he had ever sat.

"He didn't say anything to his clerk," said Cael. "I was there. He said good morning to me."

"I know," said Karis. "I'm only bringing it. I thought you'd want the whole list."

He wrote it in the binder that night, under the plate.

*None of them are true. One is close enough to true that arguing with it makes me look as though I've something to hide: the frame did catch, and an instructor did get up. A serviced detent, a woman checking a pawl, and in four hours it's a legend.*

*Nine words. That's all the board gave them. People don't fill an empty space with what's true. They fill it with what they already had, and then they'll defend it, because now it's theirs.*

*So nothing I say is going to move any of this. Only what I do, where people can see it, over a long time. That's slower than a rumour. It's also the only thing that's ever outlasted one.*

---

Ephram found him in the Crown yard the next afternoon, which was to say that Ephram spoke to him in the Crown yard, in front of perhaps sixty people, and chose to.

A practice session was breaking up. Cael was on the top tier with the notebook, where he had been for every session the yard had run since his first week. His arm was in its folded shirt inside his coat, and he was writing up the Copper rings, a Lira bout among them. He saw Ephram coming from three tiers down, unhurried, with his weight carried forward on the balls of his feet as it always was, and had about nine breaths to think and used every one.

*Ephram. Close for the first time. I've had him from the gallery for three weeks and he has not once looked up at it. Not avoidance. I've watched him not look at four other people the same way. It's a rule he keeps: he doesn't spend attention on anything he hasn't decided is worth it.*

*He's decided.*

Ephram stopped at a proper distance, a little over a pace, and did not offer his hand.

"You're the assay enrollment."

"Yes."

"I've read the transcript." He said it the way a man says a thing he expects not to be believed. "All of it. Not the summary. I sent to the stationer in Ostrand and paid the six marks. Do you know how many people on this hill have done that?"

"The stationer sent up five," said Cael. "Three went to faculty. One went to the library. Karis asked him, last week, about the fifth. He wouldn't tell her whose."

Ephram's eyebrows moved, very slightly. It was the first thing Cael had learned from him at close range, and he wrote it down afterward with the date.

"Mine," said Ephram. "Then you know what it says. And what it doesn't." He tilted his head an inch toward the yard, the boards, the tiers, the whole sunk hollow of the place. "Here's where I stand, and I'd rather you heard it from me than off a bench in the refectory. Half this hill has decided you're something extraordinary, and the other half has decided you're a fraud, and not one of them has any evidence either way. Because a hearing transcript is a record about law. It says the Compact couldn't stop you being enrolled. It doesn't say a word about whether you can fight."

"That's true."

"I know it's true. I read it." Ephram looked at him properly then, for the first time, and it was a level look and a long one, without any hostility anywhere in it, and Cael found it harder to stand under than anything Edran had ever thrown at him. "Greyvane needed you. They'd put the whole house on you and they had nothing else to put, so they needed you to be real. People who need a thing to be true stop being able to tell whether it is."

He moved one shoulder, a small shrug. Then he did the thing Cael would spend the rest of the day taking apart. He turned his head a little, toward the tiers below, where sixty people were pretending to gather up their things, and pitched his voice to carry.

"Greyvane needed him to be real. We don't. I'll wait for the semester evaluation."

And he went, down the tiers, with his weight forward, without looking back.

---

The yard emptied around Cael slowly. Nobody came up to him. Several people looked, and went on looking from a distance as they climbed out over the rim, and then they were gone, and he was alone on the top tier with the notebook shut on his knee.

Brom found him there a quarter of an hour later, having heard it on the east hall's steps from two separate people inside ten minutes.

"He's like that on the floor," said Brom, sitting down on the stone beside him. "Exactly like that. He looks at you as if you're a sum, and then he does the sum out loud so everybody can check it." He was quiet a moment. "He's never once been unkind to me. He's just never once been wrong about me either. I don't know which is harder."

Lira was less forgiving. She had come up the tiers two at a time and stood over them both with her hands on her hips.

"In front of sixty people," she said. "He could have said it to your face on a stair."

"He did say it to my face."

"And then he turned round and said it to them." She looked down the empty yard after Ephram as if she could still see him. "That wasn't for you. That was for them."

"I know," said Cael. "That's what I'm trying to work out."

He wrote it that night, carefully, crossing out more than he usually crossed out.

*Ephram, at the Crown yard, in front of about sixty. Not a challenge. Not hostile. I keep beginning to write "hostile" and striking it, because it wasn't.*

*What it was: he put off his judgment of me in public, loudly enough that everybody heard him put it off. So now everybody will look to him on the day the judgment comes. He's made himself the one whose opinion of me will count. That's the cleverest thing anybody's done to me since the gate, and I don't think he planned it. I think it's what a person does when his opinion has always counted.*

*And before I let myself be annoyed about any of it, this, because it matters more:*

*He is right.*

*The transcript is a record about law. It says the schedule has no line for me. It doesn't say I can fight, and I've let a whole academy read a legal argument as if it were a bout, because the mistake flattered me, and I haven't corrected anybody once.*

*He paid six marks and read the actual thing and worked out exactly what it proved and what it didn't, and then he told me to my face, in public, with nothing added.*

*Fair. Same terms I'd offer.*

*Nineteen weeks to the semester evaluation. The line is drawn: two capabilities, honestly measured, in Gault's hand, with a shoulder that'll ache until the weekend for the fee. From here, grow inside the line, where they can watch it happen. That's the whole of the work now.*

---

The file came to him in a room without a window, which was usual, and he read it standing, which was habit.

Chairs settled a man, and a settled man asked questions, and in a briefing room a question was a small confession. Every one of them told the officer across the desk something about what its asker had already decided. Seln had learned that early, and had learned with it that the directorates were fond of a man who took the paper, read it once and went, and he had spent the years since turning that fondness into a kind of coin. Ask nothing, and they sent you the work nobody wished to discuss. That work was usually the interesting kind, and nearly always the least supervised.

He read the brief as he read every brief, starting at the back. The authorizing signature first, then the reporting line, because those two facts settled what the rest of the paper would be permitted to say honestly. The signature belonged three grades above the officer sitting across from him. The reporting line went up past that officer altogether, as if he were a door in a corridor. Seln turned to the front and read forward, and somewhere in the second page understood that the thing had been drafted by somebody competent, which happened less often than it should and was worth noting for its own sake.

*Subject: enrollee, Halcenvane Academy, Ostrand. Fifteen. No classification of record. Adjudicated at Greyvane; challenge dismissed; status affirmed on a charter reading. Monitoring: active, resourced, visible rotation, continuous since the ruling.*

And a little further down, the lines that made the rest worth reading:

*Visible rotation has produced conduct data of high volume and negligible value. Subject modifies observable behavior in response to observation with a consistency that suggests deliberate management rather than nervousness. Recommend covered coverage.*

He read it twice and allowed himself something close to professional pleasure, since there was nobody in the room equipped to see it. *Deliberate management rather than nervousness.* Somebody had taken the trouble to know the difference. Most of the analysts he had fed product to over the years could not have told the two apart on their best day, and had built whole careers on never needing to. A watched man who behaves himself is one creature. A watched man who behaves *at* you is another. The first is afraid. The second is speaking, in a tongue without words, and there is only one sensible reply, which is to stop standing where he can see you.

He had written something close to that sentence himself once, years ago, in a colder country, about somebody else. He did not think about it.

The officer said something about the calendar, and something about cadence: a product monthly, a summary quarterly, exception reports as the matter required. Seln listened to the parts that would bind him and let the remainder pass. Then the officer said the thing that briefing officers always came round to saying in the end, in the voice they always used for it, as though it were a light they had carried into the room themselves.

"The Compact does not know what he is."

"No," said Seln, which was not agreement, and gathered up the pages.

He knew his Path's prices the way an old joiner knows his own plane, by the weight in the hand. Shadow at Bronze was not the vanishing the stories promised, and he had been quietly grateful for the stories all his working life, because they made people look for the wrong thing. What it did was manage the attention of whoever was near, from the outside. It quieted the small signals that made an eye come back to a place a second time, the flicker at the edge of sight, the faint wrongness of a body against its background. He did not disappear. He became cheap to overlook.

It ran on his own watching. He had to be attending to a room for the room not to attend to him, and four hours of it left a flat grey weariness behind the eyes that sleep never fully paid off. It could do nothing against an eye already on him; it could stop a look arriving, and could not unmake one that had. It was useless against instruments, which had no attention to manage. And it failed entirely against anybody who had thought to look for an absence instead of a presence, which most of his trade never learned, because most of his trade never met such a person.

---

The cover had been cut before they gave it to him, by the same competent hand: a teaching assistant in the assessment office at Halcenvane, on the staff of the magister who sat the provision panels.

It was, he thought, very well chosen. A teaching assistant in that office was a man built entirely of access. He would keep the evaluation calendar, and so would know where the subject was required to be, weeks ahead, without once asking. He would handle the floor schedules, and so would know where the subject chose to be. Every sheet the academy wrote about the subject would cross his desk twice, going in and coming out, as an ordinary part of his work, in front of anybody who cared to watch him do it.

It also meant he would have to do the work. Properly. The officer had not mentioned that, because officers seldom understood that a cover was not a coat to be put on but a second employment. A teaching assistant who lost a schedule became a subject of talk in a staff room. A slow copier became a small, recurring complaint that people passed to one another. Every complaint was a memory, and memories were the thing he existed not to leave behind. So he would file cleanly and copy truly, come early and go late, for as long as the posting ran, because being good at the cover was the cheapest and surest way of not being seen that anybody had ever found.

He took four days to build the man who would arrive.

The hand came first, because a hand outlasts a man in any building. He had six, and he chose the fourth: upright, small, a little cramped, the writing of somebody taught in a provincial copying-house and never encouraged out of it. Legible. Slow to look at. Nobody had remarked on it in any posting where he had used it, which was all he asked of it.

Then the voice. He set its accent a hundred-odd miles north of Ostrand and let it be ten years out of date. A current accent invited talk of home. A stale one invited nothing, because people assumed the speaker had left for reasons he would rather not discuss, and their assuming did his work for him.

Then the clothes, one grade below the dignity of the post, because an assistant dressed to his station looked like a man with plans, and a man with plans was a story people followed. Then the small incompetence he had carried since early in his service and never seen fail. He would be plainly, mildly bad at one thing: sums in his head. Some morning in the first week he would fumble an addition in front of the office and let somebody put him right, and from then on the assessment wing would have a settled opinion of him that it had reached on its own. That was the strongest kind. It would sit on the shelf where a real opinion might otherwise have grown.

He filled in his residence card on the last morning, in the fourth hand, on the mid-tier academies' common form. *Path: Shadow. Tier: Bronze.* Both true. The best cover papers lied as little as they could; he had watched cleverer men than himself burn down by inventing details they then had to carry.

Everything he owned went into two cases.

The first was the ordinary one, and it was part of the cover: clothes, a razor, four books chosen for how they would look on a shelf, a set of copying pens, a box of the small household oddments a room is expected to hold.

The second was smaller and older, with brass bands at the corners, and locked. He set it in the bottom of the first and packed the first around it, as he had done at every posting, and closed the lid, and did not open it, then or after.

He was thirty-eight. He had come to the part of a long service where the craft was whole and the hunger for it had gone, where he could still do the work better than anyone likely to check it and no longer wanted anything from having done it. He noticed this about himself as he would have noticed it about a subject, as a fact of uncertain use, and filed it.

Then he went to Ostrand.

---

The bluff stood over the river with the flat certainty of a place built on purpose, and he read it on his first walk the only way he read anything.

Not as buildings. As a problem of where to stand.

Three training halls meant three sets of doors, six approaches, and a crush at the moment one session let out and the next went in. The assessment wing sat at the north end behind its own low wall, with two doors and a single gate. That was very good for a man whose post lay inside it, and awkward for anybody who wanted to watch it from without. The lecture range had four floors and a stair at each end, and every landing could be seen along its whole length. The residence quadrangles were built round courts, so that anybody crossing between them could be seen from ninety windows, which meant in practice that nobody ever saw them from any, since ninety windows was the same as none.

And in the middle lay the Crown yard: a sunk floor ringed on three sides with tiers of stone and closed on the fourth by the faculty stand, room for something over eleven hundred by his count from the rail. It was the best position on the bluff and the worst. From its top tier a man could see the whole floor and half the approaches. And because everybody on the bluff already knew that, the top tier was where attention gathered, and gathered attention is where a watcher gets found.

He would use it anyway, because nothing else did the work. He would only use it properly: never the same seat, never the same tier twice running, never at the empty end of a bench where an eye is drawn, always the third or fourth body in a knot of people, where the knot's own shape did most of the quieting and the Path had only to finish it.

He mapped the bluff in three days without ever seeming to look at it. That was easy. A new assistant carries paper, and paper goes everywhere.

He saw the subject for the first time on the fourth day.

It was a closed panel letting out of the demonstration room: two instructors and a clerk coming into the corridor's traffic first, and then, after a little, the boy, alone, in no hurry, carrying nothing. He favoured his leading leg slightly and carried his right arm close, as a man carries an arm that has taken something it did not care for. Behind him the magister had turned back into the room. Seln was thirty feet off with a stack of forms held against his chest, and stepped out of the stream to let the corridor pass, as an assistant does.

He did not look at him.

That was doctrine, and it was the doctrine that sorted officers who lasted from officers who were found in their fourth week. The eye was what people felt. Not the nearness, not the repeated face, not the coincidence. The eye. A man would pass the same stranger nine times in a corridor and keep nothing of it, and the tenth time, if the stranger's look once met his, all nine would gather in his memory into a pattern that had never been there before. So: no direct sight of the subject for the first month. The edges only. Position, presence, the run of the corridor. Let the corridor tell what it knew. There would be time later, months of it, and by then he would be furniture, and furniture is permitted eyes.

He carried his forms past and into the office and laid them on the copying table, and did not think of the boy again for two hours, which was also doctrine.

He did note one thing, later, among the ordinary observations a new assistant might set down in an ordinary day-book anybody could read. The corridor had been full, and the corridor had been looking. Every third person in it had marked the boy as he went by: a glance, a turned head, the small hitch of somebody deciding whether to nod.

Good, Seln thought, without feeling much about it. A famous subject is an easy one. A hundred people watch him, and nobody counts the hundred and first.

---

Cael walked his second floor-time filing across to the assessment wing himself, three days after the baseline, instead of sending it by the residence clerk, because he wanted to see the wing on a day when nobody was being measured in it.

A panel day showed you what a place wished to look like. An ordinary day showed you what it was.

It was one long passage, apparatus rooms down one side and records down the other, and it smelled of lamp black and machine oil and the dry mineral dust of the chalk trays. On the records side every sheet carried two dates, the day of the thing and the day of its filing, and two sets of initials, the writer's and the checker's, and no sheet left the wing with only one of either. He liked that very much. It was what Vell had built into her ledger out of nothing but her own stubbornness, only here it had been built into the paper, so that nobody needed to be stubborn, only careful.

The office at the passage's end had four desks, a wall of scheduling ledgers as thick as paving slabs, and a copying table under the window. Gault's door stood open onto it, as it seemed always to stand. The clerk who usually took floor allocations was not at her desk. A man Cael had not seen before was at the copying table with a pen and a stack of forms, and looked up as the door moved.

"Floor allocation?"

"Second filing."

The man read it. He actually read it, top to bottom, which most clerks did not trouble to do; turned it to check the authorizations on the back; looked at the two sets of initials in the corner; and laid it on the right pile without looking at the piles. Then he wrote it into the ledger, set the filing date beside the date of the thing, blotted it, and looked up again.

"East and north halls, four sessions a week, supervised. That's what your provision carries."

"That's it."

"It's in. If a hall changes, it'll come to your residence board the evening before, from this office. Not from the halls. If it comes from the halls it isn't authorized, and you shouldn't take it." A pause of exactly the right length. "That's happened twice this term."

"Thank you."

The man nodded once and went back to his copying.

Cael stood a breath longer than he needed to and did what he had done to every new person on every road for three years, without choosing to, because the choosing had long since worn out of it. He ran the gaze. Rhythm: the pen went at one pace, and the pace did not change when he was spoken to or when he stopped being spoken to, which was either a very settled temper or a very old copying habit, and a copier would come by that habit honestly. Weight: even, unremarkable, the stance of a man who has stood at tables a long time. Breath: ordinary, a little slow. Position: a copying table, at the hour a copier would be at one, holding nothing he was not paid to hold.

All four agreed. They told one story, and the story was a man who had spent his whole life copying other people's paper.

Which, Cael thought, going back out along the passage, was exactly what he was.

He turned it over for a hundred paces and then let it go. There was nothing in it. One instance was an accident; he had one instance of an ordinary man being ordinary, and he had learned in an Ardenmere spring, underlined, what it cost to build on nothing.

That evening, coming back from the north hall by the shortest way, he went along the staff corridor at the end of the assessment wing, and read the cards.

Every door at Halcenvane had one, in a brass holder at the height of a man's eyes: a name, a Path, a tier. Not a rank. Only the tier, in the academy's own hand, changed by the residence office when it changed. Greyvane had never done it, because Greyvane believed the number on a person's paper was often wrong, and a school that believes that does not nail the number to the door. Halcenvane nailed it up because here the number was the blood in the body. Ladder, brackets, seeding, rooms: everything ran on it. His own door said *Cael. Assay provision. Evaluation by demonstration.*, in smaller letters than any other on the corridor so that it would fit. Lira's said *Wind. Copper.* Brom's said *Iron Skin. Copper.*, and Brom had looked at it on the first evening and said "Huh" and never mentioned it again. Karis's said *Ember. Iron.*, which was true, and which she was using as her measure in a quiet census of how many cards on their corridor she believed.

Three-quarters of the way down the staff corridor, a door that had stood blank since he arrived had a card in it.

*Seln. Shadow. Bronze.*

He stopped for perhaps two breaths.

He had never charted a Shadow practitioner. Greyvane had not had one in a year. The Ironyard had shown him two in eighteen months, both washouts who fought like dock porters and used their Path for nothing, because a Path that hid you was worth very little on a floor where three hundred people had paid to watch. And Bronze was a step above an assistant's post. Assistants at mid-tier academies were mostly late Copper or early Iron, graduates holding on for a year. A Bronze assistant was a man whose career had gone sideways, or a man taking a quiet year for reasons of his own. Both happened in every institution on the continent.

He filed it, and went to supper.

The common room was loud that night with the seeding. He wrote two pages of the observation notebook upstairs afterward, by the open window, with the barge horns calling on the river below: the two-signature rule, the chalk budget, the cards and what they said about the school that hung them. Then, at the foot, under the new heading he had made for the assessment wing's staff, because Ardenmere had taught him that the smallest entries paid out last:

*New TA in Gault's office. Bronze-tier, Shadow Path per the residence card. Doesn't look at me. Note: at Halcenvane, everyone looks at me. Filed.*

He read it over once, decided it was worth no more than it said, and blew out the lamp.

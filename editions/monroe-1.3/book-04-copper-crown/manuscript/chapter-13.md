# Chapter 13 — Baseline

The first trial was a single sentence, and he admired it before he had finished hearing it.

"Stand on the crossing at the fourth brass from the near wall," said Gault, "and when the board opens, be somewhere else."

That was all. Cael went out onto the oak and found the crossing and stood on it, and while he set his feet he turned the instruction over and could not find anything wrong with it. It did not say how far. It did not say which way. It named no Path and no framework, and asked for no technique. It said only: *here is a square, and on a signal you do not control, leave it.* Whatever he did to answer it would be his own architecture, offered up of his own accord, and the room would simply write down where he arrived and how long he took. He thought it was the best-made question anybody had ever asked him on a floor.

The shutter-board stood ten paces off with its nine louvres shut. Behind it the drum turned with its slow fingernail-on-comb sound, choosing. He could not see the drum. He could not guess it. That was the point of it.

He let his shoulders come down and held nothing, and waited.

The fourth louvre clacked open.

The framework began where it had always begun, low, in the hips: a drop and a loosening, the knees giving a little, the floor under him conceding the half-inch it had been holding back. Then the held half-breath at the front, which he did not choose and could not refuse. For the length of it he was hollow and light and could not have drawn air if the room had been burning. His weight was simply no longer where it had been. It was half a body's width to the left and a little more, and the oak came up to meet him.

On stone that landing would have come home hard through both heels and straight up the leading side. Here it went down into the boards, and the boards gave, a finger's width, and gave it back softer. Then the landing beat, the locked half-breath at the end in which he was nothing but a weight that had arrived and could not yet change its mind. Three years he had tried to shorten it. It was exactly as long as it had always been.

"Left of the crossing, three brass," read the clerk, without looking up. "Forward one. Interval—" and a figure off the pendulum.

Gault wrote. "Again. Same instruction."

He went back to the crossing.

Six times. The board chose its louvres and he never once knew which, and every time he answered it the same way, the drop and the held breath and the oak coming up, and every time the clerk read a crossing and an interval and Gault wrote it down. On the first three his hip said nothing at all. On the fourth it said something low and warm. On the fifth the warmth became the thin bright thread he had learned on the east hall's timber, crest to inner knee, a bill being drawn up.

On the sixth the burst went a fraction wide of where he had set it, and the landing beat ran long by perhaps a tenth. He knew it in his body a quarter of a breath before any instrument in the room could have. He stood on the crossing where he had landed and felt the pendulum tick past, and did not move until the clerk had read it.

"Left, three brass and a half. Forward one. Interval—" The clerk stopped and read the pendulum again. "Long."

Gault sat looking at the six figures in front of him for some time.

"One to five," he said, "agree with each other inside the width of a single brass. Six is wide by half a brass, and long." He looked up. "What happened at six?"

*If you cannot do a thing, say so, and it will be true.*

"Six is over my ceiling, Magister." He kept his voice level and plain. "On a floor that gives, like this one, four are free. The fifth costs me the next morning. I've never done six in a session outside a bout. The sixth was the sixth."

"You knew that before the first trial."

"Yes."

"And did not say so."

"You told me to say if I couldn't do a thing." Cael kept his feet on the brass. "I could do it. It cost."

Something happened in Gault's face. It was not quite an expression; it was more like a door in a large house being opened and closed again somewhere out of sight.

"Note for the record," he said to the clerk, without turning his head. "Enrollee states a working ceiling of four repetitions on a sprung floor, unprompted, and names the sixth trial's degradation before the instrument shows it." He looked back at Cael. "That is the single most useful sentence anybody has given me on this floor since it was laid. I will take a practitioner's honest ceiling over his best afternoon every day I am in this chair. Continue."

---

The second trial was the drop-frame.

The clerk racked the weight at the second setting, and the Mire instructor, who seemed to have been given charge of the apparatus, set the rail. It could be set in any of four positions: high or low, and near or far from the line of the floor. Cael was to stand inside a chalked square beneath it, facing away, and on the release say which of the four it had been set to.

He understood it before Gault had finished explaining, and felt a small, clean pleasure in it, the pleasure of a well-made tool fitted to a well-made purpose. This was the read with everything else stripped off. There was no opponent to guess at, no stance, no breath, no eyes. There was only a weight coming down a rail somewhere behind him, and a question about where.

The Iron-adjacent fragment had never been like the others. The Wind was a door he walked through. The read was nearer to a change in the air against his skin, a pressure that arrived on the surface of him, along the forearms and the back of the neck and the line of the jaw, a fraction before it arrived anywhere else. It told him nothing at all about what a person intended. It told him a great deal about weight and the way weight was travelling. It cost almost nothing, it was quiet, it gave no sign to anybody watching, and it could not be pushed: it went out a pace and a half and no further, however badly he needed more. He had spent his first month with it trying to make it into something larger. He had spent the three years since using it for exactly what it was.

And three weeks ago, in a low hall, he had found out what it was not.

He stood in the square with his back to the rail and thought about that, briefly. Then he put it away, because a weight on a rail was a weight, and a weight was the one thing the read had never once failed to find.

"Ready," said the Mire instructor.

The pawl clicked. Behind him the weight came down.

It arrived on him all at once, as weight always arrived when the read was clean: a press along the back of his neck and the outside of his left forearm, high, and with it the sense of where it had come from, slightly behind his left shoulder and above it.

"High," he said. "Far."

"High, far," said the clerk.

Twelve drops. He called them as they came, and the room went on with its small sounds around him: the pendulum ticking in its glass, the funnel at the end dripping into its cup, the clerk's pen. On the ninth drop the press came in wrong. It did not arrive at once. It came in two pieces, a thin early edge and then the rest a moment behind, and he had to choose between them, and chose.

"Low," he said. "Near." He turned his head a little. "That one I've got wrong."

"Low, far," said the clerk. "Incorrect."

"You are right that you were wrong," said Gault, with no humour in it at all. "How did you know?"

"It came in pieces." Cael thought about whether to say the rest, and said it. "When the read is clean, it all comes together. That one came in two parts and I picked one. I think the release was slower. I'm not complaining. It's only what I felt."

The Mire instructor got up without being asked. She went to the drop-frame and worked the trigger by hand, slowly, four times, with her head bent close to the pawl, and on the fourth she stopped.

"It's catching on the second detent," she said. "Not every time. One in five, perhaps." She looked round at Gault. "It was serviced in the spring. It shouldn't."

"Can it be trusted for the rest of the hour?" said Gault.

"If he's told when it catches." The Mire instructor came back to her chair. "He seems to know before we do."

Gault wrote for a long while. The clerk wrote something too, on a separate slip, and tucked it into the frame's housing. *For service.* Cael looked at the slip sticking out of the brass, a small white tongue of paper, and thought how easily a slip like that could be taken out by a careful hand on a busy morning and the work it asked for never done. Then he thought that it was not his frame, and not his morning, and turned his back to the rail again.

"Ten," said the Mire instructor, and the pawl clicked.

Cael finished the twelve. The last three came clean. Eleven of twelve, the clerk read, and the one wrong was the ninth, and Cael stood in the chalk square with his back to the rail and found that he had begun, without at all meaning to, to like this room.

---

The third trial was the two together, and it was the first one that was hard.

The weight was racked again, the rail set to a position nobody told him, and he was to stand in the square beneath it, read the release, and be out of the square before the weight reached the floor. The read and the framework, one after the other, with no space between them.

He ran it eight times and was clear on seven. On the eighth the read came in slightly late, through no fault of the frame this time, and he went a hair after he should have gone, and the weight hit the boards with a flat thud a finger's width from his trailing heel. The seven were right in a way the numbers could not quite catch. The press arrived along his forearms, and his hips dropped into it before he had thought, and he held the half-breath and went, sideways, into nothing, and came down on the oak, and the weight arrived where he had been.

Somewhere around the fifth he noticed he was enjoying it, and noted the fact with a small cold flicker of alarm, and kept going.

It was the room's fault. Every floor he had ever fought on had been a floor where somebody wanted something from him: a purse, a place, a crowd's noise, a verdict. This one wanted nothing but the truth of where he went, and it wanted it so plainly and so patiently that he could feel himself loosening under it, the way a man who has carried a load for a long way loosens when somebody finally takes the other end. That was dangerous. A loosened man said things. He tightened again, on purpose, between the sixth and the seventh, and felt the Ash instructor's eyes on him while he did it, and did not look round.

The fourth trial was the one where he had to not do something, and it cost more than the six displacements had.

Gault had the heaviest weight racked on the frame, and the chalk square moved in to within a pace of the rail. "This one cannot be avoided," he said. "The square is too near and the weight is too heavy. Take it on the plate."

The clerk brought one of the spring plates across on its brace and set it in Cael's hands. It was a hand plate, made to be held, with a dial in its housing that would show how much of what it took had been taken, and how much had gone through.

The Pressure-adjacent fragment would have answered the whole of it.

He felt it wake and want to. Feryn's gift had been in him since a bout he had lost fairly three years ago, and he had used it seldom, but it was close to reflex all the same. It took force that arrived on him and did not keep it; it gathered it, and turned it, and sent it on somewhere else. It was the exact tool for this question. It was, if anybody were keeping score of right answers, the correct reply to the instrument in his hands, and the instrument would have measured it beautifully, and the dial would have read something nobody in this room had ever seen.

It was not on the record.

He stood with the plate in his hands and the wanting in his arm for perhaps two breaths, which was longer than he had meant to stand, and he saw Gault notice the two breaths and say nothing about them. Somewhere at the edge of the room the pendulum ticked. He thought of Lira on the wash-house step, saying *make him look*, and of Karis in carrel eleven saying *it was mine before it was yours*, and of a page in a transcript, half a page, late and thin. Then he let the wanting go, the way he had let it go a thousand times on a thousand other floors, and it went, and the arm was only an arm.

He took the weight with an ordinary brace. Feet set wide, the plate held out with his arm loaded from the shoulder, his weight through his stance and down into the floor: the way anybody without a Path took a blow, the way he himself had taken blows for most of his first year on the Ironyard's planks. The pawl clicked. The weight came down the rail. It struck the plate and went up through his forearm into his shoulder in one hard jolt that did not spread at all, because there was nothing in him spreading it, and the joint took the whole of it, and the spring inside the plate bottomed out with a flat clack.

"Dispersal, low," said the clerk.

"Again," said Gault.

Four times. By the fourth his right forearm had gone dull from the wrist to the elbow, and there was a bright, unhelpful ache under the point of the shoulder, deep in the joint, that he knew he would be carrying until the end of the week.

"Assessment," said Gault.

"I can take that weight four times. Not eight." Cael flexed his hand and did not hide that he was doing it. "I don't disperse it. It goes into the joint."

He said it flatly, and every word of it was true, and it was also an enormous omission wearing the coat of a complete answer, and he knew both of those things at once. He had to work quite hard to keep either of them out of his face.

---

The fifth and sixth trials were procedure: the displacement again, and the read again, run cold at the end of the hour, to see whether an hour's work had moved either of them.

The displacement had lost a hand's width and gained a fraction on its recovery. His hip had its thin bright bill by then, and the sixth landing of the morning had told him exactly what he owed. He did not try to hide the loss. He let the board choose and answered it as well as he now could, and the clerk read it, and Gault wrote it down. The read had lost nothing at all. Twelve drops, twelve right. The frame did not catch once.

He walked back to the single chair afterward without being told to, and sat, because his hip had asked him to. Nobody objected. He sat with his right hand open on his knee and watched the fingers, which were not quite steady, and made them steady, and let himself look at the three people behind the long table for the first time all morning as people instead of parts of the room.

The Ash instructor was not reading. She had not been reading for most of the hour; her book lay closed on the table in front of her with one finger keeping a place she had forgotten. The Mire instructor had stopped looking resentful somewhere in the third trial and now looked only thoughtful, which on her face seemed to be much the same expression with the corners turned the other way. And Gault had watched him. That was the thing he had not quite let himself see until now. Through every trial, every drop, every clack of the plate, the heavy still man in the middle chair had watched him the whole time, from first to last, without once looking away to the door or down at the forms except to write.

*Make him look,* Lira had said. He had not had to. The room was built for nothing else, and the man in it had done nothing else, and Cael found, sitting there with his arm aching, that it felt nothing like being stared at in a corridor. It felt like being counted correctly.

Then Gault sat for perhaps four minutes over his forms, while the room waited. The pendulum ticked. The funnel dripped. The Ash instructor turned a page of her book and did not read it. The Mire instructor sat with her arms folded and looked at the drop-frame as if it had personally disappointed her.

At last Gault laid down his pen and lifted the top sheet, and read it aloud, because the charter required the note to be read into the record with the enrollee present. Cael stood on the oak and understood that he was about to hear, for the first time, the sentence that would stand for him on this bluff from now on.

"Assessment of entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee. Non-standard architecture. Execution consistent across trials. Baseline established; semester evaluation to measure against it."

That was all of it.

Four sentences, for six days of not sleeping and an hour of held breath and a shoulder that would ache until the weekend. He stood there and waited to be disappointed, and found that he was not disappointed at all. Every word of it was accurate. And *consistent across trials* was the only praise the instrument knew how to give, and it had given it.

The Ash instructor signed. The Mire instructor signed, and added a line under her signature about the drop-frame's second detent, and initialled it twice. The clerk stamped the sheet, and blotted it, and laid it in a folder.

Boring. Procedural. Stamped. He could not remember when he had last been so glad of anything.

He thought of the four pages of the list Karis had told him to keep, every sentence anybody had ever written on him: the station's bracketed word, the registry's form, the observer track, the panel's ruling, Halcenvane's record with its two signatures. Most of them had been written by people trying to decide what he was. A few had been written by people trying very hard not to. This one had been written by a man who had not even tried to decide, because deciding was not his work, and it was the plainest line on the whole list and the only one, he thought, that would still be exactly as true in a year.

He wrote the fourth trial that evening, at the desk by the window, with his right arm resting on a folded shirt because it did not like hanging.

*That's what holding back actually costs. Not the not-doing. I've not-done a thousand times. It's a decision, and I'm good at it.*

*What it costs is that I stood in a room and told a man who was being straight with me an incomplete truth, on his own floor, for his own record, and he wrote it down and thanked me for being candid, and the candour was real, and the answer wasn't all of the answer.*

*Price: the shoulder, to the end of the week. And something else I don't have a column for yet.*

*Quenna asked me once whether I could not do a thing in front of people who wished me harm. I still don't know. Today I found out I can not-do it in front of a man who doesn't wish me harm at all. I want it on paper that the second one was harder.*

---

Gault stopped him at the near door, when the two instructors and the clerk had gone out ahead into the corridor, with the folder already under his arm.

"A word," he said. "It is not on the record. Treat it as you like."

"Magister."

"There are people in this academy who want to know what you are." Gault said it without any weight at all, as he might have read out a crossing. "Some of them are curious. Some are worried. Two of them, in my judgment, are worried for the academy and not for themselves, which makes them worth hearing. Before the end of term you will be asked the question in eleven different ways."

"I've had eleven already," said Cael. "I've got them written down."

"I don't doubt it." Gault moved the folder from one arm to the other. "I want you to understand that I am not one of them. And I want you to understand why, because if you take my reason for indifference you will misread me for a year, and it will make both our work harder." He looked at Cael directly, the way he had looked at him once in the first minute of the morning.

"I don't need to know what you are, Enrollee. I need my instruments and your consistency. Give me the second; the first is not my mandate."

Cael stood in the doorway and turned that over, slowly and with great care, because he thought it might be the most useful thing anybody on the bluff had yet said to him and he wanted to be sure which of two things it meant.

"Is that a kindness," he said, "or a wall?"

For the second time that morning something moved in Gault's face.

"A wall. It protects you." He said it simply. "The moment an evaluator decides what a practitioner is, he stops measuring and begins confirming. I have watched it end careers. I watched it ruin one man entirely, a better assessor than I will ever be, who decided early what a girl in front of him must be and spent four years building trials that proved him right. They did prove him right. Instruments always agree with the man who is reading them, if he wants them to badly enough. It was only that he was wrong."

He opened the door. The corridor's noise came in, voices and feet.

"So I will not decide. I will measure you every half-year for as long as you are on this bluff, and I will write down what the instruments say, and if what they say becomes strange I will write that down too, in exactly the same hand. That is all I offer. It is not friendship, and you should not take it for protection." He paused. "But it is the one thing in this building that will still say the same tomorrow if somebody above me would like it to say something else. Good morning."

He went out past Gault into the passage, and Gault did not follow at once; he had turned back into the room to say something to the clerk, who had come back for his pens, and the door swung half to between them.

Cael went on into the corridor, which was full, as it was at every change of bell. He went slowly, because his hip had its bill and his right arm did not want to swing, and he was aware, as he was always aware on the bluff, of heads turning as he passed. Two second-years stopped talking at the door of the records room. A girl with a bundle of practice smocks pressed herself flat to the wall to let him by and stared at his arm as if she expected it to fall off. Somewhere behind him a man carrying an armful of forms stepped aside out of the stream to let the corridor go past, as clerks did, and Cael did not look at him, because there was nothing in a clerk with forms to look at, and because by then his whole attention had gone inward to the conversation at the door. He did not mind them. He was thinking about a man who would not decide, and about how strange and how steadying it was to have found, on this hill of people who had all already decided, one who had made not deciding his whole profession.

*He doesn't want to know what I am,* he wrote that night, under the plate. *He wants me to be the same twice. That's harder than being anything. It's also the first thing anybody's asked of me in a long time that I can actually give.*

*Note on the man he spoke of. Gault said the instruments agreed with him because he wanted them to. That's not a story about instruments. It's a story about me, if I let it be: about every column I've ever kept, every third entry I called a structure because I'd been hoping for one since the first. I've been the man reading the instrument my whole life. I'd better be the kind Gault is, and not the kind he watched go wrong.*

*Baseline set. Two capabilities, honestly measured, on the record for good. Four free on a floor that gives. Eleven of twelve, and the miss was the frame's. Dispersal, low. A shoulder for the rest of the week.*

*That's the line. Everything from here gets laid against it.*

# Chapter 56 — Twelve by Fourteen

The charter was already lying open on the records-hall counter when Cael came in at the first bell, turned the right way up for a reader standing on the public side.

Bracken was behind the counter with his pen behind his ear and a cup of something cold at his elbow. He did not say good morning. He laid one finger beside a paragraph halfway down the right-hand page, and kept it there while Cael read.

*An inspecting delegation may examine any enrollee whose enrollment rests on a provision or exception, in the presence of the institution's counsel and the officer of record for that enrollment, on the record, at a time set by the institution.*

"Fourth part," said Bracken. "Provisions and exceptions. Subsection eleven." He lifted the finger. "It's been in the charter as long as there's been a charter. Their counsel sent up a request under it at the eighth bell last night, very properly worded. It isn't unusual. Houses with exceptions on their rolls have it used on them now and then. This house has had it twice in my time, both times over students from across the water, and both times the most exciting thing that happened was a man asking for a second glass of water."

"So it's ordinary."

"It is perfectly ordinary." Bracken squared the great volume to the edge of the counter with both hands. "It is also the one place in this whole inspection where an officer of the Compact may put a question to you himself, out loud, and expect an answer. I'd be doing you no favours if I gave you the first half of that sentence and kept back the second."

Cael read the subsection again. *At a time set by the institution.*

"When?"

"I've set it. Fourth bell today, in the east room upstairs." Bracken took the pen from behind his ear and turned it over in his fingers. "I went up with a rule last night and measured it, to be sure. Twelve feet by fourteen. It's the smallest room in the building that will take five chairs without anybody's knees touching. The house's counsel will sit. Magister Gault will sit, because the subsection wants the officer of record and he is yours. And the book will be kept by one of my clerks, not one of theirs."

"They wanted their own?"

"Their counsel proposed one of theirs, at the foot of her request. I objected in writing. Two sides of a sheet." He set the pen down. "It took me a quarter of an hour to draft and her four minutes to accept, and I'd like you to know it was done, because you'll not see it anywhere else. It'll be in the file. Nobody reads that part of a file."

"Thank you."

"Don't." Bracken shut the charter. "Thanking me makes it sound as if I'd done you a kindness. I haven't. I've done the filing correctly." He looked at Cael over the top of the closed book. "That's the only thing on offer at this counter, and in my experience it outlasts the other thing by some distance."

---

There was nothing to prepare with, and that was the whole difficulty.

Cael had the observation notebook open on the long table in the common room at the page for the manifest's eleventh name. It was the same page it had been for nine days. One line at the top, which was the man's rank and name copied from the manifest. Under that, in his own hand, one word: *Lira*. Under that, nothing.

He had built a page on every opponent he had ever faced. He had built pages on assessors and wardens and registrars, on a chancellor, on a landlady once, for a week, out of sheer habit. He had built pages on nine of the delegation's eleven names in four days out of the library's attendance lists and published findings. The eleventh had given him three mentions in eleven years of print, a dedication and a dinner and a swearing-in, and not one word the man had ever said aloud.

"You've written nothing," said Karis.

"There's nothing to write. I haven't got a page on him and I won't have by the fourth bell." He laid the pen across the empty lines. "So it isn't a question of what he'll ask. It's a question of what I do when he asks it."

"And what do you do?"

"Tell the truth."

On the settle along the wall, where she was lying with her bad leg up on a bolster and a forearm across her eyes, Lira lifted the forearm.

"That's half a sentence," she said. "Say the rest of it. You know it's only half."

She was right, and he had known she would be. It was part of why he had said it in front of her.

"Where the record already holds a thing, I tell him the truth about it, all of it. Where he asks about something the record doesn't hold, I say I won't answer. In words. Out loud, and let it go in the book that I said so." He turned the notebook face down. "That's the change. In every other room, when there was a thing I couldn't say, I've given a true answer with a piece missing from it. At the baseline. On the plate yesterday. At the hearing. It's worked every time. It's cost me every time, and I've written down every cost.

"It won't work in that room. He isn't Gault, and he isn't a counsel, and he certainly isn't a warden. Hand him a true sentence with a piece cut out of it and he'll see the cut. He won't say so. He'll just know. And then I've shown the best reader on the continent exactly where the walls in me are." He looked round the table. "A plain refusal tells him one thing, that there's something there. He knows that already. Everybody on this bluff knows it; it's why he came. A gap in a sentence tells him where."

Brom had been sitting with his strapped hand in his lap, watching the stove. He looked up.

"Then say no the way you'd put down a plate," he said. "Flat. All the way down. No rattle."

"Yes."

Karis had laid both hands flat on the table, which was what she did instead of frowning.

"I want the risk said aloud," she said. "Now, at the table, rather than at the third hour tonight. A refusal on the record, by an assay enrollee, to an officer of the Compact, in the middle of an inspection of his enrollment. That's a document. It goes into the inspection file. People who are looking for a reason will read it. It's the single most quotable thing anybody on this bluff could hand them."

"Yes."

"And you'd still do it."

"I'd still do it. A lie he catches is worse than a refusal he doesn't like, by more than I can count." He held her eyes. "And there's a second reason. I'd like it said where you can all pull it to pieces if it's wrong.

"He's been here nine days. In nine days he hasn't seen one person on this hill put on a show for him. Withrow stood still. Bracken's file stood on its own. Rooke signed his sheets. Gault ran three hours of honest instruments on me yesterday and wrote down a flat line and defended it. The whole house has been nothing but itself, on purpose, every day he's been here." He spread his hands. "If I go in there this afternoon and I'm the one person at Halcenvane who performs, I break the only thing the house has actually shown him."

Nobody said anything for a while. The stove ticked.

"That's a good reason," said Karis at last, in the formal voice she kept for things she meant to stand behind. "It's also convenient. I'd like both of those in the minute."

"Put both in."

Lira put her forearm back over her eyes.

"Fourth bell," she said. "Don't be clever. When you're frightened you get clever, and you start sounding like a man selling a horse."

---

The east room of the records hall had one window, on the river side, and a table built for a bigger room, and five chairs.

Cael came up the stair three minutes before the fourth bell. Four of the chairs were already full. He noted that first and wrote it first, afterward: they had all come before him.

Withrow's counsel sat on the near side with her leather case shut on her knees. Gault sat beside her with a folder he had not opened, which was Gault's way of saying that the folder existed and would not be needed. In the corner, at a small sloped board of his own, sat the clerk Bracken had sent, the one with ink to the second knuckle. He had already ruled a narrow column down the left of his sheet for the time, and his pen was wet.

And across the table, with the window at his back and nothing at all on the wood in front of him, sat the Archmarshal.

Cael stopped in the doorway for half a breath, because the light was wrong, and he wanted to know whether it was wrong on purpose.

The window was behind the man. Whoever sat facing the door would be looking for half an hour into a grey river afternoon, at a face that was the darkest thing in the room. A smaller man would have arranged that.

But the window had been on the river side since the hall was built. And that chair, on that side of the table, was the one any officer would take, coming in first. The light had simply been there, and the man had sat down in front of it.

*Or he took the chair because it was the chair*, Cael thought, sitting down, *and I've just spent half a breath building a story about furniture. Careful.*

"Enrollee," said the house's counsel. "For your hearing and for the book. This is an examination under the charter, fourth part, subsection eleven. It is on the record. You may decline any question. A declined question is entered as declined, and nothing more is written about it. If at any point you wish to speak to me, say so, and the book stops. The house has set this sitting at half an hour, and the house will end it at half an hour."

"Understood."

"Archmarshal. The room is yours."

The man folded his hands on the table. They were square hands, and the left one was a little stiff in the fingers, as if the morning had been cold.

"Thank you," he said, to the counsel. It was not a formality. It had the plain weight of a man who had been handed something and was saying that he had noticed. Then he turned his eyes to Cael, level and unhurried, and Cael found that being looked at straight on by this man was not, in itself, unpleasant in the least. He had not expected that. He did not altogether like it.

"I have eleven questions," the Archmarshal said. "I wrote them on the road, and I've added none since I came. If we finish early, we finish early. Are you comfortable?"

"Yes."

"Your hip isn't."

Cael paused. "No. It isn't."

"Then shift it when it asks. Don't keep still to be polite. Nobody at this table is measuring how you sit." He did not wait to see whether Cael would. "First. Tell me what your enrollment rests on, in your own words, without the paper."

"On a clause in a founding schedule. It lets a house evaluate a practitioner by what he can show, where no registry classification fits him. The standardization directive changed the clause's words and never repealed it. My enrollment stands on it, and it's not a waiver. Every half-year I show what I can do in front of the assessment office's panel, and the enrollment goes on if I show it."

"Who found the clause?"

"Karis Dellenmoor. Ember, Iron Rank Three. She found it at Greyvane in the third week of last term, in a founding volume, and spent eleven weeks trying to prove it had been killed. She couldn't. Then she proved it hadn't been."

"Second." There was no pause between them, and no note. "What do you take to be the weakest part of your baseline?"

Cael had decided at the common-room table to give the true answer rather than the clever one.

"The landing beat."

"Go on."

"The Wind costs me a fixed moment at the end of every burst. Half a breath, after I land, when I can't change direction or do anything but finish landing. It's the same length every time. It's been the same for three years. I've never once managed to shorten it on purpose." He shifted his hip, since he had been told he might. "Nearly every time I've been hit since I got the Wind, I've been hit in that moment. Anybody who knows me and wants to beat me goes there. Lira has done it eleven times."

"And yesterday you didn't pay it."

The room went very still. In the corner the inky clerk did not stop writing, because a clerk who stops is a clerk deciding what matters.

"Yesterday I paid it into my own hip instead of into the floor," said Cael. "It isn't the same as not paying."

"No," the Archmarshal agreed. "It isn't. Third, then, and it follows. Can you do it again?"

"No."

"You're sure."

"I've tried to cut that half-breath short in training for three years, on purpose, and failed every time. The last time was a month ago." He kept his eyes level and did not dress the answer up. "Yesterday it happened once, because a frame broke that nobody meant to break, and I haven't a method for it. If I told you I could do it again I'd be telling you I had a technique. I haven't. I have an accident, and a sore hip."

The Archmarshal weighed that for perhaps two breaths. He weighed it properly, the way a man weighs a figure he had not expected from an instrument he has not yet decided to trust.

"That is the correct answer," he said. "By which I mean the accurate one, not the clever one. I want that understood." He turned his head an inch. "Fourth. Who taught you to prepare?"

---

"A circuit," said Cael. "And everyone who beat me."

For the first time something moved in the man's face. It was not a smile. It was nearer the small settling Cael had seen in Gault when a trial came back clean, a craftsman handed a reading he could use.

"The second half. Expand it."

"On the circuit at Ardenmere I lost more than I won, at the start. Every time I lost, I went home and wrote down what the person who'd beaten me had done, as exactly as I could. Then I went back the next week and watched them do it to somebody else, to see whether I'd been right." He turned his hands palm up on the table. "After a while it stopped being a habit and turned into a method. I've never learned much from anybody I've beaten. Most of what I know, somebody taught me by hitting me with it."

"And the schedule's structure. Who taught you that?"

It was a question from the margin of something. Cael could feel that it was.

"An archivist at Greyvane named Prynn showed me how a record lies in layers, and how you can tell from the layers what was put there when. And a ledger-keeper at Ardenmere named Vell taught me that a record forgets nothing, and that the worst line in it is the one that tells you what the rest are worth." He hesitated. "Nobody taught me the schedule itself. I read it. For three weeks. Karis read it with me. We found the place where it came apart because we were the only two people in that building with a reason to read the whole of it, and not just the part about ourselves."

"Fifth. Why concede the category?"

Cael sat with that one longer than the others.

It was not, he saw at once, a question about the hearing as a whole. It was about one decision he had made on a Greyvane afternoon in about four seconds. It had never been argued about in any corridor. It appeared in none of the commentary he had read on the transcript, and there had been a good deal of commentary, since anybody could buy the transcript for six marks. In front of a Compact panel he had conceded, out loud, that he did not belong in any category the schedule held. Everyone advising him had wanted him to argue his way into one. It had looked, then and afterward, like giving the fight away.

"Because arguing that I fitted a category meant arguing about me," he said. "And once an argument is about what a person is, a panel can decide anything it likes, and there's no page anybody can point to afterward. If I gave up the category, the only thing left to argue about was the text. Text stays where you put it. You can read it out loud, and everybody in the room has to look at the same words."

"You gave up the friendly ground."

"I gave up the ground where I'd have had to win somebody's opinion. I kept the ground where I only had to be right."

The Archmarshal looked at him for a moment without expression.

"Sixth," he said. "You keep records. The monitoring product on your file speaks of an extensive private notation habit. What are your records for?"

Everything in Cael went still.

There were four true answers to that. Three of them would have been a disaster. The fourth was true and whole and gave nothing away, and he had it ready, because he had written it inside the front cover of a notebook two years ago, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of this.

"So I can't lie to myself later."

The clerk's pen went on.

"Explain."

"If I write down what a thing cost me on the day it costs me, then in six months, when I want to remember it more kindly, there's a page that says otherwise." He did not look away. "Nearly everything that's ever gone badly for me went badly because some document said a thing about me and there was no way to check it. A long time ago I decided that at least I'd be able to check my own."

And then he waited for the next question.

It was the obvious next question. Anybody would have asked it. Withrow's counsel had already drawn the small breath of somebody about to hear it asked. Beside her Gault had gone utterly still, in the particular way that meant he had seen where the next question went and had no lawful means of stopping it.

*Records of what*, Cael thought. *Go on. Ask me records of what.*

The Archmarshal did not ask.

He inclined his head perhaps a quarter of an inch, as a man does when he takes receipt of a parcel, and said, "Seventh."

It took Cael the whole rest of the half-hour to get his hands steady after that.

*He didn't ask.*

*He came to the very edge of it, and he knew it was the edge. And he stopped there, and the stopping wasn't nerves, or courtesy, or a trap laid for later. He stopped because whatever was over that edge wasn't the thing he'd come to weigh.*

*A warden asks everything he's allowed to. An assessor asks everything the form wants. This man came right up to the biggest thing in my life and looked at it and left it lying where it was.*

*In my whole life nobody has ever had a thing of mine in reach and put his hands in his pockets.*

---

The seventh question was about the house.

"What does the provision cost this house to keep?"

Cael had not expected that, and found he had an answer. He gave it in Bracken's four plain sentences from the charter sitting, because they were better than any he could make. The house had to hold him on its roll. It had to set a standard and post it. It had to measure him against the standard in front of a panel every half-year. It had to keep the record where anyone with standing could read it. Then he added one of his own. "And it costs the wing a morning twice a year, and a frame it has to take to pieces now. The registrar would say the morning's cheap. The Mire instructor would want the frame on the bill."

The Archmarshal nodded, as if the frame were a figure he had already entered.

The eighth was about the ladder.

"Your two companions fought this season on the house's ladder. The registry has not reviewed either of their certificates in two years. Does the bracket measure them?"

"No." Cael did not soften it. "The ladder sorts by paper. It was built for people who'd be exactly what their certificates say, on the day they're seeded, and stay that way all season. Two of my friends spent the whole year in a cage built for people who did not exist. One of them won the cage anyway. That's how you know it was a cage."

The man did not write. There was nothing in front of him to write on, and there never had been.

The ninth was about the man sitting two feet from Cael's elbow.

"Your officer of record. Does he favour you?"

Gault did not move by a hair.

"No," said Cael. "He favours his instruments. He's never once told me what he thinks I am. He says that's where he stops, and he stops there. If his needles said I'd gone backward, he'd write it in the same hand he wrote yesterday's note in." He considered. "I'd sooner have that than favour. Favour changes when the man changes his mind."

The inky clerk ran his pen down the narrow column and wrote a figure. Twenty-two minutes.

"Two more," said the Archmarshal, "and then there are eight minutes that belong to you, and I'd rather give them back than fill them."

"Archmarshal." Cael laid both hands flat on the table. It was Karis's gesture; he had caught it from her without meaning to. "Before the last two. May I ask one?"

Withrow's counsel turned her head. Gault did not.

"You may ask," said the Archmarshal. "I may decline. If I do, the book will say so. You should take that as a real possibility and not a courtesy."

"Understood." Cael took a breath. "What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?"

The room was quiet enough to hear the river under the window.

The man considered it with the same weight he had given every reading in the demonstration room the day before. He did not shift. His hands did not move. Nothing at all was put on for show.

"The Compact filed no question," he said. "No instrument rides on my being here. No other business. No directive I have been sent to carry out. Your counsel will have told you as much. It's true, and I'm aware that of all the true things you've been told this month, it's the hardest to believe." He let a moment go by. "I came to see whether the record and the practitioner match."

"That's the whole of it?"

"That's the whole of what I was sent for. What follows from what I find is other men's arithmetic. I don't do it for them. An evaluator who starts doing the arithmetic starts producing findings in the shape of the answer." He waited a breath, and then said, in exactly the same tone, "You expected an enforcer."

"I've met the enforcers."

"I don't doubt it." He unfolded his hands. "Then take one thing out of this room with you. It's the only thing I have to give you. It costs me nothing to say, and it may be worth something to you in ten years.

"Wardens enforce. Assessors comply. I evaluate. Those are three different animals, they are wearing very similar coats, and every one of them is going to be in a room with you again. The difference will matter to you more, not less, as you rise — I would encourage you to learn to read it."

Cael held that for as long as the room would let him, which was about two breaths.

"How would I learn?"

"Watch what each one does with what you hand him." The man was already turning to his last two questions, and he asked them. The tenth was whether Cael found the provision's half-yearly demand a burden, and the answer was no. The eleventh was whether there was anything he wished entered in the record that he had not been asked. After a moment, the answer to that was no as well.

"Then we're done, at twenty-nine minutes." The Archmarshal rose. He did not push his chair in. Perhaps a man of his rank never had to, or perhaps it simply had not crossed his mind either way. "Counsel, my thanks. Magister, my thanks. Your calibration card is the best-kept piece of paper in this building, and I've read most of them. Clerk."

The inky clerk looked up, plainly astonished to be spoken to.

"Your book is clean, and your column is ruled," said the Archmarshal. "Both are rarer than they ought to be."

He went out, and his steps went away down the stair, even and unhurried, and were gone.

Cael sat on in a room twelve feet by fourteen, with a grey window and two grown people and a clerk, and found that his hands wanted to shake, and that he would have to sit for a little while longer before he could trust his legs to stand.

Gault opened the folder he had not opened all half-hour. He looked at nothing inside it, and shut it again.

"That is the most competent half-hour," said Gault, "that I have sat through in eleven years. I did not say one word in it."

Cael went down the stair a little later with a single sentence in him that he did not write down yet, because he wanted to carry it a while first and see whether it held its shape. Every system that had ever had his name in it had been muddled about him, and had asked, and the asking had been the gap he could always talk into. This one had not asked. Whatever it was, it was not confused.

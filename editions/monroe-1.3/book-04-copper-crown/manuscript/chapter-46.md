# Chapter 46 — An Inch

It began to rain a little after the fourth bell, and the covered walk filled up the way it always did in rain, from the inside wall outward.

Nobody liked the rail side when it rained. The wind off the river drove the wet in under the roof there, and so everybody crowded toward the dry stone of the inside wall and went along it in two close streams, one each way, shoulders nearly touching. Cael was in the eastbound stream with his satchel against his chest, going to the library; the boxes had come back across an hour ago, the doors were shut again, and for today, at least, the furniture had finished moving.

Rooke passed him at the arch, going the other way, with a sheaf of cohort sheets under his arm and the rain dark on his shoulders. "Enrollee," he said, as he had said it every day for a month, and not a syllable more. Cael said "Instructor," and they went on, and it was nothing at all. Cael found he was grateful for it all the same, as a man is grateful in a strange house for one chair that stays where it was.

Behind Rooke came two first-years, walking very close together and talking very low. They were discussing whether the Archmarshal could see through walls. One of them had heard it from a cousin. The other was not convinced, but was walking a good deal closer to the inside wall than the rain required, as if she were giving the matter the benefit of the doubt. Cael let them pass and did not smile, because smiling in the walk this week would be noticed by somebody, and a noticed smile was a story by supper.

He saw Havel at about thirty feet.

The records officer was in the westbound stream, coming toward him along the wall, with a flat document case held close under his left arm and the clerk who carried the lists half a pace behind him. He walked at the pace of a man going to a sitting he was not late for, with his head level and his eyes on the door at the far end of the walk.

Cael let the compound gaze open, all the way, the way he would for anybody worth the price, and left the Iron-adjacent read running coarse and cheap along his forearms underneath it. The corner of him that held his edges stayed where he had put it at dawn, and he checked that it was there before he let anything else go out.

Rhythm: even, unhurried, a step that had walked a great many corridors toward a great many tables. Weight: a little forward, onto the case. Breath: easy. Attention: on the door, and then, once, on the clerk behind, and then on the door again. It was a clean reading of an officer on his way to a sitting. It had no edges in it anywhere, and nothing in it pointed at Cael.

Twenty feet. Fifteen.

Nine feet out, Havel's near shoulder dropped and came forward.

It did not come far. It came an inch, perhaps a little more, and it stayed there, and the whole of Havel's near side went with it, so that the stream he walked in narrowed by that much against the wall. They passed. For a moment Cael had, between his own sleeve and the records officer's case, a hand's breadth of air he had not had a moment before, in a walk where nobody had a hand's breadth of anything.

Then the man was behind him, and the shoulder was level again, and the stream closed up.

Cael kept walking, without turning round and without changing his pace by a hair. He counted his steps to the end of the walk, because counting steps was a thing he often did and nobody would think twice about it, and he went in at the library door with the rain on his collar and the hold where he had left it.

It was only at the long table under the north window, with the notebook open in front of him, that he let himself look at what he had been given.

---

Havel had seen the boy at thirty feet and had known him at twenty, by the walk.

He would have known that walk anywhere. He had watched it come across a square at Ardenmere toward a pump step, on a cold bright morning two years ago, when he had worn a worse coat and been, he sometimes felt, a good deal younger than two years should account for. It had changed since, grown longer in the stride and lighter on the heel, and it had stopped hurrying. But it still took the middle of whatever space it had been given and no more than the middle, as if somebody had once explained to the boy how much room he was allowed and he had believed them.

The delegation's card was in Havel's inside pocket, beside the notebook. It was a small stiff card, printed, and every officer on the manifest had been handed one at the coast. Officers of the delegation did not converse with any party under review outside a sitting. They did not acknowledge any such party beyond ordinary courtesy. They did not carry messages, take papers, or accept anything offered.

That last line had made him put the card down on the inn table, the first time, and look at the wall for a while. It had not been written for him. It had been written years before he was born, for every officer of every delegation, by somebody who had never heard of a pump step. He knew that perfectly well. He had read it as if it were about a pear all the same.

He read the card again on the second night, the way he read every instruction, once for what it said and once for what it left out. What it left out was the thing he had thought about, in the end, for most of a stage between two inns.

*Beyond ordinary courtesy.*

So there was an ordinary courtesy. The card granted it by forbidding everything past it.

Fifteen feet. The boy's eyes were on the walk ahead, not on him, as they should have been. Havel kept his own on the door, because he did not want to see the face. If he saw it, he would read it, and if he read it, the boy might read him reading, and then there would be a conversation in that walk with not a word in it. The card forbade conversation.

Nine feet.

He let the near shoulder come down and forward, and he did not decide it at the last moment. He had decided it at fifteen feet, when he had seen how the stream was packed against the wall, and he had measured it then. It was the room a man gives a stranger in a doorway. It was the room he would have given a porter, or a girl with an armful of books, or a magistrate he did not like. It was the most the card allowed and he gave all of it, and not a hair more.

They passed. He did not feel the boy's sleeve. That was the point of the inch.

He straightened. Behind him the clerk with the lists said something about the rain, and Havel answered something about the rain, and the door at the end of the walk came up and opened and took them in.

Not until he had sat down and opened the case did he let himself think about it, and then only for a little while, the way he thought about most things now, sideways, so as not to be seen thinking.

There had been a pear, once. He had not taken it, and he still could not say why not. He thought now that he might have been afraid it would cost the boy something. A boy seen giving a pear to a district assessor would be a boy somebody asked questions about afterward. So he had refused a kindness to save the boy the price of having offered it, and had walked away with the boy's face still in front of him, puzzled and not offended, and had carried the puzzle for two years.

The inch did not make up for the pear. Nothing would make up for the pear. But it was within his instructions, and it was the whole of what was within his instructions, and it had cost the boy nothing at all, because nobody in that walk could have seen it but the boy.

He hoped the boy had seen it. He found that he hoped so quite badly, and made himself stop.

The sitting began. He opened his recording case and turned the brass glass, and wrote down exactly what was said, and nothing else.

---

It took him three tries to write it.

The first time he wrote *avoidance*, and struck it out, because the man had not been avoiding anything. The second time he wrote *manners*, and struck that out too, because manners were what you did with your hat and your voice, and Havel had done nothing with either. The third time he gave up on finding a word for it and simply put down what had happened, in order, as Karis would have wanted.

*Covered walk, rain, both streams jammed against the dry wall. Havel westbound with a case and the list clerk. Read clean: an officer going to a sitting, nothing aimed my way. Then, nine feet out, his near side drew in. Shoulder down and forward, a little more than an inch. He held it all the way past. He made his own stream narrower so that mine would be wider.*

*He wasn't getting out of my way. I was nowhere near his way. He was making room.*

He turned back to Havel's own page and read it through again, top to foot. The pump step. Four questions on a form and a fifth that was not on any form. The pear in its twist of paper. The recorder's case at Greyvane, and the brass glass turning at the end of the table. *He keeps going up a grade, and he keeps turning up wherever I am.* And at the very bottom, in Lira's square capitals, because she had taken the pen out of his hand one evening in the common room and written it herself:

*Careful men go up. Doesn't tell you what he's careful FOR.*

He sat with that for a while.

He still did not know who kept moving the man up, or why every move seemed to set him down in some room Cael was standing in. There were things in Havel's work he was not allowed to say. Cael had always been able to see that much, because the man wore it in his face like a collar a size too small. The walk had told him nothing about any of it.

What the walk had told him was smaller, and he had known it for two years and never quite let himself keep it. Whatever the man had been ordered, he had always done every scrap of kindness the orders left room for, and then stopped at the line. At the pump step it had been a fifth question asked in an ordinary voice. Today it had been an inch.

Cael had gone back and forth for two years over whether that deserved thanks. A part of him had always said that thanking somebody for so little was a kind of foolishness. It said that a boy who was grateful for an inch was a boy who had got used to being given nothing.

He looked at the struck-out words, and at Lira's capitals, and found that the argument was over.

*Settled, then. Room is room, whoever gives it, and however little. A man who isn't allowed to say a word to me found the one thing he was allowed to do, and did all of it, in the rain, where nobody could see it but me. I'm not going to be too proud to count that.*

Under it, smaller:

*Lira: careful for an inch, at least. Not an answer. Not nothing.*

He shut the notebook. The rain had stopped, and the light was coming back yellow off the river through the north window, and on the board by the library door somebody had chalked the evening's bouts in the Crown yard. The first was for the fifth line.

---

He went to the Crown yard because he always went to the Crown yard.

That was the rule he had made for the week, and it was a rule about sameness, not about bouts. Every ladder session of the season he had sat a third of the way up the east tier, in the same place, from before the first bout until after the last. If he stopped now, with eleven strangers on the bluff, his absence would be the first new thing anyone had seen him do in a month.

So he went, and sat in his place, and Brom came up after a while and sat on his left with a decent gap, as he always did, and put his forearms on his knees.

"Who's on?"

"A Force third-year and a Stone fourth-year. For the fifth line."

"Fifth line." Brom considered the boards. "That's next year's seeding."

"That's next year's seeding."

"Then they'll both be frightened," said Brom, with satisfaction, "and they'll both be careful, and it'll be very dull, and I'll enjoy it."

About a hundred and forty people had come. That was a big crowd for a bout with no crown on it, and Cael knew why, and so did everybody else on the tiers. Some had come to see the boards, and most had come to see who else came. The north tier was fuller than it ought to have been, and people kept glancing at its stair.

The fighters came out to their chalk. The Force girl was tall and narrow and very quick off the mark, and she had a habit Cael had logged in the first month. She drove her first strike of every exchange at the same height, chest-high, to find out at once whether the other fighter would give ground. The Stone fourth-year was square and slow, and he rooted. When he set his weight he went down into the oak like a post going into a hole, and for the length of a breath he could not be shifted by anything less than a cart.

The table called the first exchange.

The Force girl drove chest-high, and the Stone boy rooted and took it, and she slid off him and came back low, and touched him on the outside of the knee with two fingers as his weight was still going down, so lightly that it was very nearly not a touch at all.

"Force," said the table. "One."

On the tiers somebody muttered. Cael wrote nothing. He watched the three faculty at the table: the safety instructor at the end with his hand flat on the wood, the two adjudicators either side of him. The left one had called it and the right one had nodded, and the safety instructor's hand had not moved.

And while the fighters were walking back to their chalk, on the north tier, a man came up the stair.

He came up it at an ordinary pace and found a place at the rail among thirty or so others, at the end nearest the stair. He stood there with his hands at his sides, in his plain grey, looking at the boards. Nobody near him moved away, and nobody moved closer. The people on either side of him simply went very still.

Cael did not look at him. He looked at the boards, and let the man sit at the very edge of his eye, where a thing is known but not watched, and he felt the corner of himself that was holding his edges go tight, like a hand on a rope.

*Hold. First.*

He held, and watched the second exchange, and made himself see all of it.

It was the Stone boy's. He let the Force girl come chest-high again, and rooted again, and this time when she slid off low he was not there. He had let his own root go a breath early and stepped across, and his open hand brushed her shoulder as she turned, for about as long as a blink. It was as light as hers had been. On the tiers the same people muttered again, the other way.

"Stone. One each."

The third exchange he watched badly, and knew it while it was happening, and could not stop it. The Force girl came in at a new height, low, testing, and the Stone boy rooted early to meet it, and for three or four breaths there was a hard close scramble on the oak, all elbows and set weight. Somewhere in the middle of it, a third of Cael's attention went up and across to the north tier on its own, without asking him, to find out whether the man in grey was watching the scramble or watching something else.

He was not, quite. His face was toward the boards. But his eyes, so far as Cael could tell at that distance, were on the table.

The scramble broke. The Stone boy had touched the girl on the hip as she came out of it, square and solid, and there was no argument about it.

"Stone," said the table. "Two to one."

Cael dragged the stray third of himself back where it belonged and was angry about it. He had never in a whole season let a fighter on the boards go unwatched in the middle of an exchange. He was going to be annoyed about that for two days, and he knew it, and he was already annoyed about knowing it.

The fourth exchange began. The Force girl had to score now or lose the line, and she knew it, and she came off her chalk faster than she had all evening. She drove chest-high, and the Stone boy rooted.

And on the north tier, the man in grey turned from the rail and went down the far stair at the same ordinary pace he had come up the near one.

The exchange was still running, the table had not lifted a hand, and nobody on the boards had touched anybody. Nothing that the bout was for had happened yet, and the man was gone.

A breath later the Force girl slid off low and touched the Stone boy on the knee again, very lightly, and the table called it hers. Two each. The bout went to a fifth, which the Stone boy won by rooting on the word and simply refusing to be moved until the girl ran out of ideas, and the tiers cheered him for it, which was fair; it had been clever. He would hold the fifth line into next year.

"Dull," said Brom happily, standing up. "Told you."

Cael was looking at the north stair.

"He went before it finished," he said.

"Who did?"

"The one I haven't got a page for."

Brom looked at the north tier, where the place by the stair was empty now, and then looked at Cael, and did not ask anything else. He only said, "Supper. You can think at supper. I've seen you do it."

---

They went down to supper late, when the hall had half emptied, and sat at the end of a long table with nobody near them.

Cael pushed his bread about and did not eat it. Brom ate steadily and waited. He was good at waiting, when it was somebody else's problem; he had learned it, he said once, from being somebody else's problem for most of his life.

"Go on, then," he said at last.

"He came up at the second exchange. He left at the start of the fourth."

"I saw."

"So why?"

"Somewhere to be," said Brom, with his mouth full.

"At the sixth bell, on a Fifth-day? A man like that, on a hill like this?"

Brom considered it, and swallowed. "No. Then he was bored."

"You'd have seen it. *I'd* have seen it. There wasn't anything to see." Cael turned the bread over. "And he didn't want to be seen going, either. If he had, he'd have gone down the near stair, past everybody, when they were all looking at him. He went down the far one when they were all looking at the boards."

"Then I've run out," said Brom. "Where was he looking?"

"At the table."

Brom stopped chewing.

His eyes came up to Cael's across the end of the long table. Then he put his spoon down in the bowl, carefully.

"You do that," he said.

"Do what?"

"Stop watching us. Partway through a bout. You stop watching the two people on the oak and you watch the three people at the table instead. I've caught you at it." He picked the spoon up again. "Not every bout. Enough of them. I used to think you'd got bored of me."

Cael opened his mouth and shut it again.

It was true. He had a whole page on it, somewhere in the second month, and he had been faintly ashamed of the page and had not looked at it since. When the safety instructor's hand came up, and when it stayed down. Whether a light touch got called in a fourth exchange as readily as in a first. Whether the table ever leaned, once it could see which way a bout was going. He had watched the table because the fighters only ever told him about two fighters, and the table told him about the whole yard.

He laid it out for Brom, slowly, as it came.

"He came up after the first call. The Force girl's touch at the knee, the light one, given to her. He'd have heard the tiers grumble about it. Then he watched the second, and the second was light too, the Stone boy's hand on her shoulder, and they gave it to him. Two light touches, one each way, both called. That's enough to see whether they hold one line for both."

"And the third?"

"The scramble. That's where it tilts. That's where you find out if the table starts calling for whoever's winning." Cael put the bread down. "He watched the table right through it. Then the fourth started, and it didn't matter any more which of them was better, so he went."

"Did they hold it?"

"They did. In the fourth, after he'd gone, the Force girl got the same light touch at the knee and they gave it to her again. He didn't stay to see it. He didn't need to. He'd watched them through the third without leaning."

"So he got what he came for," said Brom slowly.

"And left."

"That's rude."

"It's exactly what I'd have done," said Cael.

Brom looked at him over the bowl.

"I know," he said. "That's the part I don't like."

That night, under the low lamp at his own window, he set it down.

*Fifth-line bout, sixth bell. The Archmarshal came for the table, not the fighters. Two calls, then the tilt, then gone before the result. He was checking whether this yard calls a touch the same for everybody, and whether it keeps calling it the same when it would be easy not to. The answer tonight was yes. He had it in about six minutes.*

*Brom says I do the same thing. Brom is right. I've had a page on it since the second month and been ashamed of the page.*

*So that's the first thing I know about him that isn't a blank. He reads a yard the way I read a yard. The difference is that I'm the one standing in it.*

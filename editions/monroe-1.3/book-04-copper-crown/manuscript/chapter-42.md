# Chapter 42 — One Very Long Minute

On the morning of the ninth of Reaping the registrar's outer office smelled of hot wax, and there were four boxes on the counter.

Karis had asked him to be at the registrar's counter at the second bell, and had not said why. So he was there, standing at the dark wood with nothing in his hands, for the last minute of a piece of work. It had begun in a Greyvane carrel in the third week of a term, and it had been finished, in its final form, in six days in the room behind this one.

The boxes were plain grey pasteboard, the kind the stationer in Ostrand sold by the dozen, with brass corners and a cloth hinge. Three of them were already closed and tied with white tape, and on each lid in Bracken's hand was a single line: a number, and the words *Enrollment basis*, and the number of the box. The fourth stood open. Bracken was laying the last sheets into it from a stack at his elbow, one at a time, squaring each against the one below with the side of his hand.

Karis stood at the far end of the counter. She had her grey notebook clasped against her chest with both arms, the way a person holds a cat that might jump, and her knuckles were still faintly blue from four days of ink.

Nobody spoke, and the senior clerk at his high desk had stopped writing and sat with his pen lifted.

Bracken laid the last sheet, which was the first page of the index and went on top, so that whoever opened the box would see it before anything else; then he closed the lid. He took the white tape from the reel, measured it against the box without looking, cut it with the small knife from his waistcoat pocket, and tied it in a flat registrar's knot that would undo with one pull and with nothing else. Then he took the pen from behind his ear and wrote on the lid, *4 of 4*, and under that, smaller, *311*.

He set the box on the shelf behind him with the other three, and turned them all a little, so that the lettering faced the room.

"Three hundred and eleven sheets," he said. "Every link in the chain copied from its own volume and sealed by the person who keeps the volume. Two searches for a repeal, by two people who never compared a line, finding the same nothing in the same places. The whole of the directive, all forty-one pages, tabbed where it matters and marked where a repeal would have to be. And one index. A reader who wishes us well will find what she needs in about forty breaths. A reader who wishes us ill will find it in about ninety, because she'll check everything twice on the way." He put the pen back behind his ear. "Whoever on that delegation asks to see the provision gets the shelf, in the order it stands, and a clerk to carry it across for them."

He looked at Cael then, as though only now noticing that the enrollee was standing at his counter with no business there at all.

Cael stayed where he was.

"Tell him the rest," said Karis, from the end of the counter. Her voice was not quite steady, and she was cross with it. "The part you told me."

Bracken looked at her, and then at Cael, for a long moment, as though deciding whether the rest was a thing a registrar said aloud in his own outer office in front of his own senior clerk. Then he decided.

"The rest," he said, "is that neither of us is needed now. Miss Karis found the clause. I filed it. Neither of those things carries any weight now. The shelf carries it." He straightened the four boxes again, though they had not moved. "That's what a defense is, Enrollee, when it's finished. It isn't a speech. It's a thing that goes on being true in a room where nobody who built it is standing."

The senior clerk lowered his pen to the page and began, very quietly, to write again.

Cael went out into the covered walk and stopped by the first pillar, because he had to stop. The sentence had gone into him somewhere under the breastbone and was still going.

He had been fourteen in the Denvash certification hall. A clerk behind a long counter there had written a word about him on a form, in about the time it takes to sneeze, and the word had followed him into every room he had entered since. It had stood there talking about him long after the clerk who wrote it had gone home to his supper. That was what files had always been, to him: true in rooms he was not in, kept by people who had long since gone home, and impossible to question or to stop.

Bracken had just taken that machine and turned it round and pointed it the other way.

He stood by the pillar for perhaps a minute. Then Karis came out of the registrar's office behind him with her notebook still clasped to her chest and her eyes very bright, and walked straight past him without stopping.

"I'm not crying," she said, over her shoulder. "Don't write that I was. I've got something in my eye from the wax."

He did not write it. He wrote, that evening, *Bracken closed the file. Karis has something in her eye from the wax*, which was not the same thing at all.

---

The Crown yard was full by the fourth bell, and it was a different kind of full from the fifteenth session.

That afternoon, a month back, the yard had come to see a champion tested. Today it had come to see a question answered, and the question was harder. Everybody on the tiers had watched Lira take the top line by lying with the truth, and everybody had gone home and thought about it, and most of them had come to the same sum Cael had: that a trick like that worked once. Fiske had had a month to learn it, and whatever Lira did today, she could not do that again.

Cael sat in his usual place on the east tier, a third of the way up, with Brom on his left and Karis on his right, and did not count the crowd. He had made himself that promise at breakfast. In a yard this size the counting would take all of him, and the hold would go, and he had a hold to keep. So he gave himself the boards and nothing else, and let everything outside the chalk look after itself.

Fiske came out first. She walked to her chalk at the north end of the boards with her hands clasped behind her back, as she always did, and stood there looking at the floor between the two marks the way a carpenter studies a frame. Her face was perfectly calm, and she had fought nobody on the top line for a month, and she looked, Cael thought, rested.

Lira came out a few breaths after. She walked to her chalk with no hurry at all and crouched and laid her hand flat on the boards, and held it there a moment longer than usual, and stood up.

"She's favouring it," said Brom, under his breath. "Already."

"Don't," said Karis.

"I'm only saying."

"She asked us not to. All of us. At breakfast." Karis did not take her eyes off the boards. "Say it in your head."

The safety instructor stood at the table beside the sand-glass, and Withrow sat in the west gallery, upright and white-haired, alone this time. In the faculty box the grey-bearded man from the law range was already leaning forward with his elbows on the rail.

At the word, Fiske did not give ground.

That was the first thing, and the whole yard saw it and made a sound, a low surprised breath, as a crowd does when a familiar piece of music goes wrong in the second bar. All season Fiske had opened every bout the same way, stepping back off her line to buy herself a minute of learning, but she had nothing to learn today. She took one step forward instead, and put her first declaration into the boards at once, a yard to the left of Lira's chalk, flat and fast, and the patch of oak there went slick.

Then a second, a yard to the right.

Lira did not burst. She walked. She went forward between the two slick patches on her own feet, at an ordinary pace, straight down the one lane Fiske had left open. Cael saw instantly that the lane was a lie. It ran straight at Fiske, and it got narrower as it went, and the third declaration was already going down across the far end of it.

Lira saw it too, a step later than he did. She stopped. She stood in a narrowing strip of honest wood with Fiske's slick on both sides of her and across her front, and every inch of the strip had been drawn, in eleven seconds, by a woman who knew exactly where Lira's feet liked to go and had simply removed all of those places before Lira got there.

"Burst," said Brom, very low. "Burst, burst."

She did not. She went sideways on her own feet, short, toward the last open edge. Fiske was already there. Her flat hand touched Lira's shoulder before Lira's foot had settled.

"Force," said the table. "One."

On the tiers the breath went out of eight hundred people at once, and somebody behind Cael said, satisfied, *well, that's that*.

Karis wrote one line in her notebook. Cael read it upside down: *One. F. needs no minute now. L. spends nothing.*

"Why won't she burst?" said Brom. "Why didn't she—"

"Because she's counting," said Cael. "She's got so many, and the hip's got so many, and they're not the same number. She won't spend one on an exchange she can't win."

"She's one down."

"I know."

The second exchange was over almost before it had begun.

At the word, Lira burst.

She did not burst away from anything. There was nothing yet to burst away from; the boards between them were clean, because Fiske had not had time to put anything on them. She burst straight forward from the chalk at the word itself, one half-breath of Wind, and the landing beat set her down two strides from Fiske, on clean oak, before Fiske's first declaration had finished gathering.

Fiske had seen it coming, of course; she had seen everything coming all season. But seeing a thing coming and having somewhere to put a slick floor in front of it are not the same. Lira had come in before there was any floor to close. Fiske turned the half-built declaration into a plain short delivery instead, the way a carpenter turns a half-cut joint into a different joint, and it caught Lira on the outside of the left arm, hard, and Lira's right hand was already flat on Fiske's ribs.

"Wind," said the table. "One each."

The yard came up off the benches and sat down again.

"There," said Brom, with his fists against his mouth. "There, there."

Cael was not looking at the touch. He was looking at Lira's leading hip as she walked back to her chalk, and at the way she put her weight down through it, carefully, as if testing a stair in the dark.

One burst gone. She had come in at the word with the cheapest burst there was, the one with no evasion in it, and it had bought her a touch. But Fiske had seen it, and Fiske would not let it work twice.

Karis wrote: *Two. L. goes before there is anything to go round. One each. That door's shut now too.*

---

Lira walked back to her chalk and did not look at the hip, because looking at it would have told the north end of the boards everything.

It was not bad yet: only the old line, crest to inner knee, lit up thin and bright from the one burst, the way a wire lights when you run too much through it. She could carry that; she had carried it on the foundry floor for two winters with a ladle of slag in her hands. What she could not carry was not knowing what came next, and she did not know, and neither, she was fairly sure, did Fiske.

Fiske was at her chalk with her hands behind her back, looking at the boards.

She knows everything I showed her, Lira thought. She's got the sequence and all four lies. She's got the half-step. She's got the walk-in from the foundry. She's had a month with all of it, and she's the best on this hill at learning a person. And I just showed her a new thing, and she's had it for thirty breaths, and that's long enough for her.

So: nothing new. Nothing at all. Let her wait for it.

At the word, Fiske laid a line.

Not a patch. A line: one long narrow declaration straight down the middle of the boards, from Fiske's chalk toward Lira's, slick as glass, so that the straight road between them was gone before Lira's weight had come off her heels. There would be no coming in at the word again, because Fiske had shut that door the moment she saw it open.

Lira did not move.

She stood on her chalk and let Fiske look at her. She gave her nothing to read: no lean, no shift, no breath held too long. It cost. The hip wanted to be moving; standing still on it was its own kind of load. She stood anyway, and watched Fiske's eyes, and saw them go to the places on the boards where Lira had always liked to go, the arc on the left, the fold, the turn, one after another, like a clerk checking that every door on a corridor was locked.

Then Fiske began to close them, one by one, without waiting.

That was new. Last time Fiske had placed where Lira was going. Now she was placing where Lira might go, all of it, every likely place on the boards, before Lira had chosen any of them. A patch on the left. A patch at the fold. A patch where the turn would have come. It was expensive, since every declaration cost Fiske something, and she was spending them like a woman throwing coins into a well. But every one she spent made the floor smaller.

Lira waited until there were three places left, and then two.

When the ring closed to the size of a hearthrug she burst out of it. She went backward, the one way she never went, over the last narrow gap between two patches, and the landing beat set her down on clean wood a stride beyond the ring with her heel a finger's breadth from Fiske's slick. The hip took it. Something in the hip went bright and hot and stayed that way.

Two, she thought. That's two.

Fiske looked at her across the boards, and Lira saw her count it too.

They spent the rest of the glass like that. Fiske closed and Lira waited; Fiske closed again and Lira slid one foot an inch on honest oak and Fiske had to decide whether the inch meant anything. Neither of them came near enough to touch, and up on the tiers somebody began a slow handclap and was hushed by the people around him. Lira did not hear it, because she was listening to the hip, and to the sand.

"Even," said the table. "No touch. Third exchange."

One each, and one even. One to go.

Lira walked back to her chalk on a leg that had begun, very slightly, to argue with her about which way was down.

The arithmetic of the fourth was simple, and everybody in the yard knew it. If the fourth ended even, the bout was drawn, and a drawn bout kept the holder on the top line, and the holder was Lira. Fiske could not wait. Fiske had to take it.

Which meant, Lira thought, standing on the chalk, that for the first time in their two bouts Fiske was the one who had to come.

So let her come. Don't burst. Don't run anything. Make the floor hers and stand in the middle of it, and make her walk across it.

At the word, Fiske made the floor hers.

She did it quickly and completely, with a kind of terrible patience. A patch, and a patch, and a patch, closer each time, round Lira in a slow spiral, until Lira stood on a square of clean oak about the size of a table, with Fiske's slick on every side of it, gleaming faintly in the afternoon light like ice on a pond. There was no way off it on foot. A burst would take her off it, and Fiske knew exactly how many bursts the hip had left, because Fiske could count as well as anybody, and the answer was one, perhaps, and a bad one.

Then Fiske stopped.

She stood at the edge of her own slick, a long stride away, with her hands loose at her sides, and she waited.

Lira understood. Fiske was not going to come across the oil to her. She had built a cage and put Lira in it and now she was going to wait at the bars for Lira to spend the last burst getting out, and then hit her in the landing beat, where everybody in the Copper column now knew a Wind fighter stood still. And if Lira did not burst, Fiske would wait until the sand was nearly gone and then come, at the last moment, when there would be no time left for anything but whatever Lira had kept.

It was the right plan, and Lira would have made the same one.

The sand ran.

It was the longest minute she had ever stood through.

She stood on the table of clean oak and did not burst. The hip burned. It had gone past bright now into something dull and deep and wide, a heat that filled the whole socket and spread down into the thigh, and it wanted, every moment, to be shifted. She did not shift it. Any shift was a lean, and a lean was a word, and Fiske was reading every word. So she stood on it, square, the way she had stood at the board at the fifth bell for three weeks with her breathing held level, and let it burn.

On the tiers the noise had stopped, and eight hundred people sat without moving. She could hear the sand. She could hear, from a long way off, a barge on the river giving its long note, and nobody answering.

Fiske waited. Her face did not change. But Lira, watching her eyes, saw them go once, quickly, to the glass on the table, and come back.

That's your tell, Lira thought, with a strange clear calm. You've never had to watch the sand before. You've always won before the sand mattered.

With perhaps ten breaths left in the glass, Fiske came.

She came straight, as she had come at the end of their first bout: one plain heavy delivery, built and committed, the Force that every other fighter on the sheet used, coming across her own slick on feet that knew exactly where the oil lay. There was nowhere to go from it. Lira could not step back; behind her was slick. She could not step aside; slick. She could burst, and land, and stand still in front of the one woman on the bluff who hit still things best.

She went forward, onto the oil.

Not with Wind. On her feet. She stepped off the edge of her clean square onto Fiske's slick on purpose, the leading foot first, and let it go.

The casting floor at the foundry had been greased with tallow at every shift change, so that the moulds would slide, and every apprentice who had ever worked it had gone down hard on it in the first week, and every one who stayed had learned the trick. You did not fight a slick floor. Fighting it was what put you on your back. You let it carry you, low, with your weight over your knees and your arms loose, and you steered with the ball of the foot and you stopped yourself with the other one, where you chose, on something that held.

Lira let it carry her.

She went in under Fiske's delivery low and sliding, faster than any step, and the delivery went over her shoulder with a noise like a door slamming in a high wind. She had perhaps a body's length of slick and then Fiske's own feet, and Fiske's own patch of honest oak where she had been standing to build. Lira's back foot found the edge of it. She stopped herself there.

The stop went through the leading hip like a nail.

It did something in there she had never felt it do. Something gave, deep and sideways, with a small sound she heard inside her own head rather than with her ears. Her right hand was already moving. It landed flat on Fiske's ribs, under the arm, inside the recovery, as Fiske's weight was still going forward over the place where Lira had been.

"Wind," said the table, into a silence like a held breath. "Two to one. Bout."

She did not hear the yard come up. She heard it afterward, the way you hear thunder after the flash, a wall of noise from every tier at once that hit the lecture range and came back. In the moment she heard nothing but her own breath, and felt nothing but the floor under her right hand, because she had gone down on one knee on the oak at the edge of Fiske's patch and could not, for the moment, get up.

Fiske was standing over her, and her face had changed.

It was not a beaten face, of which Lira had seen plenty. It was the face Fiske had worn on the step a month ago, when she had said all the things she had carried for two years, and Lira had known then what it was, and knew now.

"You didn't run it," said Fiske. "Not once."

"You'd have shut it."

"Every step." Fiske looked down at the slick going dull and dry on the boards around them, fading back into plain oak. "You stood still for a minute in my yard. Nobody's ever done that."

"It's not your yard," said Lira, from her knee. "Not today."

When Fiske held out her hand, Lira took it, and Fiske did not pull. She only held it steady, so that Lira could use it to stand, and Lira used it, and stood, and found that the leading leg would take her weight if she asked it politely and not otherwise.

"The nineteenth," said Fiske. "I'll be on the tier for it. I haven't sat on a tier for a final in two years. I'm told you can see everything from up there."

"You can't see the far mark from the east side," said Lira. "Sit north."

Fiske considered this gravely, as she considered everything.

"North," she said, and let go of Lira's hand, and walked away along the foot of the tiers with her hands clasped behind her back.

---

It took Cael most of a quarter-hour to get down to the floor. When he got there Lira was sitting on the bottom step of the east tier with the leading leg straight out in front of her. The safety instructor was crouched beside her with two fingers pressed into the side of the hip, asking short questions and getting shorter answers.

"Can you put weight on it?"

"Yes."

"Is it worse going up or going down?"

"Down."

"Does it click?"

"No," said Lira. Then, after a moment, honestly: "Once. On the oil."

The instructor sat back on his heels and looked at her with the face of a man who has spent a working lifetime being lied to by fighters and has come to prefer it when they stop.

"Ice tonight. Ice in the morning. Nothing on it for four days, and I mean nothing, not a stair you can avoid, not a turn on that floor of yours. Then come and show me how it walks." He stood up. "It's a strain, and it's deep, and it'll mend. It won't mend by the nineteenth. You'll fight on it."

"I know," said Lira.

He went. Brom had arrived, and was standing a pace away with his hands opening and closing at his sides, looking at the hip exactly the way Lira had said he would look at it, like a man looking at a sick dog. Karis was beside him with the notebook shut.

Lira looked up at the three of them.

"Nobody," she said, "talks about it."

"I wasn't going to," said Brom.

"You were going to. You were going to look at it all through supper." She held up a hand, and Brom took it, and pulled her up very carefully onto her good leg, and she leaned on his arm for a moment without pretending she was not. "Tomorrow's yours. Go and beat Merrick. Then we'll see."

Cael stood back and said nothing at all, since he had nothing in his notebook for this and did not want anything. He had watched her stand on a square of oak for a minute and choose, every second of it, not to spend the one thing she had left, and then spend something she had never once been told she owned.

Karis opened her notebook at last and showed him the fourth line, which she had written on the tier while the yard was still roaring.

*Four. One very long minute at the end, which their first meeting had never needed. L. stands still. F. has to come. L. goes onto the oil. Two to one.*

"I didn't know she could do that," said Karis quietly. "Go onto it."

"Neither did she," said Cael. "Not till she had to."

Lira, three steps up, leaning on Brom, turned her head.

"I've done it a thousand times," she said. "In the casting shed. With a ladle. Nobody ever paid to watch." She considered. "It's worse without the ladle. You don't know what to do with your hands."

They took her up to the residence between them, slowly, one step at a time, by the long way round that had no stairs in it. Karis walked in front and opened every door before they reached it, and nobody talked about the hip. Brom talked about the slow handclap on the north tier, and whose it had been, and what he would do to the man if he ever found him. Lira laughed at him twice, and both times stopped laughing quickly, and both times nobody remarked on it.

That night the leg went up on the chair with ice on it. Brom, for once, did not go down to the post, because it was the night before his own semifinal and Karis had stood in the passage with her arms folded until he gave in. He sat on the common room floor instead with his back against Lira's settle, and Karis sat at the table with the marbled book and wrote the coats.

*Two and two at the eighth bell. On the bell. Nobody at the bottom of the hill knows anything happened here today.*

"Good," said Brom, from the floor. "Let's keep it that way."

# Chapter 52 — The Exhibit

Ilsev did not know how to watch a fight, so she read the yard instead.

She had decided that on the stair coming up, and it had served her well enough for two exchanges.

A yard was a document like any other. It had a heading and a body and a hand at the foot that signed it, and if you read it in order, and did not skip ahead to the parts that excited you, it would tell you a great deal about itself.

The heading was the table: three faculty, one standing and two behind her with their hands clasped, and the glass between them on its little stand, and under the edge of the stand two folded sheets with seals on them. She had watched the registrar carry them out across the empty boards and lay them there, square to the edge, before the yard had filled. He had walked away again without a word to anybody.

Nobody had told her what they were. She did not need telling. A man does not put a sealed paper under a sand-glass before a contest unless he wishes it to be seen that the paper came first.

She approved of that more than she could have said aloud.

The body was the crowd. More than a thousand people, she judged, on stone tiers open to the sky, and for most of the first two exchanges they had made one long continuous sound with no words in it.

Its pitch had shifted all along: up for the girl's entrance, down when her foot met the chalk, uncertain all at once at the first *even*, and flat and loud together when the girl went onto her back.

Ilsev could not have said what any of it meant on the boards, but she could hear that the sound was a record of something, kept by a thousand clerks at once, none of whom knew they were keeping it.

The signature at the foot belonged to the woman in front of her.

Almost directly below Ilsev's knees, in the north tier's front row, the champion had sat through both exchanges without moving.

Her hands lay folded on her thigh, and her broad back was perfectly still, and only once, when the girl went down, had her head turned a quarter-inch and come back. Of all the signatures Ilsev had read in her working life, few had been steadier.

Beside Ilsev, on her right, the Archmarshal sat with his notebook closed on his knee and his pen lying across the cover, exactly where he had laid it when he sat down.

He watched the boards the way he watched a sitting, at an even pace, giving each part its share, and Ilsev did not try to read him. She had stopped trying on the second day of the road. A colleague's face was not her question.

Nor was the boy on the east tier, a third of the way up, beside the Ember girl with the ink on her fingers. Ilsev knew exactly where he was sitting. She had known it since she sat down, as she knew where the doors were. She did not look at him.

Her own question was a long way off by now. Four days ago she had filled a form in eleven minutes and laid it in a tray, and since then it had gone down the bluff in a courier's satchel and up the river, one level higher than the last, to a room she would never see.

She had put it down when she filed it. She did not pick it up again here, on a warm stone bench, with a contest going on below her that had nothing to do with it. A woman who carried her filed matters about with her into every room soon stopped being able to see the room.

So she looked at the room.

In all her years of hearings she had read the word *Wind* on more forms than she could count, and had never once watched it.

On paper it was a Path like any other, a line in a register, a tier and a rank and a date. Down there on the oak it was a slight young woman with a bad leg who could, when she chose, simply not be where you had been looking.

Ilsev had seen it happen twice now, and both times her eye had gone to the empty place a moment after the girl had left it, like a reader whose finger has slipped a line. She did not much enjoy the feeling. She suspected it was the whole point.

It occurred to her that a registry clerk, three years ago, had written this girl down in about the time it took to read a short page, and that nobody had read the page again since.

She marked the thought and went no further with it. It was not a finding. It was an observation about a form, and forms were her trade, and the form was not in front of her.

The table turned the glass.

At first she thought nothing was happening, because the girl did not go in.

She stood at the edge of the big young man's reach and waited, and when he came to her she simply stepped back, on her own feet, and let him come again. The crowd did not like it. Ilsev heard it not liking it: a restless low murmur, like a hall at a long reading.

Then the young man reached her guard and hit it, hard, and she took it on her forearms and went back another step, and the murmur stopped.

Backward the girl went, the whole circuit of the floor, and then the whole circuit a second time. Every so often the young man caught up with her and struck, and every time she let him, on her arms and her shoulders, and gave ground, and he followed. She was not running, as even Ilsev could see. She was walking him.

Somewhere past the third minute the sound of the yard changed again. It did not get louder. It got slower, and lower, and more careful, the way a reading room sounds when a whole table has found the same line in the same moment.

In front of her the champion's folded hands had come apart.

---

The first step told Cael everything, because it went backward.

Lira did not step backward. Three years of watching, and never once by choice. Backward was where you went when you had been beaten to a place, and she was never beaten to places. She went sideways, or through, or round, or she was simply already elsewhere.

Every page he had on her said so, nine pages of it, in a hundred different ways. The Wind did not make her fast. It made her early. She lived in the sliver of time other fighters spent deciding, and she spent it in tiny coins, and every coin bought her a square of floor that her opponent had not finished thinking about.

Now she stepped back off her chalk on her own two feet, and waited.

Brom came, and she gave him ground. When he reached her she put up her guard and let him hit it, and the crack of iron on forearm went round the yard, and she gave him more ground.

There was no burst and no Wind, nothing that cost her a held breath, only feet, and arms, and the long slow curve of the oak's rim behind her.

She walked him round the whole floor like that. Then she began again.

Cael's own forearms ached, watching. He knew exactly what Brom's iron felt like arriving on a guard; he had a month of bruises from the wash-house to prove it.

Lira had none of the things that make that bearable, because she had never once trained to be struck, and her whole body had been built round a single idea, which was that being hit meant something had already gone wrong. Each blow she caught travelled up her forearms and lodged in her shoulders. And every backward step came down on the leading leg, on a hip ten days old.

"She's spending him," said Karis, very low. "Isn't she. She's paying for his clock with her own arms."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Longer than he can. That's the whole bet."

The glass was half run before the yard caught on. Cael heard the moment it did, because the noise did not rise. It sank, and thickened, and turned careful, like a crowd watching somebody carry too many plates.

Then Brom slowed.

Cael had been waiting for it since the first backward step, with his own count running silently under everything else. After every committed strike Brom re-squared his feet before the next. All season that had taken him four counts. Now, with the glass more than half gone, it took him five.

The fifth count was what Lira had walked round the oak twice to buy.

She went into it on her third burst. She was behind his lead leg before the re-squaring had finished, and her heel went into the back of his knee, and the knee bent the way knees must, and Brom sat down on the boards with a thump that Cael felt through the stone.

"Iron Skin, down," called the table. "To Wind, the cleaner."

The tiers came up off their seats all together. Karis was up with them, both fists clenched, saying "Three, that's three," to nobody.

By Cael's private count the exchange had run four minutes and eleven seconds. He stayed sitting and watched Lira walk back to her chalk.

She had stopped pretending. The leading leg took her weight in two halves, and then in three.

On the way she laid the flat of her hand on her own thigh, briefly, the way somebody touches a wall in the dark. He had never once seen her do that on a floor. It meant there was nothing left over for hiding, and she had decided that hiding had stopped being worth its price.

Brom had seen it.

He was up and back at his own chalk, rubbing the back of his knee. Cael watched it come over him: the eyes going to her thigh, half a breath of standing quite still, and then something in the shoulders settling, the way a man's shoulders settle when he has finished an argument with himself.

*He'll go for it*, Cael thought.

The next thought came straight behind it, and he could not have stopped it if he had tried. *He should.*

---

The fourth exchange lasted eleven seconds.

Brom came for the hip. Not round it, and not near it: at it.

There was nothing hidden in it anywhere. Hiding it would have been an insult.

More than a thousand people were watching, and three faculty, and the Compact, and the two of them had fought each other honestly two years running. The one thing neither could have lived with was to fight this last one crooked.

Rooke's cohort had spent a term of bruises teaching him to leave a chalk fast, and he left it now as fast as he had ever left anything. His line went in low and to her left. It went at the leading side, where every Wind fighter alive has to come back to earth.

It was the right thing to do, and Cael would have done it himself. It said, plainly, to her and to the whole yard: *I know where it hurts. I won't pretend I don't. Beat me through that, and the bout is yours.*

Lira spent the fourth burst.

Cael felt it go in his own leg, like a bell he had been hearing for three years struck once more somewhere close.

Her knees gave and the oak breathed under her. She went light and airless and somewhere else, and Brom's line arrived exactly where it had been sent, and there was nobody there to take it, and she came down out of the Wind.

The rest of the bout lived inside the landing beat.

That beat was the Wind's one fixed price, half a breath of being locked upright and unable to turn. Cael had built his entire log round it. It had never been shortened by anyone, not by her and not by him, and Brom had known about it for two years. He turned into it and was waiting for her.

By rights that was the end of it. It wasn't, for two reasons, and Cael saw both together.

The first belonged to Brom. The third exchange had held him on the iron for four minutes and eleven seconds, and the turn of the glass had not given that back; eleven seconds into the fourth, his turn came on the fifth count instead of the fourth, a hair late.

The other was Lira's.

She did not cheat the beat. Nobody could, and she did not try. She let it run its whole length. What she chose, before she ever left the floor, was where she would come down, and she came down on the bad hip.

As she had known it must, the leg buckled, half an inch and not a hair more. And the fold took her sideways, out of his line, while the beat was still locked and running. She never had to change direction, because the injury changed it for her.

His answer cut through nothing.

She was striking already, an open palm cracking into the side of his knee in the very instant his weight came down onto it.

Over he went, heavily, rolling off the shoulder as his cohort had drilled him to fall. A safety seat's arm was up before the roll was done. The adjudicating instructor stood.

"Wind," she called. "Clean. Two touches to one. The bout."

The sound that came down off the tiers was one Cael had no instrument for, and he had spent two years building instruments for rooms.

He did not stand. He was half ashamed of that later. His legs had simply declined, and he let them.

Beside him Karis was on her feet with both hands pressed flat over her mouth, and the folded copy crushed in one of them. On the oak Lira had come down to one knee, and not to bow; the leading leg simply refused her.

The call was still in the air when Brom got up. He crossed the boards on a knee that had just been knocked sideways, and took her forearm in his hand, and pulled, and she came up onto her good leg.

Then neither of them let go. They stood holding on to each other's arms in the middle of the oak, and Cael counted three seconds without meaning to.

A yard that had been roaring fell silent to watch.

The faculty walked out onto the oak. Only then did the tiers find their voice again.

---

Ilsev had seen a great many ceremonies. She read this one as she would have read any other: for what it put on the record, and for who put it there.

The chancellor put almost nothing. She crossed the oak from the west gallery in a plain dark coat, with the porter behind her carrying the crown on a faded cushion.

She stopped in front of the two young people. She said the old formula of the Copper Crown, a dozen words that had plainly been said in that yard for more than a hundred years, and then she said nothing more.

Ilsev, who had sat through a great many chancellors, admired that very much. It is hard to say nothing more when a thousand people are waiting for you to say something.

The registrar put the record.

He came to the table and lifted the glass's stand, and took out the two sealed sheets that had lain under it since before the yard filled. He broke the seal on one. He read it out in a dry, carrying voice with no colour in it at all, the voice of a man reading the tolls on a bridge.

"Wind, Copper, Rank Two. Seeded the twenty-second. Opening bout drawn by lot, and won. Top line from the fifteenth session. Ten bouts unbeaten. None lost. Certified at the first bell this morning."

Then he did a thing Ilsev had never seen a registrar do in public. He took the pen from behind his ear and wrote a line at the foot of the sheet, there at the table, with the whole yard watching his hand. He blotted it on his own cuff.

"Final, the nineteenth of Reaping, before a witnessed panel. Won." He looked up from the sheet. "Eleven bouts unbeaten. None lost."

He folded the sheet and stepped back.

Ilsev sat very still. A record certified in the morning, before a result anybody could know; and the result added in the afternoon, at the foot, in the same hand, before a thousand witnesses.

Nothing had been rewritten. Nothing had been made after the fact and dressed to look as if it came before.

You could see the seam between the two, and the seam was the proof. That sheet, she suspected, would stay with her.

Nobody in the yard knew what to do with it, since no crowd has ever learned to cheer a registrar.

Directly below Ilsev's knees, the champion unfolded her hands.

She brought them together once, alone, in the silence, and then again. From her seat Ilsev could see the knuckles whiten.

The sound went out across the oak, small and clear, half a breath before anybody else in the whole yard had moved. A heartbeat later the whole yard took it up from her, and the noise came down off the tiers like a falling wall.

The champion went on clapping, steadily, with her back straight. Ilsev looked at the back of her head for some time. Without the bracket sheet she could not have put a name to the woman, and had no need to. She had watched a signature go onto a document, and she knew one when she saw it.

The chancellor lifted the copper off its cushion and held it out.

The girl received it with both hands and left it off her head. She turned it once, looking down at it, and then held it a little away from her body, quite still, as a clerk holds a sheet he is about to hand up to the bench.

Then her eyes came up to the north tier.

It was one second, perhaps less. It came up the stone and went along the second row like a draught under a door, and Ilsev felt it go past her.

What it meant was not hers to say, and she could not have said it. On her right the Archmarshal did not move by so much as a finger, and his notebook stayed shut on his knee.

The girl looked down again at the thing in her hands, and the yard went on roaring, and Ilsev let the look go where it had come from.

At the table the registrar had put the second sealed sheet back under the glass's stand, unopened. Ilsev noticed that too.

It was the young man's record, made at the same bell, in the same hand, and it was every bit as true as the first.

Nobody would read it aloud today, and nobody would ever need to. But it had been made, and sealed, and laid there beside the other, so that whichever of them walked off the oak with the copper, the paper would already be waiting for both.

She had known clerks who would have thought the second sheet a waste of an hour. She would not have hired any of them.

The yard began to empty in the slow, untidy way of crowds that do not want to leave. The counsel stood up and stretched, and pressed both fists into the small of her back.

"Paper all the way down, this house," she said to Ilsev, not unkindly. "Even their prize-givings come with a seal."

"Yes," said Ilsev.

"You approve."

"I noticed it," said Ilsev, which was as far as she ever went with a colleague, and the counsel laughed and went off down the stair, talking to her clerk about supper.

Ilsev stayed in her seat a little longer and let the tier clear round her. The Archmarshal had not moved.

She did not ask whether he was coming down; she had worked beside him since the road, and had learned that he went where he went.

She picked up her case, and found that her legs had stiffened on the cold stone, and went down slowly, one step at a time, in good order.

---

Cael got down to the floor a quarter of an hour later, through a crowd that did not want to go home, and found Lira sitting on the oak where the crowd had left her, with the wing's duty practitioner kneeling at her hip and the crown in her lap.

"Seventh bell," Lira said, before he could ask anything. "Gault's office. He's filing for me, and Bracken's to bring the papers, and somebody from the delegation's been asked to witness." She winced as the practitioner pressed somewhere she did not like. "I can't do his stair. She won't let me try."

"I won't," said the practitioner, without looking up.

"So you go." Lira looked at him. "Somebody has to hear it read who knows what it cost. You'll remember every word of it, and you won't turn it into anything it isn't. Tell Gault I named you. He'll want it in writing, so write it."

Cael borrowed a pencil from the practitioner's case and a corner of her bandage paper, and Lira wrote three lines on it against her knee, in capitals, and signed them. He folded it into his coat.

"And Cael." She caught his sleeve as he straightened. "Don't stand at the front. Stand at the back, where you can see them all. That's what you're for."

---

The note from the assessment office reached Ilsev a little after the sixth bell, in the delegation's working room, while she was writing up the morning.

A half-sheet, folded once, in the wing's trained hand. *The assessment office files at the seventh bell, in the Magister's office, an application of the second class. The office invites the senior evaluation seat of the visiting delegation to attend as witness, at her discretion. — Gault.*

She read it twice. Then she set it squarely on the corner of the table and thought about it for exactly as long as it deserved, which was not long.

An application of the second class was an application to a body outside the house. She knew which application this had to be, on this evening, from this office.

A witness from the inspecting delegation would put the Compact's own seat on the face of the paper, wherever it went afterward. That was why she had been asked.

She had no objection to being used for that, provided she was used honestly.

A witness signed to what she saw, and to nothing else. If the filing was in order she would sign that she had been present. If it was not, she would say so, and the office would have learned something it was better to learn now.

Coat on, she went down the stair. The wing's passage was nearly empty at that hour, and her heels sounded on its boards more loudly than she liked. She thought, going along it, about the champion's hands coming together in a silent yard, and about the registrar's pen at the foot of a sealed sheet, and she noticed that she was looking forward to the next quarter of an hour in a way she rarely looked forward to anything with a form in it. She set the thought aside before she reached the door. A witness who arrives pleased has already decided what she is going to see.

The Magister's office was at the end of the wing, a square room with one window and the posted calibration card hanging on the wall beside the door, initialled in a column of small neat checks.

There were four people in it when she came in, which made it full.

Gault sat behind his desk with a sheaf before him. The registrar stood at its corner with his pen behind his ear and two sealed sheets in his hand. The wing's clerk sat at a sloped desk under the window. Against the back wall, hands clasped behind, the enrollee stood waiting.

Ilsev noted him the way she noted the card on the wall.

"The champion is with the wing's duty practitioner," Gault said, seeing her look. "Her hip will not take the stair. In her place she has named the enrollee to hear it. That is in order. I have entered it."

"Thank you," said Ilsev, and sat in the chair the clerk had set for her, and opened nothing.

Gault read the application out. Paper of that class had to be heard by its witness as well as signed by her, and he read it in exactly the voice he used for a column of instrument checks.

It was the registry's own long form, the one with the blue rule down the margin, and Ilsev could have recited its printed lines in her sleep.

First the house making the request, and its office. Then the request itself: that the practitioner named be brought before an Arbiter of the registry to be evaluated for advancement.

Then the ground, in the form's printed words, *advancement demonstrated, inconsistent with tier of record*. Then what stood behind it, in Gault's hand. The season, certified; the final, before a witnessed panel; and the panel's own account of what it had seen, attached.

Last, the station where the evaluation was sought, and the date of the girl's last classification. It was three years old.

She kept her case closed and both hands flat on it, and listened to the end.

When Gault finished, she asked two questions.

"The season record," she said. "Certified when? Before the final, or after?"

"Before," said the registrar. "At the first bell this morning. Sealed, and laid under the panel's glass before the yard filled. The result was added at the foot afterward, in my hand, in the yard, before the panel and the yard both."

"And the panel's account. Whose hand is it in? One of the panel's own, or a clerk's?"

"By the adjudicating instructor," said Gault. "In her own hand, at the table, before she left the yard. Both safety seats have countersigned it. The clerk has copied nothing."

Ilsev nodded once.

Those were the only two answers that mattered. A record certified after its result has been told the result. An account written by a clerk is an account of what the clerk was told.

Neither was true of this paper. The paper had been made in the right order, by the right hands, and anyone who lifted it, at any desk up the river, would find it so.

"I have no objection to note," she said. "I will record that I was present."

Her small upright hand went on the witness line, with the date beside it and the bell after. The clerk blotted it, and Gault initialled under her name. The registrar laid his two sheets on the application, square, and the clerk pinned them.

That was all of it: four minutes by the clock over the door, and no speeches. Had anybody looked in from the passage, they would not have known that anything had just ended.

Ilsev stood, and thanked the Magister, and nodded to the registrar.

At the door she passed the boy at the wall. She gave him the courtesy the card allowed, a small inclination of the head, and he returned it exactly.

His face told her nothing. She had not expected it to, and she did not try to make it.

Hearing a paper on a friend's behalf was a proper errand, and he had run it properly.

She went back up to the working room and wrote one line at the foot of her day's notes: *Witnessed, at the assessment office's invitation, an advancement filing in respect of a Wind practitioner. Filing in order.* Then she turned to the morning's sheets again and finished them.

---

Cael stayed against the wall until Ilsev's steps had gone away down the passage, and only then took his hands from behind his back.

It had been like watching a hinge turn.

All term Gault had said the same thing in nearly the same words. Instruments and consistency; nothing else, to anybody.

Cael had spent months unable to tell whether the man was for him or against him, and standing at the back of that crowded little office he saw at last that he had been asking a useless question. Gault was not for anybody.

Gault was for what the needles said. This afternoon the needles had said a certain thing about a girl on a hurt leg, and Gault had sat down at his desk at the seventh bell and done precisely what they told him to do, in the right order, with the right witness, and then initialled it.

Fiske had told Lira, beside the board, weeks ago, what stood between her and an Arbiter for two whole years. One signature that was not her own. Nobody had ever picked up the pen.

Somebody had picked it up today. Cael had just watched him blot it.

Past the clerk he went, and down the wing's stair.

He had watched her take the copper from the east tier, and for half a season he had wondered how she would hold it when the day came. That night, after everything else, he wrote the answer in six words.

*She held it like an exhibit.*

Under it he wrote two lines more.

*The crown doesn't win you anything. It makes somebody with a pen spend an afternoon on you. Fiske worked that out over two years and two crowns, and then walked across a floor and told it to the one person who could use it.*

By the eighth bell the porters had swept the yard and gone, and it was empty but for the two of them.

On the east stair's bottom step Cael halted. What was happening on the oak did not belong to him.

At the oak's centre, alone, sat Lira. A cold pad was strapped round her hip, and the bad leg lay out flat before her like something she had put down and forgotten.

The duty practitioner had seen to that and gone. The crown was lying on the oak beside her, a little way off, where she had set it down and not picked it up again.

Brom came out through the south door with a bag of ice bound round his knee. He crossed the whole length of the floor at the pace the knee allowed, which was not much of a pace, and lowered himself down beside her.

He sat on the loser's side, with a decent gap between them. Neither of them owned an older habit.

Neither spoke for some time.

"Two years I've been trying to solve you," said Brom at last.

"And?"

Brom looked up at the empty tiers. He rolled the shoulder that had taken most of her afternoon, slowly, as if consulting it. He was a man who liked to agree with a thing properly, not just give way to it, and he took his time.

"Third one someday," he said.

Lira laughed. Laughing hurt, and the hurt set her off again, until she had to prop herself on one flat palm.

Cael stayed on his step. It was theirs.

Up on the north tier, two rows from the front, somebody was still sitting.

Cael had kept the north tier out of his mind since the walk-out and had not let it back in, so he did not know how long the man had been there alone. It was the Archmarshal, in his plain grey, with the little notebook open now on his knee.

While Cael watched, the man wrote a single line. He closed the notebook. The pen went back into his coat. He rose, took the north stair down, and left, without a glance at the floor.

Cael stepped off the stair and crossed the oak toward his friends. The residence steps were coming, and the boy with the iced knee could carry nobody up them.

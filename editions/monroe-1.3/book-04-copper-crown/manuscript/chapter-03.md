# Chapter 3 — Same Terms

The training hall had been swept twice before the first bell, once by the second-years whose turn it was and once by Hobb, who had not been asked.

Cael came in by the side door with his wraps already on and found the gallery full. He had expected that, and it still stopped him in the doorway for a breath. At Greyvane two hundred people was nearly everybody, and nearly everybody was here: on the benches along both long walls, three deep at the rail, sitting on the window ledges with their boots hanging down. The first-years were crammed into the corner by the door, where somebody had told them they could see best and had been wrong. The tout from the river town was moving along the back of the north bench taking wagers in crusts and kitchen duty, quietly, with a great air of not doing anything of the kind.

Three chairs stood at the head of the floor behind a narrow table. Wray had the middle one. Quenna sat on her left with a fresh pad squared in front of her and her pen laid across it, as she had sat at the head of every sitting he had ever given. The right-hand chair was empty, and stayed empty. The terms said two, and Wray did not fill chairs for show.

Hobb stood at the rail by the north mark with his arms folded. He nodded once when Cael came in, and did not do it again.

Lira and Brom and Karis had the end of the front bench. Lira had her elbows on her knees and her eyes on the floor, not on him. Brom had his pocket book out. Karis had nothing in her hands at all, which Cael noticed, and understood, and was grateful for.

Edran was already at his mark.

He stood with his weight low and easy, the off arm carried a little forward, close in along the line of his body, and he was not building anything yet. He looked up as Cael crossed the boards, and there was nothing in his face but attention.

Wray stood.

"Exhibition bout," she said, and her voice went to the back of the hall without her raising it. "Under full sanction, before the faculty, on the record, as both parties asked. Eight exchanges on my count. Standings rules. Each exchange goes to the cleaner touch, or I call it even. Any structure on the body is lawful. Any fragment or declaration the record already carries is lawful. Nothing to the head, nothing to the joints, nothing after my word." She looked at Edran, and then at Cael. "Either of you may stop it. I may stop it. If I stop it, it's stopped, and nobody argues with me afterward in the yard."

"Understood, Instructor," said Edran.

"Understood, Instructor."

Quenna picked up her pen.

Cael went to his mark and set his feet and let his shoulders come down, and ran his prices once more in his head, quickly, the way he checked the knots on his wraps.

*Three bursts free. The fourth bills the hip in the morning. The read works and tells me nothing about what he means. No Ember. Compression only if he puts a push in me I can't get out of.*

*What I don't know: what he bought with the reach.*

He had stopped trying, in the last week, to work it out from the rail. Lira had been right. He had been trying to fight the man he had beaten, and that man was gone. The one at the far mark had spent a whole term taking himself apart and putting himself back together on purpose, an hour at a time, and hating every hour that he needed it, and the only way to find out what he had built was to stand in front of it.

Across the boards Edran rolled his shoulders once and settled. Cael ran the gaze over him from the floor up, the way he would have run it over a stranger in the Ironyard: feet a little wider than they had been in the autumn, the weight even across both, the knees soft. The breath was high and slow. The off arm hung forward and close, the hand open, the forearm turned a little out so that its edge faced the line between them. The lead arm was the same as ever. Every layer agreed with every other, and none of them told him anything he had not seen from the rail.

He had wanted, for a whole week, to come to the mark with an answer already in his pocket. He did not have one. He had a question and a set of prices, and a friend on the front bench who had told him to fight the man who turned up. He found, standing there with two hundred people waiting, that the question was steadier to stand on than an answer would have been. An answer could be wrong. A question could only be asked.

He breathed in and held half of it, and let the rest go.

"First exchange," said Wray. "Begin."

---

Edran did not come forward.

That was the first thing. The old Edran had come off his mark almost at once, faster than the textbook distance, with a shell already bright along his leading forearm, driving it out to be struck. This one came a step and then let Cael come to him, with the structure forming on the lead arm only as Cael closed: a hard pale shell like ice skinning over a trough, there and finished in the space of a blink.

Cael read it. The read laid it on the skin of his forearms a pace and a half out, the weight of the shell and the line it was set along, a fraction before his eyes had it. The gaze had the rest: Edran's breath, held high; his rear foot, flat; the off arm close in, doing nothing. Everything agreed.

He went where the shell would burst. That was the old answer, and it had been a good one. A Glass shell struck hard enough shed the force outward in a spray, and for the length of the spray the arm behind it was bare. He feinted high, and Edran met the feint with the shell, and it burst in a spray of glitter, and Cael was already moving into the space where the bare arm would be.

The arm was not bare.

A second shell had come up on the off arm, which had been carried forward and close in exactly so that it could, and it was already set across the space Cael had moved into. He felt it on the read a breath too late to stop. He turned his shoulder and took the shell on the outside of his forearm, where it burst and stung, and Edran's lead hand came round inside it with the edge of the forearm turned out and laid itself along his ribs. It barely touched, but it was clean.

"Touch," said Wray. "Edran. One."

Somebody at the back of the north bench made a sound that was half a cheer and half surprise.

Cael went back to his mark with his forearm humming and the gaze running over what had happened, looking for the mistake. He could not find one. He had read the shell right, the burst right, and the bare arm right, and moved at the right speed. And the off arm had been there anyway, because it had never been out of place to begin with.

"Second exchange. Begin."

This time he did not go in. He let Edran come to him, and stayed at the edge of the read, and watched.

It took him most of the exchange. Edran came on in short steps, built a shell, let Cael strike at it, shed it, built the next on the off arm, and stepped on. Cael gave ground and slipped and gave ground and let the shells burst on his guard. Twice he saw an opening and moved at it, and both times the off arm's structure was already standing where he arrived. It did not come up to meet him. It was simply there, finished, half a beat before he was.

He tried changing his own timing. He went in early on the third shell, before it burst, to catch the off arm on its way. The off arm was not on its way anywhere. It had never left. He tried going in late, after the spray, when the off arm had closed and might be committed, and found the lead already building again behind it. Edran did not hurry. He did not need to. He gave Cael the floor, a step at a time, and took it back a step at a time, and kept his breath high and slow the whole while.

Every read he had was correct. Every read arrived at the right place half a beat after Edran did.

He understood it in the middle of a slip, with a shell bursting off his shoulder and the spray stinging the side of his neck. Edran had given up reach. With the off arm carried forward and close, it could not go out far to meet anything. But it was always nearly in place already. When a shell burst on the lead, the off arm did not have to come from somewhere; it only had to close the last finger's width. That was what the hand of reach had bought, and it was exactly the half-beat Cael's eyes needed. Edran had not hidden his seam. He had moved his recovery to the place where Cael's reading ended.

A correct read that came too late, Cael thought, was a wrong read, only more expensive.

"Time," said Wray. "Even."

---

He went back to his mark and did not look at the bench, though he could feel Lira not looking at him with the whole side of her face.

Reading the shell would not do it. The shell told him where Edran was, and Edran's whole new stance was built to be somewhere else half a beat later. He had to read something that moved before the shell did.

"Third exchange. Begin."

Edran came on, and Cael let him, and this time he did not watch the lead. He let the lead arm go to the edge of the gaze, where it could be weather. He put his attention on the off arm, carried forward and close, and on the shoulder above it.

The read gave it to him on the second shell. A fraction before the off arm closed its last finger's width, the weight came off the rear foot and into the off shoulder, and the read laid that shift on the skin of his forearms like a hand pressing. It was small, and it was early. It came before the shell burst, before anything Cael's eyes could see, and it told him the off arm was about to move across.

And when it moved across, it left the side it had come from.

He held half a breath and let the Wind take his feet.

The first burst carried him low and left, not toward the shell or the opening behind it, but to the side the off arm was leaving, which for the length of half a beat had nothing on it at all. He came out of the burst inside Edran's guard on the wrong side, the side nobody had been watching, and paid the landing beat locked and still. He laid his palm flat on Edran's breastbone from there, without any weight behind it, because he did not need any.

"Touch," said Wray. "Cael. One each."

The hall's noise came up and broke over the floor. He walked back to his mark and counted. *One.* His hip had said nothing yet.

Edran stood at his own mark a moment longer than he needed to, looking at the place where Cael had come through. Then he looked at Cael, and something in his face sharpened, and Cael knew that Edran had already started working on it.

"Fourth exchange. Begin."

Edran came on as before, and Cael waited for the shift, and the read gave it to him: weight off the rear foot, into the off shoulder, the off arm about to move across. He went with it, left and low, to the side it was leaving.

The off arm did not move.

Edran had put the weight into the shoulder and held it there and built his next shell on the lead instead, the old way, and Cael came out of his slip into exactly the place the lead shell was waiting. He got his forearm up. The shell burst on it with a crack like a branch breaking under snow, and the off arm, which had been loaded and still, came across now, late on purpose, and laid its edge along his flank.

"Touch. Edran. Two to one."

He went back to his mark with both forearms aching and his breath short, and he made himself think instead of feeling.

Edran had learned the read in one exchange. He had felt where Cael came through and given him the same shift with nothing behind it, and Cael had believed it, because it was true weight, honestly placed. The read never lied about weight. It only ever lied about what weight was for.

*Read the shift. Then wait for the arm.* It would cost him time. It would cost him the early arrival, which was the whole of the advantage. But a shift with nothing after it was a feint, and a feint could only be known by what came next.

"Fifth exchange. Begin."

It went long. Edran pressed, and Cael gave ground slipping, and twice the shift came and twice he waited, and twice it was nothing. On the third the off arm moved. It moved fast, but Cael was already turning. The framework took him round on the second figure, the turn Wray had broken his count on all term and that he had spent the whole recess mending on frozen stone, and it brought his weight over on time, not a tenth late. He came out of the pivot square to Edran's open side with his feet under him.

The second burst was short and almost easy. He had his palm on Edran's breastbone and the landing beat paid before the off arm had finished crossing.

"Touch. Cael. Two each."

Behind the narrow table, Wray made a mark on her own slate. She did not look up. Cael understood, from the fact that she did not look up, that she had seen exactly which figure the pivot had come from.

---

The sixth exchange was the worst one, and he would think about it for a long time afterward.

Neither of them waited. Edran came off his mark with a shell already up, faster than he had come all morning, and Cael understood that Edran meant to take the exchange before the read could settle. He came in close and stayed close, building and shedding on both arms so fast that the read was a smear of weight and the gaze could barely keep up. Cael slipped once, and again, and on the third shell he felt the shift and saw the off arm commit, and he went.

The third burst took him in.

But Edran had committed honestly this time, and he was quick. As Cael came out of the burst on the open side, Edran was already turning into him, shedding the lead shell in a spray that went into Cael's face and building the next on the off arm as it came round. Cael's palm went to the breastbone. Edran's structure went to Cael's chest. The spray from the burst and the touch of the palm arrived together, so close together that Cael himself could not have said which was first.

Both of them stopped.

The hall stopped with them. In the quiet Cael could hear his own breath, and Edran's, and somewhere at the back a first-year saying "Who?" in an anxious whisper and being hushed.

Wray did not speak at once. She sat behind the narrow table with her hands flat on it and looked at the two of them standing locked together in the middle of the boards, Cael's palm on Edran's breastbone and Edran's shell standing an inch from Cael's ribs, unburst.

"The shell didn't land," said Wray. "The palm did. Touch. Cael. Three to two."

There was a noise from the north bench that did not sound like a cheer.

Cael stepped back. He looked at the shell on Edran's off arm, still whole, and at the inch of air between it and where his ribs had been. If Wray had called it the other way he could not honestly have said she was wrong. Edran's structure had been coming. It might have arrived a hair after his palm or a hair before. The call was hers to make, and she had made it, and it was defensible. That did not make it the only defensible call.

Edran let the shell go. It broke outward in pale glitter, and he went back to his mark without looking at the table.

Quenna wrote something on her pad and did not look up.

He had spent three bursts, and he had two exchanges left.

His hip had begun to speak at the end of the sixth, a warmth low on the leading side, not yet the line. He knew what the line would be if he asked it for a fourth. On stone, it came due in the morning. On these boards, with Wray's sweeping in them and a little give underfoot, he did not know, and he had never paid it in front of two hundred people.

*Do not spend the fourth unless the bout is on it and nothing else will do.*

"Seventh exchange. Begin."

He tried to win it without the Wind.

He went to the read and the gaze and the framework, slipping on his own feet and taking shells on his forearms. He waited for the shift, and on the second true one he went in on foot, without a burst, using the pivot, aiming to arrive on the open side by turning instead of flying. It was slower. It was half a beat slower, which was exactly the half-beat Edran had bought with his reach. He arrived on the open side, and Edran's third structure of the sequence was standing there on the off arm, whole, thick and clean from the elbow to the wrist, without any thin place or cold sliver on its edge.

Cael looked at it for the space of a single heartbeat, with real admiration. He could not help it. It was a beautiful piece of work, a whole term of somebody's life, finished.

Then its edge was along his ribs.

"Touch. Edran. Three each."

The north bench came to its feet. The tout had stopped taking wagers and was standing on the bench with his mouth open.

Cael went back to his mark. His forearms ached from wrist to elbow. His breath was short and his hip was warm. There was one exchange left, and he had won three of them and been given one of those three, and Edran had won three outright. He stood at the mark and looked at the man at the other end of the boards. He knew he could not get to the open side in time on his feet, and that he had one burst left before the bill came due. He knew too that he was going to spend it.

He did not pretend otherwise to himself. He had written the rule. He was breaking it in full knowledge of what it would cost, and he would write that down too, in the morning, when the cost had arrived.

"Eighth exchange," said Wray. "Last. Begin."

Edran came on. He knew it as well as Cael did. He came on low and patient and let the shells come up and shed, lead and off, lead and off, and waited for the fourth burst to come. If Cael had been in his place, he would have been doing exactly the same.

Cael waited for a true shift.

The first was a feint. He felt it, and held, and the off arm stayed. The second was a feint too, and he held again. On the third, the weight went into the off shoulder and the arm began to move, and Cael held half a breath and went.

He knew at once it was wrong. The fourth burst always cost something before it cost anything else, and this time it cost him distance. He had placed it to bring him out on the open side a hand from Edran's breastbone, with his palm already there along the line of travel. It brought him out half an inch short. The landing beat locked on him, longer than it ever ran, the half-breath stretched into most of a whole one. He could not change direction and could not reach. He could only go on along the line he had chosen, with his arm bent where it should have been straight.

His palm touched the breastbone anyway. It was not much of a touch. It had none of his weight in it, only the end of the burst's travel arriving a moment after he did.

Edran's off arm finished crossing and found nothing in front of it.

"Touch," said Wray. "Cael. Exchange and bout, four to three."

The noise in the hall came up all at once, and it did not sound like the noise after the formal yard.

That one had been the noise of people who had seen something happen that they had not expected. This one was the noise of people who had watched two fighters do a thing all the way through, and knew that it had been close, and could not agree how close. Half the north bench was shouting about the sixth exchange. The tout had climbed down from the bench and was sitting on it with his head in his hands, which Cael took to mean he had laid his crusts badly.

Cael stood in the middle of the boards and let it go past him. His hip had stopped being warm and begun to be something else, a narrow brightness low on the leading side that was not yet the line and would be. He knew he would walk on it tonight and pay for it tomorrow. It did not matter yet.

Edran was in front of him.

He had let the last of his structures go, and his forearms were bare and red where the shells had sat all morning. He was breathing hard and trying not to show it. He looked at Cael for a long moment, the way he had looked at Cael through the whole bout, without anything in his face but attention.

Then he put out his hand.

That was new. He had never offered it before, not in the formal yard and not at the rail, not even when he had turned his back to say he was glad the reading had held. Cael looked at the hand for a moment before he took it. It was a hard, dry grip, and it did not let go quickly.

"Third one someday."

Cael saw it in Edran's shoulders before he heard it in the words. It was not a courtesy. It was not a challenge or a complaint. It was the next line of a ledger they were both going to keep.

"Someday," said Cael.

Edran let go and went to his corner without another word, and Hobb came off the rail and handed him a towel, and Edran took it without looking.

Wray was standing behind the table. She looked down the length of the floor at Cael, and he waited for her to say something about the sixth, or the fourth burst, or the landing that had run long in front of the whole school. She did not.

"Pivot," said Wray. "On time. Second figure." She sat down. "That's all."

He bowed to the table. Quenna's pen was still moving. She did not look up, and he did not expect her to.

---

He walked to supper on it, and walked up the stair on it, and by the time he sat down at the desk by the window the narrow brightness had become the line. It ran from the crest of the leading hip down the inside of the thigh to the inner knee, bright and exact, like a seam somebody had stitched into him with hot wire. He sat with it and did not reach for the binder at once.

He had won. He knew that. Wray had called it, and Quenna had written it, and two hundred people had seen it, and nobody could take it off the record now. He also knew how close it had been. He had won one of the eight exchanges on a call that could have gone the other way. He had won another on a burst he had promised himself he would not spend, which had landed half an inch short of where he put it. And he had lost a third to a man who had learned his best read in one exchange and handed it back to him empty.

There were two ways to write it down. He could feel both of them sitting in the pen.

The first way was easy and true in every word. *Edran, exhibition, week twenty. Won four to three in the eighth exchange. Found the recovery in the off arm in the third; corrected in the fifth; pivot on time.* Every word of that was so, and it would read beautifully a year from now, and it would be a lie by omission. A lie by omission was still a lie, and he had built his whole life since Denvash on not telling any.

He dipped the pen and wrote the other one.

*Edran. Week twenty. Full sanction, Wray presiding, Quenna second seat. Eight exchanges.*

*Margin: one exchange. Possibly none. The sixth was a palm against an unburst shell arriving together. Wray called it for me. I can't say she was wrong. I can't say she was right either. If it goes the other way I lose.*

*What he built: he gave up a hand of reach to carry the off arm forward and close, so that his recovery is already in place when the lead bursts. Every read I had arrived correct and half a beat late. A correct read that comes too late is a wrong read that costs more.*

*Correction (found in the third, broken in the fourth, mended in the fifth): don't read the shell, read the off arm's shift. Then wait to see whether the arm follows, because he'll feed me the shift empty. That costs the time I was trying to save. It's still the only read that worked.*

*Wind: four bursts in eight exchanges. One over my ceiling. I spent the fourth knowing what it would cost, because nothing else was going to get there. It landed half an inch short and the landing beat ran long, longer than I've ever felt it. Hip: the line, crest to inner knee, tonight already, on boards. Expect three days.*

*Without the fourth there's no eighth exchange, so I'd pay it again. That isn't the same as saying it was cheap. I went over my own ceiling to win a bout I'd promised myself I'd win inside it. Against somebody better, one burst over wouldn't have been enough, and I'd have been two over, or three, and still lost.*

He read it back once. It was not flattering. He found he did not mind.

Lira knocked and came in without waiting, as she did. She had a cloth folded in her hand, wet from the pump and cold enough that he could feel it across the room. She held it out. When he took it, she leaned over his shoulder and read the page in the binder without asking, all the way down to the last line, and straightened up.

She did not say anything about the sixth exchange or the fourth burst. She did not say *well fought*. She put her hand on top of his head for a moment, briefly and rather hard, as if she were testing whether it was attached.

"Three days," she said. "I'll hold you to it. No floor, no yard, no bursts. If I catch you on the stair taking it two at a time, I'll make it four."

"Three days."

"And next time," said Lira, from the door, "don't make Wray choose."

She shut it behind her. He sat with the cold cloth on his hip and the binder open in front of him, and thought she was right, and that it was the best thing anybody had said to him all day.

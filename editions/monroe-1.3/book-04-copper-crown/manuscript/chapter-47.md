# Chapter 47 — Rent

"Tomorrow's yours," said Brom. "Isn't it."

They were on the stair up from supper on the evening of the thirteenth, Brom a step ahead, with the lamp at the turn throwing his shadow back down over Cael's feet. He had not turned round to say it.

"It's ours. Sixth-day, fourth bell, hall three. They're walking the floors tomorrow and that's the floor that's in use at the fourth bell."

"I know whose floor it is." Brom stopped on the landing and turned, so that they stood level for once. "I mean what are you changing?"

"Nothing."

Brom considered him, and Cael could see him weighing the word the way he weighed a feed, for whether it was honest or only meant to look honest.

"Good," Brom said at last. "Neither am I. I'll be just as unpleasant as I always am. More, if anything. You've been getting soft on the left."

"There's one thing."

"There's always one thing."

So Cael told him, there on the landing, with his voice low and his back to the wall, because it was easier to say aloud to Brom than to write down. The hold ran all the time; that was its rent, and the rent he could pay, because he had been paying it for a month. But the read had two parts, and only one of them was free. The outside of it simply ran, the coarse cool sense on his forearms of something heavy coming, from where. The inside of it was the reach, when he went down into one forearm in particular and felt its line a fraction before it landed. That was what made their drill worth doing, and since the night on the stair the reach and the hold had drawn on one purse. It was a small purse, and it had a bottom.

"So you can't have both," said Brom.

"Not for an hour. Not with my whole head in the drill. That's how I went thin last time."

"So which goes?"

"The reach."

Brom thought about that for a while, leaning on the rail.

"You'll be turning me on the frame of it," he said. "On angle and floor. Like a first-year who's been taught the shape and never felt the weight."

"Yes."

"I'll know."

"I know you'll know."

"Do you want me to say?"

Cael had thought about that at supper, and had his answer ready. "Say whatever you'd say any other week. If you'd say something, say it. If you wouldn't, don't."

"Fair," said Brom, and went on up.

Cael went to his window and wrote it under the two words at the head of the plan, so that tomorrow's version of him, tired and under a gallery, would not have to make the decision again from the beginning.

*Hall three. When the purse runs low, the reach goes first. Never the hold. Turn him on the frame: angle, floor, count in the head. Let the number come out middling. Middling is what I'm for tomorrow.*

Karis's marbled watcher log lay on the sill where she had left it for him, open at the day's page, as it had been open every evening since the bridge. *Ferry landing, two. Road's foot, two. Changed on the bell. Nothing new.* Eleven strangers had come up the bluff, and the four grey coats below it had gone on changing every four hours exactly as they had before anybody arrived, as if the delegation were weather that did not concern them. He found that steadying, and did not quite like finding it so.

Then he put down the pen and blew out the lamp and went to bed at the eighth bell, which he had not done since he was small.

Lira was on the settle in the common room when he went through it, with her leg along the cushions and a book she was not reading. She looked at the clock over the door, and then at him, with the expression of somebody watching a fighter do the sensible thing he had never in his life been known to do.

"It's the eighth bell."

"I'm going to sleep nine hours."

"On purpose?"

"On purpose."

"Good," said Lira, and turned a page she had not read.

He slept the nine hours and woke a little before the bell with his head clear and the rent behind his eyes no bigger than a coin. He ate at the second bell, early, at a time when nobody would remark on it, and went to his lecture and sat in his usual place. On the way he passed the wing, where the door onto the covered walk was shut and the young clerk stood outside it with two black boxes in his arms, waiting to be let in. Beside him, a little apart, stood the instrument woman with her oil can and her brass rag, looking at the shut door of her own document store with the patient face of somebody who has been told to come back later and fully intends to. It was not her posted day. She had come anyway, to make sure, and Cael liked her for it more than he could have explained.

---

The delegation came into the gallery a little after the fourth bell.

Cael was at the near end of the north bay with his sleeves pushed up, turning his wrists over and back while Brom fed him slow touches on the forearm. He did not look up, because he did not have to. Hall three's gallery ran along the north wall above the floor, and its boards were old, and each one had its own small voice. He heard the stair door open at the far end, and he heard people come out onto the boards and spread along the rail, and as they settled he counted the weights: a heavy one, a light one, one that stopped and came on again more slowly, saving a leg.

Seven.

He had them placed by sound before Brom had fed him three more touches. There were two by the stair door, three bunched in the middle and one a little apart from them, and the last was at the very east end of the rail, where the gallery met the wall.

Below them, on the floor, the quiet woman from the wing sat at the north rail with her board on her knee, as she sat every week, and she did not look up either.

"Ready?" said Brom.

"Ready."

"Thirty a side."

"Thirty a side."

Brom's part of it was simple: he hardened his right forearm, took one step and drove it at Cael on a count of four, at three-quarters of everything he had. Cael's part was to meet that forearm at a slant with his own and send it down past his hip into the timber. If he did it right, the floor took the force and he took none of it. Then Brom stepped back to his mark and did it again, from the same place, at the same weight, thirty times, and then they changed sides and did thirty more, and the woman at the rail made a mark for every one of them.

They had run it so often that their feet had worn a pale track into the oak. It had been the backbone of his whole second half of term, and by the eighth repetition it wanted every last scrap of him.

That was the trouble with good work, and he had only learned it a few days ago. Good work asked for all of you, and it asked so nicely. The rhythm of the drill was Brom's four-count and the turn and the step back, over and over. That rhythm pulled at Cael the way a current pulls at a swimmer's legs, gently, never hard enough to notice, only steadily and a little at a time, toward the deep part of the river where a swimmer stops choosing where he goes. *You've done this a thousand times,* it said. *Give it everything. Let go of the rest.* And under the pull, so faint that a week ago he would never have caught it, he felt the corner of himself that held his edges begin to lean toward the drill as well.

He took the corner back. It was a small hard effort, like setting his heel into a slope he was about to slide down.

Then, at the ninth repetition, he let the reach go.

He let it go on purpose and all at once, before the purse could make the choice for him. The next time Brom's forearm came in, Cael did not go down into it but let the outside of the read tell him, coarsely, *heavy, from the right, about there*, which was all it ever told him. Then he met it on the frame alone, turning on the slant he had drilled ten thousand times. He trusted the oak to take what he sent it, and he counted Brom's four in his head instead of feeling it on his skin. The turn came out a hair late and a hair rough, but it was still a turn, and the floor still took it.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

It was like writing with the wrong hand, where every letter comes out right and every one of them is work. Brom's forearm arrived where it always arrived, and Cael met it where he had been taught to meet it, and the force went down into the bearers with a dull thump. The hold sat in its corner and did not move.

Above him the gallery went on making its small sounds. Six of the seven shifted now and then, a heel lifted and set down, a weight moved from one foot to the other. Twice, the whole rail creaked together, the way a rail creaks when somebody in the middle says something and the rest lean in to hear.

From the east end there came no sound at all.

---

Ilsev did not watch the boy.

She watched the woman at the north rail below her, the one with the board, because the woman with the board was the record. The boy was only what the record was about, and the record was the thing she had come to look at.

Under the clause, the boy had to be evaluated by demonstration before a panel once every semester, and the clause also required the institution to keep a record of the work that led up to it, term by term. The panel would not sit until the twentieth, and nobody could inspect a panel that had not yet sat. What could be inspected today was the hand that kept the record of the term. A record was as good as the hand that kept it and not one grain better. She had spent a long career finding handsome files built by careless hands, and a few plain ones built by careful hands, and she had learned to look at the hand first.

So she watched the hand.

The woman wrote with a short pencil held close to the point. She made one mark for each repetition the moment it ended, without looking down, her eyes on the floor the whole time. Most of the marks were short upright strokes. Once, on the right side, near the twentieth, the pencil made something different, and Ilsev leaned a fraction over the rail to see, and saw a small round dot.

A stroke for clean, then, and a dot for not, made at the instant. So far as Ilsev could see, nothing was ever gone back over.

She had read the term's sheets the day before, in one of the registrar's boxes, all of them, in date order. Every one was initialled, and every one bore a receiving stamp from the registrar's office dated the same afternoon as the hour it recorded, and that had told her something about the office. It had told her nothing yet about the hand. Now she had the hand in front of her, and the hand matched the paper.

She approved of that. She approved of the room as well, the bearers under the boards and the new pins in the posts along the north bay, and the chained posts at the far end. By the stair door somebody had painted a column of dates on the plaster, one for every time the floor had been surveyed. It was a well-kept room, which was not a finding; it was only the lack of a reason to look harder, which was a different thing, and she had trained herself for most of her life never to mistake the one for the other.

Two places along from her, at the east end of the rail, the Archmarshal stood with his hands at his sides.

He had not moved since he came onto the gallery, and she noticed it the way she had noticed the dates on the plaster, as a fact about the room, without wondering about it. A man stood still or he did not, and what it meant was his own business until it was written down somewhere, and nothing about it was written down anywhere she could read. She put it out of her mind and went back to the pencil.

She had once, years ago, at a district station two days' ride from anywhere, been handed four volumes of an observer's log and been asked to say whether a certain practitioner's record could be relied on. The log was beautiful, and it ran four years without one gap. Every session was there, every count, every hour, in a hand as even as print. She had read the first volume with growing pleasure, and halfway through the second she had put it down, gone out of the station into a yard full of hens, and walked round it three times before she would let herself say what she knew. No hand in the world goes four years without a gap. A sneeze, a fever, a bad night, a moment of looking out of the window: honest records are full of small holes. A hand that never missed was a hand that filled its misses in with what it supposed had happened, and called that the record. She had found the filling-in in the end, nineteen places in four years, and the practitioner's whole file had come down with it.

Beside her, the counsel was murmuring to the eldest clerk about the timber. On the counsel's other side, Havel had his case open on the rail and was writing, as he always wrote, all of it. On the floor below, the boy turned a forearm down into the oak and then another, and the woman made her strokes.

At the bell the woman stood, clipped the pencil to the board and came toward the gallery stair, to hand her sheet on to whoever in the wing would carry it. Ilsev went down and met her at the foot.

"A question, if I may. About your board. Not about the enrollee."

The woman looked at her steadily. She was perhaps forty, with ink along the side of her middle finger, and she did not seem in the least alarmed. "Assessor."

"When you mark a repetition clean, is that your judgment at the moment it happens? Or is the board reviewed afterward and the marks settled then?"

"At the moment. I never change a mark once it's made. If I'm not sure, it's a dot."

"Is this the form you use for every supervised hour? Or is there a form for this enrollee?"

"The same form for every hour the wing witnesses." The woman turned the board so that Ilsev could see its head. "This one has the provision line, here. That's all. The columns are the same as everybody's."

"Who countersigns it? And when does it leave the wing?"

"The Magister initials every sheet at the end of the day. It crosses to the registrar the same afternoon. It's never once stayed overnight in the wing, not this term."

Ilsev nodded. She had one question left, and it was the only one she had come down the stair to ask.

"If you look away," she said, "if you miss a repetition, for any reason at all. How is that entered?"

The woman did not hesitate. "As missed. There's a mark for it, a small cross. It means I didn't see, so I don't know. I don't guess." She thought about it, honestly, with her eyes on the ceiling. "I've made eleven this term. Two of them were for sneezing."

"Thank you," said Ilsev. "That's all."

She went back up the stair. Four questions, and she did not need a fifth. She had asked nothing about the boy, because the boy had not been her question; the boy's record was, and the boy's record was this woman's hand. The last answer was the one she would write down tonight, in her own book, under the date. *Eleven crosses in a term. Two for sneezing.* A keeper with a mark for *I did not see* was a keeper you could build on, and they were rarer than anyone outside her work would believe.

On the gallery, the counsel had reached her eleventh question, which was about the age of the bearers, and Havel was writing it down. At the east end of the rail the Archmarshal stood exactly where he had stood for fifty minutes, as if a surveyor had set him there, and he had asked nobody anything.

---

On the floor, Cael had felt his own count against the woman's pencil without looking, and made the right side about twenty-seven of thirty. Then they changed sides and ran the left.

The left was worse. It always was. His left turn had come late in his training and had never been as easy as his right, and without the reach behind it every repetition felt like going down stairs in the dark, one careful foot at a time, feeling for each edge. He kept it on the frame, and he kept the hold in its corner. By the twentieth there was a fine shake starting in both forearms. Part of it was the ordinary shake of contact work, and he knew that one and did not mind it. Under it was a second, further in, which came from the purse running toward its bottom, and that one he minded very much.

At the thirty-first repetition of the left side, which was one more than the drill called for, Brom stopped.

He stopped out of turn, in the middle of stepping back to his mark, and shook out his right hand at the wrist as if it had cramped. When he spoke it was at the exact volume a man uses to grumble about his own wrist to nobody.

"Read's off."

Cael met his eyes.

"On purpose," he said.

Brom rolled his shoulder slowly, the way he did when he was turning a thing over and did not want his face to do the turning for him. The hall was quiet except for the boards above.

"Then it's the right kind of off."

He set his feet, and drove the last one in on the four-count, and Cael turned it down into the oak.

At the bell the woman from the wing counted her marks and wrote a figure at the foot of the sheet and signed it. Fifty-four out of sixty, clean. On his worst week of the term he had made fifty-two, and on his best fifty-eight, and today's sat in the fat middle of his own numbers, where nobody reading a column of them would ever stop.

He had made every stroke of it with one hand tied behind his back, under seven people who had come all the way up the bluff to see what he was. Not one of them would ever know, which was all he had wanted from the afternoon, and now that he had it, it felt very strange in his hands.

He did not go back to the residence but went out through the side door of hall three into the yard behind it, where the porters stacked the spare bearers under a lean-to and nobody came. He sat down on the end of a bearer in the wind with the notebook on his knee.

The shake in his forearms made his letters jump. He wrote anyway, because the shake was part of what he was writing down.

*Shadow-adjacent, Bronze tier: working ledger. Day sixteen. Deployment: none.*

*Have: the hold. Fifty minutes of it, under a gallery with seven on it. No slips. It leaned toward the drill at the eighth and I hauled it back.*

*Spends: the steady drip, all day, like a lamp turned down low that never goes out. Call it half of what the gaze costs held wide. And on the floor, the reach. The reach and the hold drink out of one cup. Today the hold drank the lot, and the reach never got a drop.*

*Risk: not the drip. I can carry the drip. It's the cup. On the twentieth I'll want the reach for the hard trials, and it won't be there, because I'll have spent it keeping thick.*

*Won't share: the reach. Still. Same as the first day.*

*Good for: keeping me ordinary. That's all. It made nothing today, no skill, no number. All fifty minutes bought me was that nothing happened.*

*Fails: not today. I'm putting that down plainly, because a month from now I'll need to know whether today was luck.*

*After: an hour and a half, near enough, when reading anything feels like pressing on a bruise.*

*Net: it isn't a capability. It's rent.*

He read it through. Then, at the foot, he wrote the line the whole entry had been leaning toward. His hand did not want to write it, and he made it.

*Fifty minutes cost me the read. The twentieth's three hours.*

He sat looking at that while the wind came up off the river and found its way under his collar. Nobody came. Below the bluff the water went on as it always went on, with nobody watching it and nobody needing it to be anything except water. After a while the shake in his hands eased, and the other shake under it eased a little too, and he got up and went to find the others.

---

He went to the library to look for them, and Karis found him there first. She came in with her supper on a plate, which was against the library's rules, and which the library had stopped trying to enforce on Karis some time in the first month. She sat down opposite him at the long table under the north window.

"Bracken's clerk," she said, "writes everything down."

"I know."

"Every question the delegation puts during a facility review goes in the registrar's book, word for word, with the hour and the room. That's what the review is for. They're supposed to walk about asking." She cut a potato in half. "He told me at the hatch. He wasn't meant to tell me anything, so he said a number isn't a document. I said that was a very interesting legal position, and he went pink."

"What number?"

"Numbers." She ate half the potato. "In hall three, the counsel asked eleven questions. Mostly about timber. Ilsev, four. Every one of hers was about the woman with the board. You weren't in any of them. Havel asked two." She put down her fork. "The Archmarshal asked none."

"In hall three."

"Anywhere. Three to Gault, in the wing, this morning; the clerk doesn't know what. Then nothing. Not in the lecture range at the second bell. Not in the residence quadrangle at noon, or the fire-watch's store, or any of the three floors." She looked at him over the plate. "He went into every room they went into, and stood in it, and he asked nobody anything at all."

Cael did not answer that, and after a moment Karis let it lie and asked the other thing. "And yours? The drill."

"Fifty-four."

She thought about it with her fork in the air. "Your worst was fifty-two. Your best was fifty-eight. That's the middle of you." She looked at him more closely, the way she looked at a sum that came out right by a method she did not trust. "What did it cost?"

He turned the notebook round and showed her the last two lines. She read them, and then she put her fork down on the plate, very quietly, and did not pick it up again for a while.

"Three hours," she said.

"I know."

"Then that's what we're doing with the next six days," said Karis, as if it had been decided at a meeting he had missed. "Finding you something better than luck."

Cael sat with all of it for a long time, and with his own memory of the boards, and the two fitted together like the halves of a broken cup.

Six weights on the gallery had moved, a minute or two apart, each in its own fashion. The rail had creaked together twice, and the seventh weight had never made a sound. In fifty minutes there had not been a heel lifted, or a hand set on the rail and taken off it, or a shift from one foot to the other. Cael had followed the silence the whole hour, on the floor, with his eyes on Brom's forearm, the way a man in a noisy room keeps track, without ever meaning to, of the one corner where nobody is talking.

And that alone was not a finding, and he would not let it become one. A man might stand that still from long habit. He might stand still because the cold had got into his joints and moving hurt. He might simply have decided to, for reasons of his own. Cael wrote all three on the page and could not choose between them, so he wrote *stood still* under them and left it there, unexplained, which was the honest thing to do with it.

The questions were different. The questions were a thing he knew from the inside.

*All day he asked nobody anything. A man does that in a strange building for one of two reasons. Either there's nothing in it he cares about, or he brought his questions with him from somewhere else, already written, and he's already decided this isn't the place the answers are kept. I know the second kind. I've walked into four archives like that and never asked a librarian a single thing. And I've never once thought about what it looks like to the librarian.*

*It looks like a man who isn't there. That's the whole trick of it. I've been using it for two years, and today somebody used it on me.*

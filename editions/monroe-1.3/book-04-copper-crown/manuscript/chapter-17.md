# Chapter 17 — Outvoted

It rained on Sixth-day, a steady grey rain off the river that ran down the common room's windows in ropes and made the fire sulk.

Brom came in first, wet to the shoulders from the post, with the kettle he had begged from the kitchen gate held against his chest like a rescued cat. Karis came next, with four books from carrel eleven in an oilcloth and a smear of lamp black on her cheek that nobody told her about. Lira was already there. She had been there since before supper, standing at the window with her arms folded, watching the rain as if it had been sent to annoy her personally. When the other two were in, she crossed the room and shut the door, and turned the key, and put the key in her pocket.

"Well," said Brom, setting the kettle on the hob. "That's friendly."

"Cael's got something." Lira went back to the window. "I said I'd get you all here and shut the door. I've done both."

Cael had cleared the long table and laid the binder's loose pages out on it in a row, in the order he wanted them read, weighted at the corners with whatever had come to hand: an inkwell, a boot-brush, Karis's spare spectacles, a heel of bread. He had thought for most of the afternoon about how to begin, and had settled on the way he always began with the three of them now.

"I'm going to tell you some numbers," he said. "Then I'm going to tell you what I think they mean. Stop me at the numbers if they're wrong. Don't stop me at the meaning until I've finished."

"That's how you always do it," said Brom, sitting down on the settle.

"That's why I'm saying it." Cael put his finger on the first page. "This is the stair."

He took them through it page by page, in the rain: the fame tally and its column of falling fractions; the second line, written before he meant to; the wider count, with the control column beside it, the desk clerk's nine of fourteen and the oil-and-brass woman's five of eleven and the panel porters' seven of nineteen. And at the bottom, by itself, the fourth line.

Karis leaned over it. "Thirty-one."

"Thirty-one."

"And none." She did not sound surprised. "Go on."

He did not walk them through the four dead maps. He went straight to the fifth, the spider, and laid the brown ink over the black.

Karis had been copying figures in her small fast hand since the stair, and at the fifth map her pen stopped, and she sat back with her hands in her lap. Cael knew what that meant. She had reached the end by her own road, and was waiting to see whether he would arrive at the same place by his.

Brom had picked up the overlay and was holding it to the fire, squinting through the two inks.

"I said it was a bad bluff," he said. "I didn't know it was a bad bluff with somebody standing in it."

"Nineteen of nineteen," said Cael. "And four holes, all at hours the counter needs him at his desk."

He told them the gallery, and the four words in the margin, and the lamp, twice, and the crack of the Force throw that turned every head in the yard but one.

Then he took his hand off the table and gave them the meaning.

"The assistant in Gault's office is a watcher. He's good. He's very good. Shadow Path, Bronze tier, by the classification on his own card, and I believe the card. He's been on the bluff about four weeks. He has never once looked at me, because looking is the one thing that would give him away, and he stands every day where he can see everything I do without being seen to see it." He paused. "I can't prove whose he is. I don't know of anybody on this continent who'd pay a Bronze to copy forms in an assessment wing, except the people who already pay for those coats at the ferry."

Karis was the first to speak, and she did not argue with the meaning. She tested it.

"Whose else could he be?" she said. "Before we decide he's theirs. Could he be Withrow's? A chancellor might want her own eyes on a provision she's staked the school on."

"Withrow would ask," said Cael. "She sent for me on my nineteenth day and asked me to my face what I'd cost her. She doesn't need to hide a man in a wing to find things out. She calls them into her room."

"The registry, then. Bracken's office. Or the faculty. Rooke did put his objection in the minutes."

"Bracken writes letters. Twenty-two of them, to a stranger, all signed. Rooke said his piece in an open session with the whole back row listening." Cael shook his head. "Everybody on this hill who wants to know something about me has asked it, or written it down where I could read it, or said it in front of sixty people. Ephram bought the transcript and told the whole yard what he thought. This man is the only one who's taken trouble never to be seen wanting to know anything at all. The only people I've ever met who work that way are the ones I've been counting at ferry landings for two years."

Brom stirred on the settle. "And the Bronze. You said Bronze."

"A Bronze copying forms is like a carpenter sweeping floors. Somebody's paying for the carpentry."

Karis thought about that, and wrote something, and then crossed it out and wrote something shorter.

"I can't break it," she said at last. "I'd like to. It's guesswork, but it's good guesswork." She looked up. "Write *probable*, not *confirmed*. In your column."

"It's already in that column," said Cael, and showed her.

The rain went on against the glass. The kettle began, very quietly, to tick.

---

Lira spoke first, as she nearly always did, and she spoke to the window.

"Withrow," she said. "Tonight. I'll go with you."

She had been picking at the lead of the window-pane with her thumbnail while he talked, and she went on picking at it, and a little grey curl of old lead came away and fell on the sill. Cael had known her three years. When Lira's hands found something to worry, it meant her mind had already finished and was waiting impatiently for the rest of her body to catch up.

"He's in Gault's room," she said. "He's got your dates in his ledger and your sheets on his pile. Everything that keeps you on this hill goes across that table, and he's sitting at it." She turned round. "Withrow knew the Compact would come when she took the provision. She told you so. She put it on a sheet with a column for what you'd cost her. There wasn't a line on that sheet for a Compact man with a chair in the assessment office. She'd want to know. Any chancellor would."

It was a good argument. It was the argument Cael had made to himself on the first night, before the sum, and he waited to see who would answer it.

Brom answered it. He did not sit up to do it. He lay back on the settle with the overlay held up to the firelight like a man reading a letter from home, and when he spoke it was to the brown dots, not to her.

"We know where their eyes are. First time ever. Why would we give that back?"

Lira turned on him.

"Because they're on Cael."

"They were always on Cael." Brom lowered the page. "Two years, they've been on him. In Greyvane's yard, on the road, at the bridge, down there under that lamp every four hours. We've never once known where to stand so they couldn't see. Now we do. We know which post he leans on in the east hall." He shrugged one bare shoulder. "Throw him out and they'll only send another one, and we'll not know which post that one likes."

"You don't know they'd send another."

"I know they sent this one," said Brom simply.

Lira opened her mouth, and shut it, and turned to Karis, because Karis was the one who would have the figures, and Lira had always been honest enough to go to the person who would have the figures even when she expected them to go against her.

Karis had shut her notebook. She sat with both palms flat on its cover.

"I'll give you the sum," she said, "and you can tell me where it's wrong. I'd like it to be wrong." She lifted one hand and set it on the overlay beside Brom's knee. "Today we've found a man. We know his places to the stair-head. We know the one thing that makes him walk away, because Cael found it in an empty hall. We know he's never looked straight at any of us."

She took her hand back. "Tell Withrow, and by the end of the week he's gone. And then, inside a month, somebody else is in that chair, or another chair, or a room in Ostrand with a window on the bridge. Somebody we haven't found. Somebody who's been told in detail how the last man was caught, and who will not be caught that way, because that's what being told is for." She looked at Lira properly. "We'd be trading an instrument we can read for one we can't even find. I'm not saying I like it. I'm saying it's arithmetic."

"He is spying on Cael," said Lira. Her voice had gone very level, which was worse than loud.

"Yes. And he was going to. Somebody was always going to." Karis did not look away. "I want him gone too, Lira. I want it badly enough that I'm frightened of my own reasoning, which is why I'm doing my reasoning out loud in front of three people who'll tell me if I cheat. Every way I come at it, I land in the same place. Send him off and we'd feel better for about a week. Then we'd have lost the only window we've ever had into how they work."

The rain had eased. In the quiet it left, the fire settled and spat.

Lira stood with her back to the window and her thumbnail still on the lead. Then she crossed the room and sat down on the end of the settle by Brom's feet, hard, and Brom moved his feet without being asked.

"Then it's decided," said Cael.

"It's not settled, it's *outvoted*." Lira said it to the fire. "Put that in. My words. Not yours made nicer."

"Your words." He wrote them in the margin of the overlay, in ink, while she watched, and turned the page round so she could read them. She read them twice and nodded: the nod of somebody signing a paper she disagrees with because the vote went the other way and she believes in votes.

---

Deciding not to act took ten minutes. Deciding what to do instead took the rest of the evening, two more kettles, and a long argument with the fire, which Brom won in the end with a spoiled practice sheet and more patience than anybody had known he had.

They came away with three things. Cael numbered them on a clean page as they were agreed, because he had watched agreements made aloud in good faith dissolve inside a season, and a numbered page could be taken out and laid on a table.

The first Lira named, with some savagery. "Hands off," she said. "That's what you want, isn't it? Nobody touches him."

It was more than that, and they worked it out between them. Nobody was to look at the man. Nobody was to speak to him except as a student speaks to an office, about a form, and only when there was a form. Nobody was to walk differently past the wing door, or sit differently in a gallery, or stop talking when he came into a room.

"We'll all break it," said Karis.

"I won't," said Lira.

"You will. So will I. The first time he's at the counter when I've got a book to return, I'll forget how to hold a book." Karis turned to Brom, who had the decency to look guilty in advance. "And Brom will be so careful not to look at him that he'll walk into the door. So there's a rule under the rule. When you break it, you tell the rest of us the same day. You don't try to mend it on your own by being twice as ordinary next morning. Nothing on earth looks less ordinary than somebody trying hard to look ordinary."

"I'd have tried hard," said Brom humbly.

"I know. That's why it's written down."

The second was Cael's, and it came out wrong the first time, and he had to start again.

"He's going to write about me," he said. "That's his whole errand here. So it's no use wishing he wouldn't. What matters is what he writes." He found his pen drawing a little grid in the corner of the page and made it stop. "Think what I've been for two years. Floor time under supervision. The two fragments on my baseline and nothing off it. The same answer twice, every evaluation. I'm a dull read. I didn't do it to be dull; I did it because it's the only way anybody ever gets good at anything, slowly, in front of someone who signs a sheet. But it reads as dull, and a dull read is the kindest thing a watcher can carry home."

"So you go on being dull," said Karis.

"On purpose, now. I don't change a single hour. He watches me be exactly what I've always been, and writes it down, and every word of it is so, and not one word of it is interesting. Somebody in an office a long way off reads it and decides nothing's moving here." He sat back. "A man in that chair can't send home anything kinder. And it costs me nothing, because it's what I'd be doing anyway."

Karis had been writing while he spoke. When he stopped, she stopped, and he saw that she had him word for word. He did not ask her why. She kept things the way a squirrel keeps nuts, against a winter she could already smell, and she would tell him which winter when it came.

The third did not need arguing.

"And I'm going to study him," said Cael.

Nobody was surprised. The proof of it was on the table under the boot-brush. Lira's mouth twisted, not quite into a scowl.

"A Shadow," she said. "You've never had one on a page."

"Nobody has, as far as I can find. Karis?"

"Two lines in a Path survey from the founding era," said Karis at once, "both wrong, and a note in a later hand saying the author had never met one." She tilted her head. "It's a Path that keeps people from studying it. That's the trouble. You'd only ever see it from inside, and from inside you don't know there's anything to see."

"I'm inside it," said Cael. "Every day, from now till they take him off the hill. I'll be the only person who ever stood in one for months knowing he was in it. They handed me that, for nothing, the same people who'd like me gone." He looked at the overlay. "I mean to make them regret the gift."

Brom let out a long, admiring breath.

"That's the meanest thing you've ever said, Cael."

"It isn't mean. It's only accounting."

"Accounting with a *tone*," said Brom, and settled back on the settle with the air of a man who has heard a good story and means to tell it to somebody.

---

Karis caught him on the stair afterward, two steps below him, so that their eyes were level.

"What does it cost you?" she said.

He had hoped she would not ask, and had known she would.

"The counting? I don't know yet." She waited, and he gave her the rest, because he had promised them numbers first and this was a number. "The read's been late. A beat, under supervision. Three times in twelve, one morning, on cords. Not wrong. Just late."

"How long have you been counting him?"

"Thirteen days. Everywhere, every waking hour."

Karis did not say anything. She looked at him for a moment, and then at the overlay rolled under his arm, and then at him again, and he watched the thing arrive in her face and then, a heartbeat later, in his own head, and felt the blood come up into his cheeks.

"Oh," he said.

"It's the same purse," said Karis gently. "Isn't it? The watching and the read. You've been paying for one out of the other." She went down a step. "Write it down tonight. Before you go near a floor again."

She went. Lira was waiting at the foot of the stair. She had heard every word; the stair carried. She let Karis by, and then came up two steps and stood where Karis had stood.

"That's the second thing," she said.

"What is?"

"The read. Late on cords is nothing. Late against somebody who means it is a broken arm." She did not say it unkindly; she said it the way she would have named the angle of a strike. "You'll keep counting. I know you will. So I'm going to tell you the price of my vote, since I didn't win it." She held up one finger. "If he touches your paper wrong, ever, once, a date, a hall, one line in that ledger, that's the morning I go to Withrow myself. Outvoted or not. I won't ask you first."

He studied her face. She was entirely serious, and entirely calm, and he understood that this was not a threat. It was Lira telling him, as she had told him on a hundred floors, where she would be standing when the exchange began.

"That's fair," he said. "I'll write it under the other three."

"Write it in my words."

"In your words."

She nodded, and seemed about to go, and then did not.

"The gallery," she said. "When he walked past and didn't look down. What did it feel like?"

She had asked it the real way, so he gave it real thought.

"Like being stepped over," he said. "Not ignored. Ignored is when somebody doesn't see you. He saw me perfectly well. He walked round me the way you'd walk round a puddle you'd already decided not to get your boots wet in."

Lira was quiet for a moment.

"I've been looked at like a number all my life," she said. "A minute and a half, and a word on a sheet. I didn't know there was something worse." She turned to go down. "Being worked round. Like furniture." At the bottom of the stair she looked back up at him. "Count him properly, then. If you're going to pay for it."

Brom came down past them a moment later with the empty kettle under his arm, humming, and stopped on the step between them, and looked from one to the other.

"Have I come in at the end of something?"

"The middle," said Lira.

"Ah." Brom considered. "I'll go round, then," he said, and went down the rest of the stair sideways, very carefully, with his back to the wall, like a man edging past two people on a ledge, and Lira laughed in spite of herself, a short surprised sound, and went after him. He stood a while on the stair with the noise of the quadrangle coming up it, doors and voices and somebody laughing in the wet, four hundred people having an ordinary evening without the least idea that four of their number had just spent three hours deciding, very carefully, to do nothing at all.

---

He sat down that night with both books in front of him, the observation notebook and the Power Log, and picked up the notebook first, from habit, and then put it down again.

The man belonged in the notebook. But the counting was not the man's. It was Cael's own eyes and Cael's own hours, spent out of Cael's own pocket. And a thing he spent out of his own pocket went in the other book, with the prices.

*Found today, late, and with Karis's help, which I'll note because it's true.*

*The surface read is the quietest fragment I own, and it runs on attention. I always thought it ran on nothing, because it never made my hip ache or my breath short, so I never once priced it. But it draws on the same well I watch people with. For thirteen days I've let that well run into stairs and galleries and a lamp, from first bell to the last, and the read has been drinking from the bottom of the bucket. That's why it's late. Nothing is wrong with it. It's thirsty.*

*Three late in twelve on cords. One guess that went wrong and took me across the shin.*

*I'm going to keep counting anyway.*

He looked at that line, and then wrote under it why, because a decision with no reason beside it would turn into a habit the moment his back was turned.

*I can name the post on a gallery where the Compact's eyes rest in the east hall, and the tier they favour in the yard. No one I've ever met has known that much about the people watching them. That's worth a slow read for a season. It's not worth telling myself the read isn't slow, not with a semester evaluation coming that will lay every reading against the baseline. So: price paid with my eyes open, on the page tonight, before the morning it comes due, so that on that morning I can't say I didn't know.*

*And Lira's price, in her words: "If he touches your paper wrong, ever, once, that's the morning I go to Withrow myself. Outvoted or not."*

Then he turned to a clean page and wrote the entry he had carried since the lamp, by itself, under the date, the way a walker sets a stone on a cairn so that the next one up the hill will know somebody has been there.

*Shadow Path, Bronze, fifteen years' craft if he's a day. The Compact finally sent someone worth watching. So I'm watching.*

He sat back. The rain had stopped altogether, and through the open window he could hear water running off the roofs into the gutters, and under it the river, and once a barge's horn, very far down.

Then, because a study with no plan in it was only a hobby, he turned the page and wrote what he meant to find out, in order, so that he would know afterward whether he had found it or only told himself a story.

*One. The Path declaration: how it starts. Every Path I've charted has a moment where it commits, and the moment shows somewhere: the hip, the jaw, the breath. Find his.*

*Two. What it does to other people. I can't see it working on me; I'm the one person it isn't hiding him from. But I can see it working on everybody else, if I count the right thing.*

*Three. What it costs, and where its ceiling is. It must cost something. Everything does. Find where he pays.*

*Four. What it can't do. The gallery says it can't work alone. Find the rest of the edges.*

*And the read: ease off the counting for four days, once, at a time of my choosing, and see whether it comes back. If it does, that's one instance. Don't call it a rule until it's three.*

He looked down the list. It was the same list he would have made for any fighter on any floor: how he starts, what he does to you, what he pays, where he can't reach. It surprised him a little that a man who never fought would need the same four questions. It surprised him more that he had not seen, until tonight, that they were the only four questions he had ever really known how to ask.

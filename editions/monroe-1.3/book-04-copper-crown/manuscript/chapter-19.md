# Chapter 19 — The Bruise

The round Current lecturer hummed.

He had hummed through the first eleven minutes of every session he had ever signed for Cael. He hummed now, on the bench under the east hall's gallery, with his hands folded on his stomach and his eyes half shut, a tune with no particular shape that went up and down like a man walking over hills. Cael had stopped hearing it after the first week. Today he heard every note, because it was First-day, the seventy-fifth, two days after the council, and Lira had asked for the slot.

Brom had broken the agreement first, as Karis had promised he would, and he had come to breakfast that morning to confess it with the air of a man bringing a dead bird to the kitchen door.

"I walked into the wall," he said, sitting down.

Karis put her spoon down. "Which wall?"

"The one by the wing door. Yesterday, after the post." He looked at his porridge with deep sorrow. "I was going past, the way you said, ordinary, not looking. And I was thinking so hard about not looking that I forgot to look where I was going, and the passage turns there, and I didn't." He touched his forehead, where there was a faint pink mark nobody had noticed until now. "Went straight into the corner. Made a noise like a dropped bucket. Two second-years laughed."

"Was he there?" said Lira.

"I don't know." Brom looked up, honestly anguished. "That's the worst of it. I didn't look. I'd have had to look to know."

There was a short silence round the table.

"Then you kept the rule," said Karis, with great gentleness, "and broke a wall. That's allowed. Thank you for telling us." She picked up her spoon again. "Next time, look where you're going and not at him. Those are two different directions. It's easier than it sounds."

"It's much harder than it sounds," said Brom, and ate his porridge with the dignity of a man who has done his duty and been laughed at by second-years for it. Cael wrote the break into the agreement page that evening under its date, with Brom's own words beside it, *went straight into the corner*, because that was the rule under the rule, and it had worked exactly as Karis had said it would.

"Full pace," Lira had said at the same breakfast, not looking up from her bread, when Brom had finished. "First to three. I want to see something."

"See what?"

"I'll tell you when I've seen it."

The office allowed it, as it had allowed it before, because the two of them shared a residence roster and a supervised session needed a body on the floor besides the enrollee. The Current lecturer signed without looking at either of them, settled on his bench, and began to hum. Above them the gallery held seven or eight early watchers. Cael did not look to see which post had a man with forms at it. He did not need to look. He knew.

They went to their chalk. Lira did what she always did before a bout and had done since the Ironyard, which was to touch the floor with the flat of one hand, as if asking it a question, and then stand up and roll her neck once each way. Cael had asked her about it the first winter. She had said it was so her hand would know the floor before her feet had to, and then refused to say anything else about it for two years. He had come to find it reassuring. A Lira who had touched the floor was a Lira who had stopped thinking about anything in the world except the next three touches.

Lira fought him at full pace on the timber, as she had every week since the first, because the boards here gave and returned and she had said, flatly, the first morning, that it would be a crime to waste them. She came at him low and quick off her lead foot with the Wind at her heels. He read her a pace and a half out, felt her weight go left along the skin of his forearm, and slipped right on his feet without the framework, and she went by.

On the second exchange he went to the Wind early, too early, while her feed was still a feed. He had read her weight going one way and trusted it and committed, and the half-breath closed in his chest, and the framework took him a long stride back and to the side. Lira had not gone that way at all. She had stopped, a hand's width short of where her weight had promised, and simply waited for him to arrive somewhere. When he landed she was already turning to meet him, and the beat at the back of the burst held him for its half-breath like a hand on his collar. She put her palm flat on his chest and pushed, almost gently, and he sat down on the oak.

"One," said Lira.

"Mine," said Cael, getting up. "I went on a feed."

"You did." She went back to her chalk without any comment on it at all, which was the comment.

That first fall was ordinary. He had gone too early on a dummy; it was a mistake he made perhaps once a month, and Lira had been punishing it since the Ironyard, and he deserved it. He took his chalk and rolled his shoulders and told himself to wait for the weight and not the promise of it.

The bench went on humming. Somewhere behind it a door banged in the north passage. The light lay across the boards in its long low bars and the floor breathed under his feet.

On the fourth exchange it went wrong in a way that was not ordinary at all.

---

She came straight. Remember that, Cael told himself afterward: she gave him a true one. She came straight and honest down the middle of the floor, no short step, no dummy, her weight forward on the diagonal from his left, committed, the kind of feed she sold perhaps one time in ten precisely because it was true.

He read it. The press came along his left forearm, heavy and fast, and it was right. Diagonal, committed, real. He priced the burst before he reached for it, the way the Log had made him price every burst for eighteen months, and the price was fair: the second of four free on this timber, a cheap answer to a true question.

His knees softened. His hips sank. The oak took the first of his weight and gave it back, and the half-breath closed, the toll at the front, and for its length he was no heavier than the air in the room. Her whole committed weight went through the place where he had been and out the other side into nothing.

It was a perfect escape. Nothing on the floor could have done it better.

Then the landing.

He had paid it four hundred times. Every borrowed lightness came back at once at the end of a burst, a jolt up through both heels. With it came the locked beat: half a breath in which he was only a thing that had arrived, unable to turn, or reach, or do anything at all but finish arriving. Three years of trying had not taken a hair off it. Against a good fighter the whole art of the framework was knowing in advance what that beat would be spent on, so that the instant it ended he was already moving into the next thing.

He knew what this one should be spent on. Lira's weight had gone past him on the left; her recovery would come round to her right; the beat, if he used it for setting his feet square, would let him meet her coming round with his whole weight behind his hands.

Her recovery reached his skin only once the beat had him.

That was the whole of it. The read came. It came true, as it always came: her weight swinging out and round to her right, fast, exactly where he had expected. But it came the way a letter comes that was posted late, with the event it describes already over. By the time it reached him his feet had set themselves square for the wrong thing, on their own, in the dark, and the locked beat had closed round them, and Lira was not where his feet had set for her. She had come round tighter than he had planned for, and low.

Her shoulder took him under the ribs.

He went over backwards onto the oak with all his own weight and some of hers, and the boards that were so kind to a landing beat were a good deal less kind to a whole body coming down flat. The breath went out of him in one piece. For a moment he lay looking up at the beams and at the long dark rail of the gallery, where somebody was leaning to see, and could not have told anyone his own name.

On the bench the humming had stopped.

Lira was standing over him, bent with her hands braced on her thighs, blowing hard, and looking not the smallest bit sorry.

"Two," she said.

Breath came back in a thin thread. He used it to nod.

"You saw that," she said.

"I felt it."

"It wasn't the burst. The burst was lovely." Her hands stayed on her thighs. "It was after. You set your feet before you knew where I was going. You've never once done that. Not in three years. You always know where I'm going before your feet do; it's the most irritating thing about you." She tilted her head. "Your read was late."

"I know."

"You knew before we started. That's why I asked for the slot."

He got his elbows under him. "You saw it at breakfast?"

"I saw it on the stair, after the council. You told Karis it was late on cords, and you said it the way you say things you've already written down and don't want to say twice." She straightened up. "So say the rest of it out loud, then. Not in that book, where I've got to wait for it."

---

He sat up on the oak. Something under his ribs on the right side was deciding whether to hurt, and coming down on the side of yes.

"It's the counting," he said. "Whatever I watch people with, the read drinks from the same cup. I've been emptying the cup into stairs and galleries since before the council. So the read gets the dregs, and the dregs come in slow." He pressed two fingers to his ribs and found the place. "Slow enough that I set my feet for where you were going to be instead of where you were. On the floor, against you, that's this." He took the fingers away. "Against somebody who doesn't like me, it's worse."

"So I see." Lira put down her hand at last, and he took it, and she leaned back on his weight and brought him up off the boards in one pull. "And it buys what?"

"The counting?"

"The counting. You've paid me two falls for it today. Karis says it took you across the shin last week. What are you getting?"

He took his time. She let him. She stood with her hands on her hips in the middle of the floor, getting her breath, while on the bench the Current lecturer pretended very hard to be asleep.

"I'll give it you like a receipt," he said. He held up a thumb. "I can walk into this hall and know which post on that gallery the Compact is leaning on, without looking." A finger. "I can walk into the yard and know which tier, and roughly which body in the knot." Another. "I know his Path costs him, and that it runs short in the afternoons, and about when." Another. "And I know the one thing that makes him walk away from a good view, which is being alone." He looked at his hand. "Nobody I've ever heard of, anywhere, has known that much about the people set to watch them. I'd have paid twice as much for it and called it cheap."

"And the other side of the receipt?"

"A beat off the read, for as long as I keep it up. Two falls, First-day, east hall. One shin, last week, on cords." He touched his ribs. "One bruise. Here. Your shoulder."

Lira looked at him for a moment without any expression at all.

Then she laughed. It came out of her short and surprised and completely real, and went up into the beams of the empty hall, and two of the watchers on the gallery leaned over to see what was funny.

"You're the only person I've ever met who'd file a bruise," said Lira.

"Somebody ought to."

"Nobody ought to." She was still smiling, which she did not do often enough for him to be used to it. "That's what makes it so entirely you." She walked back to her chalk and turned round and planted her feet. "Again. And this time don't go on anything at all. Stand there and let me come the whole way, and call me as you feel me, and I'll tell you how late you are. If I'm going to knock you over for a whole season, I want to know exactly how hard I'm allowed."

They went on until the bell. He did not fall again; he did not use the Wind again, either. He stood on his feet and let her come at him exchange after exchange while he called her weight aloud a half-second after he felt it, *left*, *round*, *low*, and she called back *late*, *late*, *on time*, *late*. The Current lecturer, who had begun humming again at the eleventh minute out of what Cael could only suppose was tact, stopped humming at the fourteenth and watched the rest of it with both eyes open.

At the end, signing the sheet, he looked from one of them to the other for a moment, as if a question had come all the way up to his mouth. Then he signed, and handed the sheet to Cael, and said only, "Ice for the ribs. Not heat. Heat makes it worse," and went out.

It was the first thing any of the eleven had ever said to him that was not about the hour.

---

The new week's floor issue went up on the residence board the next morning, the seventy-sixth, a Second-day, in the ordinary way.

It was the usual half-sheet in the usual clerk's hand, dated twice and initialled twice in its corner, as everything the wing put out was. *Supervised floor allocation, week following.* Four lines. Cael read it standing at the board with the traffic of the passage going by behind him, as he read every issue, and on the fourth line he stopped.

The office numbered its halls on paper, as offices do, though nobody on the bluff ever said the numbers aloud. Hall one was the north hall, on stone. Hall three was the east, on timber. And his Sixth-day slot, the coming Sixth-day, the eightieth, which had been hall three every week since the allocation began, said hall one.

He knew at once how it had happened. It was the trade. Nine days ago he and Brom had swapped for a single Sixth-day, his hour on the timber for Brom's later hour on the stone, one week only, on a half-sheet he had carried to the counter himself. Somebody making up the new week's issue had copied from the wrong line of the ledger, from the traded week instead of the standing one, and carried a single week's exception forward as if it were the rule. It was the most ordinary mistake an office could make. A wing that issued paper for four hundred people must make it twice a week, and mend it whenever somebody came to the counter to complain.

He did not go to the counter.

He went to his lecture instead, and sat at the back, and did not hear a word of it. He spent the whole of it with the issue folded in his pocket, deciding.

It cost him almost nothing. That was the first thing to price. If he did nothing at all, then on Sixth-day he would walk to the north hall at the second bell, find the stone empty of any supervisor expecting him, walk across to the east hall, and lose perhaps a quarter of his session to the walk and the explaining. It was a nuisance. It was nothing more.

But to somebody who was building a map of Cael's week from the paper, it was not nothing. To that somebody, the issue said that at the second bell on Sixth-day Cael would be on the north hall's stone. So a man who read the paper and trusted it would be at the north hall's best post at the second bell, among whatever crowd the north hall gathered, looking down at an empty floor. He would lose a whole session's watching, every minute of it, to a clerk's slip that nobody had reported.

Unless he read the paper more carefully than that. Unless he read it the way he read the backs of sheets.

If the issue stayed wrong until Sixth-day, then the office did not read Cael's paper any closer than it read anybody's. If it came right on its own, without a word from Cael to anybody, then somebody in that wing was reading his sheets line by line, and minded a wrong line enough to put it right unasked.

He wrote the decision down that evening before he did anything else, because a decision he had not written down had a way of turning into an instinct he could not afterward examine.

*Floor issue, Sixth-day the 80th: hall one for hall three. The trade carried forward. Leaving it. I'll say nothing to the office, the board, or anybody. If it stays wrong, I walk across on the day and lose a quarter of an hour. If it comes right on its own, I'll know who reads my paper.*

He looked at that, and then wrote a line under it, because Lira had a claim on the subject.

*Lira's price: if he touches my paper wrong. This isn't his error. If he touches it at all, it'll be to put it right. I think that's outside her terms. I'll tell her either way.*

Then he went to bed, and lay awake for an hour, not because he was worried but because he could not stop wondering what a man who never looked at him would do with a mistake he had not made.

---

The brackets posted the next morning, and for a day and a half the whole bluff thought about nothing else, and Cael thought about it with the rest of them, and the floor issue sat folded in his coat.

The next sheet went up on the Fifth-day, the seventy-ninth, among a dozen others, and he nearly walked past it.

*Revised issue*, it said at the head, in the office's hand. Two dates in the corner, two sets of initials, and not a word anywhere about why. He read down it from habit. First line, second, third. Fourth line, the coming Sixth-day: hall three.

The east hall. The timber. His own slot, where it had always been.

He did not take it down at once. He stood in front of it with the passage going by at his back, every voice in it still arguing about the brackets, and felt something deep in his chest roll slowly over, like a fish turning in cold water, and come up delighted.

Nobody had complained. He was certain of that, because the only person on the bluff with a reason to complain was himself, and he had been nowhere near the counter for three days. The clerk who made up the week's issues had not caught it; that clerk had made the mistake in the first place. Somebody else had sat down with the ledger in one hand and Cael's issue in the other and seen the single week's trade carried into a week where it did not belong. That somebody had known it was wrong, and had known that Cael would know it was wrong, because Cael read every line of every sheet as surely as the man at the copying table read the backs. And he had known that Cael had let it stand for three days without a word.

And he had mended it.

Cael went and sat on the stair at the end of the passage, out of the traffic, with the two sheets on his knee, the wrong one and the right one, and went over it the only way he really knew how to go over anything, which was as a bout.

It had been an exchange. He saw that now. He had opened it. He had let a wrong sheet stand on the board, deliberately, and that was a feed: a little weight put forward on purpose to see what the other man would do with it.

A man who wanted to catch Cael out would have let it ride and been at the north hall on Sixth-day to see whether Cael went there. A man who did not care would never have noticed. This man had done neither. He had answered the feed without touching it. He had simply done his work correctly and on time, with his initials on it twice, as though for anybody, and in doing so he had said, as clearly as if he had stood in front of Cael and spoken: *I see what you did. I am not going to pretend I didn't. And I am going to go on doing my job properly, in plain sight, whatever you do.*

No touch either way. Called even. Both men back to their chalk.

Cael folded the two sheets together and put them inside his coat, and went up to the rim of the Crown yard, where he knew he would find Lira reading the brackets for the tenth time with her arms folded. He gave her both sheets without a word and told her the rest.

She read them, and gave them back, and was quiet.

"He touched your paper," she said.

"He mended it."

"I know what he did." She looked out over the empty floor. "It's outside my terms. I said wrong. You were right to keep it." She was quiet again. Then, much lower: "It's worse, though, isn't it? If he'd moved your hall to make you miss an hour, I'd know what he was. I'd be at Withrow's door by noon. Instead he's put it right. On time. Twice initialled." She shook her head slowly. "I don't know what to do about a man who does his work properly."

"No," said Cael. "Nor do I. I think that may be the point."

He did not write anything that afternoon. He waited until night, until the window was dark and the river was loud, and then he opened the binder to the day's page and wrote two lines under everything else, and sat over them for a long time with the pen still in his hand.

*He fixed it. Sixth-day, hall three. Nobody asked him to.*

*We're playing now. Neither of us has said so. I don't think either of us is going to.*

---

He eased off the counting for four days after that, on purpose, because he had promised the Log that he would, once, at a time of his choosing, and see whether the read came back.

It was harder than he had expected. The shorthand had become a habit in a fortnight, the way a limp becomes a habit in a week. He caught his eyes going to the turned post and the brass lamp and the second landing of the lecture stair a dozen times a day, and each time took them away again. He did not count. He did not write a single dot. He let the man stand wherever he stood, and went to his sessions and his lectures with his attention on nothing but what was in front of him, and on the fourth evening he went out to the wall to find out what that had bought.

Brom was at his post. Karis was feeding him, which was a sight Cael would have paid money to watch.

Karis could not feed. She knew everything about how a feed ought to be sold, in theory, in tremendous detail, and could have written a monograph on it, and her body did not believe a word of the monograph. She put her weight into her empty feeds with such earnest honesty that Brom turned on every one of them, and she pulled her honest ones up short out of sheer politeness, so that Brom let those go by. After ten minutes he had turned on twelve of thirteen empties and let all her real ones pass, and the two of them were laughing so hard that Brom had to hold onto the post.

"You're the worst feeder on this hill," said Brom, wiping his eyes.

"I'm the most honest feeder on this hill," said Karis. "That's the trouble. I can't make my body say something it doesn't mean." She looked at Cael. "Your turn. I'm going to sit down and be a scholar."

He took her place at the post and gave Brom a dozen feeds, honest and empty mixed, and Brom turned on seven of the eight empties, and groaned at himself, and said *again*. Then, without warning him, Cael asked Brom to feed him instead.

Brom came at him with his big hands open. The read laid his weight on Cael's forearm, left, then round, then dropping, and every time it laid it there in time. Not early, the way it had never been early. But on time, a full pace and a half, the old comfortable margin, the room he had lived in for two years without ever once noticing it was a room.

He stopped and stood still at the post for a moment, and found his hands were not quite steady.

"Back?" said Karis, who had been watching his face instead of the feeds.

"Back." He took out the Log and wrote it on his knee. *Four days off the counting. Read on time, Brom feeding, a dozen calls. One instance. Not a rule until three.*

Karis leaned over and read it upside down.

"You're going to go back to counting tomorrow," she said.

"Yes."

"And the read will go slow again."

"Yes."

"And you'll ease off again in a month to see if it comes back again, so you can write *two*." She did not sound disapproving. She sounded like a woman checking a sum. "Can I say one thing? As your scholar."

"You've never once asked before."

"I'm asking now, because it's going to annoy you." She tapped the binder on his knee, the shorthand section. "All of this. The galleries, the stair, the rail on the covered walk, the curve. It's beautiful work. It's the best thing you've ever done. And every line of it is the same instrument. Where a man stands, written down by you. You've measured it from a lot of different places, and on different days, and you've had me hold the pencil once or twice while you counted. But two hands on one instrument is still one instrument." She sat back. "You need a second leg under it. Something that doesn't come from where he stands."

"I know," said Cael. "I wrote that down."

"I know you wrote it down. I'm saying it out loud, so it's true in two places." Karis smiled. "That's what you've been teaching all of us, isn't it? Lira's been at you for a week about it. Say it, don't just file it."

Brom, at the post, groaned. "Are you two going to feed me or not?"

"Not," said Karis serenely. "We're going to sit here and be insufferable."

And they did, the three of them, along the coping in the last of the light, while Brom turned on nothing at his post and laughed at himself, and down across the river the grey coats changed at their lamp, on the bell, two and two, as plainly as gateposts.

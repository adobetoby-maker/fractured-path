# Chapter 24 — The Ninth Session

He wrote the Log entry that night at the desk under the window, with the shutter back and the rain gone off at last, and his left forearm lying on the blotter beside the page like something he had borrowed and would have to return.

It was the longest entry he had made since Greyvane. He did not shorten it. Every time his pen slowed over a sentence and he felt the old urge to tidy it, he let the sentence stand exactly as it had come, because tidying was what he had done to the Greyvane entry and he had only just learned what that cost.

*Karis's finding, in full, is in her minute. My copy is pinned inside the back board. Read that first.*

*What I've decided, in the order it was decided.*

*One. I won't make the conditions. Six ways to make them are written in the minute in my hand, every one of them refused. They are written down so that none of them can walk back in one night pretending to be new.*

*Two. If his own work brings it to me, close and in earnest, on his initiative and nobody else's, I'll reach. Directed. Awake. Not swept along.*

*Three. He can't agree to it and he'll never know it happened. There's no coin I could pay him in that wouldn't end him to take. So the cost is his, and I'm choosing it for both of us. That is exactly the thing I've hated in every person who ever had a say over me, and I'm writing it here in those words so I can't soften them later.*

*Four. I want it. Not a sixth line on the inventory: the thing itself. I have never in my life been able to go unseen, and I would like, once, to know what it is to be able to. That sentence is the reason for this whole entry. Everything above it is reasoning, and I've three years of practice at making reasoning come out where I want it.*

He sat back. The river was loud below the window, swollen with two days of rain, and a barge went down on it with its lamps swinging and its horn giving the long note and the two short, and something under the bluff answered *heard you*.

He bent to the page again and wrote the next part slowly, because he meant every word of it to be load-bearing.

*Last year's me was afraid of not-knowing what I do. This year's version is worse: knowing exactly, and deciding anyway. Write it down before, not after. That's the whole difference between me and the system.*

He read the whole entry through once and changed nothing. Then, at the foot of the page, where he put the things he could not leave out and did not want to look at, he added one more line in smaller writing.

*Karis, on the stair afterward, when the others had gone: "An instrument doesn't change sides. It changes readings." I wrote it down because it sounded wise. I've turned it over all evening, and I think it's the truest thing said tonight, and I would very much like it to be wrong.*

---

In the morning he took the Log to breakfast, open at the entry, and put it down beside Lira's bowl.

She looked at it, and then at him.

"It's the procedure," he said. "Before, in front of you. I said it in the minute. If I only write it, it's me reading my own handwriting, and I've found out what that's worth."

Lira wiped her fingers on her napkin, which she did not usually trouble to do, and pulled the Log over and read. She read slowly, as she read everything that had her name near it, and when she reached the fourth item her eyes stopped moving for a while. Brom, across the table, went on eating his porridge with great concentration and did not look at the page at all, which Cael understood was Brom's way of being in the room for it.

At last Lira pushed the Log back.

"The third one's ugly," she said.

"It's meant to be."

"I know. I'm saying it's right." She picked up her spoon. "The fourth one's the one I'd have left out. If I were you. So I'm glad you didn't." She ate a mouthful and swallowed. "Pass it to Karis. She'll want to correct your commas."

Karis did correct his commas, two of them, in pencil in the margin, with a small note that said *these are yours to keep or not*. He kept one.

---

That evening he went up to the east hall gallery for the cohort's hour, because Brom had asked him to, and because in six weeks he had only watched the taking-apart twice.

The gallery was half full. Most evenings it drew a few second-years from the Blade floor and a handful of people who simply liked watching somebody else be corrected. Cael took the bench at the east end, nearest the stair, and did not look toward the turned post at the far end of the walk. In the propped door's glass across the hall, small and bent, he saw that somebody with an armful of forms was standing there in a knot of three. He wrote nothing down.

Below him on the oak, Rooke stood at the west end of the floor exactly as he always stood, narrow and grey, with his weight even on both feet and his hands loose. He did not raise his voice once in the hour. He did not need to; fourteen people round the walls had learned to listen for him the way Cael listened for the read.

"Tarn," said Rooke.

Tarn came off the wall. He was a Blade third-year, a little shorter than Brom and much lighter, with a narrow face and very quick hands, and Cael had given him half a page since the first week because of the way he fed: short, flat, committed only as far as the wrist, so that the strike looked real right up until the instant it was not there. Brom had told Cael about him with a kind of rueful admiration. *He feeds like a man dropping coins in a beggar's cup. Small ones. You can't help reaching.*

Tarn fed. Brom held. Tarn fed again, shorter, and Brom held again, the Iron Skin coming up hard under the strike so that it landed on a forearm like a stick on a post, and no weight at all going out to turn it. Twice more. Each time Brom's breath went a little harder and his guard came back a little slower, and each time he did not turn, and on the gallery a second-year murmured to her neighbour that the big one was being *stubborn*, as though it were a fault.

On the fifth, Brom turned.

It was not a decision. Cael was watching his face and saw no decision cross it. Tarn's feed came in exactly like the four before, and Brom's body went out to meet it the way it had gone out to meet feeds since he was twelve, the hardening releasing on the near side as the drive came up on the far. For a quarter of a second there was nothing at all holding him up.

Tarn stepped into it and laid his palm on Brom's ribs.

"Stop," said Rooke.

The floor stopped.

"Again," said Rooke. "From the fourth. Slower. Everybody watch the left heel."

They did it again. Cael, on the gallery, watched the left heel with the other thirteen, and he saw it: on the fifth feed, a hair before anything else moved, Brom's left heel came up off the oak. It was the turn starting in the foot before the man had agreed to it.

Rooke did not say anything about the heel. He let them see it three times, and then he sent Tarn back to the wall and called Merrick out, and the hour went on.

Afterward, at the foot of the gallery stair, Brom found him with his wraps already off and a red line across his ribs where Tarn's palm had been, and asked him to call feeds at the post before supper, the way he had at Greyvane. Cael called. Brom fed him a dozen, mixed, empty and true.

He was late on four of them.

Not wrong; late. The press came along his forearm true every time, and on four of the twelve it came after his feet had already started to guess. Brom saw it. Brom saw everything at a post. He said nothing about it, and that evening Cael wrote it in the Log without comment: *Read: four late in twelve at the post. Counting since the season opened. Still paying.*

---

The ninth session came round on a clearing Seventh-day, with the rain gone off in the night and the boards in the Crown yard still dark at their edges where the water had got in.

Lira's bout was third on the Copper card. She had sent the challenge up four places, as she had every time, to the highest name the rule let her reach, and the name was a Mire Path fourth-year whose card said Copper Rank Six. Cael had begun a page on him in the second week of the season and had added to it twice. The Mire Path sixth-ranker was long in the leg and slow-spoken, with a farmer's way of standing as if he were waiting for weather. On the second practice ring, a month ago, Cael had seen him make the boards so treacherous under a Blade boy's feet that the Blade boy had laughed out loud and appealed to the table.

And on that same afternoon, two rows behind Cael, a pair of Iron girls had argued in furious whispers for a quarter of an hour over whether a Mire fighter could stick a Wind fighter's feet before she ever left the ground. Neither had convinced the other. Cael had written the question in the margin of the Mire boy's page with a small mark beside it, meaning *wait*.

He sat on the east side, where he always sat, with the binder on his knee.

Mire was a strange Path to watch, because most of what it did happened in the floor. The fourth-year declared without any show at all, a slight settling at the knees, and from his feet outward the boards changed. To a careless eye they did not change. To a careful one they took on a faint dull sheen, as though someone had wiped them over with a wet cloth and they had not yet dried, and the chalk dust lying on them went from white to grey. The sheen spread slowly, perhaps an arm's length for every breath he spent, and it gripped. Cael had seen a Blade boy's foot come off it with a sound like a boot lifting out of mud.

The table read the terms. First to three clean touches.

Lira went straight in, as she always went, low off her lead foot. The first burst carried her wide to the right and fast, round the spreading grey and onto clean wood at its edge, and Cael felt it in his own hip from forty feet up, the toll at the front and the jolt at the back. She landed.

And stayed.

It was not a large thing. Her right heel came down a hand's width inside the edge of the sheen, where the boards were grey, and when the landing beat let go of her the foot did not come with her. It came a half-breath late, with a small wet sound, and the half-breath was all the Mire boy needed; he was already there, unhurried, with his hand on her shoulder.

"Touch. Mire. One."

A low sound went round the tiers. Somewhere behind Cael, a girl's voice said, quite distinctly, *I told you*.

Lira walked back to her chalk without looking at anyone. Cael watched her go and did not write anything yet. He had seen something in that exchange and he wanted to be sure of it before it went on paper.

She had not been caught when she left the ground. She had been caught when she came down.

---

On the second exchange she did not burst at all for the first half-minute.

She went round him on her feet, steady, keeping to clean wood, and the Mire boy let her go, turning slowly in place to keep her in front of him and spending breath after breath, so that the grey crept outward from where he stood in a wider and wider ring. The tiers began to fidget. A Wind fighter walking was not what anybody had climbed the stair to see.

Cael was watching Lira's eyes. They were not on the Mire boy at all. They were on the floor round his feet.

He saw it a moment before she used it. The fourth-year was standing still, as he had to stand to spend steadily, and the sheen ran out from him in every direction like the ring round a stone dropped in a pond. Every direction except one. The boards under his own boots, and a half-pace round them, were clean and pale and dry, because a Mire fighter who gripped his own footing would be standing in his own trap. In the middle of all that grey there was one small island of honest wood, and he was standing on it.

Lira went.

The burst carried her straight across the grey without touching it, low and flat, and the landing beat put her down on the island, chest to chest with the man who owned it, in the only place on the floor where his Path could not reach her. For the half-breath the beat held her she could do nothing but stand there. Neither could he. He could not step back without stepping into his own sheen. When the beat let go she put her palm on his breastbone.

"Wind. One each."

The Mire boy looked down at his own feet, and then at her, with an expression of plain, mild surprise, like a farmer finding a calf on the wrong side of a gate he had shut himself.

On the third exchange he did not stand still. He had understood, and he was no fool. He moved as he spent, a slow drifting walk, so that his little island of clean wood travelled with him and never stayed long enough in one place to be aimed at. Behind him, where his boots had been, the sheen spread into his footprints.

Cael leaned forward without meaning to.

Lira had told him once, years ago at Ardenmere, that you learn more from the touch you lose than from the three you win, and for most of a minute she did not seem to be doing anything at all except watching the floor behind the Mire boy as he walked. Cael watched it with her. And then he saw what she had seen, and it was the thing he had not let himself write after the first exchange.

The grey in the footprints was not grey yet. It was only beginning. Fresh sheen was thin and pale, and it took a breath or more to set into the dark wet grip that had held her heel. The boards the Mire boy had just left were the safest boards on the floor behind him, safer than clean wood on the far side, because he had just stood on them and his Path had not yet had time to take hold.

She went after his footprints.

Two bursts, one after the other, each landing a stride behind him on boards he had left the breath before. The first landing let her close half the distance. The second put her at his back. He heard it and turned, too slowly, because a Mire fighter's whole art was to make other people slow and he had never once needed to be quick himself, and her hand was on his shoulder before he had finished turning.

"Wind. Two to one."

He did not wait for the fourth. On the last exchange he spent everything he had left at once, and the grey went out from him in a great dark sweep across half the floor, so fast that the chalk lines drowned in it. It was the answer of a man who has run out of better ones. It made the floor slow for everybody, and him most of all, because he could not move off his island without wading.

Lira did not burst. She walked. She stepped onto his freshest footprints, the ones still pale, and from those to the next, along the trail he had laid himself, unhurried, like somebody crossing a stream on stones she had watched him put down. He stood in the middle of his own floor and watched her come, and Cael thought he saw him nearly smile.

"Wind. Three. Bout."

Four bursts in all, and one of them spent learning what not to do. Cael wrote it down while the tiers were still clapping. Then, in the margin of the Mire boy's page, beside the small mark he had made a month before, he wrote the answer to the Iron girls' quarrel.

*Can a Mire stick a Wind fighter before she leaves the ground? No. Leaving is too quick. But she has to come down, and the landing beat is half a breath she can't move in. So it isn't the take-off. It's the landing. And fresh mire is slow to grip. The safest place to land is where he's just been.*

He looked across the floor. Lira was walking back to the steps with her face showing nothing, her left hand pressed flat against her thigh, as it always was now after a bout. As she came level with the Mire fourth-year he said something to her, and she stopped and said something back, and the two of them stood a moment looking down at the darkening grey trail between them, like two people who had planted a field together and were deciding what had come up.

---

By the seventh bell the sun had come round far enough to lay a bar of hard gold light across the upper west tiers, and the crowd up there began to do what a crowd does when the light changes.

Cael was not watching for it. He had told himself he was not, at any rate. He was writing the last Iron bout of the afternoon, two Shield fourth-years walking their panes at each other like men carrying doors, when the knot of six that had stood on the upper west tier for an hour came apart.

It did not come apart all at once. Two of them shaded their eyes. One turned his back to the sun. A girl stepped down a row to get out of the glare, and her friend went after her, and the rest of the knot drifted along the tier toward the end that was still in shadow, the way a herd drifts toward the wall of a field when it starts to rain. It was the most natural thing in the world.

A man with an armful of forms went with them.

He did not hurry. He did not decide anything Cael could see. He was simply part of the drift, carried along it for a few steps as anybody would have been, and when the drift stopped he stopped too, four bodies further along the tier, in shade, third in a knot of five.

Cael did not look at him. He had not looked straight at him in weeks. In the brass hood of the lamp, at the edge of his eye, he worked out the new place without wanting to. He did it the way his hands would have counted change, because it was simply what his attention did now whenever he left it alone.

The man had given up a fifth part of the floor. In exchange he had both stair heads, the north and the south, and the whole of the approach from the assessment wing's gate, which from the old place had been hidden behind a pillar. It was a better place. It was the best place on the tier for the last hour of a session, when people began to leave and it mattered more who went down the stairs than who stood on the floor. He had reached it inside a dozen seconds, inside a crowd that had moved for reasons of its own, and the crowd had swallowed him so completely that nobody in it could have said afterward that he had moved at all.

*Best place again,* Cael thought. *Again. He's the best working practitioner of anything I've ever stood near, and what he practises is not being here. Eleven weeks within a stone's throw of me, and I've found him put a foot wrong once, on a morning I built for it.*

Then the other thought came, in the flat plain voice the binder used for facts, and it came whole.

*And if his work ever brings that to me, close and in earnest, I'll take a piece of it. And he'll go home from the conversation never knowing he was in one.*

The light moved a finger's breadth further along the tier. In the brass, the small figure stepped half a pace back into the shade and was furniture.

Cael looked down at his own hand on the binder. The read had told him, a moment ago, about a strike on the floor below a full beat after he had seen the strike land. He had not even noticed until now. He was spending again, every second, on a man at the edge of a lamp, and the account he was spending from was the same one he would need on the day it mattered, if it ever came.

The minute said *awake*. He could not be awake on the dregs.

He closed the binder on the last Iron bout, unfinished, and that evening he wrote a single line under the Mire boy's answer and the four late calls at the post.

*Stopped counting. Today, the ninth session, seventh bell. Not four days this time. Until further notice. If it comes, I want the whole read for it.*

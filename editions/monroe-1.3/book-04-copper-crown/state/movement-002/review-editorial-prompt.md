# Chapter 8 — The Wall Has Students

He woke on the twenty-second morning with the sentence already in his mouth, as if he had been saying it in his sleep.

*Gault writes it once.*

The notice lay on the desk where he had left it, folded on its crease, and he did not unfold it. He knew what it said. Somewhere inside the next month, on a day he did not choose, a man he had never met would watch him in a room full of instruments and write down what he saw, and every half-year after that somebody would lay the new figures beside the old and look for the difference. It was the only paper he would ever sign that could not be amended, Karis had said, and she had been right, and he had lain awake a good part of the night turning that over without getting anywhere at all.

He went down to breakfast meaning to think about it properly, and Brom spoke first.

"Fourth bell," said Brom, into his porridge. "East hall. There's a gallery. Come and sit in it."

The other three stopped eating.

It was the first thing Brom had said about the east hall in twelve days. He had gone there every afternoon since the ninth with his new wraps rolled under his arm, and come back every evening with no bruise, no ice and no story. He had simply closed a door on that part of his day and sat with his back against it, cheerful about everything else. Lira had asked twice and stopped. Karis had not asked at all, which for Karis was a feat of strength.

"What's at the fourth bell?" said Cael.

"Me." Brom put his spoon down. "On the floor. First time."

"You've been going for twelve days."

"I've been sitting for twelve days." Brom looked at the table between his hands, half embarrassed and half something else. "He puts everybody new on the bench along the back wall. You don't run anything. You don't touch anybody. You watch the cohort for the whole hour, and at the end he asks you one question about what you saw, and if he doesn't like your answer you come back tomorrow and watch again." He turned his cup round once. "He says he won't put a man on his floor until he can see him watching. Yesterday I gave him an answer he liked."

"What was the question?" said Karis.

"Which of them was tired." Brom picked his spoon up. "I said the tall one, because he'd started setting his feet before he moved instead of while. He said yes. Then he said fourth bell." He took a mouthful and spoke round it. "I didn't want to talk about the bench. It sounded like I'd been put in a corner. But I hadn't. I don't know what I'd been put in. I learned more in twelve days of sitting than I did in my first year at the Ironyard."

Lira was looking at him with an expression Cael could not read at all.

"We'll all come," she said.

"No." Brom said it gently. "Cael. He'll write it down properly, and I'll want it written down." He glanced at Lira. "You can see the bruises after."

The east hall's gallery ran along its north wall at the height of a man's head, a narrow timber walk with a rail and a bench, reached by a stair so steep it was nearly a ladder. Cael climbed it a little before the fourth bell and sat at the far end, where he could see the whole floor without leaning.

From above, the floor looked like a pale lake. The boards ran the long way of the hall, oak, close-laid and polished by feet to a dull shine, and when the cohort came in and began to warm up he could feel it through the bench: a faint give, a faint return, as if the whole hall were breathing very slowly under them. He had written that down on his first morning on the bluff without knowing why it mattered. He wrote it again now, beside the date, with a line under it.

There were fourteen in Rooke's cohort, and Rooke himself stood at the west end of the floor and did nothing.

Cael had watched him once before, for nine minutes, through a door. From above it was clearer. Rooke was narrow and grey and past fifty, in a plain dark coat with the sleeves turned back once, and he stood with his weight settled evenly over both feet and his hands loose at his sides, and he did not move unless moving bought him something. When he wanted a pair to stop he said one word. When he wanted them to start again he said another. In the whole first quarter of an hour he said perhaps thirty words, and every one of them changed something on the floor.

Brom came in last, in his wraps, and Rooke pointed at the middle of the boards without looking at him.

Four of the cohort came out to meet him.

They did not come all at once. They came in turn, a short exchange each, then the next, and Cael understood within three turns that it had been arranged that way so that Brom would meet four different problems before he had finished solving the first. The first was a small quick girl who laughed when she was hit and was very seldom hit. The second was tall and loose and never came at him the same way twice. The third did almost nothing; she stood off at the edge of his reach and waited, and waited, and when Brom finally moved she was already somewhere else. The fourth was older than the others, seventeen or eighteen, with his weight carried forward on the balls of his feet even when he was standing still, and he did not look at Brom until the instant before he moved, and then he looked at nothing else.

Ephram, Cael wrote. He knew the name from the Iron board, where it sat at the top of the column in last season's fading chalk. *Blade. Iron Rank Six. Watches like he's paying for it.*

Then he stopped writing, because the floor had begun to go too fast for the pen.

---

Brom had been the hardest problem in every hall Cael had ever seen him in.

At the Ironyard, men twice his age had come off the boards shaking their hands out and laughing ruefully, because hitting Brom was like hitting a door that hit back. At Greyvane he had gone through the defensive cohort in a term, and Wray had needed her own two hands to find the one place in him that gave. He was big and he was patient. Iron Skin made the surface of him dense at the instant of contact, so that a strike landed on something more like stone than flesh, and then he did the thing he had built for himself in the years before anybody taught him anything. He let the strike commit, all the way, and only then took it and turned it, so that the force went on past him into nothing, and the striker went with it.

The quick girl touched him on the shoulder before he had finished setting his feet.

Rooke said, "Again."

She touched him again, on the other shoulder, and laughed, and went back to the wall, and the tall one came out. He feinted high and went low and touched Brom on the hip, and then on the next exchange feinted low and went low anyway and touched him in the same place, and Cael saw Brom's face change: not hurt, but puzzled, like a man who has opened a familiar door and found a different room. The third girl stood off and waited, and Brom waited too, for a while, and then moved, because he was not built to wait forever, and she touched him on the wrist as he came. Then Ephram came out, and touched him three times in an exchange so short Cael counted it on the bench afterward by the rise and fall of his own breath and made it four breaths.

Brom tried things. That was the part Cael would remember longest, more than the touches. He did not stand and suffer it. On the quick girl's third turn he dropped his weight early, before she came, so that she would arrive on a man already set, and she simply waited until his legs got tired of being set and came then. Against the tall one he stopped watching the feint and watched the hips, which was what Cael would have told him to do, and for two exchanges it worked; then the tall one began to feint with his hips as well. Against the waiting girl he tried waiting longer than she did, and found that she had been taught to wait by somebody far more patient than anybody who had ever taught Brom anything. Each idea bought him one exchange, sometimes two. Then the person in front of him had seen it, and it was spent.

None of them ever explained. They did not need to. Each correction they made to him was written on his body in the one language every fighter on the floor could read, and he read it, and stored it, and was touched again.

None of the touches were hard. None of them needed to be. Iron Skin would have taken a great deal more than any of them carried, and Brom stood under them like a wall under rain. But every one of them was clean, which was the whole point of them, and every one arrived somewhere Brom had not finished arriving at, and on a scored floor each of them would have gone up on the board.

He could see Brom counting. His lips moved after each touch.

Somewhere around the twentieth minute the lips stopped moving.

Cael wrote that down, because he knew exactly what it meant. Brom had counted everything for as long as Cael had known him: tallies in the pocket book, days of the clause, coppers on toll ledges. He had stopped counting because the count had begun to take some of the attention he needed for the next touch, and he had chosen the next touch. Cael did not think he had ever seen Brom choose anything over a count.

By the end of the hour Cael's own tally, kept in the margin in groups of five, stood at forty-three. Brom had touched somebody back perhaps six times, all of them against the tall one, and all of them the same way, and the tall one had stopped letting him after the sixth.

What struck Cael, sitting above it with the pen going slack in his fingers, was how little luck there was anywhere on the floor. At the Ironyard and at Greyvane a fair share of every bout had been accident: a slip, a guess, a strike thrown at the wrong place that happened to find somebody standing in it. Here there was none of that. Four people had taken turns on Brom for an hour, each of them doing precisely what they had meant to do at a speed Brom had never in his life been asked to answer, and the forty-three marks in the margin were not forty-three pieces of luck but forty-three small arguments, each of them won.

Rooke said, "Enough," and the cohort went to the walls for water.

Then he walked across the floor to Brom and said something Cael could not hear. Brom listened with his head down. He nodded once, and then again, and then he did a thing Cael had never once seen him do with an instructor: he asked a question back. Rooke answered it in two words. Brom went to the wall.

Up on the gallery Cael found he had been holding his breath, and let it go, and closed the notebook on the forty-three.

---

Brom came into the second-quadrangle common room at the fifth bell, walked past every chair in it, and sat down on the floor with his back against the wall under the window.

He did not say anything. Lira looked up from the table where she had been not filling in a ladder form for most of the afternoon, and looked at Cael, and Cael shook his head very slightly. Karis put her pen down. The room's other occupants, two Iron girls arguing about a lecture, went on arguing about it. Brom sat with his hands on his knees and his eyes shut and his breathing slow and even, and the clock on the mantel ticked through most of a minute and a half.

Then he got up, without a word, went to the cold-store at the end of the passage, came back with an ice wrap, sat down again on the floor, and laid it across his left shoulder.

Nobody had told him to. In three years Cael had never once seen Brom fetch ice for himself without being nagged, and it said more than the tallies in the margin.

"Well?" said Lira.

"Forty-three," said Cael.

"He was counting?"

"I was counting. He stopped at about twenty." Cael sat on the arm of a chair. "Four of them, in turns. Nothing hard. Nothing lucky either."

Brom opened his eyes.

"Was it forty-three?" Brom sounded interested, as if it were somebody else's figure. "I lost the count. The whole of last year, the Ironyard and Greyvane together, I don't believe anybody got through to me forty-three times." He shifted the ice. "And not one of them was trying to hurt me. That's the part I keep coming back to. Not one of them was trying to hurt me. They were all just right. Over and over again."

"You're Iron-equivalent," said Lira. She said it a little sharply, the way she said things she was going to defend. "Two years of circuit ratings. Greyvane's defensive cohort in a term."

"Against the Ironyard. Against Greyvane." Brom let the back of his skull rest on the plaster. "Wray told me there was a wall in me. She found it with her own hands and said nobody at Greyvane was fast enough to show me where it went. I've been feeling for it for a year and a half like a man feeling for a door in the dark." He was quiet a moment. Then something came into his face, slowly, from somewhere underneath, and Cael realized with a small shock what it was. It was not distress. It was the purest happiness he had seen on Brom since the bridge at Ostrand.

"I found the wall. It has students."

Karis laughed out loud, and put her hand over her mouth, and laughed again behind it.

"You're pleased," said Lira, disbelieving.

"I'm *delighted*." Brom meant every syllable. "Wray found one thing in me with her hands, once, and it took her a term. This afternoon four of them found a dozen before the bell. Two years I've been the wall everybody else breaks on. You can't grumble about that out loud. People think you're showing off. But it's like being the one grown man at a children's game. Nobody learns anything, and you least of all." He pressed the ice down. "This afternoon I was the one problem in the hall that everybody else already had the answer to, and they gave me the answer, one at a time, and they were kind about it. I don't think I've ever had a better afternoon in my life."

Karis had her notebook out by then, because she could not help it.

"What kind of touches?" she said. "Strikes, or placements?"

"Placements, mostly. A hand on you where a strike would have gone, so you'd know." Brom considered. "The little one hits a bit. She can't help it. She's so pleased to get in that she forgets to stop her hand."

"And you got none of them back."

"Six," said Cael. "The tall one, six times, the same way. Then he stopped letting him."

"Six," said Brom, with enormous satisfaction, as if it had been sixty.

Lira had not gone back to her form. She was sitting very still at the table with the pen in her fingers, looking at Brom on the floor with his ice and his shut eyes and his wide unguarded smile, and after a moment Cael understood what was in her face, because he had seen it there before, on roads, in the dark, when she thought nobody was watching.

"Four people," she said quietly, "looked at you properly for an hour."

Brom opened one eye.

"Yes," he said. "They did."

Lira looked down at the ladder form in front of her, which was four lines long and had been blank since noon. She did not pick it up. She did not say anything else. But she did not look away from it either, and Cael put that in the notebook later, on Lira's page, with the date and nothing else beside it.

"What did Rooke say to you?" said Cael. "At the end."

"He said, 'You were counting.'" Brom smiled at the ceiling. "I said I'd stopped. He said, 'I know. Why?' I said because I needed the room. And he said, 'Good. Tomorrow.'"

"And you asked him something."

"I asked him who'd find it first." Brom closed his eyes again. "The place Wray found. I asked which of them would find it. He said, 'Ephram. Three sessions.'"

---

It took Ephram three sessions, to the day, and Cael was on the gallery for all of them, and for the fortnight that came after.

By then Cael had a page for Ephram and was filling a second. He had placed him on the Iron board the first afternoon and checked him against the registrar's table on the second: Blade Path, Iron-tier, Rank Six, at the top of the Iron column, which at Halcenvane was the same as saying he was the best competitor on the bluff. There were fourteen Silver students on the hill and a Gold-tier fellow, and none of them was talked about the way Ephram was talked about, because none of them was on a column anybody could read from the yard. Here, *best* meant a place on a wall.

He did not attack the place Wray had found. Cael understood that on the fourth day, with his pen stopped over the page. He made it.

Brom's turn needed a read. That was its nature and its strength: he let a strike commit, all the way, so that he knew precisely where its weight was going, and only then took it. But a read needed something to read. So Ephram gave him something. He sold Brom a strike with three-quarters of his weight behind it, honestly, enough to set the read going, and then took it back before the turn could close. The cost of that to Ephram was a sliver of a beat, a little lost balance he recovered as he stepped. The cost to Brom was the whole gap, every time: the full stretch between the read and the turn, spent on a strike that had never arrived. And Ephram could do it again at once, and again, because a Blade practitioner could recommit almost for nothing, and an Iron Skin turn could not be begun and abandoned without paying for the beginning.

*Speed alone wouldn't do it,* Cael wrote, on the fifth day. *There isn't enough daylight between them. So he's running a trade where each repetition costs him less than it costs Brom, and he's running it over and over until the account closes. It's the cleanest thing I've seen anybody do to anybody on a floor.*

*Limit: it needs a big, patient man at the other end. Anybody quick would see the feeds for what they are and go round them. Against Brom there's almost nothing wrong with it.*

*I think I could answer the trade. I'm not at all sure I could answer the man.*

On the sixth day he tried to chart a single exchange from start to finish, stroke by stroke, the way he would have charted it for himself before a bout, and it took him a page and a half.

Ephram came off the wall with his weight forward and stopped two paces out. He sold a strike at the left shoulder, hips turning, the back foot pushing honestly into the oak, and Brom's read took it; Cael saw Brom's shoulders begin to settle into the turn. Ephram was already gone from it. He had pulled the strike back into his own centre before it reached the halfway mark, and stepped a hand to the right, and sold another at the same shoulder from the new angle. Brom's read took that too, because it was honest weight again, and Brom was a man who believed honest weight. On the third sale Brom did not turn. He held, and held, and the strike did not come, and in the instant he let himself believe it would not come at all, it came: full, clean, a flat hand laid along his ribs on the side he had just stopped guarding.

Three sales and a delivery. Perhaps four breaths. Ephram had spent a little balance three times and recovered it each time in the same step. Brom had spent the whole length of his gap three times over, and on the fourth time had nothing left in it.

Brom tried, on the eighth day, simply not to read. He stood under the feeds with his weight dead and let them go by, which was the obvious answer, and Ephram watched him do it for one exchange and then stopped feeding and struck. A man who would not read could not turn, and a man who could not turn was only large. Brom took the touch, and nodded slowly, as if he had expected it, and went back to reading, because it was the only way he knew how to stand.

Over the fortnight he watched it get worse instead of better, which he had not expected.

In the first sessions Brom fell for perhaps half the false feeds. A few days later it was more than half. By the tenth day, Ephram had stopped bothering to sell them very hard, because he no longer needed to, and Brom was turning on strikes that were barely a quarter committed, with his weight going out to meet a force that had already gone home. Cael sat on the gallery and watched his friend's best thing being used against him, a little more each afternoon, by a boy of seventeen or eighteen who never once looked pleased about it.

Rooke stood at the west end of the floor and let it happen. He did not stop it and did not correct it. Only once, on the ninth day, when Brom had turned on nothing three times in one exchange and stood afterward with his big hands open at his sides, did Rooke say anything at all, and then it was a single word, to Ephram.

"Again."

---

Brom told it on the wall behind the second quadrangle on the thirtieth evening, with his forearms on the coping and the river going dark below, and he told it in three sentences that got shorter every time he came back to them.

"He's teaching my body a lie."

Cael waited.

"Every one I fall for," said Brom, "the next comes cheaper. My body's learning to believe him." He turned his hands over on the stone. "It's like watching somebody teach a dog to come to the wrong whistle. A bit each afternoon. And you're stood there seeing it done, and you can't call the dog off, because the dog's you." He looked down at his hands on the stone. "He's very good."

"Have you told Rooke?"

"Rooke told me." Brom laughed, one short breath. "Day three. Before Ephram had even found it. He said it to me at the wall while I was drinking." He hesitated, and then did something Cael had never seen him do in three years of roads and boarding houses and yards. He unwound his left wrap, a few turns, and held the inside of it toward the last of the light.

There was writing on it, in ink, in Brom's big careful hand, run round the inside of the cloth where it lay against the wrist.

Cael stared at it. Brom did not write things down. He kept tallies, which were not writing, and he signed his name, and once, at Ardenmere, he had said that ink was where you put things when you didn't trust your body to keep them, and that he preferred to keep his things where he could use them.

"I wanted it where I'd feel it," said Brom, a little defensively. "Every time I bind up." He read it off the cloth, slowly, in Rooke's flat rhythm.

"*You built that redirect alone and it was a good piece of work for a boy with no instructor. It is now the most expensive habit you own. We are going to take it apart and you are going to be worse for a season.*"

He wound the wrap back on.

"And you said?"

"I said all right." Brom looked out at the water. "In my whole life nobody's ever said to me, *I'll make you worse.* They've all promised the other thing, and wanted it by the next bout. Wray too, a bit, though she'd never have said so." He flexed his wrist inside the cloth. "He's the first one who's ever told me the price ahead of time and asked me to pay it anyway."

"I've got the sixth day charted," said Cael. "One exchange, from his first step to the touch. Three sales and a delivery. If you want it—"

"No."

Cael looked at him.

"Not because I don't want it." Brom said it slowly, choosing each word as if it might bear weight. "Because I do. If you show me, I'll learn your answer to him, and I'll use it, and it'll work for a week. Then he'll see it, the way they all see everything, and I'll be back on the floor with my old turn and your patch over the top of it." He looked down at his wrist. "Rooke didn't say he'd patch it. He said he'd take it apart. I want to find out what's under it."

"That could take the whole season."

"He said so." Brom smiled faintly at the river. "He said I'd be worse for a season. I didn't think he meant it as a figure of speech." He straightened up off the coping, and stretched, and his shoulder cracked loudly in the dusk. "Keep your page, though. In a year I'd like to read it and see how stupid I looked."

"You didn't look stupid."

"I looked like a big man turning round on the spot for nothing." Brom was grinning now. "Forty-three times. You've got it in groups of five. Don't pretend you haven't."

Cael did not answer at once.

He was thinking of Wray on the last morning, standing on her stone with her slate under her arm. *Find somebody there who'll break your count on the third pass.* He had been on the bluff a month, and he had watched every hall and walked every corridor and filled a section of the notebook, and nobody on this hill had broken his count on any pass at all, because nobody on this hill was teaching him anything. They were measuring him. That was a different thing, and he had not until now let himself notice how different.

He looked at Brom's wrapped wrist on the coping, at the ink he could no longer see, and was surprised by how sharply he envied it.

*Brom has a man who'll make him worse on purpose and tell him so in advance,* he wrote that night. *I have a provision, a panel, and a man with instruments. Nobody here is going to make me worse for a season so I can be better after it. That's mine to do, if it's done.*

*Write that down before it turns into self-pity. It isn't, yet.*

# Chapter 9 — The Queue

Lira put her name on the ladder on the twelfth of the month, two days inside the deadline, at the registrar's table on the rim of the Crown yard, and before she did it she read out every other name on the list to Cael from memory.

She did not look at the page while she did it. She stood at the table with her hands behind her back and went down the list in the order the names had gone onto it, with a line for each: which hall, which Path, how they had walked up to the table and how they had walked away. Two hundred and nine Copper practitioners on the bluff, and something over sixty had signed by then, and she had been on the rim or in the tiers for every one of them.

"Third on the list. Stone, fourth-year. Came up the steps like he was going to argue with somebody and then didn't." She did not pause. "Fourth. Current, second-year, a girl. Signed with her left hand and then wished she hadn't. Fifth—"

"You watched all of them," said Cael.

"I said I would." Lira did not look round. "Every name before mine is somebody I saw choose to be counted. Now I know who's on the board and how they feel about it." She reached the end and stopped. "Sixty-four. And me."

The clerk at the registrar's table was a stooped young man with ink on both thumbs who had clearly been listening to all of it with growing alarm. He turned the book round for her.

"Transfer," he said, reading off the sheet clipped beside it. "Wind Path. Copper, Rank Two, formal. Re-certification." He looked up. "You understand how the seeding goes."

"Tell me anyway."

He told her. It took him a minute and a half, and he told it in the voice of a man who had told it a good many times and expected to be argued with at the end. Until the brackets posted, near the middle of term, the yard ran practice hours on its open floors, with a faculty member at this table and every result written into the practice register, which lay beside the ladder book in black boards and could be read by anybody. But when the brackets were drawn, they were seeded from last season's closing order. A practitioner who had fought last season started from wherever last season had left them. A practitioner who had not, started at the foot.

"And a transfer," said the clerk, "hasn't fought last season. Not here. So a transferred file seeds low, whatever its record says from elsewhere. It isn't personal. It's the order of the book."

"And if I'd signed on the first day?"

"Then you'd have started at the foot on the first day." He looked faintly puzzled. "It's the same foot."

"Good," said Lira. "Then I've lost nothing by watching."

She signed, in her quick slanting hand, in the sixty-fifth line, and blotted it herself before the clerk could reach for the sand.

Then she asked to see last season's closing order, and the clerk, who had plainly decided by now that she was the most alarming person he would meet that week, brought it out from under the table without argument.

Cael watched her read it. She went down the Copper column slowly, lips moving, and he knew what she was doing because he was doing it himself beside her. Last season's order stood at the top of the column; the new names would be added beneath it in the order of the book; the bottom was the sixty-fifth line, and the sixty-fifth line was hers. Between a session's paired bouts a practitioner could call out anybody above her in her own column, four names up and no further, and if she won, the place was hers and everybody she had passed slipped down one. Four rungs at a time, then, on a ladder of better than two hundred. Fewer than that, once the column settled, because a great many of the names below the seeded few would never climb at all. But even if she won every challenge she was allowed, in every window the board marked in red, she would need most of the season simply to arrive among the people she meant to beat.

"Sixteen," said Lira, under her breath. "If I win every one. If they let me call out every window." She handed the book back. "Thank you."

They walked back round the rim together, under the empty tiers.

"You could have signed a week ago," said Cael. "Same foot, same sixteen."

"I could." Lira kept her eyes on the floor of the yard, where the porter was chalking rings for the afternoon. "But a week ago I was a name in a book that nobody had looked at. Now I've looked at all of them." She glanced at him, and there was something in it he had not seen since the bridge, a hard bright humour. "They wrote me down by a number before they ever saw me. I wanted to be quite sure I'd seen every one of them first."

---

She fought her first practice bout the next afternoon, and Cael was on the third tier to see it.

The Crown yard ran differently on practice afternoons. The sunk floor was divided by chalk into three rings, and a faculty member sat at the registrar's table with the practice register open in front of him, and anybody on the ladder could put their name on a slate at the table's end and be paired with the next name of their bracket that was waiting. Bouts were short: three exchanges, each to a clean touch or called even, the supervising instructor's word final. The tiers were never more than a quarter full. But they were never empty, either, and Cael had noticed in his first week that the same faces came back every afternoon, sitting in the same places, with notebooks.

Lira was paired with a Stone Path fourth-year, Copper Rank Six, broad through the hips and thick through the neck. Cael had seen him fight twice. He fought the way Stone practitioners were taught to fight on every floor Cael had ever stood on: he stood his ground, and when a strike came he let the Path set his feet into the floor so that for a breath nothing in the world could move him, and then he struck back off that anchor with his whole weight behind it.

It was a good way to fight. Against anybody who came straight at him, it was very nearly unanswerable.

Lira came straight at him.

She came fast and low with the Wind at her heels and her weight forward, exactly as she had come at Cael on cracked stone behind the residence wing all winter, and the Stone fourth-year set his feet. Cael felt it from the third tier as a kind of change in the air of the floor, like a door shutting somewhere in a big house. The anchor took. Lira hit the space in front of it, and his answering strike came off the planted feet with his shoulders behind it, and it took her on the upper arm and turned her half round.

"Touch," said the instructor at the table, without looking up from the register. "Stone. One."

There was a small stir on the tiers. Somebody near Cael said, not quietly, "That's the transfer?"

Lira went back to her chalk and stood a moment rubbing her arm, and Cael saw her eyes go to the Stone fourth-year's feet and stay there.

She came at him again, just as fast and straight. He set his feet again. But this time, a stride out, she put her lead foot down short, a half-step that went nowhere, and the Wind did not carry her forward from it but sideways, low and quick along the chalk. The anchor had already taken. The Stone fourth-year was planted, with all the weight of the world in his heels and his strike gathering. Lira was not in front of him anymore. She was at his shoulder, and her flat hand was on the back of it, and he had not been able to turn because the Path that would have held him against anything had also held him still.

"Touch. Wind. One each."

He was quick to learn. Cael gave him that. On the third exchange he did not anchor at all; he met her coming in with his feet free and his hands up, and tried to fight her as an ordinary large man fights a small fast one. It was the right answer to what she had just done. It was the wrong answer to Lira, who had spent two years on the Ardenmere circuit being chased by large men with free feet and had never once been caught by one. She went past him on the left without a touch, so close her sleeve brushed his, and came back round behind him and touched him between the shoulder blades on the way through.

"Wind, two to one."

On the fourth he anchored again, because there was nothing else he trusted, and she gave him the short step and went sideways, and he had seen that and turned his head to follow it, and she was not sideways. She had stopped. She stood in front of him, a pace off, perfectly still, while the anchor held him and his strike gathered and found nothing to go into, and when it let go she stepped in and touched him on the breastbone.

"Wind. Bout, three to one."

The Stone fourth-year stood a moment. Then he laughed, short and surprised, and put out his hand, and Lira shook it. Cael saw her say something to him he could not hear. The fourth-year nodded slowly and said something back.

At the table the instructor dipped his pen and wrote the line in the practice register, and Cael went down afterward to read it. *Lira, Wind, Copper Two (transfer), over* — the fourth-year's name — *Stone, Copper Six. Three to one. Practice.* In the margin, in the same hand, a small mark like a tick.

"What did you say to him?" Cael asked her, on the steps.

"That his anchor's good." Lira was flexing her arm. "It is. He just tells you when he's going to use it. His shoulders go first." She glanced up at the tiers, where the notebooks were already being put away. "He'll fix it. He'll be harder in a month."

"You told him how to beat you."

"I told him how he lost." She went on up the steps. "If he can't work out the difference, he wasn't going to beat me anyway."

---

Inside ten days she had fought nine and won nine, and the practice register had every one of them written down in black ink, and the seeding had not moved by a line.

Cael checked. He had made himself check, every three days, because a thing he believed without checking was a thing he was going to be wrong about one day in public. The ladder book showed the Copper column in last season's order, with the new names at the foot in the order they had signed, and Lira's was the sixty-fifth of sixty-five. Nine practice wins sat in the register a hand's width to the left, and the two books did not speak to each other at all.

The porter, sweeping the tiers one evening, had stopped beside him at the rail and looked at the register over his shoulder for some time.

"Nine," said the porter, to the steps. "That's not usual."

It was the only comment anybody official made.

Not everybody was silent, though. The notebooks on the tiers had found her by the third bout. Cael counted them as he counted everything: four faces at first, then seven, then nine, the same people in the same places on the second and third tiers, writing when Lira walked out to her chalk and writing harder when she walked back. Some were Copper practitioners studying a name that would one day be in front of them. Two, he was nearly sure, were Iron, and had no reason on the ladder to care about a Copper transfer at all, except that they could see her as clearly as he could.

Lira had seen them too. She said nothing about them. But from the fourth bout on she never opened the same way twice. She came straight, and then she came wide, and then she came so slowly that her opponent went out to meet her and found her already somewhere else. In the sixth bout she did not move off her chalk for the whole of the first exchange and let a Current second-year come all the way to her, which Cael had never in three years seen her do, and won it on the counter. The notebooks wrote it all down. By the ninth they had nine bouts of Lira and, as far as Cael could tell, not one thing they could use, because she had given them nine different fighters.

"You're spending a lot," he said once, on the steps. "Showing them nine ways."

"I've got more than nine." She did not slow down. "And they'll only have seen each one once."

Lira read the books every evening. She had stopped saying anything about them after the first week, which Cael thought was worse, and she would stand at the board on the rim with her arms folded and her face set, and then walk away. On the thirty-fourth evening she did not walk away. She stood there long enough for him to come up beside her.

"Three a year," she said.

He waited.

"That's what the woman at intake said, more or less. Three flags sent on and three answered since the spring before last. So that's how fast it goes." She did not look at him. "Three a year. I can count. I'm in a queue that moves three a year, and I don't know where in it I'm standing."

"Three from her desk," said Cael. "In two years. That's one clerk's share. That's not the throughput of the queue."

"And how many does the whole queue move?"

"I don't know."

"That's not a comfort, it's a correction."

"I didn't say it to comfort you."

"I know you didn't." She breathed out, long and slow, through her nose. "Go on making them. Corrections I can use. I'd only like it known that I can tell the two apart."

Her eyes stayed on the sixty-fifth line.

"The bracket's not the trouble," she said at last. "I'll win the bracket. That's no boast. Nine bouts in, I've seen what the column's got, and there's nothing in it I can't answer." She unfolded her arms and laid one finger on the board, on her own name. "What I lie awake over is that it won't count. Win the whole column and you're still in the column. The only way out is an Arbiter writing a new tier on my paper. My paper tells the ladder where to put me. The ladder never writes a word back to my paper."

Cael thought about it. He thought about the short board on the end, the one headed Silver, where the school had run out of people to divide and had put everybody left into one column and given it the name of the top.

"It goes one way," he said. "Certificate to bracket. Nothing comes back up."

"Nothing comes back up." Lira took her finger off the board. "So it's a machine that takes things in and never gives anything out. What's the point of a thing like that?"

No answer came to him. That night the question went into the notebook on a clean page of its own, with two lines ruled under it, and he left the rest of the page empty, because he did not want to fill it with something clever that was not true.

---

He was on the rim for every practice afternoon by then, which was how he saw Fiske fight, and how he was at the board four days later when Fiske met Lira.

Fiske had been the name at the top of the Copper column for two years, and the name on the Copper Crown's newest brass for the same two. Cael had placed her before he ever saw her: Force Path, Copper Rank Eight, twenty years old, a fifth-year who had come up through Halcenvane's own halls. He had expected somebody large. She was not large. She was a little taller than Lira and a good deal more solid, with heavy shoulders and short brown hair pinned flat, and she came onto the floor the way a carpenter comes into a room where she is going to hang a door, looking at the frame first.

She took eleven minutes over a Copper Rank Four, a Force practitioner like herself, and Cael filled two pages and could have filled four.

The Rank Four came out hard, as Cael had seen half the Copper board come out against her in the register's history, gathering his force early and throwing it at her to have it done before she settled. For the first minute she gave ground. She was not graceful about it. She stepped back badly twice, once nearly off her chalk, and a murmur went along the rim; Cael saw the Rank Four's shoulders lift, a man who has just begun to believe something. Then, at about the minute and a half, Fiske stopped giving ground. She did not seem to do anything in particular. She set herself a little off his line, and when he gathered and came, the force he met was not in front of him at all but beside his leading foot, a short flat push into the floor where his foot was about to land. He landed on it. It was like stepping onto a stair that had been moved. He stumbled half a pace, and she was already there with her hand on his collarbone.

She did it twice more in the next nine minutes. Each time she let him come, and each time the force she spent went not into him but into the place he was about to be, and each time he found himself off balance with no idea why and her hand already resting on him. By the end he had stopped gathering at all. He was simply moving and watching his own feet, like a man crossing ice.

*Fiske. Force Path. I've charted Force on five floors and none of them used it like this. Everybody else builds force and delivers it: gather, commit, strike. Fiske doesn't strike with it. She puts it somewhere. Three uses of the Path in eleven minutes, the fewest I've charted on these rings, and every one of them went into the floor in front of his next step instead of into him. She's not stronger than the people she beats. She's earlier. She sees where they're going and closes the door before they get there.*

*Price: she has to see first. For the first minute and a half she's collecting, and she opens badly, twice today, because she hadn't finished reading him. Somebody she's never seen could take her early.*

*After that, in this bracket, I don't think anybody can. And everybody in this bracket knows it, which is why they all open against her like people who've already lost.*

*Two years of defending against everything the Copper column could send. She's fought every answer it has. There aren't any left.*

When it was over she climbed out of the sunk floor by the north steps with a towel round her neck, and found Lira at the boards, and stopped.

There were perhaps thirty people on the rim. Every one of them found something else to look at, very carefully, and every one of them went on looking.

"You're the transfer with the flag," said Fiske.

"And you've held it two years."

That was the whole of it.

But Fiske did not move on at once. She stood with the towel on her shoulder and looked at Lira for perhaps two breaths longer than the words needed. Cael was running the whole gaze on her, every layer, and he caught most of what went across her face, and he was most of the afternoon finding a name for it. It was the face of somebody who has been carrying a private diagnosis for a long time, alone, and has just seen the same thing written on a stranger.

Fiske went off to the water butt. Lira turned back to her own line. All round the rim, thirty people discovered their own business again with great suddenness.

Lira did not look after her. But on the walk back to the quadrangle she asked Cael, without turning her head, how long Fiske had spent collecting.

"About a minute and a half," said Cael. "Both times she opened badly, it was inside the first minute and a half."

Lira nodded, and said nothing else. Cael wrote it down that night on two pages, Fiske's and Lira's, with the same date, and drew a small line from one to the other in the margin. He did not know yet what the line meant, and he was not going to guess.

---

For a week Lira let it lie. When it came out at last it came out sideways, with no warning at all, in the wash-house behind the second quadrangle, over a quarrel about laundry.

The wash-house was a low stone room with a copper boiler in one corner and a stone trough along the wall and a drying rack overhead hung with everybody's shirts. Brom had put his new wraps into the copper with Karis's two good shirts, unaware that the wraps were dyed, and Karis's shirts had come out the colour of weak tea. Karis was holding one up to the window and explaining to Brom, very calmly, exactly how many days she had owned it. Brom was apologising in a way that made it plain he thought the shirt looked better. Cael was sitting on the end of the trough with the observation notebook, enjoying all of it more than he would ever admit.

"The woman at the outfitter's never said they'd run," said Brom.

"Did you ask her?"

"No. You don't ask a woman whether her cloth runs. It's like asking a carter whether his horse kicks." Brom looked at the shirt with real sorrow. "She'd have been offended."

"So instead you offended my shirt." Karis turned it round to see the back, which was worse. "Both of them. I brought exactly two good shirts four days down the road from Greyvane, Brom, because I knew there'd be a library committee and I wanted to look like somebody who could be trusted with a key."

"You've got the key."

"I've got the key because I argued for it in a grey shirt. I don't know what they'd have done if I'd come in this." She held it out at arm's length. "They'd have thought I'd been sleeping in the stacks."

"You have been sleeping in the stacks," said Lira, from the rack, without turning round. "Twice. I came to fetch you."

All that week Lira had said almost nothing that was not about the register or the weather, and Cael had let her be, because she had been thinking, and Lira thinking was best left to finish.

"Fiske isn't Copper because she fights like a Copper," said Lira.

All three of them looked at her. She was standing at the rack with a wet stocking in each hand.

"Fiske," said Lira. "She's Copper because nobody's had her reassessed. Same as me. Same queue. Only she hasn't even got a flag in it, because Halcenvane never sent one up for her, and Halcenvane never sent one because—" She stopped. Cael watched her get there, in front of them, with the stockings still in her hands. "Because she's winning. Let her lose the crown and somebody might bother. While she's got it, she's the champion of the Copper column, and a Copper champion is worth a great deal to this hill. Only she stops being worth it the day she stops being Copper."

Karis lowered the shirt.

"That's a very hard thing to say about a school," she said carefully.

"It's not an accusation." Lira hung one stocking on the rack. "That's the worst of it. Nobody chose it. Nobody ever called a meeting. Sending her flag up would cost some clerk a day's work and win him nothing, and leaving it costs nobody a thing, and the crown stays in its case. So it stays. Two years now." She hung the other stocking beside the first and stood looking at them. "Nobody's wicked. Nobody has to be."

Cael heard himself speak before he had finished the thought, and finished it as he said it.

"That's what a system is," he said slowly. "Not people deciding. It's what happens when nobody's got a reason to do the work."

The wash-house was quiet except for the copper ticking as it cooled.

Brom, who had been folding a weak-tea shirt with great care to atone for it, set it down on the bench.

"So nobody's going to send hers," he said. "And nobody's going to hurry yours."

"No," said Lira.

"Then what do you do?"

Lira took the next shirt off the pile and shook it out, hard, so that the water flew.

"Give them a reason," she said.

"That's going to be hard," said Karis. "You can't make an academy want a thing it doesn't need."

"No." Lira hung the shirt. "But I can make it very expensive not to look."

Karis studied her. Then she held up the ruined shirt again, the colour of weak tea, and studied it against the window with her head on one side.

"I'm keeping this," she said. "I'm going to wear it the day you take the crown, so that Brom has to look at it all afternoon."

"It's a nice colour," said Brom, humbly.

"It is not a nice colour. It's an accident." Karis folded it over her arm. "That's not the same as nice. It only looks the same when you're the one who did it."

Then she looked at Cael, and Cael looked at the notebook on his knee, at the question he had underlined twice on the thirty-fourth evening, with the empty space under it. He did not write the answer into the space, because it was not an answer yet. He wrote it on the next page instead, and dated it, and put Lira's name beside it, and then, because it had been his sentence and he wanted it honestly filed, his own.

# Chapter 10 — Present Everywhere

His own track arrived on a half-sheet, like everything else at Halcenvane, and it took him most of a week to see that the half-sheet was describing a hole.

*Assay-provision enrollee. Floor time: supervised, evaluation-linked, east and north halls, four sessions weekly at hours set by this office. Each session to be signed for by the supervising faculty member named on the office's rota. Lectures: open. Public proceedings of the academy: open.*

It was generous. He understood that at once and was grateful for it. He could stand on any floor the office sent him to, under any instructor it named, and run whatever the record allowed; he could sit at the back of any lecture on the bluff, on any Path; he could be present at anything Halcenvane did in public, and Halcenvane did nearly everything in public. At Greyvane he had begged for half of this and been given a quarter. Here it came folded once, in a clerk's hand, without his asking.

What the half-sheet did not say took him longer to read.

He found it on the twenty-eighth day, at the registrar's table on the rim of the Crown yard, where he had gone to read Lira's line in the practice register and had stayed to read everything else. The ladder book lay open beside the register. He read the Copper column through, every name, two hundred and nine of them in last season's order and the new ones at the foot. Then he turned the page and read the Iron column, a hundred and thirty-eight, and then the short Silver column on the last page, and then he turned back to the beginning and stood with his hand flat on the book.

The clerk with ink on his thumbs looked up from the practice register and saw him standing there.

"Signing?" he said hopefully. It was the slack of the afternoon, and he had clearly had nobody to talk to for an hour. "There's still a day in it for Iron. Copper's closed."

"I can't."

"Everybody can." The clerk reached for the sheet. "Path, tier, rank, and I'll find you a—" He stopped, because he had looked properly at the face in front of him, and Cael watched him place it. The clerk's eyes went to the book, and back, and to the book again, as if a column might have grown there while he was not looking. "Oh," he said. "The provision."

"The provision."

"There isn't a—" The clerk turned the ladder book's pages, quite unnecessarily, from Copper to Iron to the short Silver column and back. "No. There isn't, is there." He looked genuinely upset, the way a careful man is upset by a form with no box for the answer. "I'm sorry. I could write you in the margin."

"Thank you," said Cael, and meant it. "I don't think it'd count."

"No," the clerk agreed sadly. "The margin never does."

He had known his name would not be there. A ladder is fed by certificates, and no certificate anywhere carried a tier against his name for it to be fed. He had known that since the porter wiped the boards. Knowing it and standing over four hundred and twelve names in a book with his hand on the page were different things.

No line. No bracket, so no seed. No session pairings, no challenge windows, no slow climb of four rungs at a time. No crown, at any level of performance, in any year, under any circumstances at all. He could go down into the sunk floor tomorrow and put every practitioner on the bluff on the boards, one after another, with eleven hundred people watching, and not one stroke of chalk on any of the four walls would move. There was no place for it to move to.

*Present everywhere. Eligible nowhere.*

He wrote that on the steps, and then sat looking at it.

At Greyvane he had made a discipline out of this, over a year, and called it a vantage point. If you could not compete, you could watch, and nobody measured the one in the corner, and the corner saw everything. He had believed it. He had built a good part of himself on it. Now he would learn, on this bluff, how much of that belief had been real and how much had only been comfort.

*Honest answer, twenty-eighth day: mostly.*

*The vantage is real. It's better here than anywhere I've ever stood. Twenty-two Paths are taught on these three halls; I'd charted eleven before I came, which leaves eleven I've never seen up close. Lattice. Ash. Mire. Eight more I've had only from a gallery. Any one of them would have been a year's work at the Ironyard.*

*Also true, and I'd rather it was on paper than not: I stood at the ladder book today for a long time reading four hundred and twelve names, and mine wasn't one of them, and it never will be, and I minded.*

*It isn't the crown. I could not care less about the crown. It's the line I want: one row on a wall, in somebody else's chalk, saying what I did between the first bell of a season and the last, where anybody walking past could read it and argue with it.*

*I've wanted that since Denvash. It has never once been on offer. I should stop being surprised.*

*Filed. Working on it.*

---

After three weeks of sessions on the north hall's stone, the first that the office put on the east hall's timber was against Lira, and the supervisor that week was the Ash Path instructor from the lecture range.

The office permitted it because the two of them lived on the same residence roster, and because a supervised session needed somebody on the floor, and Lira had put her name down for the slot before anybody else could think of it. The Ash instructor was a thin, stooped woman of perhaps sixty, with ash-grey hair cut short and a lecturer's habit of holding a book against her chest like a shield. She signed the sheet, sat down on the bench below the gallery, opened the book, and thereafter interfered with nothing.

They used the Ardenmere terms. They had not needed to agree them; they had been the terms since the weeks after his ribs knit on the Ironyard boards. Lira fought him properly, full pace, and the instant anything showed that was not a plain body moving, she called it. *Stop.* Then he had a count of thirty, which she kept out loud, to get it down on the slate he kept at the edge of the floor before the edge of it went soft in his memory.

The slate had been Lira's idea, in the beginning, on the Ironyard boards. Paper tore and ink smeared, and a page could not be propped against a post at the edge of a floor and read from three paces while his breath was still short. A slate could. He had carried one in his pack ever since, through Ardenmere and Greyvane and down four days of road, wrapped in an old shirt so it would not crack, and he propped it now against the leg of the bench at the east hall's edge, a pace from the Ash instructor's feet. She glanced at it, and at the stub of chalk lying beside it, and went back to her book.

"You call it the instant anything shows," he said to Lira. "Not after. Not when it's finished."

"I know what I call." Lira rolled her shoulders. "I invented it. You only wrote it down."

"Ready?"

"Ready."

She came at him the way she had come at the Stone fourth-year, fast and low and straight, and he read her a pace and a half out on the skin of his forearms and slipped her on foot, without the Wind. She came again. He slipped again. On the third she put down the short step he had watched her use on the yard, the half-step going nowhere, and he did not wait to see where it went; he held half a breath and let the framework take his feet.

The first burst carried him left and back.

"Stop," said Lira.

He went to the slate. She counted.

He had expected the landing to be what it always was. A thousand times on stone he had paid it: the half-breath locked shut as each burst ended, and the jolt driving up from his heels into the leading hip, and he had braced for that without thinking, the way a man braces for a step he knows is there. The landing beat was the same length. It was always the same length; he had never once in three years been able to shorten it by a hair. But it did not come home the same way. It came down through his heels into the oak, and the oak took it, gave under it by what felt like the width of a finger, and gave it back, so that what reached his hip was not a blow but a kind of push, spread out and softened, like a wave arriving on a long flat beach instead of a wall.

*Burst one, east hall timber,* he wrote, while she counted. *Landing beat: same length. Consequence: softer. Hip: a push, not a hit.*

"Twenty-eight," said Lira. "Twenty-nine. Thirty."

They went again. He spent the second burst in the sixth minute, slipping her short step to the right, and the third in the eleventh, when she had stopped bothering with short steps and was simply faster than him on foot. Each time she called it; each time he wrote. The landings came down the same way, into the floor and back up softened. By the third his hip had begun to say something low and warm on the leading side, the way it did at the end of an evening on stone after two bursts, not three.

He stood at the slate and looked at what he had written.

"Fourth?" said Lira.

"Fourth." He put the chalk down. "If it's what I think, this is the one that tells me."

She came at him, and he let her, and when the fourth burst came it came clean, with no shortfall at the end of it, and the landing went into the boards and up into him spread and wide.

Lira did not call stop that time. She stood a pace away with her hands on her hips, watching his face, and waited until he looked up.

"Well?" she said.

"I don't know yet," said Cael. "I'll know in the morning."

On the bench below the gallery the Ash instructor had put her book face down on her knee somewhere around the fourth burst. She was not reading it. She was watching him, quite openly, with an expression he could not read at all. When he glanced at her she lifted the book again and turned a page, and went on not reading.

---

He knew in the morning.

The line came, as it always came after a fourth burst, from the crest of the hip down the inside of the thigh to the knee. But it came faint. It was the line he would have had on stone after an evening of three bursts, a thin warm thread instead of hot wire, and it was gone by the noon bell. He did not trust it. One morning was one morning. He waited for his next two sessions on the timber, under two more supervisors he had never met, and spent four bursts in each, and woke both mornings to the same thin thread, gone by noon.

Then he sat down with the binder, opened it at the page where three years ago he had first written down the Wind's price, and began to measure honestly.

*Wind-adjacent, sprung timber, three sessions.*

*Going in: no change. The half-breath I hold at the start is the framework's own toll. Stone, oak, cracked yard, I pay it the same.*

*Landing beat: unchanged in length. Still the same locked half-breath. Never shortened.*

*What the landing costs afterward: changed. Four bursts on these boards leave my hip about where three used to leave it on Greyvane's stone. Call it a third off, session for session.*

*So on a floor like this one my working ceiling is four free, not three, and the fifth is the one that bills the next morning.*

Wray had told him to measure again. He had nodded and meant it and carried her number in his head the whole four days of road anyway, because it was the only number he had. Now he had another, and it was better, and he ought to have been simply pleased.

He was not simply pleased. He sat at the desk by the window with the binder open and the river going by below and the thing underneath it came up slowly into the light, like a stone surfacing in a ploughed field.

*So the number I've been calling my ceiling was partly Wray's stone. Three years I've priced this fragment with a ruler that had a floor built into it, and I never once asked how much of the reading was me.*

*And if that's true of the Wind, I don't know that it's untrue of anything else in here.*

He turned back through the binder. Every price in it had been measured somewhere: the read on the Ironyard's planks, Compression on a stone floor in a cold hall, Ember on a training floor with Karis counting. Every one of them had been written down as though it were a fact about him, and every one had been taken in one place, under one set of conditions, and never taken again anywhere else, because he had never had anywhere else to take it.

*Every limit in this binder may be partly made of the room I measured it in. I don't know how much, or which. I'm going back through all of it, line by line, asking.*

The Compression page was the one that kept him longest. Reydan's fragment did one thing: it took a push that arrived on him and sent it down through his bones into whatever he stood on. He had measured its price on a cold stone floor in a hall where the stone took everything he gave it and gave nothing back, a forearm ache delayed a minute and lasting two hours, a hand that shook. But a floor that gave and returned was not a floor that only took. If he sent a push down through himself into oak on bearers, the oak would give under it. And then, if the east hall's boards did with a push what they had done with his landing, they would give some of it back.

He did not know where it would go when it came back. Into him, or out of him, or somewhere in between. He had never once stood on a floor that could answer.

*Compression, on sprung timber: never tried. Don't guess. Try it, under a supervisor, at the lowest weight there is, and write down what happens before deciding what it means.*

He put a ring round that and moved on to the next page, and then the next, and found every one of them asking him the same quiet question in a slightly different voice.

He started that night. Brom found him at the desk after the last bell with the lamp trimmed low and four of the oldest pages laid out side by side, and stood in the doorway reading upside down for a while.

"How long's that going to take?"

"I don't know," said Cael. "Longer than this month."

"Then go to bed," said Brom reasonably, "and take it longer tomorrow."

Cael did not go to bed at once. He sat a while longer with the four pages under the lamp after Brom's door had shut along the passage, and found that the thing he felt was not, after all, dismay. It was closer to the feeling at the long table in Prynn's archive, the night Karis had laid two books side by side and the gap between them had turned out to be a door. For three years he had treated every price in the binder as a wall. Some of them might still be walls. But some of them, it seemed, were only the floor he had happened to be standing on when he measured them, and floors could be changed.

---

He learned the rest from the supervisors, a lesson at a time, in whatever order the rota sent them.

The assessment office never sent the same instructor twice in a fortnight. He understood why after the third week; a supervisor who saw him often would form a view, and a view was a thing the office would rather nobody had. So he was handed round the faculty like a document being initialled, and in his first month on the floors he trained in front of eleven of them, none more than twice.

Every one of them watched him the same way. They watched him first with the mild, unloaded attention of a teacher minding a student who was not theirs, half on the floor and half on whatever they had brought to read. And every one of them, without a single exception, watched him differently in the second part of the session.

He began timing it in the second week. The change came between the tenth minute and the nineteenth, and it was tied to nothing he could isolate except one thing. The moment the Wind framework ran at anything faster than a walk, the supervisor's attention changed its state, as plainly as water changes when it starts to boil, and it never went back. They put their books down. They stopped glancing at the door. They watched his feet and the place where his feet would land, and they went on watching until the bell.

They did it in their own ways, which was how he learned to tell them apart. A Current Path lecturer, a cheerful round man who hummed, stopped humming at the eleventh minute and did not start again. A Shield instructor from the north hall, who had spent the first ten minutes writing letters on his knee, folded the letter in half at the thirteenth and sat on it, as though he were afraid it might watch instead of him. One elderly Stone master had come in with his eyes half shut, plainly expecting to doze, and at the nineteenth minute opened them both very wide and leaned forward on his stick and stayed leaning, quite motionless, until the bell. Cael wrote each one down: the instructor, the Path, the minute, the burst that came before it.

*Eleven supervisors. Eleven crossovers. No exceptions. Earliest at ten minutes, latest at nineteen. Mean, fourteen.*

*In a month, not one of the eleven has asked me a single thing.*

*At Greyvane people asked. All day, every day, badly, in corridors and at meals and through doors. Here, nobody asks. Eleven people who've each seen something they want to ask about have each, separately, not asked, and they've all not asked in exactly the same way.*

*That isn't eleven people being polite. That's organized. Either it's discipline of a kind I've never seen in a faculty, or somebody has told them not to.*

He sat with that for a long time.

He could find out. He could stop the twelfth supervisor at the door and ask her plainly whether she had been told not to ask, and see what her face did. He could ask the porter, who would know, and would think it a dull thing to be asked. He could ask Bracken outright. Any of those would give him the answer. Any of those would also tell the whole faculty, within a day, that the assay enrollee had noticed their silence and was poking at it, and a silence that knew it was being watched would become a different kind of silence, and he would have spent a good deal to learn something he could do nothing with.

*Not worth it. The answer would cost more than it's worth to me. Leave it standing, and watch it.*

It cost him something to write that. He had never in his life left a question standing when there was a way to an answer, and there were three ways to this one within a hundred paces of his room. But the habit of reaching for every answer was a habit, and Lira had told him on cracked stone at Greyvane what a habit was: a thing the other side could learn about you. He did not yet know who the other side was here. He would rather they did not learn it from him.

There was one of the eleven he added to the eight.

The Ash instructor had crossed over at the twelfth minute, like all the rest; he had the minute in the margin. But at the fourth burst, when her book had gone face down on her knee and she had been watching him with her whole face, she had seen him glance at her, and she had lifted the book again. It had not been embarrassment. He had watched a great many embarrassed people and knew the look. It had been a choice. She had wanted to stare and had decided, on purpose, in front of him, not to.

He did not know anybody else on the bluff who had made that choice where he could see them make it.

*Standing, fourth line. The Ash Path instructor, lecture range. Crossed at twelve minutes. Chose not to stare, and let me see her choose it. Dated, thirty-first day.*

---

On the thirty-second day Karis came into the common room carrying a single folded sheet in both hands, the way a person carries a bowl filled to the brim, and Cael, who had seen that walk perhaps twice in his life, put down his pen. It had taken her four meetings with Bracken and an afternoon in front of the library committee.

She laid the sheet on the table and stayed on her feet.

"Terms," she said. "Three of them. One." She laid a finger on the first. "Carrel eleven, a key, overnight retention. I had that already. Now it's in writing, which means nobody can decide in a bad week that I didn't. Two. The third and fourth floors of the law range without an escort. Bracken says that's faculty-only by custom, and he's granted it on the grounds that I have *demonstrated documentary competence to the institution's benefit*." She said it with great relish. "That's the kindest sentence anybody's ever written about me, and it's in a register."

"And three?" said Cael.

Her face changed. It became the face she wore for the four lines in the founding compilation, careful and narrow and very bright.

"Three is the one I fought for," said Karis. "Whatever I put together up there for your provision's defense belongs to the institution. Not to me, as a student. Bracken keeps it. The library never gets it."

Brom lifted his head off the arm of the settle. "Why does that matter?"

"Because of what happens at the end of a cycle." Karis finally sat. "The committee's charter has a clause about it. Clause six. Student work done in the library is gathered up at the close of each academic cycle and taken into the library's own holdings. Catalogued, shelved, opened to any reader on the bluff, or on the coast, or anybody who writes in with a fee." She tapped the third term. "Institutional work held by the registrar isn't. It stays in his cabinet, under his key, read by whoever he says may read it. It's a filing distinction."

"It's a filing distinction," said Brom, in the voice of a man trying to sound interested.

"Filing distinctions are the whole of it." Karis said it without heat, as a plain fact. "Everybody thinks I'm fussing, right up until the morning they need one. I want this one on paper now, while nobody needs it." She looked at Cael. "Everything I write on your provision will have your name in it, all through. I'd rather it sat in Bracken's cabinet than on an open shelf."

Cael read the third term again. He thought about a delegation's boxes coming back up a hill under wax, a volume short, and an old woman at a high desk saying nothing about the gap.

"Yes," he said. "So would I."

"The committee hated it." Karis looked delighted. "They have a charter, you know. Eleven clauses. I'd read it four times before I went in. When the chair said the request was irregular, I read her clause nine back to her, the right of written appeal to the chancellor, and asked whether she'd like me to start writing. She said that wouldn't be necessary. Bracken was sitting at the end of the table the whole time looking at the ceiling." She stopped, and some of the glee went out of her, and something quieter came in behind it. "And there's the other thing."

"Go on."

"The law range isn't a section of a library. It's a seam." She spread her hands flat on the table. "Greyvane's old books were luck. It was a road-house once, and whoever kept it left a shelf behind. This place went out and bought its law, deliberately, every decade since it was chartered, meaning to have the lot. The compilations from before the standardization. The directive itself, with its drafting papers. Forty years of consolidations. Every commentary anybody ever wrote about any of it." She took a breath. "I've spent six months on four lines of one clause. There are eleven bays on those two floors. And four of the eleven bays hold nothing but the decades my four lines come from."

"How long to read them?"

"Properly?" Karis said it the way other people talked about a feast. "Years. Years and years."

"You sound pleased."

"Cael." She looked at him with complete seriousness. "Every place I've ever been let in, I've read to the back wall. Do you understand? Every library I've ever been let into, I came to the end of. Dellenmoor. Greyvane. Prynn's, very nearly, and Prynn's was the best." She laid her palm on the document. "Today somebody gave me a place I can't come to the end of. Not in the time I've got. I don't think anybody's ever given me anything better."

Lira had come in at the door partway through and stood leaning on the frame with her arms folded, listening. She did not come further in.

"You argued them down," she said.

"I read them their own charter." Karis looked round at her. "It isn't the same thing. Arguing makes people dig in. Reading them their own clause makes them look for the door."

"I'll remember that." Lira said it lightly, but Cael saw her look at the document on the table for a moment longer than she needed to, at the three terms in Bracken's small upright hand, written down, initialled, unamendable by a bad week. Then she pushed off the doorframe. "Practice register's out. I'm eight and nil."

"Eight?" said Brom, sitting up.

"A Mire third-year. He tried to make the floor sticky." She was already going. "It didn't stick."

Karis watched her go, and then turned back to Cael, and her face had gone careful again.

"Three terms," she said. "One for me. Two for the work. And one for you, really, though it's got my name on it." She folded the document along its crease. "You'd better start keeping a list of everything I write on you, so that if Bracken's cabinet ever burns down we'll know what was in it."

"You're joking."

"Half," said Karis. "Write the list anyway."

# Chapter 11 — Silence on the Skin

The key was still on its nail.

He had walked past it most days for a month, on the path behind the assessment wing that ran down to the low hall, and every time he had looked at it and every time he had left it alone. It hung beside the door on a square iron nail at the height of his shoulder, a plain black key on a loop of leather gone shiny with handling, and the door it belonged to was locked from the outside. He had written both facts down on his first morning on the bluff, and underlined them, and had not been able to think of a single reason for either.

He had not touched it, either. That had been a rule of his own, made on the first morning without quite deciding to make it. A key left out on a nail was an invitation of some kind, and he did not take invitations he could not read. At Greyvane he had learned what it cost to walk through a door because it happened to be open, and he had learned it in front of a panel. So the key had hung there for five weeks, and he had walked past it, and looked at it, and the question had grown a little heavier every time, the way a stone in a boot grows heavier over a long day.

On the thirty-third morning he asked the porter, who was sitting on an upturned pail outside the wash-house cleaning the Copper Crown's case-key with a rag and some ash.

"The low hall," said the porter, to the key in his hands. "That's hers."

"Whose?"

"Lattice." The porter breathed on the key and rubbed. "There's one instructor teaches it on this hill, and nine students learn it, and that's her hall and nobody else's. Thirty-one years she's had it. She had the lock put on the outside the first winter." He held the key up to the light and squinted at it. "You want to go in, you take the key down off the nail. You knock. You wait. If she wants you, she opens it from the inside with her own key, and you hang hers back up. If she doesn't want you, she doesn't open it, and you hang hers back up anyway and go away."

"Why the key outside, then? If she opens it from in?"

The porter looked up at him for perhaps the first time since Cael had come to the bluff, briefly, with a kind of patient pity.

"So you have to stop," he said. "So you've got a key in your hand and a door in front of you and you can't just walk through. Lattice builds things in the air, lad. Lines. You can't see them, mostly, unless the light's right. You walk into a hall where somebody's halfway through building one, you find out where it was with your face." He went back to his rag. "Took a first-year's front tooth, the winter before the lock. She's never forgiven herself. Nor him, for running."

"Is she in my eight?" said Cael, before he could stop himself.

"Your what?"

"Nothing." He had not meant to say it aloud. The porter was the third line on the standing list, and had no idea he was on it, and would have found it very odd to be told. "Thank you."

The porter only grunted, and bent to the key again. He did not ask what the eight was. He had the incurious patience of a man who had carried every trunk on the bluff and had stopped wondering, years ago, what was in any of them.

Cael thought about it for the rest of the morning.

He had watched the Lattice pupils four times, always from too far off: twice in the north hall, where two of them sometimes worked at the far end under a high window, and twice from the Crown yard's tiers, during practice afternoons, when a Lattice third-year had fought on the Copper rings. Each time he had come away with a page of notes he did not trust, because from forty yards Lattice looked like nothing at all. The practitioners moved very little. Their opponents moved a great deal, and then stopped moving, and stood looking puzzled, or stepped carefully to one side as if there were something in the way, and Cael could never see what. He had a page headed *Lattice* in the observation notebook with eleven lines under it, and every one of the eleven was a guess, and he had marked them all as guesses.

He did not want a twelfth guess. He wanted to stand next to one.

So at the change of bell after noon, when the halls emptied and filled again, he went down the path behind the assessment wing, took the key off the nail, and knocked.

Nothing happened for long enough that he began to turn the key over in his fingers, wondering whether this was a refusal. He did not hang it back. The porter had not said how long to wait, and a rule that did not say how long usually meant longer than you thought.

Then the lock turned from the inside, and the door opened a hand's width, and an old woman looked out at him.

---

She was small and very upright, past sixty, in a grey wool dress with the sleeves pinned back above the elbow, and she had the stillest hands he had ever seen on anybody. They hung at her sides as if they had been set down there and told to wait. She looked at him, and at the key in his fingers, and at him again.

"Hang it up," she said.

He hung it on its nail. She opened the door the rest of the way.

The low hall was one room, low-ceilinged as its name, with its windows set very high along one wall so that the light came down in long slanting bars and lay across the floor in stripes. The floor was plain stone, swept bare. Two pupils were standing in the middle of it, a fifth-year girl with her hair in a tight knot and a younger boy, and both of them had stopped exactly where they were when the knock came, and had not moved since. Along the back wall a row of pegs held nine grey practice smocks, one for each student of the Path on the bluff, with a name stitched over each peg in blue thread. Below them the stone had been scored with fine straight lines, dozens of them, crossing and recrossing, like the marks a joiner leaves on a bench where he has measured the same cut a thousand times.

The boy looked at Cael with open curiosity and then, catching the instructor's eye, looked hastily at the floor.

"You're the provision," said the Lattice instructor.

"Yes, Magistra."

"I don't take the title." She did not explain. "What do you want?"

He had thought about how to answer that on the path down. He had thought about several answers that were true and short and did not say very much, and he had decided against all of them, because he did not think this woman would let him in on any of them.

"I read pressure," he said. "On the skin. A pace and a half out, I can feel where a weight is and which way it's going, a fraction before it gets there. It works on everything I've ever stood near." He kept his eyes on hers. "I've never stood near Lattice. I'd like to, while somebody builds, if you'll allow it. I want to know what I feel."

She looked at him for a long moment without any expression at all.

"Why?"

"Because if it doesn't work on Lattice, I'd rather learn that here," said Cael, "than on a floor where it matters."

Something moved very slightly at the corner of her mouth.

"Stand there," she said, and pointed with one still finger at a place on the stone a pace and a half from the fifth-year. "Do not move from it. Do not put out a hand. If you feel anything you want to tell me about, tell me afterward, not during." She turned to the girl. "Three points. Quarter speed. Count it so he can follow."

The girl glanced at Cael once, without curiosity, and lifted her hands.

She built slowly, as she had been told, and she counted under her breath while she did it, a low murmur like somebody reciting a lesson. "One, and set." Her right hand closed in the air a little above the height of her shoulder, as if she had taken hold of a thread hanging there. "Two, and set." Her left hand closed lower and farther out, almost level with Cael's forearm. "Draw." Between the two hands, for an instant, as she moved her head, he saw it: a line in the air, finer than a hair, catching one of the slanting bars of window light the way the strand of a web catches light in a hedge in the early morning, and then gone again when the light moved off it.

"Three, and set."

Her right foot slid forward a hand's width on the stone, and her right hand came down and closed at the height of her hip, and he knew without seeing it that the third line had joined the other two, because the air in the place in front of him had become, somehow, a place. He could not have said how he knew. There was nothing to see. But the space between her three closed hands, eighteen inches from his own forearm, was no longer only air, and he would have staked a great deal on its being something he could not walk through.

The read gave him nothing.

It did not give him a little. It was not thin, the way it went thin at the edge of its pace and a half, or uncertain, the way it went uncertain when two weights arrived at once and he had to choose between them. It gave him exactly what it gave him standing alone in an empty corridor at night. Quiet. The ordinary quiet of his own forearms with nothing near them.

A thing had been built eighteen inches from his skin that could have shut off two of his ways out, and the instrument he had trusted for three years above his own eyes had not stirred.

He stood very still on his place on the stone, as he had been told, and found that his mouth had gone dry. It was not fear of the frame. The frame was a lesson, built at quarter speed by a girl who did not care whether he lived or died, and it would go when she let it go. It was something colder and slower than fear, rising from underneath, the feeling of a man who has walked the same path in the dark every night for three years and has just been shown, by daylight, a well at the side of it that he never knew was there.

---

"Again," he said, when it was over. "Please. With my eyes shut."

The instructor considered him.

"Why shut?"

"Because I saw the line. Once, in the light." He kept his voice level. "I want to know whether the read is quiet on its own, or whether I'm only noticing it's quiet because my eyes are louder."

She nodded to the girl, and the girl let her frame go. It went without any sound or sign at all; he only knew it had gone because the place in front of him stopped being a place. Then she built it again, counting, and he stood with his eyes shut and his whole attention on the skin of his forearms and the back of his neck and the line of his jaw, where the read always came, and it did not come. One, and set. Two, and set. Draw. Three, and set. Nothing. He opened his eyes on a finished frame he still could not see and felt the hair stand up along his arms, not from anything the read had given him, but from the plain fact of its having given him nothing.

"May I touch it?"

"One finger," said the instructor. "Slowly. Where she tells you."

The girl pointed with her chin at a place between her two upper hands. Cael put out his right forefinger, very slowly, toward empty air.

It stopped.

There had been no warning. That was the thing. His finger was moving, and then it was not, and nothing had arrived on his skin before it stopped: no pressure building, no weight in the air, none of the early signs the read had always laid on him a fraction ahead of a strike. It was not like pushing against a wall, which pushed back. It was like reaching the end of a rule. His finger had simply been forbidden to go further, and was not going further, and that was all.

"You're listening for a push," said the Lattice instructor.

He looked round at her.

"Every other Path on this bluff is a push of some kind. Force is a push. Blade is a push sharpened. Stone is a push that refuses to move." Her hands still hung quite motionless at her sides. "A frame doesn't push. It forbids. There is nothing moving in it for you to feel. You may as well listen for the weight of a law."

He took his finger back. It tingled faintly, as if it had been somewhere it should not.

He stood a while longer looking at the place where he knew the frame was, and then, because the read was still quiet and would go on being quiet, he did the only other thing he could think of. He turned the read off the frame and onto the girl.

"Again," he said. "Once more. Please."

She built it again, and this time he felt it. Not the frame. *Her.* As she said *one, and set*, her weight went forward over her right foot by the width of a finger, and the read laid that on his forearm, small and clear. On *two*, it went a little back and left. On *three*, as her foot slid forward on the stone, it went forward again and down. Each time she set a point, her body paid for it with a small shift of balance, and her body was a weight like any other weight, and the read could feel a weight.

"I can't feel the frame," he said slowly. "But I can feel her setting it. Each point, she moves. Not much."

The girl's face went faintly pink, which was the first thing it had done since he came in.

"Yes," said the Lattice instructor. She sounded, if anything, mildly pleased with him, or perhaps with the girl. "Most of my pupils spend their first two years learning to set a point without moving anything else. Most of them never quite manage it." She glanced at the fifth-year. "She very nearly has."

"She'll have it out of me by spring," said the girl, unexpectedly, in a dry low voice, to nobody in particular.

"By spring," the instructor agreed. She turned back to Cael. "So you will be able to read her until spring, a little. You will be able to read the other eight a little longer than that. You will never read me." She said it without boasting, as a plain fact about the weather. "Hang the key on your way out."

He thanked her. She inclined her head about the width of a finger, which he gathered was a great deal, and turned back to her pupils before he had reached the door.

Outside, the noon light was very bright after the striped dimness of the low hall. He hung the key on its nail and stood with his hand still on the leather loop, looking at it. A month he had walked past it. A month it had been hanging there, at the height of his shoulder, offering him the plainest piece of information on the whole bluff, and he had left it alone because he could not read the invitation. The invitation had been perfectly simple. *Stop. Knock. Wait.* He had been too careful to see it.

Behind the door he heard the girl begin to count again, low and steady. *One, and set.* He went back up the path before he could hear the second.

---

He wrote it that night at the desk by the window, and he put the frightening part first, so that he could not soften it by getting there slowly.

*Low hall, thirty-third day. The read and Lattice: nothing. I don't mean faint. I mean the quiet I get alone on a stair at night, while a girl built, twice, a thing eighteen inches from my arm that could have pinned me where I stood.*

*Guess, unproven: the read only knows weight on the move. A frame is three held points and the shape between them. No weight travels in it. So there's nothing there for the read to find, and it finds nothing.*

*And it said nothing about finding nothing. That's what I want in big letters.*

He pressed harder than he needed to on the next part.

*When a tool breaks with a noise, you learn where its edges are and keep your hands off them. When it breaks without one, it isn't broken. It's lying, and it lies in the same calm voice it uses for the truth. I've leaned on this one in the dark, in crowds, with my back to doors, for three years. Today it went blind to an entire Path and stayed perfectly calm about it.*

*Nine people on this bluff build frames. There are eleven more Paths on this hill I've never been within reach of. Some of them it can't see. I won't know which until I'm beside them, and perhaps not even then, unless somebody tells me something's there.*

*From today: the surface read's silence is not information. Every quiet is an unknown. Every floor, every time.*

He sat back and looked at that last line for a long time.

Then he did something he had done perhaps four times since Denvash, each time for a thing he could not afford to be without. He took out the observation notebook, opened it to the Halcenvane section, and wrote the rule out again, word for word, on its own page. Whichever book he had in his hand from now on, the rule would be in it.

*Correction, to try again: read the builder, not the build. The frame gives nothing, but the person setting it is a weight. Each point costs them a small shift of balance. A pupil still pays it. A master doesn't. So this works against the young and the unfinished, and against nobody else, and it gets worse every year they train.*

*Price of the correction: reading her body tells me where she set each point, roughly, and when. It doesn't tell me what the points make. Three points could be a wall, or a corner, or a door with one side open, and from her weight alone I can't tell which. So even against a pupil I'm reading the hands of a man who's writing, not the letter. Better than nothing. Much less than I'm used to.*

*Two hidden things this month. The floor that was in my prices. The silence that was in my read. Both of them were in there all along, and I never looked, because I had no reason to think they were there.*

*Go on looking for the third.*

He did not sleep for some time after that.

He lay in the dark and went back, as he had not been able to stop himself going back, through every time in three years the read had been quiet and he had taken the quiet as clear. The back stair at the boarding house in Ardenmere, which he had climbed a hundred nights without a lamp because his forearms told him it was empty. The corridor outside the archive at Greyvane, the evening the Compact's officer had walked its length with his cases. The yard behind the residence wing, in the dark before the first bell, where he had turned and turned on frozen stone with his back to the gate. Every one of those silences he had trusted. Every one of them, as it happened, had been empty. But he had not known it. He had only felt nothing, and called the nothing knowledge, and gone on.

He thought, too, of the eleven supervisors and their eleven careful silences. There, as well, he had been given quiet and had nearly taken it for an answer. It was not one. A silence that had been arranged was not the same thing as a room with nobody in it, and he had been very close to forgetting the difference.

*Rule, second half: the same goes for people.*

He got up in the end, lit the lamp again, and wrote one more line under the rest, smaller, because he wanted it there in the morning when the fright had worn off and he would be tempted to think he had made too much of it.

*It was empty every time. That's luck, not knowledge. Don't confuse the two again.*

Five weeks to the day from the gate, the four of them were on the wall behind the second quadrangle at dusk, each busy with something of their own, and Cael had the binder open on the coping.

At the far end, where an old hitching post stood up out of the turf, Brom was turning nothing at all, as slowly as he knew how. Rooke had given him the post two days before, and the instruction with it, and the instruction was simple and cruel. Brom was to turn nothing until he was certain it was real. He was to stand at the post, and Karis or Lira or Cael would come at him with feeds, honest ones and empty ones mixed, and he was to let every empty one go by without so much as a twitch, and turn only the honest ones. It was the exact opposite of everything he had built. Tonight it was Lira feeding, from in front of the post, her hands flat, and every third feed she sold was empty, and Brom turned on two of every three of them anyway, and groaned at himself, and went back to the post.

"Worse," he said cheerfully, after one of them. "I'm definitely worse. Write that down."

"I'm writing it down," said Cael.

Cael had taken his turn at the feeds before supper and had found it harder than he expected. To sell Brom an empty strike he had to mean it nearly all the way, with his weight and his breath and his eyes, and then take it back; and Brom, who had known him three years, believed every one, because Cael had never once in three years lied to him on a floor. It had felt very strange to do it on purpose. Brom had not minded at all. *Again*, he had said, every time, beaming. *That one got me properly.*

Karis had four documents spread on the flat top of the wall, the ones she had brought out of carrel eleven for the evening light, with a smooth stone weighting each corner against the wind off the river. She was reading the third, very slowly, with her finger on the line. Every so often she said a single word aloud, to nobody, and wrote it in the margin.

Lira gave Brom one more empty feed, watched him fail to let it go, and then came and leaned beside Cael on the coping with her eyes on the water. Down at the ferry landing the two grey coats were standing under their lamp. She looked at them for a while.

"Ten past," she said. "They're late changing."

"Are they?"

"Ten minutes." Lira did not take her eyes off them. "First time in a month. Write that down too."

He did. Then, because it had been sitting on him all day, he told Brom about the low hall.

Brom came away from the post and listened with his wraps hanging loose from his hands. When Cael got to the part about the frame and the quiet, he frowned, and turned his right forearm over and looked at the inside of it, the way he looked at it after a fall.

"Mine's the same, then," he said slowly. "If yours came from me. Mine'd be blind to her as well."

"I don't know. I've never seen yours near a Lattice."

"Nor have I." Brom considered his forearm with deep suspicion, as if it had been keeping something from him. "Three years I've been telling you it's the best thing I own." He wound the wrap back up. "I'll ask Rooke. He'll know. He knows everything and tells you a quarter of it."

"Ask him what he does about it," said Cael.

"I'll ask him that too." Brom went back to the post. "He'll say, *watch*. He says that about everything."

There was no complaint in it. It sounded like a man quoting a cook whose bread had begun, against all his expectations, to rise.

Cael bent over the binder.

*Five weeks. Four of us, on four roads.*

*Brom: a man warned him he'd be made worse, and is making him worse, exactly as promised, and I've never seen Brom so glad of anything.*

*Lira: nine bouts, nine wins, in a register half the bluff reads with a pencil in hand, and still nothing in it can climb as high as her certificate.*

*Karis: a key, a seam of law with no back wall, and three terms on paper that keep her work in a cabinet and off the shelves.*

*Me: a category with a name, the best boards I've ever stood on, eleven Paths I've never been near, a read I've just caught lying, and no line anywhere.*

It had taken five weeks to be able to put the next part down. He put it down.

*Four of us, four doors, all real. A year ago none of us could have walked through any of them. Worth writing down on the days it doesn't feel like enough.*

He closed the binder. Behind him, at the post, Brom turned on another empty feed, groaned, and laughed at himself, and Lira went back to give him the next.

# Chapter 12 — Doctrine

Karis read the clause to him in carrel eleven with her finger under each word, the way Vell had once taught him to read a ledger, and on the fourth line she stopped.

"There," she said. "That's the part nobody tells you."

He leaned over her shoulder. It was Halcenvane's own copy of the founding clause, with the charter article that carried it set out beneath, and below that the assessment office's standing rule for applying it, in a dry modern hand. He had read all three before. He had read them on the first night, and again in Bracken's room, and again on the night the notice came. He had not read them the way Karis read things, which was as if each word had been put there by somebody who might be lying.

*The demonstration so made shall stand as the measure of record for the enrollee, and every subsequent demonstration shall be laid against it.*

"Laid against," said Karis. "Like a rule against a plank. You don't measure a plank by how long it is. You measure it by how far it is from the mark." She took her finger off the line. "The office doesn't ask whether you're good. Not once, anywhere in this. It asks whether you're the same as you were, and then whether you're more. That's all a semester evaluation is. The old line, and the new line, and the distance between them."

"So the first line is everything."

"The first line is the only one you get to draw yourself." She sat back in her chair. "Every one after that is drawn by the first."

He walked back to the second quadrangle in the dusk with that sentence going round in his head like a cart wheel, and that night he did not sleep, and the night after he slept very little.

It was not fear of the room. He had stood in worse rooms. He had stood in a hall in front of two hundred people with the Compact's warden at the other table, and read the schedule back to them from its first page to its last, and that had been worse than any room with instruments in it could be. This was something else. It was the plain arithmetic of the clause, and the arithmetic would not let him alone.

Whatever he showed on the day would be the line. Every half-year after it, for as long as he was on the bluff, somebody would lay a new line beside it and measure the gap. And he could choose, now, before the day came, where the first line went. Nobody else on the bluff had ever been given that choice about themselves. Every practitioner on every board had been set down by an Arbiter at fourteen and handed a number they had no say in. He was being handed a pen.

He kept thinking of the file, the actual file, a folder in Gault's cabinet with a sheet in it. He could see it quite clearly in the dark: a heavy man's handwriting, a date, a few lines. He could see it being taken out in five years by somebody he had never met, an inspector with a grudge or only with a schedule, and laid on a table, and read. Bracken had told him on the first day that somebody would come up the road one day to read his record with a grudge. Withrow had written *sooner* on her sheet. Whatever went into that folder on the day would be read by them. It would be read by every evaluator who came after Gault. It would be read, if things went the way things had a habit of going for him, in a hearing room, aloud.

*Choose with that in mind,* Bracken had said, holding out the tag on its cord. He had nodded, at the time, as if he had understood. He had not understood. He was beginning to.

On the third night he lay looking at the ceiling and worked out, for the first time with complete clarity, the two ways it could be made to cheat.

He could set the line low. He could walk into Gault's room and show less than he had, on purpose, carefully, enough to satisfy the provision and no more. Then each semester he could show a little more of what he had always had, and it would be written down as growth, and nobody would ever know the difference, because the instruments only measured the gap. It would buy him years of easy evaluations. It would buy him room to hide in.

Or he could set it high. He could show everything. Then there would be no room at all, and every semester would have to find real growth on demand, in a body that was fifteen and could not be hurried, and the first time it failed to grow on schedule the gap would read as a fall.

He lay there between the two, and neither of them was right, and he could not see where the right one was.

---

He put it to the others on the fourth evening, in the common room, because he had been carrying it alone long enough to know he was carrying it badly.

Brom was on the floor with his back against the wall and the ice on his shoulder again; Ephram had found something new that afternoon and Brom was in high spirits about it. Lira was on the window seat with her knees drawn up. Karis had copied the clause onto a clean sheet and was already arguing with it in pencil.

He laid out the low line first, because it was the one he was ashamed of being tempted by, and he wanted it on the table where they could all look at it.

"It's very comfortable," he said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's comfortable for years, and then it isn't, and when it stops being comfortable it stops all at once." He held up a finger. "It costs three ways. First, the line's a document. Gault writes it, it goes in the file, and one day an inspector reads it. Bracken's told me so twice. Withrow told me with her own pen, on her own sheet: *sooner*. If an inspector opens the file and finds a weak line, that's evidence the provision let in a weak practitioner. That's an argument against the provision. And the provision is the only floor I've got."

"Second," said Karis, not looking up.

"Second, it runs out." He put up another finger. "I've only got so much held back. If I spend it a little each semester as growth, I'll have nothing left on the day I really need to grow and can't. And the requirement doesn't stop. Every semester. Without exception. It says so on the record I signed."

"Third," said Brom, from the floor.

Cael looked at him.

"You'd have to stand there while he wrote it down," said Brom. He said it quite mildly, shifting the ice. "In his own room. Him being straight with you, and you knowing what you'd left out, and him thanking you for it." He looked up. "I couldn't do that. Not to somebody being straight with me. I'd go red, like you did over the registrar."

There was a short silence.

"And it's a lie," said Cael quietly. "Written down. In the one file I went into a hearing room to get made honest."

He laid out the high line after that, and it was shorter, because everybody in the room already knew why it was impossible. He could not show everything. Showing everything meant Pressure, which nobody had ever seen him use, and Compression, which nobody had ever seen at all, and the Ember on a floor in front of a faculty panel, and every one of them was a question with a door behind it that he could not open, and the door behind all the doors was the thing only the four of them knew.

"So it's low, or it's honest," he said. "And honest isn't possible, and low is a lie."

"It isn't," said Lira.

They all looked round. She had not moved from the window seat.

"Low or honest, you keep saying. Those aren't the two." She said it calmly, as if pointing out a step he had missed on a stair. "Three years you've kept things in your pocket on every floor we've stood on. Choosing what goes out and what stays in. I was there for all of it. I never once thought of it as lying, and neither did you."

"That was leaving things unsaid. This is putting a number on myself and signing it."

"It's picking from what's already been said about you." She swung her feet down off the seat. "Out loud, on paper, in public, by people who weren't you. Six silver marks at the stationer's in Ostrand. Tell me what's in it."

He could have recited it. Fourteen of Greyvane's monthly sittings had been read into the hearing as exhibits, one after another, and every one of them, under three assessors' names, had said the same two things about him: that he could get out of the way with the Wind, and that he could feel a weight coming before it came. A court had looked at those two and filed them where anybody could find them. Nothing else about how he was made had ever been put where the world could read it, except the Ember, and that only in a single late exhibit, half a page, so thin that showing it on Gault's floor would not confirm anything. It would announce something. Pressure and Compression were not in it at all.

"Two," he said.

"Then show them those two." Lira leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "All of them. Not most. Not the safe amount. Every bit you've got, in those two and nowhere else." She held his eyes. "A low line's a lie about you. This is the plain truth about the boy in that transcript. And when you're better in a semester's time, which you will be, because you're fifteen and it'll happen whether you want it or not, you'll be better in exactly the places they're already watching."

For a while Cael said nothing at all.

"That's it," he said.

"I know."

"You've known for days."

"Since Tuesday." Lira stood up and stretched until her shoulders cracked. "If I'd said it on Tuesday you'd have spent four days finding what was wrong with it, and then argued it out with yourself in front of Gault, halfway through a trial." She paused at the door. "And don't go writing this down as me managing you. That's how you work. I've had three years to learn it."

In the binder that night he filed it under method. Lira, he thought, would accept that.

*Baseline doctrine. The two things the record already holds, shown at the very top of what I can do with them, nothing kept back inside them. Everything the record doesn't hold stays held.*

*What I get: a way through I could defend in a hearing room. I know what those rooms are like now. One I can defend beats one that's wide.*

He had grown up at Hesk's bench, watching an instrument-maker scribe a line on brass and check it three times before he cut, and he knew what it cost to set a true line over fifty feet, in any material at all. Along the north wall stood the apparatus, and it was making its small noises.

He had spent two weeks finding out what he could about it, and could now name nine of the eleven. A drop-frame, a weight on a rail released by a trigger, with four weights racked beneath it; its pawl clicked once, softly, as somebody at the end of the room checked it. A shutter-board of nine louvres with a drum behind it that chose their order, and the drum's ratchet made a sound like a fingernail drawn slowly down a comb. And at the very end, a thing like a tall brass funnel over a cup, which dripped into the cup with a small bright sound every few seconds, and which he could not name at all.

Magister Gault was already in the middle chair. He was a heavy man in his fifties, Silver-tier, with a stillness about him that Cael read at once as trained and not natural, the stillness of somebody who had decided long ago that moving was a thing to be done on purpose. He did not stand. On his right sat the Ash Path instructor from the lecture range, the fourth name on Cael's standing list, with her book against her chest. On his left sat the Mire instructor who had resented his last session, and who did not look as though she had grown to like the hour since. The clerk, a young man with a ledger and three pens laid in a row, had already written something.

"Enrollee." Gault did not offer a hand. "Sit. I will tell you how the morning goes, once. You will have it every half-year from now on, and I would rather not repeat myself."

Cael sat in the single chair that faced the table.

"There is no opponent here." Gault put his broad palm down on the forms. "You will not be scored against anybody. You will do six things for me, and each of them is built to look at one part of you and nothing else. In the gaps I'll put questions to you, and they go in the book with the rest. My clerk writes down all of it: what I ask, what you say, and how long you stay silent before you say it."

He paused, and Cael understood that the next part was the part Gault cared about.

"Three rules of this room. The first. When you reach something you cannot do, tell me. The book will say you couldn't, and that is all it will say; it will not be held against you, it will simply be so. If I have to work out for myself that you've refused, it tells me less than if you had said so." His finger moved a little on the forms. "The second. I want sameness, not your best. Give me six runs that match each other at three-quarters, and I'll value them over one wonder and five that quarrel with it. The third." He looked straight at Cael for the first time. "This morning is your line. You will be laid against it every half-year you are here. So don't do it for me. Do it for yourself five months from now, standing on this oak, who will have to beat whatever you leave on it today."

Of all the things Cael had readied himself for, he had not thought of this one: that the man holding the measure would name, in his first minute and as a matter of routine, the very thing about the measure that had kept Cael awake for three nights.

"Understood, Magister."

"Then we begin."

# Chapter 13 — Baseline

"Fourth brass from the near wall," said Gault. "Stand on the crossing. When the board opens, be somewhere else."

Cael waited for more. There was no more.

He walked out onto the oak and found the crossing, two strips of brass meeting under his boots in a small bright cross, and while he settled his feet on it he tried to find the flaw in what he had just been told, and could not. It gave no distance. It gave no direction. It did not say *Wind* or *framework* or *evade*; it did not say what kind of practitioner was expected to answer it, or how. A square, a signal he had no say in, and the plain request that he not be on the square afterward. Whatever he did to get off it would be his and nobody else's, offered freely, and the room would only note down where he finished and how long it took him. He thought he had never been asked a cleaner question on any floor in his life.

Ten paces off, the shutter-board stood with its nine louvres closed. Somewhere behind them its drum went round with that dry fingernail sound, picking. He could not see which way it would pick. Nobody could. That was the use of it.

He let his shoulders drop and his breath settle, and kept his eyes on all nine louvres at once.

The fourth one clacked open.

He did not decide anything. He had stopped deciding the first part years ago. His knees softened and his hips sank and the oak under him gave its small breath, and then came the toll at the front, the half-breath held and paid, during which he was all air and no lungs. Then he was not where he had been. He was a long stride to the left of it, and the floor was coming up under his heels.

He had braced for stone without meaning to. What met him was give: the boards taking him a finger's width down and handing him gently back, so that the old blow up the leading side arrived as a long low push instead. Then the fixed beat at the back, the moment when he was only something that had landed, unable yet to turn or reach or do anything but finish arriving. Three years of trying had not taken a hair off it.

"Three brass left of the crossing," said the clerk, not looking up. "One forward. Interval—" and a number off the pendulum.

Gault wrote. "Again. The same."

Back to the crossing. The drum went round.

He did it six times, and the clerk read six crossings and six intervals, and Gault's pen moved six times. The first three cost him nothing he could feel. After the fourth there was something warm and low on the leading side, like a coal under ash. After the fifth the warmth had drawn itself into the thread he knew from the east hall, thin and bright, running from the crest of his hip down the inside of his leg to the knee: the bill being written out.

The sixth burst came out a little wide of where he had sent it. The beat at the back of it lasted longer than it ought to have, perhaps by a tenth, and he felt the extra tenth from the inside, plainly, while the pendulum was still swinging toward it. He stood where he had landed and made himself wait for the clerk.

"Three and a half left. One forward." The clerk frowned at the case and read it again. "Interval long."

Gault did not write anything for a moment. He sat with the six lines of figures in front of him and his heavy hands quite still on either side of them.

"Five of these sit inside one brass of each other," he said at last. "The sixth is half a brass out, and slow." He raised his eyes. "You have a figure for that, I think."

*Tell me when you can't.* Cael heard it again as clearly as if it had just been said.

"Four, Magister, on a floor that gives like this one. Four I can do without paying for. The fifth I pay for in the morning. I've never done six in one session except in a bout." He kept his feet where they were. "So the sixth came out the way a sixth comes out."

"You had that figure before you stepped onto the brass."

"Yes."

"You kept it to yourself."

"The rule was to speak up if a thing was beyond me." Cael looked at him steadily. "It wasn't. It only cost."

Something went across Gault's face and was gone, like a lamp carried past a window in a dark house.

Gault turned his head an inch toward the clerk, whose pen was already lifted. "Put this down. Enrollee gives a working ceiling of four on sprung timber without being asked, and says what is wrong with the sixth before the instrument does." He turned back to Cael. "Of everything said on this oak since the brass went into it, that is the single most useful sentence. A man's best afternoon tells me very little. His honest ceiling tells me where to stand. Go on."

The Mire instructor had charge of the drop-frame. She cranked the rail to a setting nobody named aloud, racked the second of the four weights, and checked the pawl with her thumb, all without hurry, and with the faint air of a woman doing a job she suspected had been done badly by somebody else the last time.

"Four places it can come down," said Gault. "High or low. Near or far. Stand in the chalk with your back to the rail, and tell us which, when it falls."

So there would be nothing to watch. No feet, no shoulders, no breath, no face. Only a weight on a rail behind him and the question of where it was. Cael stood in the chalk square and looked at the blank wall of the room in front of him, and felt a small, plain gladness at how well the thing had been made. It was the read and nothing else.

He had never known how to describe the read to anybody who did not have it. The Wind was a thing he did. The read was more like a thing that happened to the weather on his skin. A weight on the move pressed on the air, and the air pressed on him, a little before the weight itself could, in a thin band along the outsides of his arms and up behind his ears. It told him how heavy and which way. It had never told him why, or what anybody hoped would happen next. It was cheap and silent and gave nothing away to anybody watching, and it would not reach one inch past a pace and a half however he strained it. He had wasted a month, once, trying to make it reach. He had spent every month since letting it be what it was.

And three weeks ago, in a room with a key outside its door, it had told him a lie by saying nothing.

He let himself remember that for exactly one breath. Then he set it down. A frame was not a weight. This was a weight, and a weight was the one thing the read had never failed to find.

"Ready," said the Mire instructor.

The pawl let go.

It came to him like the first heavy drops on a roof he was standing under: all together, from up and behind his left shoulder, pressing in along the back of that arm.

"High," said Cael. "Far side."

"High, far," the clerk agreed.

Twelve times. He called each one as it came, and in the gaps the room went on making its noises round him: the pendulum, the drip at the end of the row into its brass cup, the clerk's nib. The ninth was wrong before it had finished arriving. It did not come all together. It came the way rain comes when the wind keeps changing its mind, a light early touch and then, a beat behind, the real weight, and he had two answers and had to pick one.

"Low. Near." He turned his head a fraction. "And that's wrong. I'm fairly sure."

"Low, far," said the clerk. "Wrong."

"You are quite right to say you are wrong," said Gault. He said it with no lightness whatever, the way he might have read a weight off a dial. "What told you?"

"It didn't arrive in one piece." Cael hesitated, decided the rest belonged on the record too, and gave it. "A clean one is a single blow. That one came in two parts, a little and then the rest, and I had to guess between them. I think it let go slowly. That isn't an excuse. It's only what I felt."

The Mire instructor was already out of her chair. She went to the frame and worked the release with her hand, slowly, four times, bending close to listen, and on the fourth she stopped with her thumb on the pawl.

"Sticks at the second notch," she said. "Not every time. Once in five, near enough." She looked across at Gault with real annoyance. "It had its service in the spring. It shouldn't be doing this."

"Will it hold for the rest of the morning?"

"So long as somebody's told when it sticks." She went back to her seat. "And he seems to feel it before we hear it."

Gault wrote for a long time. The clerk wrote too, on a small separate slip, and pushed it into a slot on the frame's brass housing, where it stuck out like a little white tongue. *For service.* Cael looked at it over his shoulder and thought how easily a careful hand on a busy morning might pull a slip like that out to read it and forget to put it back, and the work never be done. Then he thought that it was not his frame, nor his busy morning, and turned to face the wall.

The last three came clean. Eleven of twelve, the clerk read out, and the wrong one the ninth, and Cael stood in the chalk with his back to the rail and noticed, with some surprise, that he had begun to like the room.

The third trial put the two together.

Rail set somewhere he was not told, weight racked, Cael in the chalk beneath it; and when the weight let go he was to feel where it came from and be out of the square before it hit the boards. Read, then move, with no gap between them for thinking in.

Eight runs. Seven times he was clear. On the eighth the press came to him a hair late, through no fault of the pawl, and he went a hair late after it, and the weight came down on the oak with a flat heavy knock a finger's breadth behind his heel. The other seven were good in a way he did not think the clerk's figures would ever show: the press arriving along his arm, his hips already going before he had told them to, the half-breath, the sideways nothing, the boards, the weight landing in a place he had just stopped being.

At about the fifth, he realized he was enjoying himself. The realization arrived with a little cold prick behind it, like a draught under a door.

It was the room's doing. Every other floor he had ever stood on had wanted something out of him, a purse or a place or a noise from a crowd or a verdict. This one wanted only to know where he went, and asked so plainly and so patiently that he could feel himself easing under it, the way a man who has carried a load a long way eases when somebody finally takes the other end. Easy men talked. He tightened up again, on purpose, between the sixth run and the seventh, and was aware of the Ash instructor watching him do it, and kept his eyes on the wall.

Of the six, the fourth cost most, and what it asked of him was to leave something undone.

The Mire instructor racked the heaviest weight. The clerk moved the chalk square in, until it stood within a pace of the rail.

"No getting out of this one," said Gault. "Too close, too heavy. Catch it on the plate."

The clerk brought the plate across on its brace and put it into Cael's hands: a round of steel with a spring behind it and a small dial in the housing, made to show how much of a blow had been held and how much had gone on through.

Feryn's fragment woke up in his right arm and reached for it.

He felt it the way a hand in the dark goes to a tool it has used before, already shaping to the grip. Since a bout he had lost fairly three years ago it had lived in him, used seldom, but always close under the skin. It did not keep a blow; it gathered it, turned it, and passed it on somewhere else. It was made for exactly this. If anybody in the room had been marking answers right and wrong, it was the right answer to the question in his hands, and the little dial would have swung round to a figure nobody at that table had seen in their lives.

It was not on the record.

He stood with the plate and the reaching in his arm for two breaths, which was one breath longer than he had meant to, and he saw Gault see the extra breath and say nothing. Somewhere the pendulum ticked. He thought of Lira on the wash-house step: *make him look.* He thought of Karis in carrel eleven: *it was mine before it was yours.* He thought of half a page of transcript, late and thin. Then he let the reaching go, as he had let it go on a thousand other floors, and his arm was only his arm.

He braced the plain way. Feet wide; the plate held out on an arm loaded from the shoulder; everything down through his legs into the oak. It was how a body with no Path takes a knock, and how he had taken every knock in his first winter on the Ironyard's planks. The pawl let go. The weight came down the rail and met the plate, and the shock went up the bones of his forearm and stopped in the shoulder like a nail driven into a joist. Nothing in him carried it anywhere else. The shoulder had all of it. Behind the plate the spring hit the end of its travel with a dull clank.

"Held, low," said the clerk.

"Again," said Gault.

Four of them. After the fourth his right arm was numb from the wrist halfway to the elbow, and deep under the knob of the shoulder sat a hard bright ache that he knew would still be there when the week was out.

"Your account," said Gault.

"Four of those I can stand." Cael opened and closed his right hand in plain sight. "Eight, no. It doesn't go anywhere. The shoulder keeps it."

Every word was so. Taken together, the words were a coat cut to look like the whole man, with most of the man left at home, and he knew it while he said them. He had to hold his face very still.

---

The last two trials were the first two again, run cold, to see what an hour on the oak had taken out of him.

The board picked its louvres and he answered them, and the clerk's figures came back a palm short of the morning's, with the stiff beat at the end of each a hair slower. His hip had its bill by then and he did not try to argue with it. He let it show. The drop-frame he answered twelve times out of twelve. It did not stick once.

He did not mind the loss being written down. That was the point of a cold run: to show what the morning had cost, honestly, so that next half-year somebody could see whether it cost less. A line that hid its own tiredness would be a line he could never grow past.

He went back to the chair afterward without being told and sat, because the hip asked him to. Nobody minded. He watched his right hand on his knee until its fingers were still, and then, for the first time that morning, let himself look at the three people behind the long table as people.

The Ash instructor's book lay shut in front of her, with a finger in it at a place she had long since stopped keeping. The Mire instructor no longer looked resentful; she looked thoughtful, which on her face was nearly the same expression turned the other way up. And Gault had watched him. Cael understood it only now. All through the morning, every run and every drop and every clank of the plate, the heavy still man in the middle chair had watched him, start to finish, and looked away only to write.

*Make him look*, Lira had said. He had not needed to. The room was built for nothing else, and the man in it had done nothing else, and sitting there with his arm aching Cael found that it was nothing like being stared at in a corridor. It was like being counted, carefully, by somebody who meant to get the number right.

Gault spent perhaps four minutes over his papers. The pendulum went on. The funnel dripped.

Then he lifted the top sheet. The charter wanted the note read aloud with the enrollee in the room, and so he read it, and Cael listened to the words that would stand for him on this bluff from now on.

"Assessment of entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee. Non-standard architecture. Execution consistent across trials. Baseline established; semester evaluation to measure against it."

That was the whole of it.

Four short sentences, against six days without proper sleep and an hour of held breath and a shoulder that would complain until the week was out. He waited to be let down by them, and was not. There was nothing wrong in any of them. *Consistent across trials* was the only good word the instrument knew how to say, and it had said it.

The Ash instructor signed. The Mire instructor's name came next, and under it a note on the sticking notch, initialled twice. The clerk pressed the stamp down, blotted, and closed the folder.

Dull. Exact. Stamped. Cael could not remember the last time he had been so glad of anything.

He thought of the four pages of Karis's list, every line anybody had ever written about him: the station's word in brackets, the registry's forms, the observer track, the ruling, Bracken's record with its two signatures. Most of them had been written by people deciding what he was. A few by people taking great care not to. This one had been written by a man who had not tried at all, because deciding was no part of his work, and it was the plainest line on the list, and the only one, he thought, that would read exactly as true a year from now.

He wrote the plate up that evening at the desk by the window, with his arm on a folded shirt because it did not like to hang.

*The not-doing is cheap. I've done it a thousand times; it's one choice, and I'm practised.*

*The dear part is the man across the table. He was straight with me from the first minute, and I gave him something true with a hole in the middle of it, in his own room, for his own book, and he took it down and thanked me for being frank. I was frank. It still wasn't everything.*

*Bill: the shoulder, to the end of the week. Plus something I've no column for yet.*

*Quenna's question was whether I could hold my hands still in front of people who'd like to see me hurt. That one's still open. This morning I held them still in front of a man who wants nothing from me but accuracy. For the record, I think the second one was harder.*

---

The two instructors and the clerk had gone out ahead into the passage, and Gault caught him at the near door with the folder under his arm.

"A word before you go," he said. "Off the book. Do what you like with it."

"Magister."

"Half this faculty would give a good deal for one plain sentence about your nature." Gault said it flatly, as he had read out the crossings. "Some ask out of curiosity, some out of worry. Two of the worried ones, as I judge it, are worried for the school and not for their own skins, which is why they are worth your attention. You'll hear the question put a dozen ways before the term's out, give or take."

"Eleven so far," said Cael. "I've written them down."

"I expect you have." Gault moved the folder to his other arm. "I am not among the askers. I'd like you to know that, and to know the reason, because if you mistake the reason for not caring you will read me wrong all year and we will both find the work harder for it." He looked at Cael directly, as he had in the first minute of the morning.

"I don't need to know what you are, Enrollee. I need my instruments and your consistency. Give me the second; the first is not my mandate."

Cael did not move from the doorway. He held it as he might have held a full cup. He had the sense that it was the most useful thing the bluff had handed him yet, and that it could be taken two ways, and he did not want to walk off with the wrong one.

"Do you mean that kindly?" he said. "Or is it where you stop?"

Gault's face did its brief thing again, a lamp passing behind a shutter.

"It's where I stop. And the stopping is for your sake." He said it simply. "Once an assessor makes up his mind what a practitioner is, he has given up measuring her and started proving himself right. I have seen that finish men's careers. I saw it finish one man altogether. He was a better assessor than I shall ever be. He decided early what a girl in front of him must be, and he spent four years building trials that showed he was right, and they all did. A man's instruments will agree with him if he wants it badly enough. He was simply wrong."

He pushed the door open. The passage's noise came in, feet and voices.

"So I don't decide. Twice a year, as long as you're here, I measure you, and I set down what the needles say. If the needles start saying odd things, I set that down as well, and my hand will look no different on the page. That's the whole of what I offer. Don't call it friendship. Don't lean on it as a shield." He paused. "But nothing else in this building will go on giving the same answer when somebody above me would like a different one. Good morning."

Cael went out past him into the passage, and Gault did not follow at once; he had turned back into the room to say something to the clerk, who had come back for his pens, and the door swung half to between them.

The passage was full, as it always was at the change of bell, and Cael went along it slowly, because his hip had sent its bill and his right arm would not swing. Heads turned as he went by. Two second-years stopped talking outside the records room. A girl with an armful of practice smocks flattened herself against the wall to let him pass and stared at his arm as if she thought it might drop off. Somewhere behind him a man carrying a stack of forms stepped out of the stream, as clerks do, to let the passage go past; Cael did not look at him, because there was nothing in a clerk with forms to look at, and because by then his whole attention had turned inward, to the conversation at the door.

He was thinking about a man who would not decide. On a hill where everybody had already made up their minds about him, eleven ways at once, he had found one man who had made not deciding his trade.

*He doesn't want to know what I am,* he wrote that night, under the plate. *He wants me to be the same twice running. That's harder than being anything. It's also the first thing anybody's asked of me in a long while that I can actually give.*

*About the man he spoke of. Instruments agree with whoever wants them to: that isn't a story about instruments. It's a story about me, if I let it be. Every column I keep, every third entry I've been hoping for since the first. I've been the one holding the instrument all my life. I'd better be Gault's kind and not the other.*

*Line drawn. Two capabilities, measured honestly, on the record for good. Four free on a floor that gives. Eleven of twelve, and the miss was the frame's. Held, low. A shoulder till the week's out.*

*Everything from here gets laid against it.*

The panel had been closed. By the fourth bell that afternoon, the whole bluff knew what had happened in it, and none of what the bluff knew was so.

He worked out how before supper, because working out how was the only part of it he could do anything with. Nobody in the room had talked. He was sure of that by the evening and surer by the end of the week; he went at it four separate ways and found no road out of the demonstration room that any word could have taken. What had happened was simpler and, he thought, rather worse. The charter required that the bare fact of every provision-status evaluation be posted for the academy to read, on the board by the theater door in the administration range, where the clerk pinned the minutes of every open session and every other act the institution did. Halcenvane had kept its business in the open since its charter. So a little after the noon bell, the clerk had pinned up a slip.

*Entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee: satisfied. Baseline established and filed.*

Nine words. They said nothing at all about what he had done. And into that nothing, inside four hours, poured all eleven of the stories about him that had been going round the bluff since the gate, until the space behind the nine words was so full that a person reading them could no longer see the wall they were pinned to.

He found it oddly hard to be angry with anybody about it. The slip was accurate. The charter that required it was a good charter. He had read the brief from the back, as he read every brief. Whoever had signed it sat three grades over the officer's head. Then the line the reports would climb: past that man, past his superior, to somewhere the officer had perhaps never been. Those two things told Seln how honest the rest of the document was allowed to be. Then he had turned to the first page and read it forward, and before he was halfway down the second he had known that whoever wrote it could think.

*Subject: enrollee, Halcenvane Academy, Ostrand. Fifteen years of age. No classification of record. Greyvane adjudication: challenge dismissed; status affirmed on the charter's reading. Monitoring: open, resourced, rotating, unbroken since the ruling.*

And then, a few lines further down, the reason the thing had been written at all.

*Visible rotation has produced conduct data of high volume and negligible value. Subject modifies observable behavior in response to observation with a consistency that suggests deliberate management rather than nervousness. Recommend covered coverage.*

He had read that twice. It was the nearest he came, these days, to pleasure in the work, and there was nobody in the room who would have known what it looked like on him. *Deliberate management rather than nervousness.* Somebody up there knew the difference. In all his years of passing product upward he had met perhaps three readers who could have told the two apart. A watched man who goes carefully is frightened. A watched man who goes carefully *at* you is talking, without using a single word, and the only sensible thing to say back is nothing, from somewhere he cannot see you.

He knew the sentence. He had once set down its twin himself, about another man, in a colder country. He did not let himself go further with that.

The officer had been talking about cadence: a product each month, a summary each quarter, exception reports when the matter called for them. Seln had kept the parts that would bind him and let the rest go past like weather.

It was a sound brief. He turned that over as the officer talked. Whoever had drafted it had looked at a year of open watching, two men and a horse-change every so many miles, and seen what it had bought, which was a great deal of paper describing a boy being careful. The recommendation followed from that as plainly as a sum. You could not learn what a man did when unobserved by observing him in plain sight. You had to be in the room without being an event in it.

That was Seln's whole trade. He had been in a great many rooms.

Now he tucked the pages under his arm and inclined his head to the officer, who seemed faintly put out, and went out into a corridor that had windows all along one side, and found that it was raining. He stood a moment at the nearest window and watched it run down the glass. Then he went to find the clerk who would give him his travel warrant, and was perfectly pleasant to her, and she forgot his face before he had reached the stair.

He built the man in a posting-house room in Ostrand over four evenings, with the rain on the shutters and the barge horns calling to each other down on the river.

The hand came first, because in any building a hand outlasted the man who wrote it. He had six of them and could write any of them asleep. He tried each on the back of the ferry tally and chose the fourth: small, upright, a little cramped, the hand of somebody taught letters in a country copying-house and never told he could do better. It looked slow. It was perfectly legible. In every place he had used it, nobody had ever once remarked on it, and that was the whole of its merit.

The voice he tried on the posting-house boy who brought his supper. He set it a hundred-odd miles north of the river and let it be ten years behind the times there, so that the vowels belonged to a town that no longer quite spoke that way.

"North, is it?" said the boy, setting down the tray. "Been away long?"

"A while," said Seln, in the stale voice, and looked at his plate.

"Ah," said the boy, and went away, and did not ask anything else that evening or any evening after. A man with a fresh accent got asked about home. A man with an old one got left alone, because people decided he had his reasons, and what people decided for themselves they never troubled to check.

The coat he chose a grade below what the post would warrant. An assistant who dressed to his station looked like a man going somewhere, and people watched a man going somewhere to see where he went. Then there was the small failing. He had carried it from posting to posting and never known it to let him down. In his first week in the office he would get a sum wrong in his head, an easy one, in front of somebody, and let himself be put right. After that the office would know something about him, and would be proud of having found it out without help, and the shelf where a real opinion might have grown would already be occupied.

On the fourth evening he wrote out his residence card on the form the mid-tier academies used. *Path: Shadow. Tier: Bronze.* He wrote both in the fourth hand. Both were so. He had watched cleverer officers than himself burn their covers down by inventing things they afterward had to remember.

Then he packed.

There were two cases. Into the larger went the man he would be: shirts, a razor, copying pens, a box of the oddments rooms are expected to hold, and four books he had chosen by their spines. The other case was a little thing, old, its corners bound in brass, its lock shut. He laid it at the bottom of the first, as he had laid it at the bottom of every case he had packed in his working life, and filled the first up round it, and shut the lid. He did not open the small one. He did not open it after, either.

He went down to the common room for an hour before bed and let the Path run while he ate, because it was good to keep a tool warm.

It worked, as it always did, on the room. Nobody's eye came back to the man in the grey coat in the corner a second time. The Path did not hide him; it only made him cheap to overlook, so that each glance slid past and found somewhere better to rest. But a small girl at the next table, perhaps four years old, had looked straight at him before he sat down, and she went on looking, over her mother's arm, with the frank interest of the very young, all through her supper. Nothing he did could lift that look off him. The Path worked on glances still on their way. A glance already landed was beyond it.

He let her look. Her mother never once turned round.

By the end of the hour there was the old flat greyness behind his eyes that came from holding a room's attention away from himself. It would be there in the morning. It was always there in the morning, whatever he slept. The Path cost him his own watching; to keep the room from noticing him he had to keep noticing the room, without a pause, and four hours of that wore a man thin. It was no use at all against instruments, a ledger or a counting-board or a pendulum, which had no attention to be managed. And it was useless, entirely and at once, against the rare kind of watcher who went looking for a hole where a man should be instead of the man. He had met two such people in his life. Both had been very good. He did not expect to meet a third in a provincial academy.

He was thirty-eight years old. Somewhere in the last few postings his skill had outlived his wanting. There was nobody likely to read his product who could have done it better, and there was nothing he wanted any more from having done it well. He set that aside as he would have set aside a subject's quirk: noted, of no known use.

In the morning he crossed the bridge.

He walked the bluff for three days with a satchel of paper on his shoulder, and he never once looked at it the way a visitor looks at a place.

He looked at it the way water looks at a slope. Where would people go, and where would they stop, and from where could they be seen stopping? Three halls on the north edge, each with two sets of doors: six streams that ran together for a quarter of an hour at every change of bell and then broke apart again. The assessment wing behind its own low wall, one gate in, two doors to the building. Very good for a man employed inside it. Very poor for anyone who wanted to stand outside and watch the gate. The lecture range, four floors high, a stair at either end and every landing open along its whole length, so that a man on one landing could be seen from the far stair by anybody who happened to be looking. The quadrangles built round courts, so many windows facing in that nobody ever looked out of any of them on purpose.

And the sunk yard in the middle, with its tiers of stone on three sides and the faculty stand on the fourth. Room for more than eleven hundred. The top tier gave a man the whole floor and half the ways in and out of it, which made it the best place on the bluff to watch from. Everybody on the bluff knew that. So the top tier was where eyes gathered, and a place where eyes gathered was a place where a watcher got noticed, which made it also the worst.

He would use it regardless. Nothing else would serve. He would only use it carefully: a different seat each time, never the same tier two days running, never alone at the open end of a bench where a glance naturally lands. Always somewhere in the middle of a group of four or five, where the group's own shape did most of the work and the Path had only to smooth the edge.

The satchel took him everywhere. A new assistant carries paper, and paper has business in every corridor on a hill.

On the fourth morning he was in the assessment wing's passage with an armful of forms when the door of the demonstration room opened at the change of bell.

Two instructors came out first, and a clerk with a folder, into the stream of people going by. Then, after a little, the boy. He came out alone, unhurried, carrying nothing, favouring the leading leg a little and holding his right arm close against his side the way a man holds an arm that has been asked to take something it did not care for. Behind him the magister had turned back into the room. Seln was perhaps thirty feet away. He stepped out of the stream, as a clerk does, to let it go by.

He did not look at the boy.

The face was the thing. That was what he had once tried to explain to a young officer, a long time ago, who had not listened and had not lasted. People did not feel a stranger's nearness, or how often they had passed him, or how odd it was that he kept turning up. They felt his eyes. A man could pass the same stranger nine times on a stair and remember none of it, and on the tenth, if their eyes met once, all nine would come back to him at once as a pattern, and the stranger would be finished. So for the first month there would be no looking. Only what came in at the edges: where the boy stood, how the passage moved round him, what the people round him did. There would be months for looking later, when Seln had become part of the furniture, and nobody minds a chair with eyes.

In the office he squared the forms on the copying table. For two hours after that he did not let the boy into his head at all, and that, too, was method.

Late that day, in the plain day-book a new assistant keeps and anybody may read, he noted one ordinary thing among the other ordinary things. The passage had been crowded at the change of bell, and the passage had been watching. He had counted, without looking, perhaps one person in three turning a head as the boy went by, or pausing, or deciding whether to nod.

That suited him, and he noted it without warmth. The more people already stared at a man, the less anybody would notice one more.

Karis's census of the residence cards had reached the staff corridor by then, and she had made Cael promise to read every card on it the next time he passed that way, since it was on his road back from the north hall and not on hers.

"Count the ones you'd bet your supper on," she said. "And the ones you wouldn't. I'm up to sixty-one doors. I believe forty."

Every door on the bluff carried one, in a small brass frame at the height of a man's eyes: a name, a Path, a tier. Greyvane would never have hung such cards. Greyvane had been built on the suspicion that papers lied about people, and you do not fix to a door a figure you suspect. Halcenvane put the number on every door because here the number ran everything, the ladder and the brackets and the rooms and the seeding, and a corridor of cards was simply the ledger with its pages hung up where everybody walked. Cael's own card read *Cael. Assay provision. Evaluation by demonstration.*, in letters squeezed smaller than any other on the corridor so that the words would fit. Lira's read *Wind. Copper.* Brom's read *Iron Skin. Copper.*, and Brom tapped it with a knuckle every night on his way in, as if to remind it he was there. Karis's read *Ember. Iron.* It was the one card she said she would have bet her supper on, and she used it to measure all the others.

He had a filing to hand in on the way, so he went to the assessment wing first.

It was his second floor-time allocation, and he walked it over himself, three days after the baseline, instead of leaving it for the residence clerk. He wanted to see the wing on a day when nobody was being measured in it. The demonstration room's door was shut. The passage smelled of lamp black and machine oil and the fine dry dust of the chalk trays, and on the records side every sheet he saw bore a pair of dates and a pair of initials, one for whoever had written it and one for whoever had checked, so that no paper could leave the wing with a single person's word on it. Vell had kept her ledger that way out of plain temper; here it was built into the forms.

The usual clerk was at her desk in the end office, frowning at a tally. The man at the copying table under the window was new. He took Cael's sheet without a word and read it right through, which most clerks never did. He turned it over to look at the authorizations on the back, checked both sets of initials in the corner, laid it on the right heap without glancing at the heaps, and wrote it into the big ledger with the two dates side by side.

"East and north halls. Four a week, supervised. If a hall changes, the notice comes from this office to your residence board the night before, never from the halls direct. If it comes from a hall, it's not authorized, and you're not to take it." He blotted the line. "That's been tried twice this term."

"Thank you."

The clerk at the desk looked up. "Four a week, and how many weeks to the semester? I've got to carry it forward."

The man at the copying table thought about it, lips moving slightly. "Nineteen weeks. Seventy—" He stopped, and frowned. "Seventy-two."

"Seventy-six," said the clerk, quite kindly, and wrote it.

"Seventy-six," the man said after her, mildly, colouring a little, and his pen went back to its line.

Cael stood a moment longer than he needed and ran the gaze over him, without deciding to, as he had run it over every new face on every road for three years. The pen moved at one steady pace, and it had not changed when he spoke or when he stopped; a settled nature, or a lifetime at copying tables, and a copier would have come by that honestly. His weight sat evenly, the habit of years on his feet at copying tables. His breath was ordinary, a little slow. He was where an assistant ought to be at that hour, holding what an assistant ought to hold. All four layers said the same, and what they said was that here was a man who had spent his life writing out other people's words, and was not very good at sums.

Walking back up the passage, Cael could find no reason to think him anything else. He turned it over for a hundred steps and then let it drop. One time was an accident. He had one time of an ordinary man being ordinary, and he had learned the price of building on nothing, underlined, in an Ardenmere spring.

Then he went on along the staff corridor, counting cards for Karis.

Near the far end, on a door whose frame had stood empty all term, there was now a card.

*Seln. Shadow. Bronze.*

He stopped for a breath, perhaps two.

He had never once charted a Shadow practitioner. Greyvane had not had one. At the Ironyard he had seen two in eighteen months, both of them washouts who brawled and left their Path idle, since being hard to notice pays nothing in a ring that people have bought tickets to stare at. And Bronze was above an assistant's post. Assistants at mid-tier schools were Copper mostly, or new Iron, graduates holding on for a year. A Bronze man at a copying table had either had his road bend under him or chosen a quiet year for some private reason. Both were ordinary. Both could be found in any school on the continent.

He put the card in Karis's count as one he would believe, and went to supper.

He wrote up the day afterward in the observation notebook, by the open window, with the barge horns calling their long call and their two short ones on the river below: the two dates and two initials, the chalk dust, the cards and the census and what a corridor of cards said about the place that hung them. Last, beneath the new heading for the wing's staff, he put down the smallest entry of the day. Ardenmere had taught him that the small ones were the ones that came due last.

*New TA in Gault's office. Bronze-tier, Shadow Path per the residence card. Doesn't look at me. Note: at Halcenvane, everyone looks at me. Filed.*

He read it back once, judged it worth exactly what it said and not a word more, and put out the lamp.

## Rhythm calibration (measured, 2026-10-01)

Book 1 Movement 1 — the first movement drafted from this brief — overshot the formula in
the short direction: sentence mean 8.8 words (target 14.6), 41% of sentences at five
words or fewer (target ~28%), 0.3% at forty-plus (target ~3.3%), Flesch-Kincaid grade 2.8
(target 6.8), about 106 dialogue tags per 10k words (target ~41). Paragraph median (15)
and scene spacing were close. So, when drafting:

- Let thought, action and description run in full, well-built sentences. Compound and
  complex sentences with a clear hierarchy are the house texture, not the exception.
- Join a run of three or four clipped statements into one sentence when they are one thought.
- Keep short sentences for beats that land — a recognition, a hit, a turn — not as default.
- Give each chapter a few deliberately long, readable sentences (forty words or more)
  where an action or a thought earns the length.
- Drop "he said" / "she said" when the paragraph already makes the speaker clear.
- Keep short paragraphs (median ~18 words) and scene breaks about every 950 words.

## No scripted prose surgery (2026-10-01)

Rhythm and length repairs are done by reading, sentence by sentence, never by a script that
splits sentences or paragraphs at clause or sentence boundaries. Two repairs did that and
each needed a recheck to find the splits that broke a thought. Where the formula and a
character's speech disagree, keep the speech: one-to-three-word dialogue lines and the short
landing beats of a fight are not drift to be repaired.

## Working ranges and accepted drift (coordinator, 2026-10-01, after five repairs)

Five movement repairs show three of the formula's numbers pulling against the brief's other

thought turns). Repairs that chase one of them push another out. So, until the owner rules

the decimals:

## Source-reuse gate tightened to 8 words (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

The 10-word gate let distinctive source phrasing through in 6–9-word fragments (the first
movement's opening line, for example, echoed the current edition's). Measured at 8 words, movements
written after authors began self-checking run 1–6 shared runs per 10k; the worst ran ~30. From now
on `ed.sh overlap` and every editorial review use runs of **8 or more** words. Every listed run that
BOOK_MAP does not protect is rewritten — re-composed, not a synonym swapped. Names, Path names,
ranks and plain facts of canon will sometimes share a short run; re-compose the sentence around them.
Movements closed under the 10-word gate get one book-level 8-word sweep at book completion.

## Draft from your own event list, not from the source page (coordinator, 2026-10-02)

Book 3 Movement 6's review found the retold scenes in ch42–44 to be close paraphrase: 23–32% of
sentences followed a source sentence word for word in order, with words varied enough to pass the
8-word gate. Everything the author invented was clean. The cause is drafting with the source open.
So, for every movement:

1. Read the source chapters the packet names, once, for what happens and who these people are.
2. Close them. Write a private event list in your own words — what happens, in what order, who
   wants what, what changes. Keep it in your working notes, not in the manuscript.
3. Draft from that list and the book map. Do not reopen the source chapters while drafting.
   Choose your own entry point into each scene and your own order of beats; the source's scene
   shape is not canon, only its events are.
4. Only protected wording (BOOK_MAP) is copied, exactly, from the map — not from the source page.

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

## The World: Valdris

A single continent of tiered city-states. Physical geography is not unusual — mountains, plains, coastlines, edge territories — but the governing structure is entirely organized around the Path system. Every settlement above a village has concentric tiers of access: outer districts for low-rank or unranked citizens, inner districts for higher ranks, with the administrative core accessible only to Bronze and above.

The spaces between city-states are called the **edge territories** — ungoverned, monster-populated, and the only place in Valdris where rank means less than survival skill. Most people never go there. The companions spend the entire third arc there.

**PROVISIONAL:** The edge territories contain ancient ruins that predate the current Path system. What the ruins were for, and who built them, is a major discovery of Arc 3.

---

## The Path System

**LOCKED:** The foundational civic and metaphysical structure of Valdris. Every human being in Valdris has latent Path potential — an internal energy architecture that becomes active at age 14.

### Kindling

At age 14, every person undergoes Kindling: the moment their latent Path potential activates. Kindling is involuntary — it happens regardless of whether the person is ready. An **Arbiter** appears at the moment of Kindling: a small glowing sigil that only the Kindling person can see and hear. The Arbiter evaluates the person's internal architecture and assigns their Path classification.

The classification is recorded in the Compact Registry — the official continental record of all Path holders — and is effectively permanent. Classification cannot be appealed, reassigned, or removed.

### The Tiers

**LOCKED:**

| Tier | Color | Population who hold it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | Dim orange | ~60% of active practitioners | Entry tier; most adults plateau here |
| Iron | Silver-grey | ~25% of active practitioners | Journeyman level; professional fighters, tradespeople |
| Bronze | Warm gold | ~10% | Guild officers, minor academy graduates, respected figures |
| Silver | Bright silver | ~4% | National-level respected; academy honors graduates |
| Gold | Deep amber | <1% | Regional events; legendary status while living |
| Platinum | White-blue | Historical figures only | No living Platinum holders as of Book 1 |
| Void | Unknown | Mythological | Not confirmed to exist by the general population |

Each tier contains ten ranks (Rank 1 through Rank 10). Rank 10 is the threshold for advancement to the next tier. Advancement requires both rank accumulation through use and a formal evaluation by a registered Arbiter station.

### Ability Acquisition

As a practitioner advances through ranks, new abilities manifest — presented by the Arbiter as visible ability declarations, experienced as text-like constructs in the practitioner's perception. These are called **Path declarations** and are specific to the practitioner's Path type.

**Example format (Iron-tier Blade Path practitioner):**

## The Fractured Path

**LOCKED (existence and surface mechanics); SECRET (true nature — revealed progressively)**

The classification [SHATTERED] has appeared in the Compact Registry four times in recorded history. In each prior case, the practitioner was eliminated within weeks of Kindling. The official records describe all four as dangerous instabilities who posed systemic risk.

**SECRET:** All four were eliminated by the Guilds Compact on Architect-system instruction. The Arbiter system flags [SHATTERED] classifications to a deep-layer protocol that the Compact inherited without understanding it. When a [SHATTERED] appears, the Compact receives pressure — administrative, social, legal — to resolve the anomaly. They have always complied. Until Cael survives long enough to make compliance difficult.

### What the Fractured Path actually is — revealed in layers

**Book 1-3 (what Cael believes):** His Fractured Path is a collection of unrelated ability shards — fragments of multiple Paths, none complete. The shards work, individually, but he has no Path declaration sequence, no tier advancement, no Arbiter guidance.

**Book 4-6 (first real discovery):** The shards can integrate witnessed abilities. When Cael observes another practitioner use a Path declaration, he can absorb a version of it into his own shard structure — permanently. It is not copying. It is closer to digestion: the absorbed ability becomes native to his architecture, not a foreign element.

**Book 7-9 (second discovery):** The Fractured Path predates the classification system. Ancient records from before the Compact use a different word for Cael's condition — not [SHATTERED] but [UNBOUND]. The distinction matters: [SHATTERED] implies breakage. [UNBOUND] implies the absence of a container that was never supposed to be there.

**Book 10-12 (the Architect's confirmation):** The Architect's preserved will, now active, confirms the truth by trying to eliminate it: Cael's Fractured Path is what all human Path potential looked like before the Architect designed the classification system. The system was not built to organize existing potential. It was built to contain and cap it.

**Book 13-15 (full understanding):** The Fractured Path is the primordial source — the raw, unlimited, individual potential that existed before anyone decided it needed to be structured. Cael is not an aberration. He is what everyone would be if the system had never been built.

### Fractured Path mechanics — visible to reader

Cael does not receive standard Path declarations. Instead he receives what he privately calls **fragment notices** — irregular, incomplete, different in format from the standard Arbiter declaration:

```
FRAGMENT ACQUIRED
[unnamed] — Wind-adjacent. Duration: undetermined. Integration: partial.
Tier equivalent: unknown.
```

As he advances through the series, the fragment notices become more complete, more named, and eventually begin to look like declarations — except they span multiple Path types simultaneously, which is structurally impossible under the standard system.

## Tide Path — PROVISIONAL (defined in Book 7, 2026-09-04)

Flow architecture.

# Chapter 45 — The Panel's Order

On the thirteenth of Reaping, before he lit the lamp, Cael did nothing at all, and he did it on purpose.

He lay on his back in the dark with the blanket to his chin and found the half-metre of air round his own body, and he gave one small hard corner of his attention to it and told it to stay there, as a man sets a stake in a field before he lets the horses out. Only when it was settled did he let the rest of him wake up and start thinking about the day. Below the window a barge on the river gave two short notes, *heard you*, and somebody on the far bank answered it.

The ache came up behind his eyes as he sat, the dull one, the rent the sixth fragment charged for its idle state. It was cheap in the mornings. By evening it would have corners.

He lit the lamp low and opened the notebook at the delegation's pages.

There were eleven names on the manifest and he had pages on nine of them. The library had given him three of the four escort riders out of eleven years of attendance lists, the counsel out of her published findings, and the three clerks out of a registry yearbook that nobody had borrowed in a decade. Havel had a page with a question of Lira's at the foot of it. Ilsev had the thickest page and the least use, because six findings told him what she would do with a document and nothing at all about what she did with a morning. The fourth rider was too new to be anywhere. And the eleventh name had one line on its page, and under the line, in his own hand, a single word: *Lira*.

He read the eleven through once and closed the book. Then he opened the plan instead, at the page with two words ruled off across its head, written on the eve of the twelfth in a hand that had not been quite steady: *Hold. First.*

None of it was the first task any more. That had changed in a corridor two days ago, with Brom standing square across the flagstones like a post. Reading the delegation was the second task. The first was to stay exactly as thick as an ordinary boy for every hour of every day they were on the bluff, and the first task did not care how interesting the second one got.

Lira came down to breakfast without the stick.

She came down the residence stair one step at a time, with her hand on the rail and her face arranged to suggest that this was simply how she had always preferred to come down stairs, and that anybody who thought otherwise was welcome to say so. Her four days were over. The safety instructor had said four days with nothing on it, and four days had ended at midnight, and Lira had always kept to the letter of an order she disliked.

Brom half rose from the bench. She looked at him, and he sat down again and became very interested in his porridge.

"Well?" said Karis.

"It holds me up." Lira lowered herself onto the bench beside Cael with the leading leg out straight, and set her bowl in front of her, and did not look at anybody. "It isn't happy about it. It holds me up."

"Six days," said Brom, to his spoon.

"Six days," said Lira. "Nobody talks about hips."

In six days she would walk out onto the oak for the Copper final, Copper Rank Two on her certificate and Iron-equivalent by every honest ledger on the bluff, on a leg the safety instructor had not cleared, against the one fighter in the bracket who knew it best.

Karis had the grey notebook open beside her bread, at a page Cael knew. Down its left side ran a column of small ruled boxes, one for every night between the semifinals and the final, and every box above this morning's line had a neat cross in it. It was the second round of Rooke's nineteen exercises, the ones Brom did by lamplight in the corner of the common room. Karis had ruled the boxes out to the eighteenth, the night before the final, and she had told nobody but Cael why she stopped there.

"You didn't miss one," she said to Brom.

"I never miss one."

"I know. I'm writing it down so that you'll know I know." She inked a cross into last night's box and blotted it with the side of her hand. "They signed for the chests, by the way. Yesterday. Bracken's clerk made every one of them sign before he'd let a box through the door. The counsel signed with a face like a cat in a bath."

"Good," said Lira.

"I thought you'd like that."

"What are you doing today?" Brom asked Lira. He asked it carefully, the way a man walks out onto ice.

"Lectures. Then I'm going to walk the length of the Crown yard and back, twice, slowly, with nobody watching." She ate a spoonful of porridge. "Then I'm going to sit down and decide whether I liked it."

"I could walk with you."

"You could not. You're the other half of the final." She pointed her spoon at him. "If you walk beside me you'll watch how I put the foot down, because you can't help it, and then you'll know, and then I'll know you know, and we'll be playing chess again by supper."

Brom thought about it, and nodded slowly, and went back to his bowl.

"I'll walk with her," said Karis. "I don't know anything about feet."

"That," said Lira, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me in four days."

Cael ate and listened and minded his edges. Across the hall a table of Mire second-years were telling each other in low voices what the Archmarshal had eaten at supper, which none of them could possibly know. At the next table a Blade fourth-year was explaining to a first-year, at length and with diagrams drawn in spilt water, exactly how an inspection worked, and Cael listened to that for a while because the fourth-year was wrong in a very confident and orderly way. Then he stopped listening, because the stopping was also practice.

"You've got your morning face on," said Lira, without turning her head.

"I've got a page with one line on it."

"You've got a page with my name on it." She broke her bread in two. "Eat. If you go thin at breakfast I'll know, and I'm sitting on the side with the bad leg, so I'll have to tread on you with the good one, and it'll hurt us both."

---

Through the wall of the guest floor, the counsel was talking to her clerk while she dressed.

Vastin had heard her through walls for four nights of inns, and he no longer heard the words. He heard the shape. It went on low and level, about the water in the jug and the state of a collar and something a magistrate had once said to her on a stair. Then it stopped in the middle, and there was a silence about as long as a page, and then it went on again from the exact syllable where it had broken off, as if nothing at all had come between the two halves. She had been handed something to read. He had noticed the habit on the second day of the road and had not written it down, because he did not take notes on colleagues. He only noticed that he had noticed, and let it go.

He sat at a scrubbed table under the window with his coat on, because the guest floor was cold before the fires were lit, and his watch lay face-up beside his hand.

It was a plain brass watch, older than the clerk who had brought up his water. Every Seventh-day for longer than he cared to count, wherever he happened to be, he had set it against the best clock in the building and written the difference on a card in its lid, and so he knew that it gained in the cold, and by how much. A thing that measured anything had to be checked, now and then, against some other thing that measured. Otherwise it was only a thing with a number on its face.

On the table in front of the watch lay the wing's filings.

They had come across the evening before, under the assessment wing's own seal, in answer to a requisition the counsel had written on the road in the ordinary form. The wing had answered it in the ordinary time. Pink tape. A covering index on top, in a hand that was not the hand of the filings under it. Six filings, with an appendix behind each, covering one term of embedded coverage on an enrollee who held his place under a provision.

He had put them at the bottom of everything, on purpose. Everything else was read now. So he cut the tape at the knot, where it could be tied again exactly as it had been, and began.

Nine minutes, by the watch. He went down every line and did not lift his pencil once. At the end he squared the sheets and laid the index back on top. Then he sat with his hands flat on either side of the stack and looked out of the window at a wet roof, on which a gull was standing on one leg and disapproving of the morning.

Complete in its form. Correct in every appendix. One term of a capable officer's work.

He had made a great deal of such work himself, in his time. For years he had been the man at the edge of the room with a board on his knee, writing down what could be seen from the chair he had been given. He had been good at it. He had been told so, honestly, and the work had been honest too. It was out of those years, long after, that he had made a sentence for himself. He had said it aloud perhaps half a dozen times since, always to somebody young, and had never written it anywhere.

*Observer product is testimony about the observer.*

He did not mean it unkindly. He meant it as a sum. Any filing carried three things inside it, and only one of them was the thing it said it was about. It carried what a person had noticed. It also carried the chair that person had been put in, and the list of what they had been told to look for, and nobody in the whole history of the service had ever found a way to wash those two out of the ink afterward. You could not ask a filing to see round its own chair. You could lean on it a little. You could not lay a floor across it and stand.

These six told him, plainly and well, where a good officer had been asked to sit for a term, and what he had been asked to watch for from there. That was a true thing to know. It was a thing about the chair.

He set it in its place, a long way under the record and a little way under the exhibits, and turned the watch face-down.

There was a knock, and the counsel put her head round the door with her collar still undone.

"Archmarshal. Will you sit with us today?"

"Not today."

"The provision's paper is up this morning. The registrar's boxes." Her eyes went to the tied stack by his hand and came away again without a question. "I'd value the company. I'm told their index is better than ours, and I'd like a witness when I admit it."

"The first three days are yours," said Vastin. "If I sit at your table, your clerks will write down what I look at instead of what you ask. I'll come on the fifteenth."

She considered that, and him, with her head a little on one side.

"That's a kindness," she said, "dressed up as a procedure."

"Most procedures are," said Vastin. "It's what they're for."

She laughed once, a short surprised sound, and went away down the passage talking. Through the wall he heard her take up her sentence to the clerk at the exact word where she had left it.

---

The record he had been through four times on the road, in the oldest of the three carriages.

He had taken that one because nobody else had asked for it. It rode hard, and its door had a device painted out so long ago that the paint had worn thin over it, and nobody wanted to be seen getting down from it. That suited him. A carriage nobody wanted was a carriage nobody came to talk in. For four days the road's grit had ticked against the half-closed shutter. The papers had ridden on the bench opposite him, and somewhere ahead the counsel's voice had gone on and on, faint, like a stream on the other side of a wall.

At the coast he had tied the papers into three bundles, each in a different tape, so that his hand would always come to them in the same order without his having to think. Grey tape round the record itself. Red round everything tendered in it. Plain string round what other officers had written about the boy over two years.

The order was the panel's. It was the order the panel had been given the matter in, and so it cost the most to read, and he had paid that price on every file for most of his life.

He read the grey bundle first and all of it, from the top: the magistrate's opening words, which the clerk had asked to have spoken twice because he had dropped his pen; the roll of the room; every dull procedural quarrel, including a long one about whether an academy's day-book was a paper of the academy or of the man who kept it. Then the red bundle, in the order the papers had been handed up, which was never the order anyone would have chosen afterward. Two years of demonstration sittings, sheet on sheet. A framework of movement that the record called Wind-adjacent, measured on a stone floor. A read along the skin, logged trial by trial. And at the very back, late and thin, one sheet about a spark. Last, the string bundle. He had untied that one once.

The red bundle he had read the most slowly of the three. Two years of a boy's sittings, signed in turn by two officers of the academy, and the numbers in them agreed with one another. They agreed from sitting to sitting and from month to month, a good deal more closely than numbers usually agree when the person producing them is fourteen and then fifteen and growing out of his boots between one sitting and the next. He had made no mark about that, because agreement was not a question. It was simply a thing that was so. But he had read those sheets twice where he read most things once, and he had noticed himself doing it.

People liked to believe that order did not matter. It mattered more than almost anything. A digest was always laid out for its own ending, so that the reader arrived at the ending feeling he had walked there himself. And once a man knows where a road comes out, every bend of it looks like a turn toward that place. He had watched able men read that way all his working life. He had once done it himself. That was the reason for the tape.

His marks were in the margins of all four passes, one colour of pencil to each, and every mark was a question.

It was the only part of his method that had ever embarrassed him a little. In the registry's lower vault there was a long drawer of his old working copies, kept because a rule somewhere said they must be. On a wet afternoon once he had reckoned, idly, that the drawer held about two thousand of his margins. He did not believe a clerk could go through it from end to end and come away with six conclusions.

Three of the margins in this record carried the rest.

The first stood beside the respondent's opening. A boy of fifteen, with nobody beside him at the table, had begun by agreeing with the challenge. He had handed it, flatly and before anyone had pressed him, the one thing the whole room had come there to watch him fight over, and then he had calmly built his entire argument on the far side of the gift.

*Why concede the category?*

The second stood beside the stretch where the boy had counted the schedule's places out loud for the panel, five of them and then a sixth bound in at the back, and had treated the count as a wall. He had understood that a list which says *these and no others* shuts out its own author as firmly as anybody else. Men with long registry careers forgot that every year.

*Who taught him the schedule's structure?*

And the third stood at the foot of the record's first sheet, beside two dates the Compact's own clerk had entered there: the day the challenge was filed, and the day the first sitting was called. He had drawn two lines under it.

*Verify: three weeks' preparation — from what baseline?*

He never underlined to make a thing louder. Two lines meant a note to the man who would read this copy next, who was always himself. It said: *everything else will rest its weight here, so watch yourself, because the cheap answer will look very good.*

---

Three weeks was in the record, and no one had argued with it. Both dates were in the Compact's own hand.

So there were two ways to read it. Either the three weeks were wrong in the record, which happened, quietly and for years. Or something in the two years before them had made a boy who could do that much with three weeks. And nowhere in the file, in two years of district filings or in any page of the warden's long reports, did a single sentence say what. There was a great deal about what the boy had done. There was nothing about what had made him able to do it.

There was the gap. He had come on it in the first pass, somewhere on the coast road with a gull's cry outside the shutter. The next three passes had not closed it by a hair. It was the most interesting gap that had crossed his table in a long while. He meant that in the plainest way a craftsman can mean it, the way a joiner is interested in a joint that ought to have failed and has not.

He had no theory about it. He took some care not to grow one.

He had been taught that care at twenty-three, at a scarred table in a cellar office that smelled of lamp soot and wet wool coats. The man across the table had spoken to him perhaps twenty times in two years. Once, though, he had leaned his elbows on the scars and said a sentence that had outlasted everything else about him: his face, his voice, and very nearly his name.

*An evaluation that begins from its conclusion is a report about the evaluator.*

Vastin had heard it and copied it into the back of a book and gone on not understanding it for four years. He understood it in a bad month, sitting on the end of a narrow bed in a posting-house with his boots still on. A finding of his had come apart that afternoon at its first review, and he had sat there and traced it back. He had built it step by step from a suspicion he had liked holding, and every step had looked to him like evidence, because he had been walking toward the place where it came out the whole time and had never once turned round to look. The finding had not lived. His career had come within a sheet of paper of going down with it.

He had read files in the panel's order ever since, and grown no theories he could help.

His left hand had begun to complain on cold mornings, a stiffness across the knuckles that eased by the second bell. It was complaining now. For the last two winters it had wanted the pen held a little further back, and he had let it have its way and said nothing. A man who argues with his own hand writes worse, and he still had a great deal to write.

Below the window, across the wet quadrangle, a boy was standing on an upturned bucket under an arch. He was writing on a slate that hung on the wall there, a figure at a time, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth. Then he got down and looked at what he had written from the cobbles, and got up again, and changed one figure. Vastin watched him do it twice. He had no idea what the slate was for. Whatever it was, the boy had decided it ought to be right, though there was nobody anywhere in the quadrangle to check it.

He tied the wing's filings in their pink tape again, knot over knot, as they had come, and set them by the door for the clerk.

The notice that had sent him here was a true notice. It named an academy that had done an unusual thing lawfully, and an inspection of the ordinary kind, and nothing else. He had filed no question. He knew perfectly well that not one person on this hill would believe it. Years ago he had given up trying to close the space between his work and the work people imagined for him. It was a useful space. It showed you what each of them feared he had come for. But what it showed was theirs, not his, and he left it to them.

He would walk the building. He would watch its rooms being used. Rooms told you what a house had decided to be, if you let them talk long enough.

This house had answered the notice by changing nothing, and it had not troubled to hide that it was doing so. He had seen it in the courtyard yesterday. There was a gutter mended badly three times and left that way. The cobbles had not been swept for visitors. And the registrar had told the first man through his door exactly where the file stood and how many boxes it filled, instead of how hard it would be to find. A house that changed nothing for an inspector was either very sure of itself or very well advised. Either was worth knowing. Neither was a finding.

A bell began somewhere below, the first of the day. He laid the grey bundle on top of the others, the right way up, and went down.

---

An inspection, it turned out, was mostly doors.

He had read the schedule a dozen times, and nothing in it had warned him how small the whole business would look from the floor. The hill did not stop for eleven people. It made room for them, the way a house makes room for a guest's trunk in the hall: everyone steps round it, and nobody mentions it, and by noon nobody quite sees it any more.

But three doors that had stood open all season were shut now, from the second bell until the fourth. One was the records hall's back room, where Bracken had built the file. One was the long upper room over the lecture range, where the faculty argued on Fifth-days. The third was the wing's document store behind Gault's office. Cael had never before seen it closed. He had always known it by the instrument woman's oil can, standing on its sill.

And the covered walk filled with boxes.

They were black, with brass hasps, and they went across in the morning and came back at the fourth bell. A young clerk carried them at a fast walk in front of his chest, breathing through his mouth. A second clerk walked beside him with a list. The third came along behind, at his own pace, and never carried anything.

He was the eldest of the three, a grey man with a careful face, and his left knee was bad. Cael saw it on the walk's two shallow steps, which the man took sideways, one at a time, the way somebody does who has worked out to the farthing what a step will charge him. He wrote with his left hand as well, on a board pressed flat against his coat. He was the one who said which box went. The young man only carried what he was told.

So whatever crossed the walk at the second bell was what the sittings would read by the third. Whatever came back at the fourth was finished with.

Cael did not have to do anything to learn it. He only had to stand at the walk's rail on his way to the morning lecture, as he had stood there every morning of the term, and see what was in the young clerk's arms as the young clerk went hurrying past. At the second bell it was two boxes that wore the registrar's index labels. At the fourth those two came back, and one went over the other way, labelled in the wing's hand.

*They're on the provision's paper today,* he wrote at noon, on the clerks' page. *The wing is tomorrow. The old man's knee tells you half a day early. Nobody has to say a word.*

The counsel he had in four minutes, on the same walk, before the first box had come back.

She came along it with Havel a half-pace behind her, and she was talking. She was always talking, low and pleasant, to whoever was nearest, about anything at all. Then the young clerk came up beside her with a single sheet. She took it and stopped dead in the middle of the flagstones, so that the crowd in the walk had to part round her as water parts round a post, and read it to the bottom without a sound, while a first-year with an armful of books stood stranded behind her and did not dare go round. She handed it back. She walked on.

And she went on talking from the very word where she had stopped.

Not the start of the sentence. Not some new sentence. The word. Cael had heard the first half of it from the rail before the sheet came. He heard the second half after it went, fitted on as neatly as if nothing at all had come between.

He took that to carrel eleven at noon, where Karis was eating an apple over a sheet of the archive's lending rules. Bracken had forbidden her the records hall until the fifteenth, in writing, when she was to sit at the table for the enrollment basis and not one day before. She was being very good about it, in a way that made her bite apples hard.

"Her talking and her reading keep to different rooms in her head," he said. "She can argue with you and read your file at the same time, and she won't lose her place in either."

Karis put the apple down.

"That's the one who's going to take the boxes apart."

"Yes."

She looked at the lending rules for a while without reading them.

"Good," she said at last. "I want her good. If she's good and she can't get in, then it holds. If she's bad and she can't get in, it only means she was bad." She picked up the apple again. "I'd rather lose to somebody good than win against somebody stupid. You can't keep a win you got off somebody stupid. It doesn't stay won."

"Bracken says the file has to work on strangers."

"Bracken's right. Bracken's nearly always right. It's very tiring." She bit the apple. "Which boxes?"

He told her the labels. She shut her eyes and said the index numbers back to him in order, and what each box held, and what in each of them a good counsel would go for first. She was right about all of it. He could see her being right, the way you can see somebody's hands itch.

"I can't go in till the fifteenth," she said, with her eyes still shut.

"I know."

"I built it, and I have to wait outside like a parcel." She opened her eyes. "That's the whole point of it, of course. I understand that. I'd just like it noted that I mind."

"Noted," said Cael, and she threw the apple core at him, and missed on purpose.

She was quiet for a moment after, turning her pen.

"And the eleventh?"

"Nothing. I haven't tried."

"Good." She said it at once, as if he had passed a small test he had not known was set. "Don't. Not from across a courtyard, not on your own. You read him with everything on the tier yesterday, and Lira says you stayed, and I believe her. But I'd rather you didn't find out how many times you can do that."

"I'm not going to look for him."

"I know you're not going to *look*," said Karis. "I'm asking you not to go and stand where he'll be."

Ilsev crossed the walk a little before the third bell, carrying her own case.

It was the plain brown one with the strap. She went down the exact middle of the flagstones at a pace that never altered, and her eyes did not leave the door at the far end. She did not look at the rail, or the clock over the arch, or the knot of first-years who had found an urgent reason to be at the north end of the walk at that hour. She reached the door, went through it, and was gone.

He had watched every step and got nothing that surprised him, and that was the finding. That night he wrote it at the foot of her page, under six findings' worth of notes, and boxed it.

*Exactly the person the papers describe. She reads the text, goes where the text goes, and wants nothing out of it. Of the eleven, she's the only one who looks in the flesh just as she looks on paper. Nobody else on the manifest does. It's the rarest thing they brought.*

The rest of the morning he spent being ordinary at exactly the rate of every other morning.

He sat his lectures in his usual seat. He did his supervised hour in his usual hall. At the end of the morning he gave his coursework to the Ash instructor, on the day it was due. He had thought about giving it in a day early, to be safe, and had seen the trap in the thought before it was finished. Early was a change. A change was a performance. And Withrow had stood in front of the whole school and told it that a performance was the one thing an inspection could really see.

The Ash instructor took his pages without looking at him and laid them on her pile.

"On the day," she said.

"Yes, Instructor."

"Hm." She squared the pile with two taps. "Half the second year gave theirs in yesterday. In their best hands." She looked at the top sheet, which was not his, with deep suspicion. "I shall be marking handwriting for a week."

He went out with the hold steady and the ache behind his eyes just starting to grow its corners.

From the walk's rail on the way back he could see down into the Crown yard. Two figures were crossing its long floor from end to end, very slowly, with nobody else on the boards and nobody on the tiers. The shorter one was talking with both hands, about something that was plainly not feet. The taller one walked with the leading leg set down a little too carefully each time, like a person putting down a full cup. At the north end they turned, and stood a moment, and started back.

He did not read the hip. He could have. He could have told Lira, from the rail, how many days it was from sound and where on the stride it caught. He kept his eyes on Karis's hands instead, which were drawing something in the air that might have been an index, and he let the two of them walk the length of the yard twice, as Lira had said, with nobody watching.

Six days, he thought. Ten days old by the time she fights on it.

Nine pages, one line, and one name under it. The eleventh he left alone. To try it he would have to stand near the man, and nothing on the bluff that morning had given him an honest reason to.

The schedule on the board would give him one tomorrow: the fourteenth, hall three, the fourth bell. It was his own ordinary Sixth-day hour. The board in the wing had told him so a week ago, and that same evening he had made up his mind to change nothing about it at all.

# Chapter 46 — An Inch

It began to rain a little after the fourth bell, and the covered walk filled up the way it always did in rain, from the inside wall outward.

Nobody liked the rail side when it rained. The wind off the river drove the wet in under the roof there, and so everybody crowded toward the dry stone of the inside wall and went along it in two close streams, one each way, shoulders nearly touching. Cael was in the eastbound stream with his satchel against his chest, going to the library; the boxes had come back across an hour ago, the doors were shut again, and for today, at least, the furniture had finished moving.

Rooke passed him at the arch, going the other way, with a sheaf of cohort sheets under his arm and the rain dark on his shoulders. Cael found he was grateful for it all the same, as a man is grateful in a strange house for one chair that stays where it was.

Behind Rooke came two first-years, walking very close together and talking very low. They were discussing whether the Archmarshal could see through walls. One of them had heard it from a cousin. The other was not convinced, but was walking a good deal closer to the inside wall than the rain required, as if she were giving the matter the benefit of the doubt. Cael let them pass and did not smile, because smiling in the walk this week would be noticed by somebody, and a noticed smile was a story by supper.

He saw Havel at about thirty feet.

The records officer was in the westbound stream, coming toward him along the wall, with a flat document case held close under his left arm and the clerk who carried the lists half a pace behind him. He walked at the pace of a man going to a sitting he was not late for, with his head level and his eyes on the door at the far end of the walk.

Cael let the compound gaze open, all the way, the way he would for anybody worth the price, and left the Iron-adjacent read running coarse and cheap along his forearms underneath it. The corner of him that held his edges stayed where he had put it at dawn, and he checked that it was there before he let anything else go out.

Rhythm: even, unhurried, a step that had walked a great many corridors toward a great many tables. Weight: a little forward, onto the case, the way a man carries a thing he would be in trouble for dropping. Breath: easy. Attention: on the door, and then, once, on the clerk behind, and then on the door again. It was a clean reading of an officer on his way to a sitting. It had no edges in it anywhere, and nothing in it pointed at Cael.

Twenty feet. Fifteen.

Nine feet out, Havel's near shoulder dropped and came forward.

It did not come far. It came an inch, perhaps a little more, and it stayed there, and the whole of Havel's near side went with it, so that the stream he walked in narrowed by that much against the wall. They passed. For a moment Cael had, between his own sleeve and the records officer's case, a hand's breadth of air he had not had a moment before, in a walk where nobody had a hand's breadth of anything.

Then the man was behind him, and the shoulder was level again, and the stream closed up.

Cael kept walking, without turning round and without changing his pace by a hair. He counted his steps to the end of the walk, because counting steps was a thing he often did and nobody would think twice about it, and he went in at the library door with the rain on his collar and the hold exactly where he had left it.

It was only at the long table under the north window, with the notebook open in front of him, that he let himself look at what he had been given.

---

Havel had seen the boy at thirty feet and had known him at twenty, by the walk.

He would have known that walk anywhere. He had watched it come across a square at Ardenmere toward a pump step, on a cold bright morning when he had been a much younger man with a much worse coat. It had changed since, grown longer in the stride and lighter on the heel, and it had stopped hurrying. But it still took the middle of whatever space it had been given and no more than the middle, as if somebody had once explained to the boy exactly how much room he was allowed and he had believed them.

The delegation's card was in Havel's inside pocket, beside the notebook. It was a small stiff card, printed, and every officer on the manifest had been handed one at the coast. Officers of the delegation did not converse with any party under review outside a sitting. They did not acknowledge any such party beyond ordinary courtesy. They did not carry messages, take papers, or accept anything offered.

That last line had made him put the card down on the inn table, the first time, and look at the wall for a while. It had not been written for him. It had been written years before he was born, for every officer of every delegation, by somebody who had never heard of a pump step. He knew that perfectly well. He had read it as if it were about a pear all the same.

He read the card again on the second night, the way he read every instruction, once for what it said and once for what it left out. What it left out was the thing he had thought about, in the end, for most of a stage between two inns.

*Beyond ordinary courtesy.*

So there was an ordinary courtesy. The card granted it by forbidding everything past it.

Fifteen feet. The boy's eyes were on the walk ahead, not on him, as they should have been. Havel kept his own on the door, because he did not want to see the face. If he saw it, he would read it, and if he read it, the boy might read him reading, and then there would be a conversation in that walk with not a word in it. The card forbade conversation.

Nine feet.

He let the near shoulder come down and forward, and he did not decide it at the last moment. He had decided it at fifteen feet, when he had seen how the stream was packed against the wall, and he had measured it then. It was exactly the room a man gives a stranger in a doorway. It was the room he would have given a porter, or a girl with an armful of books, or a magistrate he did not like. It was the most the card allowed and he gave all of it, and not a hair more.

They passed. He did not feel the boy's sleeve. That was the point of the inch.

He straightened. Behind him the clerk with the lists said something about the rain, and Havel answered something about the rain, and the door at the end of the walk came up and opened and took them in.

Not until he had sat down and opened the case did he let himself think about it, and then only for a little while, the way he thought about most things now, sideways, so as not to be seen thinking.

There had been a pear, once. He had not taken it, and he still could not say why not. He thought now that he might have been afraid it would cost the boy something. A boy seen giving a pear to a district assessor would be a boy somebody asked questions about afterward. So he had refused a kindness to save the boy the price of having offered it, and had walked away with the boy's face still in front of him, puzzled and not offended, and had carried the puzzle for two years.

The inch did not make up for the pear. Nothing would make up for the pear. But it was within his instructions, and it was the whole of what was within his instructions, and it had cost the boy nothing at all, because nobody in that walk could have seen it but the boy.

He hoped the boy had seen it. He found that he hoped so quite badly, and made himself stop.

The sitting began. He opened his recording case and turned the brass glass, and wrote down exactly what was said, and nothing else.

It took him three tries to write it.

The first time he wrote *avoidance*, and struck it out, because the man had not been avoiding anything. The second time he wrote *manners*, and struck that out too, because manners were what you did with your hat and your voice, and Havel had done nothing with either. The third time he gave up on finding a word for it and simply put down what had happened, in order, as Karis would have wanted.

*Covered walk, rain, both streams jammed against the dry wall. Havel westbound with a case and the list clerk. Read clean: an officer going to a sitting, nothing aimed my way. Then, nine feet out, his near side drew in. Shoulder down and forward, a little more than an inch. He held it all the way past. He made his own stream narrower so that mine would be wider.*

*He wasn't getting out of my way. I was nowhere near his way. He was making room.*

He turned back to Havel's own page and read it through again, top to foot. The pump step. Four questions on a form and a fifth that was not on any form. The pear in its twist of paper. The recorder's case at Greyvane, and the brass glass turning at the end of the table. *He keeps going up a grade, and he keeps turning up wherever I am.* And at the very bottom, in Lira's square capitals, because she had taken the pen out of his hand one evening in the common room and written it herself:

*Careful men go up. Doesn't tell you what he's careful FOR.*

He sat with that for a while.

He still did not know who kept moving the man up, or why every move seemed to set him down in some room Cael was standing in. There were things in Havel's work he was not allowed to say. Cael had always been able to see that much, because the man wore it in his face like a collar a size too small. The walk had told him nothing about any of it.

What the walk had told him was smaller, and he had known it for two years and never quite let himself keep it. Whatever the man had been ordered, he had always done every scrap of kindness the orders left room for, and then stopped, exactly at the line, as if he had measured it beforehand with a rule. At the pump step it had been a fifth question asked in an ordinary voice. Today it had been an inch.

Cael had gone back and forth for two years over whether that deserved thanks. A part of him had always said that thanking somebody for so little was a kind of foolishness. It said that a boy who was grateful for an inch was a boy who had got used to being given nothing.

He looked at the struck-out words, and at Lira's capitals, and found that the argument was over.

*Settled, then. Room is room, whoever gives it, and however little. A man who isn't allowed to say a word to me found the one thing he was allowed to do, and did all of it, in the rain, where nobody could see it but me. I'm not going to be too proud to count that.*

Under it, smaller:

*Lira: careful for an inch, at least. Not an answer. Not nothing.*

He shut the notebook. The rain had stopped, and the light was coming back yellow off the river through the north window, and on the board by the library door somebody had chalked the evening's bouts in the Crown yard. The first was for the fifth line.

He went to the Crown yard because he always went to the Crown yard.

That was the rule he had made for the week, and it was a rule about sameness, not about bouts. Every ladder session of the season he had sat a third of the way up the east tier, in the same place, from before the first bout until after the last. If he stopped now, with eleven strangers on the bluff, his absence would be the first new thing anyone had seen him do in a month.

So he went, and sat in his place, and Brom came up after a while and sat on his left with a decent gap, as he always did, and put his forearms on his knees.

"Who's on?"

"A Force third-year and a Stone fourth-year. For the fifth line."

"Fifth line." Brom considered the boards. "That's next year's seeding."

"That's next year's seeding."

"Then they'll both be frightened," said Brom, with satisfaction, "and they'll both be careful, and it'll be very dull, and I'll enjoy it."

About a hundred and forty people had come. That was a big crowd for a bout with no crown on it, and Cael knew why, and so did everybody else on the tiers. Some had come to see the boards, and most had come to see who else came. The north tier was fuller than it ought to have been, and people kept glancing at its stair.

The fighters came out to their chalk. The Force girl was tall and narrow and very quick off the mark, and she had a habit Cael had logged in the first month. She drove her first strike of every exchange at the same height, chest-high, as if she wanted to find out at once whether the other fighter would give ground. The Stone fourth-year was square and slow, and he rooted. When he set his weight he went down into the oak like a post going into a hole, and for the length of a breath he could not be shifted by anything less than a cart.

The table called the first exchange.

The Force girl drove chest-high, and the Stone boy rooted and took it, and she slid off him and came back low, and touched him on the outside of the knee with two fingers as his weight was still going down, so lightly that it was very nearly not a touch at all.

"Force," said the table. "One."

On the tiers somebody muttered. Cael wrote nothing. He watched the three faculty at the table: the safety instructor at the end with his hand flat on the wood, the two adjudicators either side of him. The left one had called it and the right one had nodded, and the safety instructor's hand had not moved.

And while the fighters were walking back to their chalk, on the north tier, a man came up the stair.

He came up it at an ordinary pace and found a place at the rail among thirty or so others, at the end nearest the stair. He stood there with his hands at his sides, in his plain grey, looking at the boards. Nobody near him moved away, and nobody moved closer. The people on either side of him simply went very still, the way people go still beside a dog they have not been introduced to.

Cael did not look at him. He looked at the boards, and let the man sit at the very edge of his eye, where a thing is known but not watched, and he felt the corner of himself that was holding his edges go tight, like a hand on a rope.

*Hold. First.*

He held, and watched the second exchange, and made himself see all of it.

It was the Stone boy's. He let the Force girl come chest-high again, and rooted again, and this time when she slid off low he was not there. He had let his own root go a breath early and stepped across, and his open hand brushed her shoulder as she turned, for about as long as a blink. It was as light as hers had been. On the tiers the same people muttered again, the other way.

"Stone. One each."

The third exchange he watched badly, and knew it while it was happening, and could not stop it. The Force girl came in at a new height, low, testing, and the Stone boy rooted early to meet it, and for three or four breaths there was a hard close scramble on the oak, all elbows and set weight. Somewhere in the middle of it, a third of Cael's attention went up and across to the north tier on its own, without asking him, to find out whether the man in grey was watching the scramble or watching something else.

He was not, quite. His face was toward the boards. But his eyes, so far as Cael could tell at that distance, were on the table.

The scramble broke. The Stone boy had touched the girl on the hip as she came out of it, square and solid, and there was no argument about it.

"Stone," said the table. "Two to one."

Cael dragged the stray third of himself back where it belonged and was angry about it. He had never in a whole season let a fighter on the boards go unwatched in the middle of an exchange. He was going to be annoyed about that for two days, and he knew it, and he was already annoyed about knowing it.

The fourth exchange began. The Force girl had to score now or lose the line, and she knew it, and she came off her chalk faster than she had all evening. She drove chest-high, and the Stone boy rooted.

And on the north tier, the man in grey turned from the rail and went down the far stair at the same ordinary pace he had come up the near one.

The exchange was still running, the table had not lifted a hand, and nobody on the boards had touched anybody. Nothing that the bout was for had happened yet, and the man was gone.

A breath later the Force girl slid off low and touched the Stone boy on the knee again, very lightly, and the table called it hers. Two each. The bout went to a fifth, which the Stone boy won by rooting on the word and simply refusing to be moved until the girl ran out of ideas, and the tiers cheered him as if he had done something clever, which in a way he had. He would hold the fifth line into next year.

"Dull," said Brom happily, standing up. "Told you."

Cael was looking at the north stair.

"He went before it finished," he said.

"Who did?"

"The one I haven't got a page for."

Brom looked at the north tier, where the place by the stair was empty now, and then looked at Cael, and did not ask anything else. He only said, "Supper. You can think at supper. I've seen you do it."

They went down to supper late, when the hall had half emptied, and sat at the end of a long table with nobody near them.

Cael pushed his bread about and did not eat it. Brom ate steadily and waited. He was good at waiting, when it was somebody else's problem; he had learned it, he said once, from being somebody else's problem for most of his life.

"Go on, then," he said at last.

"He came up at the second exchange. He left at the start of the fourth."

"I saw."

"So why?"

"Somewhere to be," said Brom, with his mouth full.

"At the sixth bell, on a Fifth-day? A man like that, on a hill like this?"

Brom considered it, and swallowed. "No. Then he was bored."

"You'd have seen it. *I'd* have seen it. There wasn't anything to see." Cael turned the bread over. "And he didn't want to be seen going, either. If he had, he'd have gone down the near stair, past everybody, when they were all looking at him. He went down the far one when they were all looking at the boards."

"Then I've run out," said Brom. "Where was he looking?"

"At the table."

Brom stopped chewing.

He looked at Cael across the end of the long table for a moment. Then he put his spoon down in the bowl, carefully, the way he put down a thing he meant to come back to.

"You do that," he said.

"Do what?"

"Stop watching us. Partway through a bout. You stop watching the two people on the oak and you watch the three people at the table instead. I've caught you at it." He picked the spoon up again. "Not every bout. Enough of them. I used to think you'd got bored of me."

Cael opened his mouth and shut it again.

It was true. He had a whole page on it, somewhere in the second month, and he had been faintly ashamed of the page and had not looked at it since. When the safety instructor's hand came up, and when it stayed down. Whether a light touch got called in a fourth exchange as readily as in a first. Whether the table ever leaned, once it could see which way a bout was going. He had watched the table because the fighters only ever told him about two fighters, and the table told him about the whole yard.

He laid it out for Brom, slowly, as it came.

"He came up after the first call. The Force girl's touch at the knee, the light one, given to her. He'd have heard the tiers grumble about it. Then he watched the second, and the second was light too, the Stone boy's hand on her shoulder, and they gave it to him. Two light touches, one each way, both called. That's enough to see whether they hold one line for both."

"And the third?"

"The scramble. That's where it tilts. That's where you find out if the table starts calling for whoever's winning." Cael put the bread down. "He watched the table right through it. Then the fourth started, and it didn't matter any more which of them was better, so he went."

"Did they hold it?"

"They did. In the fourth, after he'd gone, the Force girl got the same light touch at the knee and they gave it to her again. He didn't stay to see it. He didn't need to. He'd watched them through the third without leaning."

"So he got what he came for," said Brom slowly.

"And left."

"That's rude."

"It's exactly what I'd have done," said Cael.

Brom looked at him for a long moment over the bowl.

"I know," he said. "That's the part I don't like."

That night, under the low lamp at his own window, he set it down.

*Fifth-line bout, sixth bell. The Archmarshal came for the table, not the fighters. Two calls, then the tilt, then gone before the result. He was checking whether this yard calls a touch the same for everybody, and whether it keeps calling it the same when it would be easy not to. The answer tonight was yes. He had it in about six minutes.*

*Brom says I do the same thing. Brom is right. I've had a page on it since the second month and been ashamed of the page.*

*So that's the first thing I know about him that isn't a blank. He reads a yard the way I read a yard. The difference is that I'm the one standing in it.*

# Chapter 47 — Rent

"Tomorrow's yours," said Brom. "Isn't it."

They were on the stair up from supper on the evening of the thirteenth, Brom a step ahead, with the lamp at the turn throwing his shadow back down over Cael's feet. He had not turned round to say it.

"It's ours. Sixth-day, fourth bell, hall three. They're walking the floors tomorrow and that's the floor that's in use at the fourth bell."

"I know whose floor it is." Brom stopped on the landing and turned, so that they stood level for once. "I mean what are you changing?"

"Nothing."

Brom considered him, and Cael could see him weighing the word the way he weighed a feed, for whether it was honest or only meant to look honest.

"Good," Brom said at last. "Neither am I. I'll be just as unpleasant as I always am. More, if anything. You've been getting soft on the left."

"There's one thing."

"There's always one thing."

So Cael told him, there on the landing, with his voice low and his back to the wall, because it was easier to say aloud to Brom than to write down. The hold ran all the time; that was its rent, and the rent he could pay, because he had been paying it for a month. But Brom's fragment, the Iron-adjacent read, had two parts, and only one of them was free. The outside of it simply ran, the coarse cool sense on his forearms of something heavy coming, from where. The inside of it was the reach, when he went down into one forearm in particular and felt its line a fraction before it landed. That was what made their drill worth doing, and since the night on the stair the reach and the hold had drawn on one purse. It was a small purse, and it had a bottom.

"So you can't have both," said Brom.

"Not for an hour. Not with my whole head in the drill. That's how I went thin last time."

"So which goes?"

"The reach."

Brom thought about that for a while, leaning on the rail.

"You'll be turning me on the frame of it," he said. "On angle and floor. Like a first-year who's been taught the shape and never felt the weight."

"Yes."

"I'll know."

"I know you'll know."

"Do you want me to say?"

Cael had thought about that at supper, and had his answer ready. "Say whatever you'd say any other week. If you'd say something, say it. If you wouldn't, don't."

"Fair," said Brom, and went on up.

Cael went to his window and wrote it under the two words at the head of the plan, so that tomorrow's version of him, tired and under a gallery, would not have to make the decision again from the beginning.

*Hall three. When the purse runs low, the reach goes first. Never the hold. Turn him on the frame: angle, floor, count in the head. Let the number come out middling. Middling is what I'm for tomorrow.*

Karis's marbled watcher log lay on the sill where she had left it for him, open at the day's page, as it had been open every evening since the bridge. *Ferry landing, two. Road's foot, two. Changed on the bell. Nothing new.* Eleven strangers had come up the bluff, and the four grey coats below it had gone on changing every four hours exactly as they had before anybody arrived, as if the delegation were weather that did not concern them. He found that steadying, and did not quite like finding it so.

Then he put down the pen and blew out the lamp and went to bed at the eighth bell, which he had not done since he was small.

Lira was on the settle in the common room when he went through it, with her leg along the cushions and a book she was not reading. She looked at the clock over the door, and then at him, with the expression of somebody watching a fighter do the sensible thing he had never in his life been known to do.

"It's the eighth bell."

"I'm going to sleep nine hours."

"On purpose?"

"On purpose."

"Good," said Lira, and turned a page she had not read.

He slept the nine hours and woke a little before the bell with his head clear and the rent behind his eyes no bigger than a coin. He ate at the second bell, early, at a time when nobody would remark on it, and went to his lecture and sat in his usual place. On the way he passed the wing, where the door onto the covered walk was shut and the young clerk stood outside it with two black boxes in his arms, waiting to be let in. Beside him, a little apart, stood the instrument woman with her oil can and her brass rag, looking at the shut door of her own document store with the patient face of somebody who has been told to come back later and fully intends to. It was not her posted day. She had come anyway, to make sure, and Cael liked her for it more than he could have explained.

---

The delegation came into the gallery a little after the fourth bell.

Cael was at the near end of the north bay with his sleeves pushed up, turning his wrists over and back while Brom fed him slow touches on the forearm. He did not look up, because he did not have to. Hall three's gallery ran along the north wall above the floor, and its boards were old, and each one had its own small voice. He heard the stair door open at the far end, and he heard people come out onto the boards and spread along the rail, and as they settled he counted the weights: a heavy one, a light one, one that stopped and came on again more slowly, saving a leg.

Seven.

He had them placed by sound before Brom had fed him three more touches. There were two by the stair door, three bunched in the middle and one a little apart from them, and the last was at the very east end of the rail, where the gallery met the wall.

Below them, on the floor, the quiet woman from the wing sat at the north rail with her board on her knee, as she sat every week, and she did not look up either.

"Ready?" said Brom.

"Ready."

"Thirty a side."

"Thirty a side."

Brom's part of it was simple: he hardened his right forearm, took one step and drove it at Cael on a count of four, at three-quarters of everything he had. Cael's part was to meet that forearm at a slant with his own and send it down past his hip into the timber. If he did it right, the floor took the force and he took none of it. Then Brom stepped back to his mark and did it again, from the same place, at the same weight, thirty times, and then they changed sides and did thirty more, and the woman at the rail made a mark for every one of them.

They had run it so often that their feet had worn a pale track into the oak. It had been the backbone of his whole second half of term, and by the eighth repetition it wanted every last scrap of him.

That was the trouble with good work, and he had only learned it a few days ago. Good work asked for all of you, and it asked so nicely. The rhythm of the drill was Brom's four-count and the turn and the step back, over and over. That rhythm pulled at Cael the way a current pulls at a swimmer's legs, gently, never hard enough to notice, only steadily and a little at a time, toward the deep part of the river where a swimmer stops choosing where he goes. *You've done this a thousand times,* it said. *Give it everything. Let go of the rest.* And under the pull, so faint that a week ago he would never have caught it, he felt the corner of himself that held his edges begin to lean toward the drill as well.

He took the corner back. It was a small hard effort, like setting his heel into a slope he was about to slide down.

Then, at the ninth repetition, he let the reach go.

He let it go on purpose and all at once, before the purse could make the choice for him. The next time Brom's forearm came in, Cael did not go down into it but let the outside of the read tell him, coarsely, *heavy, from the right, about there*, which was all it ever told him. Then he met it on the frame alone, turning on the slant he had drilled ten thousand times. He trusted the oak to take what he sent it, and he counted Brom's four in his head instead of feeling it on his skin. The turn came out a hair late and a hair rough, but it was still a turn, and the floor still took it.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

It was like writing with the wrong hand, where every letter comes out right and every one of them is work. Brom's forearm arrived where it always arrived, and Cael met it where he had been taught to meet it, and the force went down into the bearers with a dull thump. The hold sat in its corner and did not move.

Above him the gallery went on making its small sounds. Six of the seven shifted now and then, a heel lifted and set down, a weight moved from one foot to the other; twice, the whole rail creaked together, the way a rail creaks when somebody in the middle says something and the rest lean in to hear.

From the east end there came no sound at all.

Ilsev did not watch the boy.

She watched the woman at the north rail below her, the one with the board, because the woman with the board was the record. The boy was only what the record was about, and the record was the thing she had come to look at.

Under the clause, the boy had to be evaluated by demonstration before a panel once every semester, and the clause also required the institution to keep a record of the work that led up to it, term by term. The panel would not sit until the twentieth, and nobody could inspect a panel that had not yet sat. What could be inspected today was the hand that kept the record of the term. A record was as good as the hand that kept it and not one grain better. She had spent a long career finding handsome files built by careless hands, and a few plain ones built by careful hands, and she had learned to look at the hand first.

So she watched the hand.

The woman wrote with a short pencil held close to the point. She made one mark for each repetition the moment it ended, without looking down, her eyes on the floor the whole time. Most of the marks were short upright strokes. Once, on the right side, near the twentieth, the pencil made something different, and Ilsev leaned a fraction over the rail to see, and saw a small round dot.

A stroke for clean, then, and a dot for not, made at the instant. So far as Ilsev could see, nothing was ever gone back over.

She had read the term's sheets the day before, in one of the registrar's boxes, all of them, in date order. Every one was initialled, and every one bore a receiving stamp from the registrar's office dated the same afternoon as the hour it recorded, and that had told her something about the office. It had told her nothing yet about the hand. Now she had the hand in front of her, and the hand matched the paper.

She approved of that. She approved of the room as well, the bearers under the boards and the new pins in the posts along the north bay, and the chained posts at the far end. By the stair door somebody had painted a column of dates on the plaster, one for every time the floor had been surveyed. It was a well-kept room, which was not a finding; it was only the lack of a reason to look harder, which was a different thing, and she had trained herself for most of her life never to mistake the one for the other.

Two places along from her, at the east end of the rail, the Archmarshal stood with his hands at his sides.

He had not moved since he came onto the gallery, and she noticed it the way she had noticed the dates on the plaster, as a fact about the room, without wondering about it. A man stood still or he did not, and what it meant was his own business until it was written down somewhere, and nothing about it was written down anywhere she could read. She put it out of her mind and went back to the pencil.

She had once, years ago, at a district station two days' ride from anywhere, been handed four volumes of an observer's log and been asked to say whether a certain practitioner's record could be relied on. The log was beautiful, and it ran four years without one gap. Every session was there, every count, every hour, in a hand as even as print. She had read the first volume with growing pleasure, and halfway through the second she had put it down, gone out of the station into a yard full of hens, and walked round it three times before she would let herself say what she knew. No hand in the world goes four years without a gap. A sneeze, a fever, a bad night, a moment of looking out of the window: honest records are full of small holes. A hand that never missed was a hand that filled its misses in with what it supposed had happened, and called that the record, and in the end she had found the filling-in, nineteen places in four years, and the practitioner's whole file had come down with it.

Beside her, the counsel was murmuring to the eldest clerk about the timber. On the counsel's other side, Havel had his case open on the rail and was writing, as he always wrote, all of it. On the floor below, the boy turned a forearm down into the oak and then another, and the woman made her strokes.

At the bell the woman stood, clipped the pencil to the board and came toward the gallery stair, to hand her sheet on to whoever in the wing would carry it. Ilsev went down and met her at the foot.

"A question, if I may. About your board. Not about the enrollee."

The woman looked at her steadily. She was perhaps forty, with ink along the side of her middle finger, and she did not seem in the least alarmed. "Assessor."

"When you mark a repetition clean, is that your judgment at the moment it happens? Or is the board reviewed afterward and the marks settled then?"

"At the moment. I never change a mark once it's made. If I'm not sure, it's a dot."

"Is this the form you use for every supervised hour? Or is there a form for this enrollee?"

"The same form for every hour the wing witnesses." The woman turned the board so that Ilsev could see its head. "This one has the provision line, here. That's all. The columns are the same as everybody's."

"Who countersigns it? And when does it leave the wing?"

"The Magister initials every sheet at the end of the day. It crosses to the registrar the same afternoon. It's never once stayed overnight in the wing, not this term."

Ilsev nodded. She had one question left, and it was the only one she had come down the stair to ask.

"If you look away," she said, "if you miss a repetition, for any reason at all. How is that entered?"

The woman did not hesitate. "As missed. There's a mark for it, a small cross. It means I didn't see, so I don't know. I don't guess." She thought about it, honestly, with her eyes on the ceiling. "I've made eleven this term. Two of them were for sneezing."

"Thank you," said Ilsev. "That's all."

She went back up the stair. Four questions, and she did not need a fifth. She had asked nothing about the boy, because the boy had not been her question; the boy's record was, and the boy's record was this woman's hand. The last answer was the one she would write down tonight, in her own book, under the date. *Eleven crosses in a term. Two for sneezing.* A keeper with a mark for *I did not see* was a keeper you could build on, and they were rarer than anyone outside her work would believe.

On the gallery, the counsel had reached her eleventh question, which was about the age of the bearers, and Havel was writing it down. At the east end of the rail the Archmarshal stood exactly where he had stood for fifty minutes, as if a surveyor had set him there, and he had asked nobody anything.

---

On the floor, Cael had felt his own count against the woman's pencil without looking, and made the right side about twenty-seven of thirty. Then they changed sides and ran the left.

The left was worse. It always was. His left turn had come late in his training and had never been as easy as his right, and without the reach behind it every repetition felt like going down stairs in the dark, one careful foot at a time, feeling for each edge. He kept it on the frame, and he kept the hold in its corner. By the twentieth there was a fine shake starting in both forearms. Part of it was the ordinary shake of contact work, and he knew that one and did not mind it. Under it was a second, further in, which came from the purse running toward its bottom, and that one he minded very much.

At the thirty-first repetition of the left side, which was one more than the drill called for, Brom stopped.

He stopped out of turn, in the middle of stepping back to his mark, and shook out his right hand at the wrist as if it had cramped, and when he spoke it was at the exact volume a man uses to grumble about his own wrist to nobody.

"Read's off."

Cael met his eyes.

"On purpose," he said.

Brom rolled his shoulder slowly, the way he did when he was turning a thing over and did not want his face to do the turning for him. The hall was quiet except for the boards above.

"Then it's the right kind of off."

He set his feet, and drove the last one in on the four-count, and Cael turned it down into the oak.

At the bell the woman from the wing counted her marks and wrote a figure at the foot of the sheet and signed it. Fifty-four out of sixty, clean. On his worst week of the term he had made fifty-two, and on his best fifty-eight, and today's sat in the fat middle of his own numbers, where nobody reading a column of them would ever stop: a Copper-baseline redirect drill, done at Copper baseline, exactly as the record expected.

He had made every stroke of it with one hand tied behind his back, under seven people who had come all the way up the bluff to see what he was. Not one of them would ever know, which was all he had wanted from the afternoon, and now that he had it, it felt very strange in his hands.

He did not go back to the residence but went out through the side door of hall three into the yard behind it, where the porters stacked the spare bearers under a lean-to and nobody came. He sat down on the end of a bearer in the wind with the notebook on his knee.

The shake in his forearms made his letters jump. He wrote anyway, because the shake was part of what he was writing down.

*Shadow-adjacent, Bronze tier: working ledger. Day sixteen. Deployment: none.*

*Have: the hold. Fifty minutes of it, under a gallery with seven on it. No slips. It leaned toward the drill at the eighth and I hauled it back.*

*Spends: the steady drip, all day, like a lamp turned down low that never goes out. Call it half of what the gaze costs held wide. And on the floor, the reach. The reach and the hold drink out of one cup. Today the hold drank the lot, and the reach never got a drop.*

*Risk: not the drip. I can carry the drip. It's the cup. On the twentieth I'll want the reach for the hard trials, and it won't be there, because I'll have spent it keeping thick.*

*Won't share: the reach. Still. Same as the first day.*

*Good for: keeping me ordinary. That's all. It made nothing today, no skill, no number. All fifty minutes bought me was that nothing happened.*

*Fails: not today. I'm putting that down plainly, because a month from now I'll need to know whether today was luck.*

*After: an hour and a half, near enough, when reading anything feels like pressing on a bruise.*

*Net: it isn't a capability. It's rent.*

He read it through. Then, at the foot, he wrote the line the whole entry had been leaning toward. His hand did not want to write it, and he made it.

*Fifty minutes cost me the read. The twentieth's three hours.*

He sat looking at that while the wind came up off the river and found its way under his collar. Nobody came. Below the bluff the water went on as it always went on, with nobody watching it and nobody needing it to be anything except water. After a while the shake in his hands eased, and the other shake under it eased a little too, and he got up and went to find the others.

---

He went to the library to look for them, and Karis found him there first. She came in with her supper on a plate, which was against the library's rules, and which the library had stopped trying to enforce on Karis some time in the first month. She sat down opposite him at the long table under the north window.

"Bracken's clerk," she said, "writes everything down."

"I know."

"Every question the delegation puts during a facility review goes in the registrar's book, word for word, with the hour and the room. That's what the review is for. They're supposed to walk about asking." She cut a potato in half. "He told me at the hatch. He wasn't meant to tell me anything, so he said a number isn't a document. I said that was a very interesting legal position, and he went pink."

"What number?"

"Numbers." She ate half the potato. "In hall three, the counsel asked eleven questions. Mostly about timber. Ilsev, four. Every one of hers was about the woman with the board. You weren't in any of them. Havel asked two." She put down her fork. "The Archmarshal asked none."

"In hall three."

"Anywhere. Three to Gault, in the wing, this morning; the clerk doesn't know what. Then nothing. Not in the lecture range at the second bell. Not in the residence quadrangle at noon, or the fire-watch's store, or any of the three floors." She looked at him over the plate. "He went into every room they went into, and stood in it, and he asked nobody anything at all."

Cael did not answer that, and after a moment Karis let it lie and asked the other thing. "And yours? The drill."

"Fifty-four."

She thought about it with her fork in the air. "Your worst was fifty-two. Your best was fifty-eight. That's the middle of you." She looked at him more closely, the way she looked at a sum that came out right by a method she did not trust. "What did it cost?"

He turned the notebook round and showed her the last two lines. She read them, and then she put her fork down on the plate, very quietly, and did not pick it up again for a while.

"Three hours," she said.

"I know."

"Then that's what we're doing with the next six days," said Karis, as if it had been decided at a meeting he had missed. "Finding you something better than luck."

Cael sat with all of it for a long time, and with his own memory of the boards, and the two fitted together like the halves of a broken cup.

Six weights on the gallery had moved, a minute or two apart, each in its own fashion. The rail had creaked together twice, and the seventh weight had never made a sound. In fifty minutes there had not been a heel lifted, or a hand set on the rail and taken off it, or a shift from one foot to the other. Cael had followed the silence the whole hour, on the floor, with his eyes on Brom's forearm, the way a man in a noisy room keeps track, without ever meaning to, of the one corner where nobody is talking.

And that alone was not a finding, and he would not let it become one. A man might stand that still from long habit. He might stand still because the cold had got into his joints and moving hurt. He might simply have decided to, for reasons of his own. Cael wrote all three on the page and could not choose between them, so he wrote *stood still* under them and left it there, unexplained, which was the honest thing to do with it.

The questions were different. The questions were a thing he knew from the inside.

*All day he asked nobody anything. A man does that in a strange building for one of two reasons. Either there's nothing in it he cares about, or he brought his questions with him from somewhere else, already written, and he's already decided this isn't the place the answers are kept. I know the second kind. I've walked into four archives like that and never asked a librarian a single thing. And I've never once thought about what it looks like to the librarian.*

*It looks like a man who isn't there. That's the whole trick of it. I've been using it for two years, and today somebody used it on me.*

# Chapter 48 — The Ruler

At the sixth bell Cael went to the wing's counter for a floor sheet, because it was the evening he always went for one.

The counter was the plainest place on the bluff. It was a long scrubbed board with a brass rail along the front, a chained pen, a pot of sand, and a slot for slips. Behind it the desk clerk sat on his high stool with the wing's ledger open. Behind him, at the copying table under the window, the teaching assistant was writing out somebody's timetable in a small cramped hand. He did not look up when the door opened. He never looked up when the door opened. Cael had a whole term of his not looking up, on a page that said almost nothing, and the page had not needed changing all week.

"Sixteenth," said Cael. "Hall three, the fourth bell, supervised."

"Sixteenth." The desk clerk took the slip, checked the line against the ledger, and began to write. "Same as ever."

"Same as ever."

The door behind him opened again while the clerk was blotting. It was the delegation's list clerk, the one who walked beside the young man and never carried anything heavier than a sheet, and he was carrying something now. It was a flat case in grey board, tied with pink tape, with an index card slid into a brass frame on its face. Cael knew it, though he had never seen it before, the way a person knows the shape of a parcel they have been told about. The wing's compilation had gone across to the guest floor two evenings ago, and now it was coming home.

"Returned with thanks," said the list clerk to the desk clerk. "Receipt on the face."

The desk clerk signed for it in the ledger without hurry, and then the teaching assistant got up from the copying table, took the case from the list clerk with both hands and turned to set it on the shelf behind him, among the wing's other sealed things.

For about a second, as he turned, the case's face was toward the counter, upside down, four feet from Cael's hand.

Cael read it in that second because he could not have helped reading it. The wing's index card was in its frame. Under the card, across the bottom of the face, the delegation's receiving stamp had been pressed in blue, square and clear, with the date of the twelfth and a set of initials beside it. The pink tape was tied in the wing's own flat knot, the knot he had seen on every sealed thing that ever left that office, tied back exactly as it had come. And on the whole of the face there was nothing else. There was no slip pinned to it. There was no pencilled query in a margin and no second stamp. Nothing anywhere said *see me* or *noted* or *refer*.

Then the case was on the shelf with its back to the room, and the teaching assistant was at his copying table again, writing out the timetable in the small cramped hand, and the second was over.

Cael took his floor sheet from the desk clerk and said thank you, and did not move from the counter, because the inner door to the back of the wing had opened and Gault had come through it.

He had a folder under one arm. He saw Cael, and stopped at the end of the counter, and stood there in the way he stood everywhere, heavy and still, as if moving were a thing he did only on purpose.

"Enrollee."

"Magister."

"I'll tell you this once, so that you don't have it some other way." Gault's voice had not gone up or down by a hair since the first morning of term, and it did not now. "The Archmarshal put three questions to me in the wing this morning. All three were about the resistance posts. How often they're calibrated. Who does it. What it's checked against." He shifted the folder under his arm. "He did not mention you."

He was out through the far door before Cael had an answer ready. Gault said what he had come to say and then left, and somewhere in the second month Cael had learned to hear the leaving as the last word of the sentence.

---

Lira was on the settle when he came in, with her leg up along the cushions and the safety instructor's leather strap looped round her foot. She was easing the hip into a stretch a hair's width at a time, with her jaw set, and she did not stop when she saw his face.

"Sit," she said. "You look like you've been handed a bill."

He sat on the floor by the cold grate, where he could see her without her having to turn, and he told her about the posts first, because the posts were simpler: three questions, all about how often the resistance posts were calibrated, who did it, and against what. Not one word about him.

Lira let the strap go slack. She looked at the ceiling for a while.

"He's checking the beam," she said.

"The what?"

"The weighbeam." She pulled the strap again, gently. "At the foundry, before every pour, the master had them prove the beam. There was a set of brass weights in a box with a lock on it, and only he had the key, and before anybody put a single ingot on the beam he'd hang the brass on it and see what it said. My second year there, a beam had been filed. Just a little, on the pivot. Some man had been selling us short for months, and nobody knew, because everybody trusted the beam." She shifted the leg and winced and went on. "After that the master wouldn't believe any number at all until he'd watched the beam proved. Not even his own."

Cael sat very still on the hearthstones.

He thought of every hour he had worked under the wing that term, and of every figure the quiet woman had sent across to the registrar in her neat hand on the same afternoon, and each one of those figures had been made on some post at some setting. He thought of his baseline in the second month, the entry demonstration that had set the measure for his whole provision, taken against plates with their dates of calibration painted on the housings. He thought of the twentieth, when Gault's panel would lay his new numbers beside his old ones and say whether he had grown. And he thought of the posts in hall three, every one of them re-pinned this term after a pin had sheared in the north bay at the seventh setting, with Cael's own forearm the first thing it hit. Was a post with a new pin the same post? Did it read the same? Who said so?

The instrument woman said so. A woman with an oil can, who came on the posted day of every month and initialled a yellowed card by Gault's door that nobody had ever told her to keep. And this morning she had stood outside her own shut store, not on her posted day, to make sure.

"He's not weighing me," Cael said slowly. "Not yet. He's weighing the thing that weighs me."

"That's what I said."

"By the twentieth he'll know what a number out of that wing is worth. Before I've done a thing. The price will be on it before I'm on the floor."

"Good," said Lira. "Then it'll be the right price." She let the strap go and lay back against the arm of the settle, breathing out slowly through her teeth. "What's the other thing? There's another thing. You've got two faces on."

So he told her about the case.

He told her how it had turned on the shelf for a second, upside down, four feet from his hand. He described the blue receiving stamp, the date of the twelfth, the initials, and the wing's own flat knot tied back exactly as it had gone out. There had not been a pencilled mark on it anywhere, not a slip, not a query. The whole term's filings from the copying table had been read at the very top of the delegation, and handed back with nothing written on them at all.

Lira understood it faster than he had.

"So your man at the copying table writes things about you that don't hurt you," she said. "That's what we've thought, all term."

"Yes."

"And the big one reads every word of it and puts no mark on it either."

"Yes."

"And the day the big one does write something—" She stopped, and looked at him, and he watched her see the rest of it. "Then it doesn't matter what the small one wrote. Does it."

"No."

The fire was not lit. The common room was cold, and quiet, and somewhere above them Brom was doing his exercise for the night by lamplight, slowly, with the floorboards giving now and then under his weight.

"Everything sits on one man," said Cael. "And he's the one I can't read."

Lira was quiet for a long moment.

"Then don't read him," she said at last. "You've tried. You've told me what you got. Nothing." She looked at the ceiling again, where Brom's board creaked. "Read what he does. You've already started. A stair, a rail, three questions. That's not nothing. That's a page."

He wrote it at the window when she had gone up, in fewer words than he had said it.

*Gault: three questions, all about the posts. Lira says he's proving the beam before the pour. She's right. Every measurer I've ever had began with the boy. Not him. This one started with the ruler.*

*The case came back to the wing with his stamp on it and nothing else. Seln writes true lines about me that can't hurt me. Then the Archmarshal takes them, reads every line, and hands them back clean. That holds exactly as long as he goes on not marking them, and not one day longer.*

*Everything sits on one man, and he's the one I can't read. So, as Lira says, I'll read what he does.*

The other entry, the one he had been circling for days, he would not write at the window at all.

After supper he went up to the Crown yard and climbed to the top tier, where the benches were narrowest and nobody ever sat except on final days. The yard was empty. The porter and his boy were down on the boards with a broom and a pail of damp sawdust, scattering the sawdust in an arc, sweeping it up again with the day's dust in it, and moving along a yard to do it again, so that they worked their way slowly from the north chalk toward the south.

Cael sat at the east end of the top tier with the notebook on his knee.

He had been circling it for three days. It was not a fact. It was a way of sorting facts, and he had been afraid that if he set it down too soon it would set hard in the wrong shape. Now he had a rail, a stair, a case and three questions, and the shape had stopped moving.

*Four of them, now. I've been watched by four kinds of people since my Kindling. I think I can name the kinds.*

*Coss enforced. His reports were arguments, and he'd chosen how every one of them ended before he picked up the pen. I could beat him, in the end, because I could always see the ending coming at me from the first page.*

*Havel complied. He writes down what's said, all of it, exactly, and nothing more. His pages are honest because they're small. Whatever he thinks about any of it, he doesn't put it on the paper they gave him.*

*Seln operates. His pages are built to be read by somebody else, in a particular way, and every line of them is true and placed. I've watched him place people for a whole term. I'd be a fool to think he doesn't place sentences.*

*This one evaluates.*

He stopped there for a long time. Below, the porter's boy had got sawdust in his shoe and was hopping on one leg on the boards while the porter waited, leaning on the broom.

*He doesn't write to win, like Coss. He doesn't write what he's told, like Havel. He doesn't arrange, like Seln. He writes questions. He takes a thing apart to see whether it was built true. He looked at a yard and wanted to know if the table held its line. He looked at a wing and wanted to know who checks the posts. He read a term of filings about me and tied them back up and wrote nothing on them, and I don't yet know what that means. It might mean nothing. I'm not allowed to decide it means something just because I'd like it to.*

*He'll get to me. He's started with the building I'm standing in. I know the method because it's mine. I've never seen anybody else use it. I've never once had it used on me.*

*The Compact finally sent someone who takes notes the way I take notes.*

He did not write anything under it. He sat on the narrow bench and let the notebook lie shut on his knee, and looked down at the boards.

In five days the porter would chalk two lines on that oak, forty feet apart, and Lira would walk out to one of them on a hip that would be ten days old by then, and Brom would walk out to the other with Rooke's words in pencil on the outside of his wrap. Karis would be on the east tier with the tied batons in her lap, holding them as if they were a document. And Cael would be in his usual place, a third of the way up, knowing more about what was happening on those boards than anybody else in the yard, and holding his own edges the whole time, in front of the eleven, without anyone knowing that either thing was happening.

The day after that he would stand on a sprung oak floor for three hours in front of a panel, and the man who had started with the ruler would be somewhere in the gallery.

He found that he was not frightened of the twentieth, exactly. He was frightened of the second hour of it, when the cup the hold and the reach both drank from would be running low, and there would still be an hour to go. That was new, and he did not like it, and he noted it, as he noted everything, so that he could look at it again when he was less tired.

The porter and the boy finished the boards and started on the bottom tier, and he watched them work their way up, bench by bench, until the porter looked up and saw him there against the sky.

"Locking at the eighth, Enrollee," the porter called up. "Unless you mean to sleep there."

"Coming," said Cael, and closed the book, and went down past the sawdust.

Nobody sits down to explain how to change a thing that has already died. The drafters had known there were assay enrollments; they had known there were practitioners standing on that clause in the very year they sat down to write, because they had sat down and written out what was to be done with them. They had not killed the practice. They had cut a door out of it, and posted a sign telling everyone to use the door, and left the clause standing exactly where it was.

"They discouraged it," said Withrow. "They gave it new words. They built a way out of it. And they left it standing. Every one of those was a decision, made by people holding pens. "Nobody drafts a conversion route for a dead letter."

At the end of the academy's side, Karis's pen had begun to move again. From behind her shoulder Cael could see her page. Her handwriting was the most level thing he knew. At the transitional article it had gone off at a slant, as if the page had tilted under it, and it had not come back.

Cael watched every attempt with the gaze opened as far as he dared to open it. After three days of the Archmarshal giving him nothing, she gave him everything, and it was like drinking water. Under pressure her rhythm did not break; it tightened, like a rope taking a load. She did it before the attempt through the directive's preamble, and before the one through custom, and again before the one through the plain meaning of the word *existing*, which Withrow's counsel answered in two sentences without once looking up. By the third he could have leaned forward and whispered to the back of Bracken's head what was coming next, a breath before she knew it herself.

He kept still. Nobody had asked him, and the chair he had been given was against a wall for a reason.

The last attack broke. The counsel sat back and said, "Nothing further on repeal, Chancellor," in exactly the tone she had used to praise the index, and Withrow inclined her head. Did the registrar's seal cover the clerk, or only the registrar? Bracken had a sheet in a box for every one of these questions. It was slow, and it was necessary, and there was nothing in it anybody could watch.

And it was in the slack of that stretch, between one sheet and the next, that the hold went thin.

He did not feel it go. That was the whole trouble with it, and he knew it, and it still caught him. One moment he was listening to a clerk explain a seal. The next, with no seam between them, the clerk was saying something else, and there was a short piece of the sitting he could not account for, as if somebody had turned a page in his head while he was not looking. He took the corner back at once. Nobody had looked his way. The inky-knuckled clerk on the next chair was writing.

A little later, during a question about the date on the comparing clerk's sheet, it happened again. It was shorter this time, two breaths, perhaps three, and again he only caught it from the far side, after it was over.

A floor had never done this to him. On a floor his attention was either all in the drill, or held back from it by main force, and he had learned in a corridor how to hold it back. A sitting was different. A sitting took his attention in long slow pulls and then let it go, and in the slack between the pulls there was nothing to brace against, and that was where he went, in the gaps, the way a sleeper goes under in the pause between one breath and the next.

He sat very straight on the hard chair and set the stake again. He wrote nothing, because there was nowhere he could write that would not be seen.

At the end of the attestation the delegation's counsel looked up at the clock over the door.

"Chancellor, with your leave," she said. "A recess."

# Chapter 49 — Live on an Active File

Recesses were where findings went wrong, in Ilsev's experience, so she did not take them.

People talked in recesses. They stood at windows with cups and told each other what the morning had meant, and by the time the sitting began again, half of them had agreed on a meaning that nobody had read anywhere. She had seen it happen in a hundred halls, and so when the counsel rose and went out talking, with two of the clerks trailing after her, and the academy's people drifted to the far windows to stretch, Ilsev stayed in her chair and opened the file she had asked for.

She had asked for it at the first bell, before anyone else was in the room. The afternoon would come round to what the clause required term by term, and that touched the monitoring of the enrollee, and the monitoring was in the routing file. Havel had nodded and written the request in his ledger, and at the recess he brought it down the table and laid it at her elbow, square to the edge, and went back to his place at the foot without a word. She liked that about him. He never told you he had done what you asked. He simply did it, and let the doing speak.

The routing file was a long narrow ledger bound in grey, its pages ruled in faded red. Every request, transmittal and transfer that had ever touched the subject's file stood in it, one to a line, in a dozen hands over two years. She went down it from the top, because the top was where it began.

A district office, a first filing, a monitoring class she had seen on that file before and never on any other. A records request in an autumn, with the designation sitting in its priority field, two grades above anything the request's own signatory could have entered. Greyvane: the delegation, the hearing, her own query, the return, her referral. A transfer to this house, months after, entered in a hand she did not know. And at the very end, the delegation's own requisition for the wing's filings, in the counsel's clerk's round letters, and their return to the wing the evening before.

Behind the ledger, in a pocket of the back board, was the file's correspondence. Most of it was ordinary paper: acknowledgements, a transfer notice, a slip from a district clerk apologising for a smudge. At the very back, under a brass clip, lay a single folded sheet with the registry's stamp on it. Pinned to it was a narrow docket in Havel's hand: *Correspondence in. Delegation's courier, up. Eighth bell.* It had come up the bluff the evening before.

She noted the hour on the docket. The eighth bell. Havel had had it in his hands last night, and had not brought it to her door, or laid it on her corner this morning. He had put it in its place in the file and let her come to it in her own order. She approved of that, and did not stop to ask herself why it pleased her quite so much.

She knew what it was. She unfolded it anyway, because knowing what a thing is had never once been the same, in her life, as having read it.

*Return, from the chain of review, to the senior evaluation seat, on referral of the matter at Greyvane. Designation: Suppression-Advisory Watch, Priority Level 4.*

*Determination: no originating authority of record. Designation predates file creation. No action available at this clearance.*

She turned the sheet face down on the table and squared it to the edge with two fingers. She sat for a moment looking at its blank back. Then she turned it face up again, which was a thing she had never in her life done to a document before, and did not do again.

---

She had met the first two sentences before.

At Greyvane, on a Saturday after the fifth bell, they had come to her on a sheet laid square on the corner of a long table. *No originating authority of record; designation predates file creation.* That had been the answer to her query, which had been the plainest question her form allowed: who had entered this mark, and on whose authority? She had read the answer twice. Then she had done what the rules asked of a senior evaluator in that position. She had written a referral and pinned the return to it. She had marked it an irregularity of administration, and sent the whole thing up on the Monday courier, to the office that sat above the office that had answered.

She had received such paragraphs before. Twice in her career a mark she could not place had turned out to be a district's old shorthand, carried over from a roll the registry had retired before she was born. Both times a polite letter had come back from somewhere above, explaining it in six lines, with a reference to the old roll. Both times she had been faintly embarrassed not to have known, and had written the old shorthand into the back of her own book so as never to ask again.

She had pictured that office, a little, while she wrote. A room with better lamps. Older men, with longer memories, who would know some convention from before her time, some old mark carried over from a system long since replaced. They would write back a paragraph. She would read the paragraph and think *of course*, and put it in the file, and forget the whole thing by the end of the week.

That had been eleven months ago.

And here were the better lamps. They had read her referral and the return pinned to it, and they had sent back the same two sentences, word for word. Then they had added a third, the way a man adds a latch to a door he has already shut.

She took the three sentences one at a time, as she would have taken three witnesses.

The first witness said that nobody had done it. That was not possible on its face. A mark in the registry was not rain; it did not fall on a file out of the sky. Somebody entered it, and entering was an act, and the whole reason the registry existed was so that an act could be traced to a hand. Anybody with standing could ask who did this, and be told. She did not hold that as a belief. Every finding she had ever put her name to stood on it.

The second witness said the mark was older than the file it was written in, and that was the one she could not get round.

Ink goes onto a sheet. The sheet does not grow up round the ink, so either the file had been made over at some time, and the mark carried across from an older file, in which case the older file was the true record, and her question had been sent to the wrong door twice. Or else the second witness meant precisely what it said. There had been a value in that field before there was any field to hold it.

She took her working sheet from her case and wrote on it, small:

*Misrouted, or no answer at all. Both go up. Neither is mine to settle.*

There was a next thought. She could feel its edge, just past the end of the line, the way your foot feels the edge of a stair in the dark. She did not take it. She knew she was not taking it, and she was quite comfortable with that.

She had known a man once, early in her service, who had been right about a thing a full year before he could prove it. He had said so in writing, because he was sure, and he had been right, and when the proof came at last it proved him right in every particular. He had spent the rest of his career being right about it in a much smaller office. She had learned more from him than from anyone, and she had never once been tempted to be him. A mark in a registry with no author anyone could name was a thing. Things like that were slips, or they were somebody's practice. She had not one line of paper that told her which, and a guess was not a finding just because the guesser was senior.

---

The third witness said: *and you may not ask.*

*No action available at this clearance.*

She had left that one till the end on purpose. Whatever she did next would turn on it, and she wanted the other two already weighed before she came to it.

It was a well-made sentence. She could admire it as work, even while it was being done to her. It was courteous. It was final. It asked nothing of the person who received it, and it cost that person nothing to accept. It had plainly been made to be accepted. Most people who received it would read it, and nod, and lay it in the file, and go to stand at the window with the others and a cup. And by every rule she knew they would be right to.

She read it a second time for its grammar, which was a habit nobody had ever been able to cure her of. It did not say that no action existed. It said that none was *available*, and then it said to whom: to whoever held the clearance it had been sent to. Somewhere, then, the sentence admitted, there was a clearance at which some action was available. It simply was not hers. She did not let herself go on to wonder whose it was. She only noted, in the margin of her working sheet, *available: at this clearance*, and underlined the last three words once, because they were what the sentence actually said.

It left her the same choice it left everybody. She could take it as her answer, note it in the file, and go on with the afternoon. Or she could send it up again.

The hall had nearly emptied round her. Somewhere behind her a clerk was moving chairs that did not need moving. One of the registrar's young men came along the table with a tray, and set a cup of tea at her elbow without a word, and went away. She did not look at it.

Then the date and reference of the sheet in front of her. Then the return's working words, written out in full, every one, with nothing put into her own words. A question that has been put into somebody's own words can be answered by answering their words instead of the question, and she had watched whole matters die of exactly that. Then, in the box at the foot headed *grounds for escalation*, one sentence. She wrote it without a pause, because she had known it since she reached the third line of the return.

He stopped, the way a man stops at the end of a column. Then, in exactly the same voice, he added one more line.

The counsel did not answer at once. Cael watched her forearms on the table. Her weight did not come forward. It stayed exactly where it was.

"Then I have what I need." Ilsev capped her pen.

# Chapter 50 — A Working Method

Bracken was in the back room when Cael got there, which he had not expected, with his coat off and his waistcoat unbuttoned and a founding-era volume open across his forearm like a waiter's napkin.

Karis was at the long press against the far wall. She had her sleeves pushed above her elbows and ink to the second joint of every finger, and she was sliding volumes into their places one at a time, reading each pencilled shelf-mark on the inside board before she let go of it. She did not look round when the door opened, and she did not have a pen in her hand, and Cael realised that he could not remember the last time he had seen her without one in the whole of the term.

"Enrollee," said Bracken. "Good. Take the low shelves. I can't bend the way I could, and Miss Karis won't let me near the high ones in case I put something back in the wrong century."

So Cael took the low shelves, and for a while the three of them worked without talking, in the smell of old paper and lamp oil and the beeswax Bracken rubbed into his box corners. It was the kind of quiet Cael had grown used to in carrels and archives over the past year, where nobody needed to say anything because everybody was doing what the others would have done.

Bracken broke it, which surprised him.

"I'll tell you a thing I didn't know until today," said the registrar. He was holding a volume up to the lamp to read its board. "Twelve years I've had this office, and I've always believed the best day a file can have is the day you close it. You tie the last box. You write the count on the lid. It's done, and it's right, and you go home." He slid the volume into its place and squared it with one finger. "It isn't. The best day a file can have is the day somebody who wishes it ill opens every box, with a good mind and a whole morning, and can't find anything wrong."

"She said your index was good," said Cael.

"She did." Bracken did not smile, quite. "I've had my work praised by people who liked me. It's pleasant. I'd never before had it praised by somebody who'd come all the way up the river hoping to dislike it." He reached for the next volume. "It's a different thing altogether. I find I'm too old to know what to do with it."

He worked a while longer, and then he took the last empty box under his arm, looked once along the press with the air of a man counting soldiers, and went to the door.

"Lock up behind you, Miss Karis. The key's on the nail. Not later than the eighth bell, please, or the porter will think we've been robbed and wake the whole range."

They heard his steps go away down the records hall, slow and even, and the outer door close.

Karis slid a volume home and did not let go of it at once.

"Did you see my writing?" she said, without turning round.

"From the wall. At the transitional article."

"I thought you might have." She took her hand off the spine. "I could feel it go. Like a table tipping. I tried to straighten it and it wouldn't straighten, so I left it." She turned round at last. "It isn't a finding. I want you to know that before I say anything. Nothing I'm about to say would stand up in that hall."

"All right," said Cael.

On the long table lay the one book that was not a founding volume. It was the directive, all forty-one pages of it, the copy Bracken's clerk had made and a librarian had sealed, and down its fore-edge stood a row of small green paper tabs.

Karis opened it without sitting down.

"You know the green ones," she said. "Bracken put them in. One for each place in the whole directive where it takes an old word out of the law and puts a new word in." She ran her finger down the four of them, just above the paper. "And this one," she turned to a page with a white slip pinned to it and a thread of red through the slip, "is the transitional article. Havel read it out this morning. It's what won."

She laid one forefinger on the last green tab and the other on the white slip, and held them there, as people hold two fingers on a map to measure a road.

"Count the articles between," she said.

He leaned over and counted, and it did not take long.

"Four."

"Four." Every word came out level and even, as though somebody else had written them and she was only the one reading them out. "One directive. One drafting. As near as anybody could ever say, one table of men with the same ink. On this side of my finger is the article that tells every academy how to take enrollment by demonstration and change it into something else. It doesn't end the practice; it walks everybody out of it. And on that side of my finger is the last of the articles that take old words away."

Cael said nothing, because he had seen where she was going as soon as she touched the tabs, and he wanted to hear her go there in her own way and at her own pace, without any help from him.

"You've got it in your old notebook," said Karis. "In a box you drew round it when you were younger and didn't know what it meant. I've got it twice in mine. We read all three copies aloud to each other at Greyvane, in Prynn's archive, at the reading shelf, a few days before the whole of that archive went under the Compact's seal. Do you remember the later hand? The black ink that didn't cross its sevens?"

"*Per standardization directive*," said Cael. "It didn't say which."

"It didn't say which, because nobody writing it ever thought there would be a question of which. There's only one." She tapped the green tab, very lightly, with the nail of her finger. "It's this one. So the article that told that clerk to change the word is one of these four. It's here, in this book, on this table, four articles from the article we won on this morning."

She lifted her fingers off the page and gripped the table's edge with both hands.

"*Shattered* was written in at that table, by somebody holding a pen, over an older word. And the older word meant unbound. Four articles along, somebody took the old system's only way of judging a person by what that person could actually show, instead of by which box they would go into, and turned it into a road out of itself, and put a sign up telling everybody to use the road." Her hands had tightened on the wood. "And the old way works. That's what I keep coming back to. It isn't a museum piece. It's working tonight, in this house, on the record, with seals on it. I sat at that table from the second bell to the seventh and watched it bear weight."

He waited until he was sure she had finished.

"And you're not saying they're connected."

"I'm not saying anything about them at all." It came out quick and firm, like a hand coming down on a lid, and there was nothing frightened in it. "I haven't got a single sheet that joins them. I've no drafter's name for either article. There's no minute and no memorandum. There isn't one letter from a man at that table to another man at it. I've got two articles in one book with four articles between them, and that's all I've got." She looked down at the green tabs. "I know what that looks like. It looks like a pattern. And a pattern with no paper under it was the warden's whole method. I watched him build a case for two years out of things that looked alike, and I watched you beat him by reading what was actually written. I won't do it his way the same week we won by doing it ours. Two articles in one directive, four apart, is nothing to stand on. It's a coincidence with good posture."

"Then what have you got?" said Cael.

Karis did not answer at once. She reached into the satchel that hung on the back of a chair, and took out the grey notebook, and opened it, and turned it round on the table to face him.

It was a fresh page, and at the top of it, in her small upright hand, which had come back level, there was one line. Under the line the whole page was empty, to the foot.

*Why retire a working method?*

"I wrote it this afternoon," she said, "while the clerks were reading the list. It goes on a page by itself, with nothing under it. That way, whenever I open the book, I'll see it's still waiting, and I won't be tempted to fill the page with things I'd like to be true."

He read the line three times over and said nothing.

"That's all of it," said Karis. "I'm not asking who did it. I'm not asking about the word. I'm not asking any of the things I'd give a year of my life to know. I'm asking one thing. Somebody turned it into a door and walked everybody out through the door, and nobody, anywhere in forty-one pages, wrote down a reason." She closed the notebook. "I don't know the answer. I don't know where I'd begin to look. The archive that held the other half of it is under the Compact's seal at Greyvane, and nobody alive can tell me whose hand held the black ink. I've got one line on one page."

"Do you think it's something?" he said. "Or nothing?"

Karis put the notebook back in the satchel and buckled it before she answered.

"I think I proved something today," she said. "A real thing, with seals on it, that will go on being true whether I'm here or not. I'm going to go to bed holding that.

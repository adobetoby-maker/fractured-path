## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|

# Chapter 44 — No Tells

On the tenth of Reaping, the morning after the file was closed, the woman who kept the wing's instruments came into the wing's outer office with her oil can and her brass rag at the third bell, as she did on the posted day of every month, and checked the instruments.

Cael was at the counter for a floor sheet and watched her do it. She took the long measuring rods down from their rack one at a time and sighted along each against the window. She opened the case of the pressure gauge and wiped the dial and wound it, and tapped the glass with one fingernail, and listened. She set the little brass weights from the wing's balance in a row on a cloth and touched each one with a clean rag. Then she took a printed card from her apron, the wing's schedule of checks, which had been pinned on the wall by Gault's door since the first week of term and had gone yellow at the corners. She initialled the day's line in pencil, and went away to her next room.

Nobody had told her to do it today, and nobody had told her not to. It was the posted day.

That was how the days went, from the closing of the file on the ninth to the eve of the twelfth. Cael watched them the way he watched a bout from the tier, without blinking at the wrong moment.

Nothing moved. That was all, and every bit of it was hard. A building full of frightened people, waiting for eleven strangers and a man with no stated business, did not want to stand still. It wanted to sweep its passages and re-ink its ledgers and move its awkward things out of sight, the way a household scrubs the front step before a visitor it is afraid of. Cael saw the want everywhere. He saw a Stone instructor stop in the second quadrangle with a broom she had picked up from somewhere, and look at it, and look at the flagstones, and then lean the broom carefully back against the wall where she had found it and walk away with her hands in her pockets. He saw two clerks from the bursar's office arguing in low voices at the foot of the lecture stair about whether the lamp-oil ledger ought to be copied fair before the twelfth. Then one of them said *posted day*, and the other said *posted day*, and they went back up the stair together without copying anything.

The final stayed on the nineteenth, and the evaluation stayed on the twentieth. Rooke's cohort worked its ordinary hours at its ordinary marks in the east hall, and the supervision sheets went across the quadrangle to the registrar the same afternoon they were signed, as they had gone every afternoon since the night of the notice. The fire-watch boy stood on his bucket at the covered walk's north arch each morning and chalked the night's hours on his slate in his flat-footed figures. Withrow walked once round the first quadrangle at the fifth bell with her spectacles in her hair, as she had walked it every day of the season, and stopped at the same place under the clock to speak to the same porter about the same leaking gutter. In the registrar's outer office the four grey boxes sat on their shelf with their lettering turned to the room, and nobody touched them.

Withrow had told five hundred people not to perform. Then the whole of Halcenvane went three days without performing, the ninth and the tenth and the eleventh, and it was, Cael thought, standing at the covered walk's rail on the evening of the eleventh, the hardest feat of athletics he had watched all season. Lira on her table of oak had held still for a minute with a leg on fire. This was a whole hill holding still for three days with nothing on fire at all, and it was harder, because there was no sand running and no crowd to see it.

He wrote it that night, the eve, by the low lamp.

*Three days of a whole hill not doing anything. It's harder than doing something. I know, because not doing anything is what I've done on my own account for two years, and I've never once seen anybody else try it. Withrow's bet is that a visit can only see what's been changed for it. So she's changed nothing, and made five hundred frightened people change nothing with her. I didn't expect the first person I ever watched play my own game to be a chancellor, and play it better.*

*Bumps: one, in hall three, the one Brom caught. Otherwise none for three days, and I've paid for every one. I know now what free feels like. Free is the thing to be frightened of.*

*They come tomorrow. Final in eight days. The twentieth, nine.*

Havel rode up the bluff road in the second carriage, facing backward, with the counsel's knees an inch from his own and the counsel talking.

She had talked since Ostrand bridge and, if he was honest, most of the way from the coast, four days of it. Her voice was low and pleasant and never rose and never stopped, and it went on about the inns, and the state of the road, and a case she had argued nine years before in which a schedule had turned out to have two different page eleven. She did not need answers. She needed somebody to aim at, and Havel, facing backward with his case on his knees, was the nearest thing in the carriage with ears. He had learned by the second day to nod at the end of her sentences and think about something else in the middle of them.

Beside the counsel, Assessor Ilsev sat with her own case on her own knees and her eyes shut, though she was not asleep. Havel had sat beside her for four days now, and through the whole of the Greyvane hearing before that, and he had never once seen her asleep. She shut her eyes in carriages the way other people shut a book they had finished with, so that nobody would ask her what she thought of it.

The carriage leaned into the first bend of the bluff road, and Havel looked out of the window at the river falling away below.

He did it properly. He had been trained to arrive at a place properly, and he did it now out of a habit so old it no longer felt like a decision. First the road behind them: the bridge with its toll board, the town, the ferry landing on the near bank with its lamp post and two men in road-grey coats standing under it. He noted the two men. He noted them the way you note your own service's coats in a strange town, briefly and without staring, and looked away again, because a records officer who stares at a rotation from a carriage window is a records officer who will be asked by somebody, later, why. Then the road ahead: the bends, the wall, the gate. Then the slope of the hill itself, the roofs above the wall, the long range of grey stone along the top of the bluff with its tiers of seating, open to the sky, standing up against the morning like the side of a ship.

The Crown yard. The academy's papers had a plan of it, and he had studied the plan on the road.

He knew who would be up there.

He had known it since the coast. He had sat at the end of the delegation's table at Greyvane through every sitting, with his recorder's case open and his brass glass turning, and written down every word the boy said. That had included all of the schedule the boy had read aloud in a level voice that stopped at the end of every entry for exactly as long as a pen needed. Before that there had been a cold bright morning in the Unranked District at Ardenmere, a pump step, a form with four questions on it, and a fifth question that had not been on any form. And a pear, held out in a twist of paper, that he had wanted and had not taken. He still did not know why he had not taken it. He had thought about it more often since than a man ought to think about a pear.

A boy like that, on a morning like this, would want to see the delegation come in. He would want to see it from somewhere legitimate, where nobody could say he had no business to be. He would want a clean line down the last of the road and across the courtyard. He would want to be high, and empty, and early.

The upper tier of the Crown yard, then. The east end of it, most likely, where the morning sun would be behind him.

Havel looked at the plan of it in his head for a moment longer. Then he decided not to look up there.

It was a small decision, and he made it the way he made most of his decisions now, quickly and without enjoying it. A man who looks straight at the place where he knows someone is standing has told that person something: that he knew, and that he thought about it on the road, and that the knowing mattered to him. Havel did not want to tell the boy any of that. He was not sure it was true. He was not sure it was his to tell. And there was a correct way to arrive at an inspection, which was to look at the building, and the door, and the officer waiting at the door, and the ground. He would do it the correct way.

The carriage came up the last bend, and leveled, and went in under the gate.

He had risen twice since the pump step, and he thought about that sometimes in the evenings, the way a man counts coins he does not mean to spend. The first rise had come not long after Ardenmere. He had been taken off the district rota and given a recorder's case and a seat at the end of hearing tables, where the work was to write down exactly what was said, and nothing else, and to be thought careful. He had been thought careful. The second had come after Greyvane, and it was the one that had put him in this carriage. Records officer to a delegation was a grade that sat at the main table and not the end of it. It was a grade that handled the delegation's papers before the senior seat saw them.

Neither rise had come with an explanation, because promotions in the service did not. A sheet arrived with a new grade on it and a date, and you moved your desk.

Some things on the boy's file stood above his clearance, and he did not think about those in carriages. He had a notebook with a cardboard cover in his inside pocket, and in it, among dates that meant nothing, there were three short entries in pencil that he had written over two years and did not read in front of anyone. He did not take it out now, since there was nothing to add to it this morning. There might never be. A careful man kept such a notebook the way he kept a spare button sewn inside his coat: not because he expected to need it, but because when you needed it, there would be no time to sew.

---

The carriage stopped.

The counsel was still talking as she gathered her gloves. Havel opened the door for her and got down first and held it, and she came down the step after him, talking. On the cobbles of the outer courtyard she stopped, and looked up at once at the roofline of the long building in front of her, the one with the stone hood over its door.

"That gutter," she said to the clerk who had come across from the first carriage, "has been mended three times, and badly, and the third mend is newer than the second. Write it down."

The clerk wrote it down, and Havel did not look at the gutter.

He looked at the courtyard. He took it in the way he had been taught, the corners first and the centre last, because the corners are where people stand who do not want to be in the centre. The north-west corner, with a water butt and a boy sitting on an upturned crate with a bucket at his feet. The north-east, where the covered walk came out under an arch, and a knot of perhaps twenty students were standing about with the elaborate unconcern of people who had found a reason to be crossing a courtyard at exactly this hour. The south corners, empty, swept, with the morning sun lying across the cobbles. Then the door of the long building, under its stone hood, and the man waiting at it: a man in a dark coat with ink on one cuff and a pen behind his ear, standing very straight, with a younger clerk at his elbow. The registrar, Havel thought; he had read the name in the papers. Then the ground, the cobbles between the carriages and the door, which were clean and dry and had not been swept that morning, because the dust along their edges was a day old.

He did not look at the long grey wall of the Crown yard rising above the courtyard's east side. He did not look at the tiers along the top of it, or at the east end of the upper tier where the sun was. He knew exactly where it was. He kept it at the very edge of the side of his eye, where a thing is not looked at but is still known, and he kept his face toward the door.

It was harder than it sounded. Every instinct a records officer owns runs the other way, toward looking, toward the high place and the far corner and the window with a shape behind it. He had learned at a desk by a window, with a manual open in front of him, that a correct officer and a careful one are not always the same man. A correct officer looks at everything he can see. A careful one decides which of the things he can see he is going to be seen seeing. For most of his life Havel had been correct, and been praised for it. For some time now he had been trying, with no instructor and no manual, to be careful as well, and he was still not sure that he was any good at it.

His right hand ached in the web between the thumb and the first finger, as it always did at the end of a long road, from years of pens. He flexed it once against the handle of the case and kept walking.

Behind him the second carriage creaked as Ilsev got down. He heard her feet on the step and then nothing, because she had stopped on the cobbles and was standing still, as she always did when she arrived anywhere, while everything round her moved. She did not need help with her case. Nobody offered any. In four days Havel had never once seen anybody offer, and he suspected that the last person to try had been very young.

Then the third carriage.

Havel did not turn round for it, because he did not need to. He heard the clerks from the first carriage go across the cobbles toward it at a quick walk. He saw, without looking straight at him, the ranking man of the escort move four paces to his left without anybody saying a word. He heard the counsel, beside him, stop talking in the middle of a sentence about the gutter, for the first time since the bridge.

He had ridden behind that carriage for four days and eaten in the same inns, at a different table. He had stood in the same yards while the horses were changed. And he could not, if anyone had asked him on oath, have said one thing about the man inside it beyond his rank and his name, because the man had given him nothing to say. That was not a complaint. Havel had simply noticed it, the way he noticed a short pencil in a row of long ones.

He walked toward the registrar's door with his case held close against his side, at an ordinary pace, looking at the door.

The registrar met him on the step. He was not what Havel had pictured from his signature on the enrollment record: he had a pen behind his ear and ink along the edge of one cuff, and he did not bow.

"Records officer," he said. "You'll want the enrollment basis first, I expect."

"Whatever the senior seat asks for first, Registrar."

"Of course." The registrar inclined his head. "Whenever she asks, it's on a shelf in my outer office. Four boxes, with an index on top. I'll have a clerk carry them to your table."

Havel looked at him for a moment. In a good many years of arriving at institutions he had met a great many registrars, and nearly all of them had told him first, at the door, how difficult the records were to find. He had never before been told at the door exactly where a file was and how many boxes it filled, by a man who seemed to think that was the ordinary thing to say. It was the manner, he thought, of somebody who had built a thing for strangers and was quite content to let strangers have it.

"Thank you," said Havel, and meant it, and wrote nothing down.

Inside, under the stone hood, the hall was cool and dim and smelled of old paper and lamp oil, and his footsteps went ahead of him along the flags. He let himself think, once, about the high tier above the courtyard behind him, and the east end of it with the sun on it, and who was standing there. He did not turn round. There would be days on this bluff for looking. This was not one of them.

---

Before the carriages were even in sight, Cael set the stake.

He did it at the rail of the Crown yard's upper tier, at the east end, with the morning sun warm on the back of his neck and the whole of the bluff road's last bends laid out below him like a ribbon dropped on a hill. He had written it at the head of the plan the night before, ruled off from everything under it: *Hold. First.* So before he looked at anything, before he let himself begin to read, he took one small hard corner of his attention and gave it to the half-metre of air round his own body, and told it to stay there. The rest of him could go where it liked. That corner was not for reading.

Lira stood at the rail beside him with the safety instructor's stick hooked over it and her weight on the good leg. She had climbed every step of the tier on that stick, against orders, and had informed Cael on the way up that a stair a friend asks you to climb is not a stair you can avoid. Karis had asked. It was written at the foot of an empty page in Cael's notebook, under a name that had nothing else on it: *Lira*.

"I'm not watching them," Lira had said, at the top. "I'm watching you. If you go thin, I'll stand on your foot."

"That isn't in the plan."

"It's in mine," said Lira.

Karis herself was down in the records hall with Bracken. She had said long ago that she would sooner read what a delegation wrote than look at what it wore, and nothing on the bluff could have shifted her.

Below them the outer courtyard was filling with people who all happened to be crossing it, and Cael did not count them. He had stopped himself at forty, on purpose, and put the number away.

The carriages came round the last bend at the fourth bell: three of them, and four riders.

The first was a plain travelling coach, and it carried chests. Four of them came down from its roof, iron-banded, two riders to a chest, and went across the cobbles toward the stone hood of the records hall. Cael saw Bracken's young clerk step out from the door to meet them with a ledger open on his forearm. The clerk signed for every chest, one by one, before a single person from the delegation had been greeted, and Cael liked that very much. Across the quadrangle, he thought, the grey boxes would be sitting on their shelf in the registrar's outer office with their lettering turned to the room, waiting for the chests to come and find them.

The second carriage. A woman came down from it already talking, and her voice carried up to the tier in pieces, *mended three times*, *and badly*, while a clerk at her elbow wrote. The delegation's counsel, then: the woman whose findings went for where a paper came from before they asked what it said. Cael thought of librarians' brass seals on three hundred and eleven sheets, and something in his chest that had been clenched for a week let go by about a finger's width.

Then the man who had held the carriage door for her.

Cael knew him before he had properly looked. It was the way the man held his case: flat against his left side, close, with his hand spread across the lid as if the case might try to open itself. A young assessor had held a case exactly like that, crossing a square at Ardenmere toward a pump step, on a bright cold morning that felt a great deal longer ago than it was. The young assessor had stopped being young, and his coat fitted him better than it had. There was a newer mark at his collar than the one he had worn at Greyvane.

He walked across the courtyard toward the registrar's door, and he did a thing that Cael noticed only because he was looking for something else.

He did not look up.

Not at the clock over the arch. Not at the roofs. Not at the long grey wall of the Crown yard or any of the tiers along its top. He looked at the corners of the courtyard, one and then the next, the water butt, the arch, the swept empty south side, and then at the door and at Bracken in it, and then at the cobbles, and he kept his chin exactly level the whole way across. Nobody does that in a strange place. Everybody arriving anywhere new looks up at least once, at the roofline, at the height of the walls, at the sky. Havel walked the whole width of the outer courtyard like a man walking under a ladder he has made up his mind not to notice.

"That's the pear one," said Lira. She had not been looking at the courtyard; she had been looking at Cael's face, and she must have seen the man arrive there.

"That's him."

"He's gone up."

"Twice."

"Is he looking at you?"

"No," said Cael. "That's how I know he knows where I am."

Lira thought about that, and did not ask him to explain it. He was glad, because he was not sure he could have. He only knew that the upper tier was the one place on the bluff from which a person could see the whole courtyard without being in it. A careful man who had once refused a pear would have worked that out on the road. And a man who works out where you will be, and then keeps his eyes off it, has decided something. He did not know what, and he wrote nothing down about it. Some things you do not put in a notebook until you know what column they go in.

The second carriage gave up one more passenger.

She was small, and upright, and wore a dark coat, and she came down onto the cobbles and then stood quite still for the space of several breaths while everything in the courtyard went on moving round her. It was the stillness of a reader who has come to a heading and stops on it before going on. She had a plain brown case in her hand, with a strap. One of the clerks half reached for it as she passed him and then, very wisely, thought better of it, and she carried it to the door herself.

Assessor Ilsev. She had read his Kindling across a desk in a district office with a wall so thin he had heard the next clerk sneeze. She had read a Greyvane hearing's exhibits to the last page and followed them where they went with a warden glaring at her across the table. Six of her findings were in his head, and he had still not settled whether she was the worst danger on that manifest or the best hope on it. Seeing her walk did not decide it. She went in at the door as if the building were a document and she had just found the first page.

---

The third carriage was the oldest of the three.

Its varnish had gone the colour of weak tea. Somebody had painted out the device on its door so carefully, and so long ago, that the shape of the old device still showed faintly through the paint, the way a word rubbed out of a page still shows when you tilt it to the light. Cael looked at that door and knew, before it opened, that the carriage had been chosen. Nobody arrives at the head of an eleven-name manifest in the shabbiest coach by accident. And he knew, in the same moment, that he would never be able to show anybody how he knew it.

The door opened.

And the count went wrong.

It did not come back empty. He knew empty; empty was a number too. It came back arguing with itself. In two years of counting rooms, his method had given him too little and too much and once, at Ardenmere, nonsense. It had never before given him three answers at once that would not agree.

*One.* The courtyard leaned. The two clerks from the first carriage were crossing toward the third before its door was fully open. The ranking rider of the escort moved four paces to his left without anyone telling him to. The counsel, at the records hall's door, stopped in the middle of a word about the gutter and stayed stopped. Everybody in that courtyard bent toward the third carriage the way grass bends toward a door that has blown open in a wind. Cael had seen people do that round Withrow every day of the season, and he could have drawn it.

*Two.* The man who stepped down gave the leaning nothing to lean on.

He came down the step without touching the rail and moved aside from the door so that the clerk behind him could get down. Then he stood on the cobbles with his hands at his sides, and the whole careful shape that had gathered itself round his carriage was left holding the air, the clerks and the rider standing in a ring round a man who was not using the ring. He did not wait to be attended. Nor did he make a show of not waiting, the way a certain kind of great man carries his own gloves so that everyone will see him carry them. He was simply there.

*Three.* His collar spoke, and the rest of him kept silent.

He wore the plain grey that inspectors wore on the road. At his collar was a mark Cael had seen nowhere, ever, on anybody. He had read of it once, in the appendix of marks at the back of a regulation book in Halcenvane's library: a narrow bar on a plain ground, no colour, no device, alone at the foot of the last page. Every other mark the Compact handed out was made to be read from across a room. That was the purpose of a mark. A warden's coat shouted before the warden did. Coss had shouted with his whole body, every day, in every room, and it was why Cael had been able to read him inside a week. This man's collar named a height in the service that had stopped a magistrates' counsel in the middle of a word. Nothing else about him reached for it.

He looked at the records hall's door. Cael counted two.

He lifted his eyes to the tiers. Cael counted two again, and somewhere in the middle of the second count the man's glance crossed the east end of the upper tier, and crossed Cael. It neither stopped nor hurried. It went over him at precisely the speed it went over the empty bench to his left, and the empty bench to his right, and Lira's stick on the rail. Then it moved on to the roofline, and the man walked in under the stone hood, and the door took him.

Cael's hands were flat on the cold stone of the rail.

He read the man anyway, with everything he had. An empty answer only counts if the question was really asked. He went through every layer of the gaze, the way he had with each watcher in turn since the very first. There had been Coss on a road and Coss in a hall, a young assessor at a pump step, the open coats on the Greyvane road who had let themselves be counted, a copying clerk at a gallery rail whose eyes went everywhere except to Cael.

Rhythm. The step down from the carriage and the steps across the cobbles came at exactly the same pace. Not tired, then. Not hurried. Nothing more.

Weight. Low, settled, declaring nothing. A man of that height in the service had surely carried a Path for most of his life, and Cael could not find it anywhere in the way he stood.

Breath. Too far to see.

Attention.

That was the one that stopped him. All season he had measured where other people's attention went, in yards and halls and on stairs. In all that time he had never once thought to turn the measure round and ask where one man's own attention went. He asked it now. The answer was *everywhere, at one speed.* The door had two. The tiers had two. The roofline, as the man turned, had two. An empty bench had exactly what Cael had.

It was not carelessness. Carelessness has a slackness to it, and there was nothing slack anywhere in that man. It was a reader running his finger along a page at one even speed from the first line to the last, so that nobody watching the finger could ever tell which line he cared about.

He had been doing a rough cousin of it himself for two years, sampling crowds he could not count. But he sampled to save effort. This man did it so as to give nothing away.

The last clerk went in under the stone hood, and the door shut. The riders led the horses off toward the stables, and a boy ran out for the baggage. In about the time it takes to tie a boot, the outer courtyard was only a courtyard again, with the sun lying across its swept south side.

"What?" said Lira, at his shoulder.

He did not answer at once.

"Everyone they've ever sent has had something showing," he said at last. "Every one. A want, or a fear, or an order they didn't like being given, or a trick. Even Seln. What Seln hides has edges to it, and I can feel along the edges." He took his hands off the stone. "Something always shows."

"And that one?"

"That one crossed a courtyard," said Cael.

Lira was quiet for a moment, and then she unhooked the stick from the rail and leaned on it, and looked at him sideways.

"You stayed," she said.

He looked at her.

"The whole time. You were reading him the way you read everybody, all of you at once. I could see it in your face. And you stayed exactly where you were." She tapped the stone with the end of the stick. "I was ready to tread on you. I never had to."

He had not known. He had set the stake before the carriages came, and minded it, and then a man in grey had come down a step and Cael had forgotten there was any stake at all. And it had held, because he had driven it in first, at the head of everything, before he tied anything else to it.

"I didn't know," he said.

"I did," said Lira. "That's why I came up."

He wrote one line that night. He had five others ready, and he struck every one of them out in his head before the pen reached the page, because each of them was a guess, and guesses had a column of their own at the back of the Log, and this was not it.

*New opponent. No tells. Begin.*

Then he sat a while longer by the low lamp. Below the bluff the river went on, and at the fourth hour the two grey coats at the ferry landing changed under their lamp, on the bell, as they had changed every night since the bridge. The delegation was inside. Somewhere in the long building under the stone hood, a small woman who read everything to the end was reading. The final was in seven days, the evaluation in eight. And the one man on the bluff he could not read had looked at him for exactly as long as he had looked at an empty bench, and gone indoors.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

> **OPEN:** Whether Warden Coss survives the series.

> **RUMOR (source: Compact Registry):** [SHATTERED] classifications indicate dangerous instability.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

# Chapter 29 — Four Exchanges

Karis wrote one line for each exchange. Cael read them afterward on her grey page, four lines in her small upright hand, and found that between them they told the whole bout more truly than his six.

*One. L. spends nothing. Why?*

Before the word, Lira did what she had done before every bout since the Ironyard. She crouched at her chalk and laid the flat of one hand on the boards, as if asking them something, and stood, and rolled her neck once each way. The first winter, he had asked why. She had said it was so her hand would know the floor before her feet had to, and had refused to say another word on the subject for two years. Fiske, across the chalk, kept her hands clasped behind her and studied the boards between them, as a carpenter studies a frame before hanging the door in it.

At the table's word, Fiske gave ground.

She always did; Cael had four pages that said so. Her art lived in arriving early, at the place another fighter was about to be, and she could not arrive early anywhere until she knew the fighter, so for the first stretch of every bout she was only learning, and learning made her clumsy. She stepped back off her line in the first breaths, and then again, nearly over her own chalk. Two openings, exactly as the pages promised, and on the tiers a hundred people sat forward at once.

Lira let both of them go by.

She did not even look at them. She stood on her chalk for a long moment after the word, quite still, with her weight on the ball of her right foot. Then she began, at a walk, to run her evasion sequence.

Cael knew it the instant he saw the first step. Everybody who had ever trained beside her knew it. It was the drill she ran on the north hall's stone at half pace whenever her hip would let her do nothing else: eleven movements, always in the same order, a step left and a check, a turn, two steps right along an arc, a fold and a rise, round and back to where she began. She had run it ten thousand times. She ran it now on the yard's boards in front of eight hundred people at the pace of somebody showing a child how to tie a knot, with no Wind in it anywhere, and when she reached the end she began it again from the start.

Fiske watched it.

Cael watched Fiske watch it, and felt the back of his neck go cold. Lira was teaching her. Step by step, slowly enough for anyone to follow, she was laying out the order her feet liked to go in and the places they liked to go, and the woman across from her, who lived on knowing where people would be, was taking down every word. He saw Fiske's head tilt a hair at the turn. He saw her weight shift a hair toward the arc before Lira reached it, the second time round. The third time, Fiske's eyes were on the place where the fold would come before Lira had begun the step that led to it.

Lira tested her guard twice in passing, a hand going out low, a hand going out high, with nothing behind either. They were only part of the drill.

The sand ran down, and at the table every eye went to the glass.

"Even," said the instructor. "No touch. First exchange."

A groan went round the tiers like wind through standing corn, the noise of people who have paid for a fight and been shown a dance. Behind Cael somebody said distinctly, *she's thrown that away*. Down on the south tier the cohort sat in their dark knot and looked at one another and did not seem sure.

"Why?" said Karis, beside him, very low. The pencil sat idle where the line ended.

"She asked me last night how long Fiske collects." Cael kept his eyes on the boards. "I told her a minute and a half. So she gave her two minutes. She gave her everything she wanted to know, and every bit of it was true. That's her sequence. That's what she does when she's left alone."

Karis's gaze went from him to the page and stayed there, and the pencil did not move again.

*Two. F. closes the doors. F. one.*

---

Fiske took the second exchange, and took it the way a locksmith takes a door.

At the word, Lira went into the sequence again, at speed now, the first step left and the check. Fiske was already there. Not at Lira. At the place the step after the check would have gone. Her declaration went into the boards there, short and flat, without any show at all, and the boards there turned to something no foot could trust, a smooth hard slickness lying over the oak like oil on a stair. Lira saw it and broke off the turn.

Fiske had already shut the arc.

A second declaration, three beats after the first, into the place the sequence went next. Lira broke off again, and the third went down behind her, into the stretch of floor the fold would have brought her through, and in eleven seconds there was nowhere on the boards that the sequence could reach. Lira stood in a narrow patch of honest wood with oil on three sides of it, and every edge of the patch had been drawn by Fiske, from the copy Lira herself had handed her a minute earlier.

Cael found that he was gripping the binder hard enough to bend its board.

He had charted Force on five floors. Nobody he had charted could have done that, and she had not hit anybody. She had read a page and closed it, line by line, faster than the girl who had written it could turn over.

He wrote it down, honestly, on Fiske's page and not Lira's, because a page that only ever praised his friends would have been no better than a letter home.

*She isn't fighting Lira. She's fighting the drill Lira showed her. She's right about every step of it, because every step of it was true.*

Lira went for the last open side on her own feet, quick and low, and Fiske was there before her. Her flat hand landed on Lira's shoulder.

"Force. One."

On the tiers a long breath went out, and somebody behind Cael said *there*, in the satisfied voice of a man who has seen a picture straightened on a wall.

"One to none," said Brom, from Cael's left. His voice was very quiet. "Two left."

"She needs both," said Karis. "If it ends level, Fiske keeps the line."

"I know," said Brom. "I'm only saying two is a lot."

Lira walked back to her chalk. At the turn she glanced down at the slick patches already fading from the boards, and then, for an instant, across at the place behind Fiske where nothing had been closed.

*Three. L. runs the sequence and lies at four places. F.'s fourth goes into the floor. One each.*

---

At the word, Lira went into the sequence again.

The same first step left, the same check, and Fiske put her declaration into the place where the turn came next, as she had in the second exchange, and Lira's turn did not come. A burst came instead, the first Wind of the bout, and it took her not round the turn but straight past it, a stride beyond where the sequence had ever gone in ten thousand runs, onto clean wood.

Fiske's force lay on the oak behind her, a slick patch on a stretch of floor nobody was standing on.

The arc next. Fiske shut it, from the copy, a beat early, exactly right. The second burst took Lira out of the arc's middle in the wrong direction, inward instead of out, toward Fiske, which the drill had never once done. Fiske's force went down on the arc's far end and found nothing on it.

Then the fold.

The fold was where the sequence always paused, a half-breath down and up. It was also, Cael saw with a shock that went through his own hip, where a third burst would end if a fighter had taken two before it: the exact place the cohort's plan had marked, the third landing, the beat when a Wind fighter on this sheet stood locked and spent in front of a fresh opponent. Every fighter in the Copper column knew that beat now, Fiske among them. Her third declaration was already going down under it.

Lira burst into it. Then, from inside the last of the landing beat, she took a half-step.

It was nothing. A shift of one foot sideways, short, with no Wind in it, so small that most of the yard never saw it and those who did thought she had slipped. It moved her a foot, and Fiske's third went into the boards a foot from Lira's heel instead of under it.

The fourth burst came from there, from a place nobody counting landings would ever have stood to wait.

Cael felt all four go in his own leading hip, forty feet up. Four held breaths, not one returned; the last she paid for with breath borrowed from tomorrow. Four landing beats, four half-breaths locked in place, every one of them standing still in front of the one woman on the bluff who could hit a still thing before it knew it was still. And the hip. From the tier he watched the leading side take the load, and take it, and take it again, and the line it was drawing down her thigh was one he had drawn in himself at two bursts and never dared carry to four.

All four went to a single purpose. She had run her own sequence, the true one, the one she had shown Fiske, and lied with it at four places. Four times Fiske's force had gone where the copy said the girl must be. Four times the girl had gone somewhere else, not by any trick of her weight or her lean, but because the burst did not come out of her weight or her lean at all.

Fiske's fourth declaration was building.

Cael saw her see it: shoulders squaring, the force gathering, and the place it was for already empty. She stopped it. A Force practitioner can stop, if she will pay everything she has built to do it, and the force has to go somewhere when she does. Fiske sent it down. It went into the oak at her own feet with a flat, wrong crack, like a beam splitting in a roof, and eight hundred people drew one breath at once.

Lira's hand was on Fiske's ribs before the sound had finished.

"Wind. One each."

*Four. F. stops placing. L. walks in. L. two to one.*

---

Neither of them had anything left to stand behind, and both of them knew it.

Fiske could not read the girl any more. Whatever she had been reading had stopped meaning what it said, and she knew it. Cael watched the knowledge settle into the line of her mouth as she walked back to her chalk. So at the word she did the only thing left to a champion whose instrument has been broken over her head in front of her whole hill. She stopped placing. She built, and committed, and came straight at Lira with a plain heavy delivery, the Force that every other Force fighter on the sheet had always used.

Not one bout all season had she fought like the rest of the column. Now she did, and from her, even so, it was frightening.

Four bursts gone, the whole of her ceiling. If one remained, it would be slow, and short, and paid for all week. Cael sat on the tier with his hands locked on the binder and did the sum and came to that number just before Lira came to the moment of needing it.

She did not need it.

She stepped in on her own feet. Not the Wind's feet: the feet a foundry floor at Ardenmere had given her over two winters, when she had been younger and poorer and had learned that a burst you can't pay for is a burst you haven't got. She went toward the delivery at a slant, so that its edge caught her across the left shoulder instead of its whole weight across the chest. It hit her hard; her knees dipped and the last of her breath went out of her in a short hard sound. She came up out of the dip with no distance left between them at all, inside Fiske's recovery, with her palm flat on Fiske's breastbone.

Four seconds. Close and graceless, and as plain as a signature.

"Wind," said the table. "Two to one. Bout."

The yard came apart.

Not slowly, as it had after Nyle. It went up all at once, every tier, so that the noise of it hit the lecture range opposite and came back off the stone. In the faculty box a grey-bearded member of the law range had stood up and was clapping with his hands over his head like a boy at a fair. Cael was on his feet without having decided to stand. Brom beside him had both fists pressed hard against his mouth. And Karis, on his other side, sat perfectly upright with the pencil in her fingers, writing nothing, with tears running down her face that she did not appear to have noticed.

Down on the boards, Lira stood where she had touched, her hand still half raised, until the safety instructor walked out and laid his own hand very gently on her arm.

In the west gallery Withrow had not stood. She sat exactly as she had sat all afternoon, upright and white-haired, with her two Ostrand guests on their feet on either side of her, clapping. She was not watching the floor. She was watching their faces, the way she always watched the faces beside her on these afternoons, and from the east tier Cael saw her turn her head and say something short to the woman on her left, who nodded slowly, twice, and took a small book out of her coat and wrote in it.

And in the brass of the lamp, high on the east side, in the shade, a small figure with something pale against its chest had not moved at all, through any of it. Cael saw that without looking for it, and let it go.

---

It took Cael most of a quarter-hour to get down through the crowd on the stair. When he reached the floor's edge he found them both still there.

Lira sat two steps up from the boards, bent over her knees, taking her breath back a mouthful at a time. The leading leg was folded under her at an angle he did not like, and she had not tried to stand. She was keeping her face entirely empty of that, and of everything else, in front of the boards.

Fiske had a cup of water in her hand. She crossed to the step and stood over Lira.

By the long board, threescore people found the chalk suddenly fascinating.

Fiske spoke first. "You gave me a false first exchange."

Lira raised her head.

"I gave you a true one. You just assumed it was all of them."

Fiske's face changed.

Cael knew how a beaten champion's face went. This was not that. Cael had watched that one all season, on Fiske's opponents, and knew it to the eyelid. This was what he had seen once before and failed to name: at the board in the first month, the transfer with the flag, Fiske with a towel round her neck, standing two breaths longer than the words needed. That afternoon he had failed to name it. He read it now from four paces away, entire, and wondered how he had ever missed it.

It was relief. Both times, it had been relief.

"There's a form," said Fiske. "For an advancement evaluation. The registry sends an Arbiter if the form goes up. It wants one signature that isn't mine." She turned the cup in her hands. "Two seasons I've sat on top of that sheet, with Rank Eight on my certificate. Two crowns. Every bout on this hill worth having. The porter polishes the stud beside my name on Fourth-days. And in all that time nobody has picked up the pen. Not because anyone decided not to. Because nobody ever had a reason to. I was winning. Why would anybody send the winner away?"

Lira said nothing. Every bit of her attention was on Fiske.

Fiske looked down at her. "So win it. Win the crown. Then make them *look* at you. It's the looking that matters — it was always the looking. The crown's just how you force it."

Fiske drank, and then studied what was left in the cup.

"I didn't know how. I thought if I won enough, it would turn into a reason on its own. It doesn't turn into anything. They chalk it up, and once a thing's chalked up, people feel they've dealt with it."

Her gaze went past Lira to the long board. The brass stud still sat beside her name, a line lower than it had sat at noon. Until the final, it was still hers.

"Six weeks," said Fiske. "I'll see you in the final."

"You will," said Lira.

She held out her free hand. Lira looked at it a moment and took it, and Fiske did not pull her up by it, as a lesser fighter might have done in front of the boards to make a show of being gracious. One shake, and she let go.

Fiske walked away along the bottom of the tiers, cup in hand. Round the board, the onlookers began all at once to discuss the weather.

Lira stayed on the step a while longer. Then she put a hand flat on the stone and pushed herself up, slowly, in two stages, and Cael kept his own hands where they were. In front of the boards she would have refused his help, and he had known that since the Ironyard.

---

That night Brom did his fifth exercise of the week at the post behind the second quadrangle, by lamplight, because Karis had found him at supper and said the date aloud.

Cael fed him. Karis had the coping, the lamp and the grey notebook. And Lira, who should have been in bed with her leg up, sat on the coping beside Karis with her leg out straight along the stone and a wet cloth folded over the hip, and watched Brom hold and shift and hold and shift, forty times, with his heel trying to lift on every one and being set down again by main force.

On the thirty-first, without any warning, she said it.

She said all of Fiske's words in order, about the form and the signature and the stud and the pen, and the four sentences in the middle, and the board, and *six weeks*. She said them to the dark beyond the lamp in Fiske's flat voice, as though setting down something she had been asked to carry across a room. It took her the better part of a minute. She did not drop a single word. Brom stopped holding, and Cael stopped feeding. Nobody wrote anything down while she talked; Cael had learned that much at Ardenmere, in the first month, and had not forgotten it.

When she stopped, the lamp hissed.

"All of it?" said Cael.

"All of it. She said each thing once. There wasn't any show in it." Lira moved the cloth. "I've heard people say true things before. Plenty. I've never heard anybody put down a thing they'd had to carry by themselves for two years. She'd been carrying it around looking for somebody who could take it off her."

"And it was you."

"It was the first time anybody on that sheet was able to." She said it without any pride, the way she would have told him the time. "That's an ugly thing to find out about where you live."

Karis had the pencil out. "May I write it down? Exactly as you said it."

"You can write it down exactly if you never call it *the file*. Not once. Not to anybody."

"I won't," said Karis, and wrote, and didn't.

Lira looked out at the dark where the ferry landing was, and the two grey coats under their lamp, on the bell.

"And now I like her," she said. "I've no idea where to put that."

"Nothing," said Brom.

He was still at the post with his forearms up and the sweat running into his eyes, on the thirty-first of forty, with Rooke's sheet folded in his back pocket and a row of Karis's ticks on it, and a row of small crosses in his own hand beside them for the days he had wanted to stop and hadn't.

"You beat her," said Brom. "And then she told you something true. That's her being a good sort. You don't have to file it."

"Karis is going to file it."

"Karis files everything. That's Karis being a good sort." He set his heel down hard and shifted his weight. "Thirty-two. Feed me, Cael, before my arms forget what they're for."

Cael fed him. Karis, between ticks, turned back two pages in the grey notebook and held it out across the coping to Lira, open at the four lines she had written on the tier.

Lira read them by the lamp, and Cael watched her eyes go down the page and stop at the first line, at the question mark, and stay there.

"Why," said Lira.

"I didn't know," said Karis. "Not then. Cael told me on the tier, and I still didn't really know till the third." Back came the notebook; Karis frowned at her own question mark. "I wanted to cross it out after. I thought it looked stupid. Then I thought it was the most honest thing on the page, because it's what eight hundred people were thinking, and I'm the only one who wrote it down."

"Leave it," said Lira. "It's a good question. Fiske asked it too. For the whole minute and a half." She shifted the wet cloth on her hip and looked at Brom straining at the post, his heel lifting and being forced down. "She just didn't get the answer till it was too late to use. Neither did you."

"Did you know?" said Brom, through his teeth, on thirty-five. "Before the word?"

"I knew what I meant to do." Lira considered. "I didn't know if it would work. You never know that. You only know if you've built it so that if it doesn't work, you'll find out early."

"That's Rooke," said Brom.

"That's the foundry," said Lira. "Rooke just says it in fewer words."

---

It was very late when he opened the Log for the session. The river was loud below the window and the lamp turned all the way up.

He put the fragments first, as he had at the head of every session entry since Ardenmere, one line to each.

*Five confirmed fragments. All five as they were.*

Then one line he had been carrying in the second column for most of three weeks, and now, with a certain ceremony, ruled through and copied into the first.

*Iron-adjacent read. Off the counting four days, back to full: three times running, four days each time. Then under load at the post in hall three, seventeen days rested, on time and true when everybody else in the hall was a beat behind. Confirmed. The watching and the read drink from one well. Four days fills it.*

Then the bout. He found he did not want to write it as he usually wrote a bout, exchange by exchange, because Karis had already done that in four lines and done it better. He wrote what it had been instead.

*Fifteenth session. Two to one on the final's terms; second line to first on the standings. Nine and nothing on the season now, the drawn bout counted.*

*Every exchange took one more pretence off. In the first, Lira showed Fiske the truth about herself and nothing else. In the second, Fiske believed it and was right to. In the third, Lira used the same truth to go somewhere it couldn't have told anybody. By the fourth there was nothing left to show or believe, only two people, and Lira's feet had been made on a foundry floor.*

*What the third one cost her, in my own hip's language. Four bursts, all borrowed against tomorrow. Four half-breaths stood still in front of the one fighter on the bluff who could have used any of them. A hip I'd guess at three days before it lets her walk, and then the semifinal on top of it. A left shoulder that took the edge of a Force delivery so she wouldn't have to give up a hand's breadth of distance.*

*What it bought. The top line and the earlier semifinal, so a day's more rest before the final. And a woman who's held the crown two years walking across the floor to give her the one honest thing anybody on this hill has said aloud since we came up the road.*

*Now the other column, because a page with only the good column in it is how this whole place keeps its books.*

*She'll win the crown. I've thought so since the first week. And the morning after, Bracken's sheet will still say Copper, Rank Two, in the tier column beside her name, the same as this morning. All of today goes into a ring of beaten metal in a glass case, and a new line of brass riveted under the old ones.*

*Fiske's had two years at the top of that sheet to find out what it's for. She told Lira. "It's the looking that matters." So the crown was never the thing. The crown is a lever. Put a result up in front of a whole hill, a result nobody can argue with, in front of a place that's never once needed to think about you, and you've handed them the reason they never had. That's what this season is. It has been since the morning at the board.*

He read it through. One thing had lain under all the rest of the day, waiting. He gave it the foot of the page, in a smaller hand.

*At the fifth bell, most mornings, she's at the board alone. She's been going for weeks and never mentioned it. The morning after the tenth, I came up the north stair and found her there in front of six and nothing, standing square on both feet with her breathing held level, which is how she stands when a thing is costing her.*

*I went back down without speaking. I keep going back over it. I think I was right to. I'm not certain, and I want the not-being-certain on the page. The day I'm sure I know when somebody wants to be left alone is the day I've started deciding for people what they need. I've spent my life being decided for. I know what it's like from the inside.*

He blotted the page and closed the Log and sat a long while with his hands on its cover, listening to the river.

Somewhere along the passage, through the thin wall, he heard Lira laugh once in her sleep, short and surprised, and then go quiet.

## Unscored reader response

The movement caught me immediately through Karis rather than through danger: the biscuit tin of exact little notes in Chapter 22 makes the group feel lived-in before the ethical problem is named. The stronger hook arrives when Karis admits she stopped recording for six days because she disliked where the evidence led. From there, the central question—whether Cael may deliberately take a fragment from a hostile observer who cannot consent—has real moral traction because nobody is allowed an easy answer.

My attention slipped during the third pass over that question: Chapter 22 establishes the finding and the want, Chapter 23 argues the positions, and the opening Log entry in Chapter 24 states the whole procedure again. Each version contains excellent lines, but the cumulative effect briefly feels like minutes of a meeting being read back after I have already signed them. The movement accelerates decisively at Lira's Mire bout and does not lose me again. The resistance-post failure in Chapter 27 is the sharpest suspense sequence; its missing grind, wrong-shaped read, involuntary Compression response, and dangerous landing beat form a clean chain of perception, choice, cost, and consequence.

Cael matters most to me. His unusual gift is inseparable from the habit that can deform him, and the book makes self-scrutiny useful without pretending it makes him innocent. Lira is nearly as compelling: her refusal to explain herself is not emotional blankness, because the prose keeps showing what it costs her to be watched, classified, injured, and underestimated. Karis and Brom are not satellites; each changes what the others can know and do.

I expect the next consequential event to be the convergence of three pressures: Lira's semifinal/final and the advancement form Fiske has finally named; the delayed response, if any, to Seln's report; and Cael's opportunity to acquire Seln's fragment now that the reader knows Seln deliberately protected him. I would continue. The movement pays off its local promises while leaving those pressures genuinely unresolved.

- **Power limits:** The Wind fragment's held breath, landing beat, burst ceiling, and hip cost remain active during success. Compression triggers automatically, disperses force rather than erasing it, and behaves differently on sprung oak than on stone. The Iron-adjacent read competes with sustained observation for one resource and restores over four days; the post incident confirms it under indifferent danger. Seln's Shadow work carries an attention toll and depends on positioning. These limits are unusually legible and prevent the powers from becoming convenient answers.
- **Knowledge boundaries:** Cael knows Seln witnessed an anomalous response but does not know Seln omitted it; the reader learns the omission in Chapter 28. Jask sees impossible speed but accepts a partial true account. Lira privately understands more of her tactics than Cael's pages do, and Cael revises his model when the bouts expose that difference. Fiske's advancement problem is disclosed only after Lira becomes the first person able to take the top line. I found no accidental knowledge transfer.
- **Reserved disclosures:** No list of reserved revelations was supplied, so I cannot determine whether Seln's protective report, the “one well” finding, or the advancement-form mechanism arrives earlier than planned. Within the manuscript, each disclosure is prepared and has a clear recipient boundary.
- **Apparent inconsistency / ambiguity:** Chapter 25 calls the board record “6/0,” then says “Six bouts and six wins, one of them the drawn bout”; Chapter 29 similarly says “Nine and nothing… the drawn bout counted.” A draw may be included in an undefeated total, but “wins” makes the arithmetic sound contradictory. This is a clarity issue, not a verified canon error. Chapter 27's map is internally solvable—eight observers plus Cael's cross, then Seln as the ninth dot—but the phrasing can momentarily make Seln sound like a tenth person after the prose has already enumerated nine bodies.

## Overall judgment

This is a strong movement with a rare degree of causal and thematic integration. Its best achievement is not any single bout; it is the repeated demonstration that watching is never neutral. Cael spends the same faculty on care, combat, surveillance, self-deception, and rescue. Lira weaponizes the truth of her own habits. Seln protects Cael by writing a report whose facts are accurate and whose conclusion is false. Karis records uncertainty so that certainty cannot quietly replace it. Those variations give the movement intellectual weight without depriving it of pleasure.

The main tradeoff is pace. The opening ethical deliberation deserves room, but Chapters 22–24 explain and restate the same compact often enough that the reader can feel the machinery before the story moves again. Once the Mire bout begins, the balance is excellent: substantial action, visible learning, humor, injury, and institutional consequence reinforce rather than interrupt one another.

## Repair brief

1. **Location:** Chapter 22 from Karis's finding through Chapter 24's opening Log entry. **Observed issue:** The conditions, consent problem, refusal to manufacture an encounter, acknowledged desire, and advance accounting are each stated multiple times in closely adjacent forms. **Effect on the reader:** The moral problem remains strong, but its urgency flattens before the first physical turn; a movement-only younger reader may feel the story has paused to reread its own minutes. **Proposed scope:** Make a focused compression across the Chapter 23 conclusion and Chapter 24 opening, removing only explanations the signed minute has already made unambiguous; preserve the later insight that Cael can build either side of an internal argument to win. **Strength to preserve:** The four distinct moral voices, Karis's procedural honesty, Cael's “hats” list, and the refusal to make acquisition clean by rhetoric.

2. **Location:** Chapter 25 board scene (“Copper 2 — 6/0”; “Six bouts and six wins, one of them the drawn bout”) and Chapter 29 Log (“Nine and nothing… the drawn bout counted”). **Observed issue:** A draw is grammatically included among wins, while the notation appears to track wins/losses rather than bouts/losses. **Effect on the reader:** The standings arithmetic momentarily looks contradictory and weakens confidence in an otherwise exact system. **Proposed scope:** Add or revise no more than a phrase at the first occurrence to define whether the figure means undefeated bouts, wins/losses, or challenges survived, then use the same term in Chapter 29. **Strength to preserve:** The cold fifth-bell board scene and the emotional force of Lira achieving the minimum possible climb without a wasted session.

3. **Location:** Chapter 29 after the table calls the bout, especially the Fiske conversation, the lamplit post scene, and Cael's closing Log. **Observed issue:** All three passages interpret the victory's meaning, and parts of the crown-versus-recognition conclusion recur after Fiske has already expressed it powerfully. **Effect on the reader:** The emotional landing remains satisfying, but the denouement risks explaining the revelation past its strongest moment and slightly diffuses the final image. **Proposed scope:** Trim only duplicated interpretation in Cael's Log; retain the full Fiske exchange, Brom's exercise, and Cael's uncertainty about leaving Lira alone. **Strength to preserve:** Fiske's relief, the crown as institutional leverage, the friends returning to ordinary work after spectacle, and the final sleeping laugh.

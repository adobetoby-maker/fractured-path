## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |

# Chapter 14 — Filed

The panel had been closed. By the fourth bell that afternoon, the whole bluff knew what had happened in it, and none of what the bluff knew was so.

He worked out how before supper, because working out how was the only part of it he could do anything with. Nobody in the room had talked. He was sure of that by the evening and surer by the end of the week; he went at it four separate ways and found no road out of the demonstration room that any word could have taken. What had happened was simpler and, he thought, rather worse. By charter, the plain fact of any provision-status evaluation had to go up for the whole academy to read. It went on the board beside the theater door in the administration range, among the minutes of open sessions and every other act the institution performed. Halcenvane had kept its business in the open since its charter. So a little after the noon bell, the clerk had pinned up a slip.

*Entry demonstration, assay-provision enrollee: satisfied. Baseline established and filed.*

Nine words. They said nothing at all about what he had done. Inside four hours, every one of the eleven stories that had followed him round the bluff since the gate had rushed in to fill that nothing, until a reader could no longer make out the wall behind the slip for the crowd of them.

He found it oddly hard to be angry with anybody about it. The slip was accurate. As charters went, it was a good one. The people who had filled the space were only doing what people always did with a space, and he had watched them do it at Greyvane, and on the road, and in the stew line on his thirteenth evening here. It was only that he had hoped, without quite admitting it to himself, that a room full of instruments might be the one thing on the bluff a rumour could not reach.

Lira brought the first two to supper.

"You broke the drop-frame," she said, sitting down opposite him with her bowl. "A Current girl in the third quadrangle has it that you laid a hand on the rail and the rail snapped." She took a spoonful. "And you refused to demonstrate at all, and sat in a chair for an hour with your arms folded, and Gault passed you anyway because he was afraid of the Compact. That's from two Iron boys outside the north hall, and they didn't agree with each other about why he was afraid."

"Both of those?"

"Both at once. Nobody minds." Her eyes went to his right arm, lying along the table because hanging hurt it. "What did you actually do to the arm?"

"Held a plate."

"That'll be the next one," said Lira. "Wait."

Brom brought the next one. He sat down beside Cael with his tray, and did not say anything at first. Then he said, very carefully, as if it might go off, "There's a fourth-year in the cohort says you did something in there that made an instructor get up and walk out of the room."

Cael put his spoon down.

"The Mire instructor left her chair," he said slowly. "To find out why the frame had caught. She was gone about as long as this takes to say, and then she sat down again."

"I'll tell him."

"Don't." Cael picked the spoon up again. "It'll only make it sound as if I'm trying."

Karis carried in the last one as the meal ended. It was the story going round with the most certainty, passed hand to hand down from the library stair: Gault, it said, had walked out of the room and told his clerk he had never sat a stranger session.

"He didn't say anything to his clerk," said Cael. "I was there. He said good morning to me."

"I know," said Karis. "I'm only bringing it. I thought you'd want the whole list."

That night it went into the binder beneath the entry for the plate.

*Not one of them is so. The nearest is about the frame. It did stick, and a woman did leave her chair to look at it, so if I deny that one I sound as though I'm hiding something, and if I let it be, it grows. A notch that wanted oil and an instructor checking a pawl, and four hours later it's a legend.*

*Nine words. That's all the board gave them. People don't fill an empty space with what's true. They fill it with what they already had, and then they'll defend it, because now it's theirs.*

*So nothing I say is going to move any of this. Only what I do, where people can see it, over a long time. That's slower than a rumour. It's also the only thing that's ever outlasted one.*

The porter was wiping down the Copper board the next afternoon, and Cael was sitting on the rim above him reading his own notes on the morning's practice rings, when the Iron column's best fighter came up the tiers toward him at an even walk.

There were perhaps sixty people still in the yard. The session had broken up a few minutes before, and the stone seats were emptying in the slow, sociable way they always emptied, with knots of second-years arguing about a bout and somebody calling a name across the floor. Ephram came up through all of it without hurrying. His weight rode forward on the balls of his feet, as it always did, so that even climbing a stair he looked like a man about to step into something.

For most of a month Cael had watched him from the east hall's gallery, and not once in all that time had Ephram lifted his eyes to it. It wasn't shyness; Cael had settled that early. He had seen Ephram leave four other people unlooked-at in exactly the same way, people who plainly wanted his notice. His notice was a coin he paid out only after he had weighed what it would buy.

He had decided now, and Cael shut the notebook on one finger.

He came to a halt just outside arm's reach, his hands at his sides.

"I had the stationer in Ostrand send up the transcript," he said. "All of it, every sitting. Six silver." There was no boast in it. It was a receipt. "I'd wondered how many others on this hill had bothered."

"He sent five up the bluff this year," said Cael. "Three went to faculty. One's in the library. Karis asked him who had the fifth, and he wouldn't say."

Ephram's eyebrows rose by the width of a hair. Cael wrote it down that night: the first thing he had ever learned from Ephram at arm's length.

"Mine," said Ephram. "So you know what I'm working from, and what I'm not." He moved his head an inch, taking in the yard, the boards, the sunk floor, all of it. "I'll give you where I stand myself, so you don't have to piece it together from the refectory. Half the people on this hill have made you into something remarkable. The other half have made you a fraud. Not one of either half has seen you do anything. What they've got is a law report. It tells me the Compact lost an argument about paper. It tells me nothing at all about whether you can stand up on a floor."

"No," said Cael. "It doesn't."

"I know it doesn't. I read it." Then, for the first time, Ephram truly looked at him: long, level, with no dislike anywhere in it. Edran's worst glare had weighed less. "Greyvane had nothing else to bet the house on, so they bet it on you. A place that badly needs a thing to be true can't see any more whether it is."

He lifted one shoulder, very slightly. What he did next, Cael would be turning over until dark. He turned his face a little away, toward the tiers below, where sixty people had gone very quiet over the gathering-up of their belongings, and let his voice carry to them.

"Greyvane needed him to be real. We don't. I'll wait for the semester evaluation."

He went back down the tiers the way he had come, at the same even walk, and did not look round.

Below on the floor, the porter finished the Copper board and stood back to look at it. Whether he had heard, nobody could have told. He took up his pail and moved along to the Iron.

Cael sat where he was with the notebook shut on his finger and found his face was hot, which annoyed him. Nobody had said anything unkind or raised a voice. A boy of seventeen or eighteen had walked up the tiers, said three true things and one fair one, and walked down again, and Cael felt as though he had been taken apart on a floor in front of the whole school. He made himself sit with that and look at it. It was not shame, exactly. It was the feeling of a man who has been carrying a coin for months, letting people admire it, and has just had somebody bite it in public to see whether it was gold.

Whether it was gold, he did not yet know. That was the trouble: nobody did.

Brom came up the tiers a quarter of an hour later, having heard it twice on the east hall's steps inside ten minutes, and sat down on the stone beside Cael without a word.

"He's like that on the floor," he said at last. "That exact way. Looks at you as if you were a sum, and then does the sum out loud so the rest of the room can check his working." He picked at the strapping on his knuckles. "He's never once been unkind to me. He's never once been wrong about me, either. I can't decide which is harder to sit next to."

Lira arrived soon after, faster, two tiers at a stride.

She stayed standing. "With all of them listening. A stair would have done. Just you and him."

"He did tell me. To my face."

"And then he turned round and told them." She looked down the emptying yard after the place where Ephram had gone. "The first half was for you. The second half wasn't."

"I know. That's the part I'm trying to understand."

Hands on hips, she stayed up a moment more. Then, to Cael's surprise, she sat down after all, on his other side.

Karis found them there a little later, with a book under her arm and an expression of deep satisfaction.

"The fifth copy," she said, before anybody could speak. "I heard. So it was him." She sat down on the tier below and turned round to look up at them. "He sent to the stationer for the primary source and paid for it out of his own purse, at seventeen, to settle an opinion. Do you know how rare that is? Half the faculty haven't done it." She considered. "I'm afraid I rather like him."

"You would," said Lira.

"I would," Karis agreed, unrepentant. "He's wrong about one thing, mind. He thinks the transcript says nothing about whether you can fight. It says a great deal. It says fourteen sittings of it, in exhibits. He's only decided not to believe exhibits written by a school that needed you." She faced the floor again. "Which is fair. I wouldn't either."

The four of them stayed on the top tier without saying much more while the porter chalked the Iron column below them, name by name, Ephram's at the top, and the sun went off the floor.

"Does he ever change his mind?" Cael asked Brom, as they went down the tiers together in the dusk.

Brom thought about it honestly, which took him most of a flight of steps.

"Once," he said. "Second week. He'd told the cohort a girl's footwork was wrong, and two days later he stood up at the start of the hour and said he'd been wrong about it, and why, and sat down again. Rooke didn't say anything. Nobody did." He shrugged. "So he does. Only once it's true, that's all."

"The Compact does not know what he is."

They always kept that line for near the end, and this officer was no different; he set it down like a lamp he had carried in himself and wanted admired.

"No," said Seln.

Agreement it was not, and hardly an answer either. He gathered the pages together and squared their edges on his palm, and the officer, who had perhaps expected to be asked something, watched him do it and then looked at the door.

Seln had been standing the whole time, as he always did. A chair let a man settle, and a settled man began to wonder aloud, and in a room like this every wondering was a small admission of what the man had already made up his mind about. It was an old lesson. Those who handed out the work, he had found, were fondest of the officer who took his paper, read it once and left without a word, and he had let them be fond of him for it ever since. A man who never asked was the man they sent to places nobody wished to discuss. Those were the good places, mostly. They were also the ones nobody came to check.

The room had no window; they never did.

He had read the brief from the back, as he read every brief. Whoever had signed it sat three grades over the officer's head. Then the line the reports would climb: past that man, past his superior, to somewhere the officer had perhaps never been. Between them, those two facts set the limit on how honest the rest could afford to be. The front came last. Halfway down its second page, he had the drafter marked as a thinking man.

*Subject: enrollee, Halcenvane Academy, Ostrand; age fifteen; no classification of record. Greyvane: the challenge failed and the charter reading stands. Watch to date: open, funded, on rotation, without a break since the ruling.*

Lower down came the reason anybody had troubled to write it.

*Visible rotation has produced conduct data of high volume and negligible value. Subject modifies observable behavior in response to observation with a consistency that suggests deliberate management rather than nervousness. Recommend covered coverage.*

He had read that twice. These days that was as close as the work came to pleasing him, and nobody in the room could have recognised the look on him. *Deliberate management rather than nervousness.* Somebody up there knew the difference. In all his years of passing product upward he had met perhaps three readers who could have told the two apart. Care in a watched man usually means fear. Care aimed *at* the watcher is speech without words, and the only sensible reply is silence, from a place he can't see.

He knew the sentence: once, in a colder country, he had written its twin about another man. He went no further down that road.

The officer had been talking about cadence: a product each month, a summary each quarter, exception reports when the matter called for them. Seln kept what would bind him and let the rest blow by.

It was a sound brief. He turned that over as the officer talked. Whoever had drafted it had looked at a year of open watching, two men and a horse-change every so many miles, and seen what it had bought, which was a great deal of paper describing a boy being careful. The recommendation followed from that as plainly as a sum. You could not learn what a man did when unobserved by observing him in plain sight. Somebody had to sit in the room and never happen in it.

Seln's trade consisted of nothing else. He had long since lost count of the rooms.

Now he tucked the pages under his arm and inclined his head to the officer, who seemed faintly put out, and went out into a corridor that had windows all along one side, and found it was raining. He stood a moment at the nearest window and watched it run down the glass. Then he went to find the clerk who would give him his travel warrant, and was perfectly pleasant to her, and she forgot his face before he had reached the stair.

He built the man in a posting-house room in Ostrand over four evenings, with the rain on the shutters and the barge horns calling to each other down on the river.

The hand came first, because in any building a hand outlasted the man who wrote it. Six were at his command, each sure enough to write half asleep. On the back of the ferry tally he tried them all and settled on the fourth, a small, upright, slightly cramped script, the kind a country copying-house teaches a boy and never tells him he could improve. It looked slow and was perfectly legible. Nobody, anywhere he had used it, had ever commented on it. That was all it was good for, and it was enough.

The voice he tried on the posting-house boy who brought his supper. He set it a hundred-odd miles north of the river and let it be ten years behind the times there, so the vowels belonged to a town that no longer quite spoke that way.

"North, is it?" said the boy, setting down the tray. "Been away long?"

"A while," said Seln, in the stale voice, and looked at his plate.

"Ah," said the boy. He asked nothing more, then or on any later evening. A man with a fresh accent got asked about home. A man with an old one got left alone, because people decided he had his reasons, and what people decided for themselves they never troubled to check.

The coat he chose a grade below what the post would warrant. An assistant who dressed to his station looked like a man going somewhere, and people watched a man going somewhere to see where he went. Then there was the small failing. He had carried it from posting to posting and never known it to let him down. In his first week in the office he would get a sum wrong in his head, an easy one, in front of somebody, and let himself be put right. After that the office would know something about him, and would be proud of having found it out without help, and the shelf where a real opinion might have grown would already be occupied.

On the fourth evening he wrote out his residence card on the form the mid-tier academies used. *Path: Shadow. Tier: Bronze.* He wrote both in the fourth hand. Both were so. Cleverer officers than he had burned through their covers by making up facts and then having to keep them straight.

Then he packed.

There were two cases. Into the larger went the man he would be: shirts, a razor, copying pens, a box of the oddments rooms are expected to hold, and four books he had chosen by their spines. The other case was a little thing, old, its corners bound in brass, its lock shut. He laid it at the bottom of the first, as he had laid it at the bottom of every case he had packed in his working life, and filled the first up round it, and shut the lid. He did not open the small one. He did not open it after, either.

He went down to the common room for an hour before bed and let the Path run while he ate, because it was good to keep a tool warm.

It worked, as it always did, on the room. Nobody's eye came back to the man in the grey coat in the corner a second time. The Path did not hide him; it only made him cheap to overlook, so each glance slid past and found somewhere better to rest. But a small girl at the next table, perhaps four years old, had looked straight at him before he sat down, and she went on looking, over her mother's arm, with the frank interest of the very young, all through her supper. That gaze was out of his reach. The Path worked on glances still on their way. A glance already landed was beyond it.

He let her look, and her mother never once turned round.

By the end of the hour there was the old flat greyness behind his eyes that came from holding a room's attention away from himself. It would be there in the morning. However long he slept, the morning kept it. The Path cost him his own watching; to keep the room from noticing him he had to keep noticing the room, without a pause, and four hours of that wore a man thin. Instruments were beyond it entirely; a pendulum, a counting-board, a ledger had no attention for anyone to steer. And it was useless, entirely and at once, against the rare kind of watcher who went looking for a hole where a man should be instead of the man. He had met two such people in his life. Both had been very good. A provincial academy seemed an unlikely place for a third.

He was thirty-eight years old. Somewhere in the last few postings his skill had outlived his wanting. There was nobody likely to read his product who could have done it better, and there was nothing he wanted any more from having done it well. He set that aside as he would have set aside a subject's quirk: noted, of no known use.

In the morning he crossed the bridge.

He walked the bluff for three days with a satchel of paper on his shoulder, and he never once looked at it the way a visitor looks at a place.

He read it as water reads a hillside. Where would people go, and where would they stop, and from where could they be seen stopping? Three halls on the north edge, each with two sets of doors: six streams that ran together for a quarter of an hour at every change of bell and then broke apart again. The assessment wing sat behind a low wall of its own: one gate, then two doors into the building. Excellent, if you worked inside. Very poor for anyone who wanted to stand outside and watch the gate. The lecture range, four floors high, a stair at either end and every landing open along its whole length, so that a man on one landing could be seen from the far stair by anybody who happened to be looking. The quadrangles built round courts, so many windows facing in that nobody ever looked out of any of them on purpose.

In the middle lay the sunk yard, ringed with stone tiers except where the faculty stand took up one whole side. Room for more than eleven hundred. The top tier gave a man the whole floor and half the ways in and out of it, which made it the best place on the bluff to watch from. Everybody on the bluff knew it, so the top tier was where eyes gathered, and a place where eyes gathered was a place where a watcher got noticed, which made it also the worst.

He would use it regardless, since nothing else would serve. But carefully: never the same seat, never one tier two days together, never alone at a bench's open end where glances fall of their own accord. Always somewhere in the middle of a group of four or five, where the group's own shape did most of the work and the Path had only to smooth the edge.

The satchel took him everywhere. A new assistant carries paper, and paper has business in every corridor on a hill.

On the fourth morning he was in the assessment wing's passage with an armful of forms when the door of the demonstration room opened at the change of bell.

Two instructors came out first, and a clerk with a folder, into the stream of people going by. Then, after a little, the boy came out alone, unhurried, carrying nothing, favouring the leading leg a little and holding his right arm close against his side the way a man holds an arm that has been asked to take something it did not care for. Behind him the magister had turned back into the room. Seln was perhaps thirty feet away. He stepped out of the stream, as a clerk does, to let it go by.

He did not look at the boy.

The face was the thing, as he had once tried to tell a young officer long ago who had neither listened nor lasted. People did not feel a stranger's nearness, or how often they had passed him, or how odd it was that he kept turning up. They felt his eyes. A man could pass the same stranger nine times on a stair and remember none of it, and on the tenth, if their eyes met once, all nine would come back to him at once as a pattern, and the stranger would be finished. No looking, then, for the first month. Only what came in at the edges: where the boy stood, how the passage moved round him, what the people round him did. Later there would be months of it, once Seln had become furniture; nobody minds a chair with eyes.

In the office he squared the forms on the copying table. For two hours after that he did not let the boy into his head at all, and that, too, was method.

Late that day, in the plain day-book a new assistant keeps and anybody may read, he noted one ordinary thing among the other ordinary things. The passage had been crowded at the change of bell, and the passage had been watching. Without looking he had counted about one in three of them turning a head as the boy passed, or checking a step, or weighing whether to nod.

That suited him, and he noted it without warmth. The more people already stared at a man, the less anybody would notice one more.

Karis's census of the residence cards had reached the staff corridor by then, and she had made Cael promise to read every card on it the next time he passed that way, since it was on his road back from the north hall and not on hers.

"Count the ones you'd bet your supper on," she said. "And the ones you wouldn't. I'm up to sixty-one doors. I believe forty."

Each door on the bluff had its card, eye-high in a little frame of brass, giving name, Path and tier. Greyvane would never have hung such cards; it had been built on the suspicion that papers lied about people, and you do not fix to a door a figure you suspect. Halcenvane put the number on every door because here the number ran everything, the ladder and the brackets and the rooms and the seeding, and a corridor of cards was simply the ledger with its pages hung up where everybody walked. Cael's own card read *Cael. Assay provision. Evaluation by demonstration.*, in letters squeezed smaller than any other on the corridor so that the words would fit. Lira's read *Wind. Copper.* Brom's read *Iron Skin. Copper.*, and Brom tapped it with a knuckle every night on his way in, as if to remind it he was there. Karis's read *Ember. Iron.* It was the one card she said she would have bet her supper on, and she used it to measure all the others.

He had a filing to hand in on the way, so he went to the assessment wing first.

It was his second floor-time allocation, and he walked it over himself, three days after the baseline, instead of leaving it for the residence clerk. He wanted to see the wing on a day when nobody was being measured in it. The demonstration room's door was shut. The passage smelled of lamp black and machine oil and the fine dry dust of the chalk trays, and on the records side every sheet he saw bore a pair of dates and a pair of initials, one for whoever had written it and one for whoever had checked, so that no paper could leave the wing with a single person's word on it. Vell had run her ledger like that from sheer temper; Halcenvane printed it into its forms.

In the end office the regular clerk sat frowning over a tally. Under the window, at the copying table, sat somebody Cael had not seen before. He took Cael's sheet without a word and read it right through, which most clerks never did. He turned it over to look at the authorizations on the back, checked both sets of initials in the corner, laid it on the right heap without glancing at the heaps, and wrote it into the big ledger with the two dates side by side.

"East and north halls. Four a week, supervised. Any change of hall reaches your residence board from this office, the evening before. Never from a hall. A notice from a hall is unauthorized; ignore it." He blotted the line. "That's been tried twice this term."

"Thank you."

The clerk at the desk looked up. "Four a week, and how many weeks to the semester? I've got to carry it forward."

At the copying table the new man worked at it, his lips shaping figures. "Nineteen weeks. Seventy—" He stopped, and frowned. "Seventy-two."

"Seventy-six," said the clerk, quite kindly, and wrote it.

"Seventy-six," he repeated, with a faint flush, and returned his pen to its line.

Cael lingered a moment and, without choosing to, ran the full gaze over him, as three years of roads had taught him to with every new face. The pen kept one pace throughout, through speaking and through silence: a calm temperament, or a working life spent copying, which a copier would come by honestly. His weight sat evenly, the habit of years on his feet at copying tables. His breath was ordinary, a little slow. He was where an assistant ought to be at that hour, holding what an assistant ought to hold. All four layers said the same, and what they said was that here was a man who had spent his life writing out other people's words, and was not very good at sums.

Walking back up the passage, Cael could find no reason to think him anything else. He turned it over for a hundred steps and then let it drop. One time was an accident. What he had was a single ordinary man being ordinary once, and an Ardenmere spring had taught him, underlined, what it cost to build on nothing.

Then he went on along the staff corridor, counting cards for Karis.

Near the far end, on a door whose frame had stood empty all term, there was now a card.

*Seln. Shadow. Bronze.*

He stopped for a breath, perhaps two.

Shadow was a Path he had never charted. Greyvane had not had one, and at the Ironyard he had seen two in eighteen months, both of them washouts who brawled and left their Path idle, since being hard to notice pays nothing in a ring that people have bought tickets to stare at. And Bronze was above an assistant's post. Mid-tier schools mostly filled their assistant posts with Copper, or with Iron fresh from its own course, people hanging on for a year. A Bronze man at a copying table had either had his road bend under him or chosen a quiet year for some private reason. Both were ordinary, and both could be found in any school on the continent.

He put the card in Karis's count as one he would believe, and went to supper.

He wrote up the day afterward in the observation notebook, by the open window, with the barge horns calling their long call and their two short ones on the river below: the two dates and two initials, the chalk dust, the cards and the census and what a corridor of cards said about the place that hung them. Last, beneath the new heading for the wing's staff, he put down the smallest entry of the day. Ardenmere had taught him that the small ones were the ones that came due last.

*New TA in Gault's office. Bronze-tier, Shadow Path per the residence card. Doesn't look at me. Note: at Halcenvane, everyone looks at me. Filed.*

One reading back: it was worth what it said and nothing beyond. He put out the lamp.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

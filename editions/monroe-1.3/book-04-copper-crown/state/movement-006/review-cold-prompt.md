editorial judgments, not calibrated measurements. Do not average the two readers

# Chapter 38 — Registry Grey

Karis wrote the coats down at breakfast, as she had every morning since the bridge, in the marbled book she kept beside her bowl.

*Third-day. Two and two at first bell. Landing: the tall one and the stiff knee. Road's foot: the pair with the dog.* She blotted it and turned the book so that Cael could see it, which was their arrangement, and he read it and nodded, which was his half of the arrangement, and that was the whole conversation. Nobody at the long table looked twice at either of them. A season of mornings had made the marbled book part of the furniture.

He had his own line for the day already, at the head of the page, under the date.

*Hold ordinary. Count the bumps.*

It had been none yesterday, and none the day before that, and he meant it to be none again. By now he knew the work's exact shape. He minded the edges of himself the way a ferryman minds the current, without staring at it, with the part of his attention that never quite went to sleep. He paid for the minding with a dull weight behind the eyes that arrived about the middle of the afternoon and stayed until he lay down. It was rent: Lira had named it, and the name had stuck because it was true. You paid it whether you used the room or not.

After breakfast he went to the covered walk's east end, where the rail looked down over the last bend of the bluff road, and stood there through the change of the second bell with a lecture's notes open in his hands. It was a good place to be ordinary in. People streamed past behind him in both directions, going to the lecture range and coming from it, and none of them needed him for anything, and he gave every one of them exactly the ordinary amount of room.

That was why he saw the courier.

The man came round the last bend on foot, alone, walking fast, with a satchel strapped flat across his chest so that it could not swing. That alone was wrong for the hour. The bluff's morning traffic came up slowly and in company: carters with flour, a guild factor with a clerk, instructors back from the town with parcels under their arms, all of them climbing the way people climb a hill they know, stopping at the bend to look back at the river. This man did not look at anything. He climbed like somebody whose day was measured from the moment he set off.

Then the coat.

The watchers at the road's foot wore grey, and Cael had spent a season learning their grey: road grey, the grey of dust that has been rained on, a colour chosen so that nobody would remember it. This was the other grey. It was cold and faintly blue, like slate after rain, and it was the grey of the registry. He had seen it on the clerks behind the long counter at the certification hall in Denvash, and once on a young assessor standing in front of a pump step, in boots too new to have creased. He had never once mistaken it for anything else.

Cael noted as well that the two watchers at the road's foot had let the courier by without moving. They had looked at him for exactly as long as they would have looked at a cart.

He did not follow the man up to the gate. He wanted to, and he noticed himself wanting to, and stayed where he was at the rail with his notes open, because a boy who leaves his place to trail a courier has told every watcher on the bluff that couriers matter to him. Instead he watched the satchel go in under the arch, and counted twelve breaths, and saw the porter come out of his lodge at a trot, which the porter never did, and go across the first quadrangle toward the chancellor's door with the man in grey a pace behind him.

Then he looked down at his own edges, the way you glance at a pot on the fire. They were where they ought to be. Whatever had come up the road, he had not gone thin at the sight of it.

He wrote the bell in the margin of his notes, small, and went to his lecture.

---

By the third bell there was nobody on the bluff who did not know that a sealed thing had come up the road.

Nobody had read it, and nobody needed to. Withrow's runner had gone from her door at a run, which runners on the bluff did not do, and had knocked at four other doors in turn. Four people had come out of those doors and crossed the second quadrangle toward the records hall with the particular walk of people who have been asked to bring nothing and come now. Bracken was one, and Gault another, carrying a folder. Rooke came last, with his hands in the pockets of his dark coat, and stopped in the middle of the quadrangle to say something to a cohort boy who ran off toward the halls. By the time the lecture range let out, the second-years were telling one another it was a death in the chancellor's family, and the third-years were telling one another it was money, and a Current boy on the lecture stair was telling anybody who would stand still that it was war.

Cael was coming down the lecture range's outside stair when Karis met him at its foot, out of breath, holding two folded slips. The wing's runner had gone looking for him in the library and found her instead, and had given her both, because the runner was a first-year and Karis looked like a person who would know where anybody was.

His was in Bracken's hand.

*Enrollee (assay provision). Records hall, now. You are named.*

"Named in what?" said Cael.

"Whatever came up the road." Karis turned and set off across the quadrangle without waiting for him, buttoning her coat as she went, and buttoning it wrong. "It's how she runs things. If a scope names you, you read the scope. You don't get it third-hand on a stair. Bracken told me in the second week, when I asked him why the minutes go up on the board where anybody can read them."

"A Current boy on the stair says it's war."

"Then he's going to be very disappointed in a minute," said Karis.

They went in under the records hall's stone hood, past the mossy bench in the shallow doorway, where a man had once sat through the whole of the fire-watch's round without being seen. Cael did not look at the bench. He had been inside this building at every hour of the clock that month, in the dark and by lantern, and never once by daylight through the front door with a summons in his hand. It felt like coming into a house he had burgled and being offered a chair.

Somebody had cleared the long table to the bare wood and laid nine chairs round it. Withrow had not taken the end. She sat halfway down one side, with her back to the windows and her spectacles pushed up into her white hair, so that whoever read aloud would be reading toward everybody at once; her counsel sat opposite with a leather case open on the wood. Bracken was at Withrow's elbow, a pen in his fingers and its brother behind his ear. Gault sat at the far corner with a closed folder under one big hand. Two faculty whose faces Cael knew and whose names he had never learned, a Stone man and a law lecturer, sat together as though they had been called out of the same argument and meant to finish it afterward. Rooke had a chair and was not using it; he stood with his back against the cold fireplace and his arms folded.

Karis sat in the last chair but one and put both hands flat on the table, and Cael sat in the last.

"Read it," said Withrow. "From the top. Leave nothing out."

So the counsel read it, in the dry, careful voice of somebody who has noticed over a long career that the louder a document is read, the less people trust it.

*To the Chancellor of Halcenvane Academy, chartered. The Guilds Compact gives notice that a delegation of the Compact will conduct a formal charter inspection of this institution, commencing on the twelfth day of Reaping, for such time as the delegation requires. Scope: charter compliance; enrollment-basis review; observation of provision-status evaluations.*

After it came an appendix with a schedule in it, and a manifest of names, and the seal on its red ribbon, which she held up a moment for the table to see.

*Provision-status evaluations.* Halcenvane had exactly one provision-status enrollee. He was sitting in the last chair, with his hands in his lap.

The Stone man let out a long breath. "Well. Inspection. They come round."

"Not this one," said the counsel.

She held up three fingers, and put the first of them down.

"We're on a five-year round, like every chartered house of our size. Our last visit was a little over two years ago. It was two officers and three days, and they found us in full compliance, but for one remark about how the fire-watch keeps its oil book. By the round, nobody is due here for nearly three years. They are coming anyway, and the notice doesn't say why. It doesn't have to."

Bracken wrote something very small.

She put the second finger down and turned the manifest on the wood, and then, instead of reading it from the top, she began at the bottom and read upward, the way a clerk reads a column to check its sum.

"An escort of four. Three clerks. Counsel, of the magistrates' grade. A records officer. A senior evaluation seat." She paused. "An ordinary visit sends two people with a ledger. This sends eleven. You don't bring a magistrates' counsel and three clerks to check a fire-watch's oil. You bring them when somebody above you means to read what you write, and you need it to hold."

"And the last line," said Withrow. "Which is the first."

The counsel put the third finger down and read it.

---

*Archmarshal Vastin.*

Cael always read the hands first. He watched the name travel round the table, and every pair of hands took it in its own way.

Bracken laid his pen down. He laid it parallel to the edge of the table, very exactly, the way a man sets down a tool he does not trust himself to keep holding. Gault's big hand lay on his folder and did not move by a hair, and Cael had watched Gault long enough to know that this perfect stillness was Gault being startled. The Stone man laughed. It was one short sound, the noise of somebody hearing a sum come out absurd, and he cut it off at once and looked down. The law lecturer leaned across and asked, very politely, if she might see the page, as if the counsel might have misread a plain word. Karis wrote the name in the grey notebook and ruled a line under it, and then a second line under the first.

Rooke, at the fireplace, unfolded his arms and folded them again the other way.

The counsel turned to Withrow. "I can tell this table what the law says about every name on that sheet except the top one," she said. "There isn't any law about the top one. There's custom. In nineteen years of registry practice I have never needed the custom, and I'd rather not guess at it in front of the faculty."

"May I ask what one is?" said Karis. Her voice came out higher than she meant, and she cleared her throat. "An Archmarshal. Exactly. There are three mentions of the rank in our whole library, and every one of them is a list of who sat where."

Withrow looked down the table at her for a moment, as though pleased to be asked by the person in the room least likely to need it explained twice.

"I'll answer that. I sat at a registry desk for twenty years before I sat in this one, so I'm the least ignorant person here, and that's not saying a great deal."

She took her spectacles out of her hair and folded them, and held them.

"Think of the Compact as three roads. The wardens' road. The assessors' road, and the evaluation seats with it. The registry's road. Down here, where we live, the three of them run side by side and quarrel about the verges. A warden can't order an assessor about. An assessor can't make a registrar write a word. You've all planned whole terms round the weather that comes of it." She turned the folded spectacles over once. "Far up, the three roads come in through one gate, and one desk sits in it, and everything from all three goes across that desk. The Archmarshals sit at it. I don't know how many there are. Nobody who tells you a number knows either."

"And they inspect academies?" said the law lecturer.

"They do not. Wardens come to academies. Assessors come all the time; Bracken gives them tea and a chair by the fire and they go away pleased. In twenty years I heard of one Archmarshal sitting on an inspection in person." Withrow put the spectacles back up into her hair. "The house he sat on was older than this one by three centuries. It's a grain store now."

Nobody at the table moved.

"Then what's he coming for?" said the Stone man.

"Nothing." Withrow did not hesitate. "I take the notice at its word. There isn't a line on that sheet that's his. No business with his seal on it, no request, no question. Only his name, heading a list that would otherwise bore you." She reached across and closed the counsel's case herself, with a small flat click. "The inspection is ordinary. His presence is the message. Somebody very high wants their own eyes on this, and they have sent the best pair they have."

For a moment nobody said anything. Cael was aware of the dull weight behind his eyes, which had come early today, and of his own edges. He minded them hard and gave the room exactly the ordinary amount of himself, and it cost more than usual.

"So we'll be inspected in the ordinary way," said Withrow. "Bracken, the documentary work begins this afternoon. I don't want it built to win an argument. I want it built to be filed and to sit in a cabinet and be true. Magister Gault—"

"My wing changes nothing," said Gault.

Until then he had not spoken a word. Every head at the table turned toward him, and he waited, as he always waited, until they had finished turning.

"Nothing in my wing will be set or checked or moved this month that wasn't on the wall in the first week of term. If a finding ever says otherwise, it will be wrong, and I'll put my seal to a sheet saying so." For the space of one breath his eyes came to the last chair at the table, and went away again. "The enrollee's evaluation is on the twentieth. Same floor, same panel, his own baseline. If the delegation wishes to watch, there are chairs in the gallery."

"Good," said Withrow.

Gault shook his head, very slightly. "It is not good or bad. It is the mandate."

---

Rooke was waiting at the foot of the records hall's steps when Cael came out, which was the first surprise. The second was that he fell in beside him without a word and walked him all the way across the second quadrangle toward the halls, as if they had arranged to go somewhere together.

Cael had not prepared for this, and knew it at once.

"Two things," said Rooke, at the covered walk. "Then you can go."

"Yes, Instructor."

"By tomorrow half this yard will be telling the other half that I foresaw today. I didn't. I said in the minutes, in the second week of term, with you sitting in the back rows, that an enrollment like yours would draw attention and we'd pay for it at inspection. I said it to the chancellor's face so nobody could say afterward that I'd found my nerve late." He walked on a few paces. "Nobody foresaw an Archmarshal. What I said was two things together. That the visit would come before its time. And that it would find us with a strange enrollment on the books and no habit of standing behind one."

"And?"

"The first was a forecast, and it came true. The second wasn't a forecast. It was a job. It was mine, and I didn't do it." There was no heat in it. It was how he told a pupil a stance was wrong. "A great deal of what an inspector reads to judge whether a house can hold an enrollment like yours is supervision paper, and supervision paper is my cohort's. Mine has sat in drawers all season, because nobody asked me for it in a shape a stranger could read. I was too busy being right in the minutes to put it in that shape. That's poor work. You sat in the room when I made the objection, so you're owed the correction as well."

They had come to the north arch. Rooke stopped under it, and the traffic of the change of bell went round them both like water round a post.

On the page headed *Rooke*, in the first month, Cael had written six words: *Fights the institution, not the person.* And under them: *Evidence only.* He stood under the arch and felt that page go out of date inside his coat.

"That's the first thing," said Rooke. "The second. Every signature my cohort holds crosses the quadrangle tonight to the registrar's office, in date order, from the first week. When a delegation clerk asks for three supervision sheets, he can have three hundred and a chair to read them in." His grey eyes rested on Cael for about the length of a breath. "Your floor sheets cross my desk. Four supervised hours a week, all season. Not one late. Not one exception asked for. Keep doing that. Nothing you could do between now and the twentieth would be more use to this academy. It's exactly as boring as it sounds."

Then he was gone toward the halls with his hands back in his pockets, before Cael had found anything to say.

Cael wrote it up at the window after supper with Hesk's pen, on the *Rooke* page. He did not cross out the first month's six words. He ruled a line under them, and dated it, and wrote beneath it in the steady new hand:

*Half wrong. Fights institutions, and himself, out loud, in front of the person he fought about. Evidence moves him. So does owing. Came and found me to say he'd been right about the wrong half, and then went to do the dull part, which nobody will ever thank him for.*

He sat over that for a time, and then put one more line beneath it, because it was true, and because Karis would have asked for it.

*I'd have bet against that. I'd have lost.*

---

The unit met in the yard at the sixth bell, as the sheet on the residence board had said it would, and Seln taught it exactly as if nothing whatever had come up the road that morning.

Nine of them stood in a loose line under the lecture range's wall, in coats, and counted the people who looked up at the records hall's windows in the space of a quarter of an hour. Gwen counted forty-one and wrote a large *41* with an eye drawn in the loop of the four. Cael counted thirty-eight and wrote thirty-eight. Seln took the slates and made a column of the figures in the round copying hand. Then he said, in his flat voice, that the yard had been looking at a building because it believed the building had news in it, and that this was the commonest error in the trade, since buildings rarely had news in them and people nearly always did. Then he dismissed them. His eyes passed along the line and did not stop anywhere in particular, and Cael had stopped expecting them to.

Walking back across the yard, Cael found himself turning that last sentence over. The notice had come up the road addressed to the chancellor and sealed, and it would have crossed the copying table on its way to her, because everything sealed crossed the copying table. So the man who had just taught nine students to stop staring at a building had very likely read the building's news before any of them, and had then stood in front of the building for a quarter of an hour, in plain sight, and let them stare. Cael could not decide whether that was a lesson, or a joke, or simply a man doing his job on an ordinary Third-day. He suspected that Seln would have said there was no difference.

The common room after supper was a different instrument altogether.

Lira had her bad leg along the settle, and was rolling a wooden pin up and down the outside of her thigh with both hands, slowly, wincing at the same place on every pass. Brom sat in the big chair with his knees apart and his elbows on them, holding a mug he had not drunk from. Karis sat on the floor with her back to the cold grate and the grey notebook on her knees, and had read the whole of the morning out to them from it, the deltas and the name and both of Withrow's speeches and Gault's two sentences, in order, without a word added.

"An Archmarshal," said Brom, when she had finished.

"Yes."

"What's an Archmarshal *for*?"

"I've been trying to work that out all afternoon," said Karis. "Honestly, I don't know. Nothing in the library says. There are three references to the rank in the whole of the stacks, and all three are lists of who attended something."

"Then guess."

"I don't guess in front of people," said Karis, a little stiffly. "I guess in my room, alone, with the door shut, and then I burn it."

Lira stopped the pin halfway up her leg.

"I'll guess," she said. "I don't mind. They've sent the biggest thing they own to look at one boy and a clause. Which means somebody up there's stopped believing the reports." She looked at Cael. "Or never did."

Nobody answered that. Down on the river, a barge called its long note, and another answered from the bend.

"What did Gault's look mean?" said Lira. "Karis says he looked at you."

"For one breath," said Karis. "I counted."

Cael thought about it honestly, because she had asked honestly. "That he'd decided before he came into the room. He wasn't telling the table anything; he was telling me. The evaluation is the evaluation. Same floor, same instruments, same standard. Whoever's sitting at the rail." He turned his cup on the arm of the chair. "It was the kindest thing anybody said in there, and he said it with his eyes, so it can't go in a minute."

"And you?" said Lira.

"Me what?"

"How are you?" She did not stop rolling the pin. "Not the provision. You."

He had a sentence ready, a good flat one about holding ordinary and the bumps column, and he heard it start to come out and stopped it.

"Frightened," he said. "Not of the evaluation. Of not knowing what's going to be looking at it."

"Good," said Lira. "Now it's said, and it's smaller." She went back to her leg. "Write it down tonight, before it grows back."

"The coats," said Cael.

"Unchanged." Karis did not need to open the marbled book. "Two and two at the fourth bell. Two and two at the eighth. Same faces in the same order, on the bell to the minute. Whatever came up the road this morning, the men at the bottom of it haven't been told."

"Or have," said Lira, "and were told to change nothing."

Brom put his mug down at last.

"It doesn't matter which," he said. "Rooke said keep doing it, and Gault said keep doing it, and the registrar's going to say keep doing it, because that's what registrars say. Cael keeps doing it. Lira keeps winning. I keep losing to Merrick in the taking-apart until I stop." He looked round at them all. "I don't see what changes. We were always going to be looked at on the twentieth. Now we get looked at by somebody important. That's not worse. That's just more chairs."

"It's eleven more chairs," said Karis.

"Eleven more chairs," Brom agreed, and picked his mug up again, and drank from it this time.

Cael went up early. He sat at the window with the lamp low and did not write anything for a long time, and the weight behind his eyes had grown into something with corners. He had held ordinary all day, through a courier and a name and a covered walk with Rooke in it, and the bumps column for the day said *none*. He had not stopped minding his edges once, not even at the moment the counsel read the name, when every other part of him had wanted badly to go somewhere else.

He wrote that down, short.

*Bumps: none. Even at the table. The hold doesn't care what kind of day it is. That's the first useful thing I've learned about it that isn't a cost.*

*Told Lira I'm frightened. She was right. It's smaller now it's said.*

Across the dark second quadrangle there was a lamp still burning on the ground floor of the administration range, in the window of the registrar's outer office. While Cael watched, a cohort boy came across the flagstones from the direction of the halls with a stack of paper held flat in both arms, as carefully as Brom had carried the basket, and knocked at that door with his elbow. It opened, and closed behind him, and a little later he came out empty-handed and went back the way he had come, and came again with a second stack.

Cael counted four trips before he put the lamp out. He did not write them down, because some things are better kept as a picture.

# Chapter 39 — Filing-Grade

On the morning after the reading the door of the registrar's back room stood open, and Cael, waiting at the outer counter with a request slip of Karis's, could see straight through it into a kind of weather.

There was paper on every flat surface in the room. It lay on the long table in squared stacks and on the window seat in fanned ones, and on the floor along one wall in a row of open boxes, each with a card propped against its side. Three of the registrar's clerks sat at the table with their sleeves pinned back, writing. A fourth stood at a slope desk by the window copying from a heavy volume, with a librarian at his elbow who checked each line as he finished it and pressed a small brass seal to the foot of the page. The room smelled of lamp oil and new ink, and nobody in it was talking.

Karis was on her knees beside the row of boxes with a card in her teeth.

Bracken came out to the counter himself, wiping his fingers on a rag that had long since given up any hope of being clean. He took Karis's slip, stamped it, and gave it back. Then, because Cael did not leave, he waited.

"I'd like to help," said Cael. "If there's anything. Carrying. Copying. I write a fair hand."

Bracken considered this with the full seriousness he gave every request at that counter, which was one of the things about him Cael had come to depend on.

"You write a very fair hand," he said. "And there is nothing in that room I can let you touch. Not one sheet." He folded the rag. "Every document on that table is about you. If a delegation counsel ever finds that the enrollee carried his own file, or copied a line of it, she will spend a whole morning asking what else he carried, and she'll be right to. Your hand on any of it makes all of it weaker."

"Then what can I do?"

"Be dull," said Bracken. "Go to your lectures. Train in your hours and sign for them. Hand your work in on the day it's due, and not a day early, because early is interesting. Don't put your name to a single sheet the calendar didn't ask for." He leaned his forearms on the counter. "Strong files are made of dull months, Enrollee. Give me a dull month."

"There are fifteen days to the evaluation."

"Then give me fifteen dull days," said Bracken, "and I'll write them up as a month."

He said it without a flicker, and it took Cael a moment to understand that the registrar had made a joke, and another moment to understand that he had made it on purpose, for Cael's sake, because the boy at the counter looked as if he needed one. Cael nearly laughed. Through the open door, Karis took the card out of her teeth and propped it against a box, and he saw that it read, in her small hand, *41 pp. — tabbed — DO NOT RE-SORT*.

"The argument's already won, though," he said. "Karis won it at Greyvane. Your counsel sealed it before I ever came up the road. What's left to build?"

Bracken did not answer at once. He took the second pen from behind his ear, looked at its nib, and put it back.

"An argument persuades a person. A file survives a person leaving the room." He said it plainly, as he might have said what bell it was. "Miss Karis persuaded me. My counsel persuaded the chancellor. Both of those were people. In a week there will be a room full of strangers on this bluff, none of whom were persuaded of anything, and some of whom would rather not be. A file has to work on them. It has to work if Miss Karis is in her bed with a fever and I'm at the bottom of the river. That's what's being built in there." He nodded at the door. "Not a better argument. Something that stands up with nobody holding it."

---

Over the next four days Cael watched it being built, from the counter and the passage and once, by invitation and with his hands kept firmly in his pockets, from the back room's doorway. He understood it the way he understood a fight he was not in, by its decisions.

The first decision was that nobody should ever have to trust Karis.

Her chain of proof ran through thirty-odd volumes, from the founding schedule that held the clause to the charter article that took it into Halcenvane. At Greyvane she had built it out of her own transcriptions, careful ones, in her small upright hand. Bracken would not use a single line of them. For every link in the chain a clerk went to the actual volume, in whatever library held it, and copied the passage whole. Then the librarian who kept that volume read the copy against the page, word by word, and sealed the foot of it. Where the volume was not on the bluff, a letter went down the river by the first coach to the house that held it, asking for a copy under that house's seal, and a dated note of the asking went into the box in its place, so that even the gap was on paper. By the end of the third day there was not a word of Karis's handwriting anywhere in the chain. She had stood at the table and watched the last of it go, and Cael had watched her face, and it had not looked like loss.

The second decision was harder, and he would not have thought of it himself.

At Greyvane, Karis had spent eleven weeks proving a negative: that the clause had never been repealed, by searching every place a repeal could have been recorded and finding nothing in any of them. She could have done the search again with her eyes shut. That was exactly why she was not allowed to. Bracken took a clerk from his own office who had been away on leave for most of the term and had never seen a page of her notes, gave him the list of places a repeal would have to be recorded, and put him in a separate room on the floor above with the door shut. He worked alone up there for four days. On the evening of the fourth he came downstairs with his findings tied in string, and Bracken laid them on the long table beside Karis's, and the two searches by two people who had never compared a word reached the same nothing in the same places.

Cael was at the counter when the clerk came down. He was a thin young man with spectacles and a bad cold, and he carried his bundle in front of him with both hands, like a dish he expected to be blamed for. Karis was at the long table. She had been forbidden to speak to him about anything but the weather, and she kept to it with a kind of ferocious exactness.

"Is it still raining?" she said.

"It's stopped," said the clerk, and put the bundle down, and fled.

Bracken laid the two searches side by side on the table and went through them place by place, his finger moving down one and then the other. Karis stood beside him and did not touch either. At the end Bracken said, "Same," and nothing else, and wrote one line in the index. Karis read the clerk's last page twice and then went out into the passage and stood with her forehead against the cold wall for a while.

The third decision was about the directive. The standardization directive that had swept away the old terminology ran to forty-one pages. Most of its readers, Bracken said, had read the four pages that mattered to them and trusted the rest. So the whole of it went into the file, all forty-one pages, copied and sealed. The four passages where the old words were replaced by new ones were tabbed with green paper, and at every place in the text where a clause repealing the assay provision would have had to stand, if one existed, a narrow white slip was pinned with a line in Bracken's hand saying that nothing stood there.

The last decision was the index, and Bracken wrote it himself.

It was a numbered list of every sheet in the file. Beside each number stood one line, never more, saying what that sheet proved and nothing else. There were no adjectives anywhere in it, and nothing that argued. Cael read the first page of it over the counter, upside down, and saw at once what it was for. A delegation counsel with four hours, a sore back from the road and no patience at all could open the box, run her finger down the index, find the sheet that carried the weight of the whole matter, and have it in her hand inside a minute and a half, without asking a single person on the bluff for anything.

On the fifth afternoon Karis tested it. She did not ask anyone's leave. She simply took the clerk with the cold, who had never seen the file's contents and had only searched for the absence of one thing, and sat him down at the long table in front of the first box with the index on top. Then she turned over the little sand-glass she used for timing her own reading. "The question," she said, "is whether this enrollment rests on a clause that has been repealed. Find me the sheet that answers it." The clerk ran his finger down the index, stopped, turned to the number, lifted out a sealed copy of a schedule with a white slip pinned to its margin, and laid it in front of her. Karis looked at the glass. A little under half of it had run. She turned it back over and wrote the figure in her notebook, and did not say anything at all, and Cael, from the doorway with his hands in his pockets, thought it was the most satisfied silence he had ever heard.

He had built plans for a hundred bouts. This was the first he had seen built for an opponent the builder had never met, would never be allowed to speak to, and could not predict at all, except to know that she would be tired and suspicious and in a hurry. Nobody draws up a plan to beat a person like that. You built a thing that would still be standing whatever kind of person she turned out to be.

He wrote it that night, with Hesk's pen, under a heading of its own.

*How the registrar fights. He takes himself out of it. He takes Karis out of it. He takes me out of it. Every link a stranger can check without trusting anybody who was here. The opponent is whoever walks in tired. Build for them, not against them.*

*Everything that ever went wrong for me went wrong on a sheet of paper that nobody could check. This is the same weapon, held the other way round.*

On the fourth evening he found Karis on the floor.

The clerks had gone. The back room's lamps were down to two, and she was lying flat on her back on the boards between the row of boxes and the long table, with her feet up on an empty box and her hands folded on her stomach. Her fingers were black with ink to the second knuckle. There was a long smear of it along her jaw where she had pushed her hair back with the wrong hand.

"Are you hurt?" said Cael, from the doorway.

"I'm resting." She did not open her eyes. "Bracken said I could stay till the lamp-man comes. Come in, if you keep your hands in your pockets. He'll know if you don't. I don't know how, but he'll know."

He came in with his hands in his pockets and sat on the window seat, which was the only surface in the room without paper on it.

"The clerk's search came back," said Karis. "You heard."

"I heard. Same places. Same nothing."

"Same nothing." She let out a long breath at the ceiling. "Do you know what that file is now? It isn't mine. That's the whole point of it. At Greyvane it was mine. I'd dug it up, and people believed it partly because I was standing there ready to answer for it. Take me away and you took half the reason they believed. Bracken's spent four days taking me out of it. One link at a time. Every place my hand was, there's a seal now, and a librarian's name, and a clerk who never met me." She opened her eyes. "I could fall off the bluff tomorrow and the enrollment would hold without me."

"That's a grim thing to say about a good afternoon."

"It's the *best* thing anybody could say about an afternoon," said Karis, with great firmness, and closed her eyes again. "I've been waiting all my life for a piece of work that didn't need me. Do you know how tired you get, being the only person who can explain a thing? "Of course he wouldn't." Karis sounded faintly scandalised. "You're the subject. The subject doesn't carry his own file. That's the first thing anybody learns." What he had found in the library, by then, was nine names out of eleven.

It was quicker work than he had feared. The Compact printed its findings, every chartered house was bound to keep a set, and every finding named its panel at the foot. Halcenvane kept them in a long bay on the library's second floor, bound by the year in brown cloth, with an index at the back of every volume that nobody had touched since it was printed. Delegation counsel had a long run of findings on enrollment basis behind her, and a habit, plain once he had read six of them in a row, of attacking where a document came from before she ever asked what it said. Then the records officer.

*Havel.*

For a long moment he did nothing at all but look at it.

He had met Havel twice. The second time was at Greyvane, in the hearing room. The same man had sat at the end of the delegation's table with a recorder's case open on his knees and a little brass sand-glass beside him, turning the glass every quarter of an hour and writing down every word that anybody said, including all of Cael's.

Now he was records officer on an Archmarshal's manifest, which was another grade, and a grade above the last one.

Cael wrote on the page, at the top, *Havel*, and under it, after a while: *He keeps going up a grade, and he keeps turning up wherever I am. I've never decided whether that's chance. I'm not going to decide tonight.*

He showed the page to Lira at supper, folded back so that only Havel's lines showed. She read it with her spoon in her other hand.

"The pear one," she said.

"The pear one."

"You told me about him the same night. You said he wanted the pear and wouldn't let himself." She gave the page back. "And then he sat at Greyvane and wrote down every word you said for the whole of the hearing, and never once looked as if he liked or minded any of it."

"That's him."

"Careful men go up," said Lira, and went back to her soup. "That's all going up is, mostly. Somebody notices you've never once been wrong in writing." She considered that. "It doesn't tell you what he's careful *for*."

He wrote that under the rest, because it was the better question.

The senior evaluation seat was the last of the nine, and he already knew her name before he found it.

*Assessor Ilsev.*

The library held six of her findings. He read all six through on the second afternoon, and then read them again that evening, although he had known by halfway through the first reading that he would not need a second. She had sat the senior evaluation on him the year he Kindled, in a district office with a wall so thin he had heard the next room's clerk sneeze. She had sat the senior seat at Greyvane. In all six findings she did one thing, over and over, with a kind of quiet ferocity that did not show until you put them side by side. She read the text she was given all the way to the end, and went where it went, even when the room did not want to follow.

At Greyvane the text had gone in his favour, and she had followed it there in front of a warden. He wrote that down. Underneath he wrote that a woman who followed text wherever it went would follow it against him just as readily, on the day it went that way, and that he could not tell, from six findings, whether she was the gravest danger on that list or the best hope on it.

*Probably both,* he wrote. *Probably those aren't different.*

That left one name.

He went looking for it on the third afternoon with the slip in his hand and the whole of the long bay to himself. He went through the indexes for eleven years, carefully, the way he would have counted a crowd. Then he went through them again. Then he went down to the main floor and asked the duty librarian for the general catalogue, which covered the whole library and not just the findings, and went through that.

There was nothing.

It was not a thin file. A thin file is a shape; it tells you that someone was there and did little. This had no shape at all. No panel had ever been printed with him on it. He had signed nothing the library owned: not a finding, not an opinion, not so much as a note on procedure or a circular to the house.

In the end he went and found Karis, because Karis read faster than he did and wanted things less.

She came up to the long bay with ink still faint on her knuckles and went through every index he had been through, and the general catalogue after them, and then, without being asked, the visitors' books for the last forty years, which were kept in a press in the corner and smelled of mice. It took her most of the evening, and she came back to his table with three slips.

"Three," she said. "In the whole library. A list of the officers present at a dedication, eleven years ago, down the coast. A list of seats at a dinner given for a retiring magistrate. And a list of those who stood witness at a swearing-in." She laid them in a row. "Every one of them is his name in a list. Not one of them is a word he said."

"So he doesn't write."

"He writes. Of course he writes." She sat down across from him. "Assessors' work ends up in print. That's why Ilsev is on our shelves six times over. His doesn't. It goes into drawers that only open for people a long way above us, and we'll never see the inside of one. I can't find what he thinks, because nobody below that desk is allowed to read it."

"Then I can't build a page."

"No," said Karis, after a moment. "You can't build a page."

He took a fresh sheet out of the observation notebook and wrote the name at the top of it, and sat looking at it for a long time with the library going dark round them. Karis did not hurry him. Somewhere below, the duty librarian was walking the floors with her keys, closing the presses one by one.

At last he wrote one line under the name and left the rest of the page empty.

*Every opponent I've ever had, I read on paper before I read them on the floor. Every one. The shelves have nothing on this one. I'll have to read him standing up.*

Karis read it upside down across the table, which she did with everything, and did not pretend otherwise.

"Standing up," she said. "On a tier somewhere, I suppose, when they come up the road."

"Wherever there's a clean line."

She was quiet for a little, turning one of the three slips over and back.

"You go thin when you read hard," she said. "You know that. It's in your own ledger, in your own hand. Reading, thinking, anything that takes all of you. That's when the hold slips, because the hold needs a corner of you, and when you read hard you don't leave any corners."

"I know."

"The day those carriages come up, you're going to want to read every soul in them at once, with everything you've got." She laid the slip down. "Take Lira with you."

"Why Lira?"

"Because she'll be watching you, and not them," said Karis. "Somebody ought to be."

He did not argue. He wrote *Lira* at the foot of the empty page, small, under the one line, and closed the notebook, and they went down together through the dark stacks to the stair, where the duty librarian stood waiting with her keys and an air of great patience.

# Chapter 40 — Temperature

Lira found it first, which was fitting, since half of it was hers.

She was standing in front of the main board under the covered walk's north arch when Cael came through at the change of the second bell on Fifth-day, with her weight on the good hip and her hands in her coat pockets, quite still among the people going past. She was not reading, because Lira never read a thing twice; she read it once and had it. She was doing sums.

"Look," she said, without turning round. "They've put us next to each other."

All season the left half of the main board had belonged to the Copper column, ninety-one names long in the porter's white paint, with Lira on the top line and the brass stud on the line below it, beside Fiske. The right half was where notices went to be ignored. That morning somebody from the wing had taken down a warning about lamp oil and a lost glove and pinned up a single printed sheet in their place, so that it hung level with the season's dates, a hand's breadth away.

"*Copper column. Semifinals: the ninth and the tenth of Reaping. Final: the Crown yard, the nineteenth. Panel: three faculty. Witnessed.*"

"That's me."

"It doesn't say your name."

"It doesn't need to," said Cael. "There's only one."

The last time a pinned sheet on a board had carried a date that was about him, it had been at Greyvane, in a narrow passage with a draught in it, and the date had been a hearing, and his name had been printed out in full above it in the registry's square type. He had been fifteen. He had stood in front of it and understood that a system had written him into its calendar without asking, and he had thought at the time that nothing a board could say would ever be worse.

He looked for that feeling now, honestly, the way you press a bruise to see whether it is still there. It was not there.

Neither date on this board was aimed at anybody. The final's had been chalked into the margin since the brackets went up, by a registrar who had never heard of a notice. The bluff's temperature went up for three days, and it went up in small ugly ways, as the temperature of a place does when it is frightened and cannot say so.

The first sign was a rumour. It started in the Iron bracket the day after the reading and had worked its way down to the Copper benches by supper: the inspection had been asked for. Another academy, jealous of Halcenvane's season, had written to the Compact to complain about the provision enrollment, and this was the answer. By the second morning the rumour had grown a name. It was a river academy two days downstream, which had lost three good Stone students to Halcenvane the year before and had never forgiven it. By the third morning there were people in the refectory who could tell you who had signed the letter, and what colour the wax was.

"There isn't any letter," said Karis, at breakfast, with the marbled book open beside her bowl. "A complaint from one house about another goes to the regional office and comes back as an ordinary visit with two officers and a ledger. You don't get eleven names and an Archmarshal for a quarrel about Stone students. Nobody who knows anything believes it."

"Nobody who knows anything is spreading it," said Lira.

"That's the trouble with rumours," said Brom, through bread. "The people telling them never know anything. That's how you can tell they're rumours."

The second sign came at Bracken's counter, on the afternoon of the second day.

Cael was in the queue with a library request of Karis's. Ahead of him stood two second-years from the law range, a tall one and a short one, who had plainly come together so that neither would have to ask alone. When the short one reached the counter he laid both hands flat on it.

"Registrar," he said, "we wanted to ask. If the delegation found against an enrollment, say a provision enrollment, would the enrollment be suspended? While they decided? Before there was a finding?" He went a little pink. "We're asking about the law."

Bracken looked at him for a moment over the top of the counter.

"No," he said. "Not by a delegation and not by the chancellor. An enrollment stands until a finding is filed against it and answered in the proper forum, and then it stands until that forum rules. That is the law, and you may cite me." He made a small note on a slip, which might have been anything. "Is there something else you'd like to ask me?"

There was a pause in which it was clear to everybody in the queue that there was, and that it was not about the law.

Bracken did not wait for it, but looked past the two second-years, down the queue, to Cael.

"Enrollee," he said. "Miss Karis's request. Box nine of the founding-era copies, up from the store by the fourth bell." He held the stamped slip out across the counter in exactly the voice he used for every slip he had ever handed anyone, a voice with nothing in it at all. "Initial it, please."

Cael stepped up and initialled it. The two second-years stood aside to let him, and looked at him, and looked at Bracken, who had already gone back to his ledger. The third sign was a first-year.

He was a Mire boy, young even for a first-year, with ink on his collar, and Cael met him in the covered walk on the morning of the third day. The boy stopped dead in front of him. It was the fame problem again, come back in a new coat.

Cael had lived with it since the bridge. On the fourth morning Withrow spoke to the whole of Halcenvane in the lecture range's great hall. Karis timed it by the clock over the east door, and it took eleven minutes.

The hall had benches for six hundred, and Cael guessed five hundred in it: faculty in the front rows, then every year of enrollee in rising tiers behind them, more people than he had ever seen in one room on the bluff. He stood at the back by the east door with Lira and Brom, where the latecomers stood. He had decided beforehand not to read the room. A hall of five hundred was exactly the kind of crowd that pulled all of him outward into the reading of it, and he had a hold to keep. So he gave himself one thing to watch, and chose the clock.

Withrow walked out to the front without anybody announcing her and stood there with nothing in her hands.

"I'm told that clock runs two minutes slow," she said. "I'll be done before it notices."

She gave them exactly as her counsel had given them round the long table, in the same order and with the same plainness: early, and nobody had said why; large, eleven where two would do; and at the head of it, an officer of a rank that did not come to academies. She did not dress any of it, and she did not soften it either. She laid the three things out in front of five hundred people like three stones on a table and let them look.

"Now," she said. "What follows from that? For you, nothing new. I have no instruction to give you that I haven't given every week this term. Go to your lectures. *No undisclosed capability observed.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

He knew the words a reader would write under that. He had written them himself, once, at a long table up the coast, under a colleague's stack that had looked very like this one, and the colleague had been brought home inside the month.

*Complete, consistent, and containing nothing.*

Nobody needed to suspect anything to write that. The sum wrote it.

"She asked us not to. All of us. At breakfast." Karis did not take her eyes off the boards. "Say it in your head."

The safety instructor stood at the table beside the sand-glass. Withrow sat in the west gallery, upright and white-haired, alone this time. In the faculty box the grey-bearded man from the law range was already leaning forward with his elbows on the rail.

At the word, Fiske did not give ground.

That was the first thing, and the whole yard saw it and made a sound, a low surprised breath, as a crowd does when a familiar piece of music goes wrong in the second bar. All season Fiske had opened every bout the same way, stepping back off her line to buy herself a minute of learning. She had nothing to learn today. She took one step forward instead, and put her first declaration into the boards at once, a yard to the left of Lira's chalk, flat and fast, and the patch of oak there went slick.

Then a second, a yard to the right.

Lira did not burst. She walked. She went forward between the two slick patches on her own feet, at an ordinary pace, straight down the one lane Fiske had left open. Cael saw instantly that the lane was a lie. It ran straight at Fiske, and it got narrower as it went, and the third declaration was already going down across the far end of it.

Lira saw it too, a step later than he did. She stopped. She stood in a narrowing strip of honest wood with Fiske's slick on both sides of her and across her front, and every inch of the strip had been drawn, in eleven seconds, by a woman who knew exactly where Lira's feet liked to go and had simply removed all of those places before Lira got there.

"Burst," said Brom, very low. "Burst, burst."

She did not. She went sideways on her own feet, short, toward the last open edge. Fiske was already there. Her flat hand touched Lira's shoulder before Lira's foot had settled.

"Force," said the table. "One."

On the tiers the breath went out of eight hundred people at once. Somebody behind Cael said, satisfied, *well, that's that*.

Karis wrote one line in her notebook. Cael read it upside down: *One. F. needs no minute now. L. spends nothing.*

"Why won't she burst?" said Brom. "Why didn't she—"

"Because she's counting," said Cael. "She's got so many, and the hip's got so many, and they're not the same number. She won't spend one on an exchange she can't win."

"She's one down."

"I know."

The second exchange was over almost before it had begun.

At the word, Lira burst.

She did not burst away from anything. There was nothing yet to burst away from; the boards between them were clean, because Fiske had not had time to put anything on them. She burst straight forward from the chalk at the word itself, one half-breath of Wind, and the landing beat set her down two strides from Fiske, on clean oak, before Fiske's first declaration had finished gathering.

Fiske had seen it coming. Of course she had; she had seen everything coming all season. But seeing a thing coming and having somewhere to put a slick floor in front of it are not the same. Lira had come in before there was any floor to close. Fiske turned the half-built declaration into a plain short delivery instead, the way a carpenter turns a half-cut joint into a different joint, and it caught Lira on the outside of the left arm, hard, and Lira's right hand was already flat on Fiske's ribs.

"Wind," said the table. "One each."

The yard came up off the benches and sat down again.

"There," said Brom, with his fists against his mouth. "There, there."

Cael was not looking at the touch. He was looking at Lira's leading hip as she walked back to her chalk, and at the way she put her weight down through it, carefully, as if testing a stair in the dark.

One burst gone. She had come in at the word with the cheapest burst there was, the one with no evasion in it, and it had bought her a touch. But Fiske had seen it, and Fiske would not let it work twice.

Karis wrote: *Two. L. goes before there is anything to go round. One each. That door's shut now too.*

Lira walked back to her chalk and did not look at the hip, because looking at it would have told the north end of the boards everything.

It was not bad yet: only the old line, crest to inner knee, lit up thin and bright from the one burst, the way a wire lights when you run too much through it. She could carry that; she had carried it on the foundry floor for two winters with a ladle of slag in her hands. What she could not carry was not knowing what came next, and she did not know, and neither, she was fairly sure, did Fiske.

Fiske was at her chalk with her hands behind her back, looking at the boards.

She knows everything I showed her, Lira thought. She's got the sequence and all four lies. She's got the half-step. She's got the walk-in from the foundry. She's had a month with all of it, and she's the best on this hill at learning a person. And I just showed her a new thing, and she's had it for thirty breaths, and that's long enough for her.

So: nothing new. Nothing at all. Let her wait for it.

At the word, Fiske laid a line.

Not a patch. A line: one long narrow declaration straight down the middle of the boards, from Fiske's chalk toward Lira's, slick as glass, so that the straight road between them was gone before Lira's weight had come off her heels. There would be no coming in at the word again. Fiske had shut that door the moment she saw it open.

Lira did not move.

She stood on her chalk and let Fiske look at her. She gave her nothing to read: no lean, no shift, no breath held too long. It cost. The hip wanted to be moving; standing still on it was its own kind of load. She stood anyway, and watched Fiske's eyes, and saw them go to the places on the boards where Lira had always liked to go, the arc on the left, the fold, the turn, one after another, like a clerk checking that every door on a corridor was locked.

Then Fiske began to close them, one by one, without waiting.

That was new. Last time Fiske had placed where Lira was going. Now she was placing where Lira might go, all of it, every likely place on the boards, before Lira had chosen any of them. A patch on the left. A patch at the fold. A patch where the turn would have come. It was expensive, since every declaration cost Fiske something, and she was spending them like a woman throwing coins into a well. But every one she spent made the floor smaller.

Lira waited until there were three places left, and then two.

When the ring closed to the size of a hearthrug she burst out of it. She went backward, the one way she never went, over the last narrow gap between two patches, and the landing beat set her down on clean wood a stride beyond the ring with her heel a finger's breadth from Fiske's slick. The hip took it. Something in the hip went bright and hot and stayed that way.

Two, she thought. That's two.

Fiske looked at her across the boards, and Lira saw her count it too.

They spent the rest of the glass like that. Fiske closed and Lira waited; Fiske closed again and Lira slid one foot an inch on honest oak and Fiske had to decide whether the inch meant anything. Neither of them came near enough to touch. Up on the tiers somebody began a slow handclap and was hushed by the people around him. Lira did not hear it, because she was listening to the hip, and to the sand.

"Even," said the table. "No touch. Third exchange."

One each, and one even. One to go.

Lira walked back to her chalk on a leg that had begun, very slightly, to argue with her about which way was down.

The arithmetic of the fourth was simple, and everybody in the yard knew it. If the fourth ended even, the bout was drawn, and a drawn bout kept the holder on the top line, and the holder was Lira. Fiske could not wait. Fiske had to take it.

Which meant, Lira thought, standing on the chalk, that for the first time in their two bouts Fiske was the one who had to come.

So let her come. Don't burst. Don't run anything. Make the floor hers and stand in the middle of it, and make her walk across it.

At the word, Fiske made the floor hers.

She did it quickly and completely, with a kind of terrible patience. A patch, and a patch, and a patch, closer each time, round Lira in a slow spiral, until Lira stood on a square of clean oak about the size of a table, with Fiske's slick on every side of it, gleaming faintly in the afternoon light like ice on a pond. There was no way off it on foot. A burst would take her off it, and Fiske knew exactly how many bursts the hip had left, because Fiske could count as well as anybody, and the answer was one, perhaps, and a bad one.

Then Fiske stopped.

She stood at the edge of her own slick, a long stride away, with her hands loose at her sides, and she waited.

Lira understood. Fiske was not going to come across the oil to her. She had built a cage and put Lira in it and now she was going to wait at the bars for Lira to spend the last burst getting out, and then hit her in the landing beat, where everybody in the Copper column now knew a Wind fighter stood still. And if Lira did not burst, Fiske would wait until the sand was nearly gone and then come, at the last moment, when there would be no time left for anything but whatever Lira had kept.

It was the right plan, and Lira would have made the same one.

The sand ran.

It was the longest minute she had ever stood through.

She stood on the table of clean oak and did not burst. The hip burned. It had gone past bright now into something dull and deep and wide, a heat that filled the whole socket and spread down into the thigh, and it wanted, every moment, to be shifted. She did not shift it. Any shift was a lean, and a lean was a word, and Fiske was reading every word. So she stood on it, square, the way she had stood at the board at the fifth bell for three weeks with her breathing held level, and let it burn.

On the tiers the noise had stopped, and eight hundred people sat without moving. She could hear the sand. She could hear, from a long way off, a barge on the river giving its long note, and nobody answering.

Fiske waited. Her face did not change. But Lira, watching her eyes, saw them go once, quickly, to the glass on the table, and come back.

That's your tell, Lira thought, with a strange clear calm. You've never had to watch the sand before. You've always won before the sand mattered.

With perhaps ten breaths left in the glass, Fiske came.

She came straight, as she had come at the end of their first bout: one plain heavy delivery, built and committed, the Force that every other fighter on the sheet used, coming across her own slick on feet that knew exactly where the oil lay. There was nowhere to go from it. Lira could not step back; behind her was slick. She could not step aside; slick. She could burst, and land, and stand still in front of the one woman on the bluff who hit still things best.

She went forward, onto the oil.

Not with Wind. On her feet. She stepped off the edge of her clean square onto Fiske's slick on purpose, the leading foot first, and let it go.

The casting floor at the foundry had been greased with tallow at every shift change, so that the moulds would slide, and every apprentice who had ever worked it had gone down hard on it in the first week, and every one who stayed had learned the trick. You did not fight a slick floor. Fighting it was what put you on your back. You let it carry you, low, with your weight over your knees and your arms loose, and you steered with the ball of the foot and you stopped yourself with the other one, where you chose, on something that held.

Lira let it carry her.

She went in under Fiske's delivery low and sliding, faster than any step, and the delivery went over her shoulder with a noise like a door slamming in a high wind. She had perhaps a body's length of slick and then Fiske's own feet, and Fiske's own patch of honest oak where she had been standing to build. Lira's back foot found the edge of it. She stopped herself there.

The stop went through the leading hip like a nail.

It did something in there she had never felt it do. Something gave, deep and sideways, with a small sound she heard inside her own head rather than with her ears. Her right hand was already moving. It landed flat on Fiske's ribs, under the arm, inside the recovery, as Fiske's weight was still going forward over the place where Lira had been.

"Wind," said the table, into a silence like a held breath. "Two to one. Bout."

She did not hear the yard come up. She heard it afterward, the way you hear thunder after the flash, a wall of noise from every tier at once that hit the lecture range and came back. In the moment she heard nothing but her own breath, and felt nothing but the floor under her right hand, because she had gone down on one knee on the oak at the edge of Fiske's patch and could not, for the moment, get up.

Fiske was standing over her, and her face had changed.

It was not a beaten face, of which Lira had seen plenty. It was the face Fiske had worn on the step a month ago, when she had said all the things she had carried for two years, and Lira had known then what it was, and knew now.

"You didn't run it," said Fiske. "Not once."

"You'd have shut it."

"Every step." Fiske looked down at the slick going dull and dry on the boards around them, fading back into plain oak. "You stood still for a minute in my yard. Nobody's ever done that."

"It's not your yard," said Lira, from her knee. "Not today."

Fiske held out her hand. Lira took it, and Fiske did not pull. She only held it steady, so that Lira could use it to stand, and Lira used it, and stood, and found that the leading leg would take her weight if she asked it politely and not otherwise.

"The nineteenth," said Fiske. "I'll be on the tier for it. I haven't sat on a tier for a final in two years. I'm told you can see everything from up there."

"You can't see the far mark from the east side," said Lira. "Sit north."

Fiske considered this gravely, as she considered everything.

"North," she said, and let go of Lira's hand, and walked away along the foot of the tiers with her hands clasped behind her back.

# Chapter 43 — Nobody Talks About Hips

Cael thought about Merrick's advice all morning, and in the end he sat where he always sat.

It had taken him most of breakfast to see it properly. Merrick had stood on the gallery stair two days ago with his grave courteous face and told him that Brom looked for him on the tier before every bout, and found him, and only then began. Then Merrick had advised him to sit somewhere else, somewhere Brom would have to look for him.

It had sounded like kindness, and Cael had carried it about for two days as kindness. It was only at breakfast, watching Brom eat porridge with the steady attention of a man stoking an engine, that he turned it the other way up and saw the shape underneath. If Cael sat somewhere else, Brom would look up at the usual place before the word and not find him there. Then he would look for him. He would look for perhaps two breaths, perhaps four, and every one of those breaths would be a breath in which Brom was thinking about the tier instead of the boards. Merrick had not offered advice. Merrick had offered a feint, and offered it to the one person on the bluff who would carry it straight to Brom without knowing.

"He's very good," Cael said to Karis, on the way to the yard.

"Who is?"

"Merrick. He tried to move me."

Karis considered this. "And you're not going to be moved."

"I'm going to sit exactly where I always sit," said Cael, "and be the most boring thing on the east tier."

So he sat where he always sat, a third of the way up the east tier, with Karis on his right and the space on his left empty, because Brom was on the boards today and not beside him. Lira had refused to stay in bed. She sat on the bottom step of the tier, below them, with the leading leg straight out in front of her along the stone and a folded coat under the knee, and nobody on the bluff was foolish enough to say anything about it.

Brom came out and walked to his chalk at the south end. Before he crouched, he lifted his head and looked up at the east tier, straight at the usual place, and found Cael there in about the time it takes to blink. Something in his shoulders settled. Then he crouched and laid his wrapped right hand on the oak and stood, and was looking at Merrick, and nothing else.

Merrick, at the north chalk, saw it. Cael watched him see it: a small grave nod, to nobody, like a man noting that a door he had tried was locked.

The semifinal ran on the ladder's terms, not the final's, since neither of them held the top line: first to three clean touches, as many exchanges as it took.

Merrick did not feed cheap strikes, and Brom had been right about that. He came off his chalk at the word with a real strike, honestly weighted, at the left shoulder, and Brom took it square on the forearm and the shoulder behind it without turning, as Rooke had built him to, and went forward through it at once, hard, driving while the struck side was still hard.

He drove into nothing. Into less than nothing. A yard in front of his chest the air went flat and bright for an instant, like a sheet of glass catching the light, and Brom's drive hit it and stopped dead, as though he had run into a door. Merrick had set a pane. He had set it not between himself and Brom but in the line Brom's counter would take, a breath before the counter came, and Brom had driven his whole weight into it and bounced. In the half-beat while he was still finding his feet, Merrick's open hand came round the edge of the pane and touched him on the ribs.

"Shield," said the table. "One."

Brom went back to his chalk shaking his head like a man who has walked into a low beam.

"Oh," said Karis softly. "Oh, that's clever."

"He's learned the repair," said Cael. "He's been learning it all season, from three feet away. He knows Brom won't turn any more. So he isn't trying to make him turn. He's waiting for the drive, and putting a wall where the drive goes."

"Can Brom see the pane coming?"

"There's a tell." Cael kept his eyes on Merrick. "Every Shield fighter on the sheet has the same one. Breath, plant, pane. They take a breath and plant the back foot, and the pane comes a beat after. The Shield fifth-year's was so slow that juniors practised on him. Merrick's is fast. But it's there."

The second exchange Brom took. He absorbed Merrick's strike and drove, and drove at an angle this time, off the line, so that Merrick's pane went up a foot to the left of where he was going, and Brom went past the edge of it and touched Merrick on the hip with his whole forearm.

"Iron Skin. One each."

The third went back to Merrick. He set two panes this time, one at either angle, a narrow corridor of bright air with only the straight road open between them. Brom drove straight because straight was all there was, and Merrick was waiting at the end of the corridor with his weight already back and his hand already up, and the touch came off Brom's shoulder clean.

"Shield. Two to one."

On the tiers somebody whistled. One more and the bout was Merrick's, and the season was, for Brom, over.

Brom walked back to his chalk slowly, his eyes nowhere near the tier. He stood on the chalk for a long breath with his eyes on the floor in front of Merrick's feet, and Cael, watching him, saw him stop looking at Merrick's hands and start looking at Merrick's back foot.

At the word, Merrick came in with his strike. Brom took it on the forearm. And this time, instead of driving the moment he had taken it, he watched. He watched for no longer than a heartbeat. Merrick took his breath and planted his back foot, and in the beat between the plant and the pane, Brom went.

It was the stall. It was the old two-year stall, the gap between taking and going that every good fighter on the bluff had found in him, the gap Rooke had spent a season and nineteen exercises shrinking until Cael could barely see it from the gallery. "Iron Skin. Two each."

"You keep them," said Lira. "Till the nineteenth. You keep things."

Karis took the bundle in both hands, as if it were a document, and looked at the knot with professional disapproval, and did not retie it.

"Receipt," she said, and wrote one line in the marbled book, and put the batons under her chair.

Cael had said nothing all this time; he was down by the cold grate, cross-legged, the notebook closed in his lap. Lira, who missed nothing, turned her head to him.

"You've got a face on," she said. "Out with it."

"It isn't mine to say."

"Two years you've been putting the pair of us in that book. On the nineteenth you'll be sitting up there knowing more about what's happening on those boards than anybody in the yard, the panel included." She shifted the leg on the settle. "You've earned a sentence. Say it."

He thought about which sentence. There were a great many in the notebook, and nearly all of them were about feet.

"On the nineteenth," he said slowly, "every person up on those tiers is going to watch a Copper final, because that's what the board says it is. And it isn't one. It's two Iron-equivalent practitioners nobody has ever let fight anyone their own size, getting to do it at last, in a bracket that was never built with either of you in mind." He turned the notebook over on his knee. "I've tried to write that down all season. It always comes out sounding like a letter home. It isn't. It's just what it is."

Brom said, "Huh." For Brom, that was a speech.

"And I can't be on that sheet," said Cael. "So I'll be on the tier. I used to mind that. I've stopped. Somebody up there ought to know what it's costing the two of you to be on those boards, and I'm the only one who does. So that's my job on the nineteenth. I'll do it properly."

Lira leaned over and jabbed him in the ribs with the end of the stick. With Lira, that meant the talk had been a good one and was now over.

"Nine days," said Lira. "Nobody talks about hips."

Karis stayed after the other two had gone up. She stayed at the long table with the marbled book shut and the tied batons under her chair, and when Cael got up to go she said, without looking round, "Sit down a minute. I want to ask you three things."

He sat.

She took her slate out of her satchel, the small one she used for sums she did not want to keep, and drew two lines down it with the chalk, so that it had three columns.

"What does the panel want from you on the twentieth?" she said.

"Growth."

"Why?"

"Because the provision isn't a door you walk through once." He had never put it like that before, and found that he liked it. "It's a door you have to walk through every term. Each time you have to be bigger than the time before, or it shuts. Gault took my baseline in the first month so that this term would have something to be measured against. If I stand in front of him on the twentieth exactly the size I was then, I haven't kept the terms."

Karis wrote *GROWTH* at the head of the first column.

"What does the room want?"

"The panel—"

"Not the panel. The room. Everybody in it with a pen and a reason to be there." She tapped the slate. "The gallery."

He thought about it.

"Not to be surprised," he said. "Not less from me. That's what I keep getting wrong. They don't need me to show less than I've got. They need everything I show to be the next ordinary step from something already on paper. As far as four supervised hours a week for a term would carry anybody. In the direction the supervision would push. Nothing anybody in the gallery has to find a new word for."

*CONTROL*, wrote Karis, over the second.

"And what does the record let you show?"

"Five." He said them as she wrote. "The Wind. The Pressure. The Iron read. The Compression. The Ember. They're all on paper somewhere already. Vell has them in her ledger. Greyvane's panel noted them. The transcript has them. And none of the sixth. Not a flicker. If the gallery sees a thing on that floor, it has to have seen it on paper first."

She wrote *FIVE* at the head of the third column, and under it, small, *and not one more*. Then she turned the slate round on the table so that it faced him, and put the chalk down, and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "Show me where they fight."

He looked at the slate.

He had been braced for a week, he realised, for exactly this question. He had been expecting the three to pull against each other, growth against caution, caution against honesty, and he had been bracing himself to choose between them and to dislike whatever he chose. He looked for the place where they fought, and went on looking.

There wasn't one.

"They don't," he said.

"No," said Karis. "I didn't think they did. I wanted you to find it, though, and not me. You'd only have argued with me."

He sat with the slate in front of him for a long time.

The banking doctrine had ridden in him for two years like a flinch. *Show only what the record already holds.* It had started as one, at fourteen, in a Denvash hall, and for two years it had felt like one every time he used it: shoulders up, a little ashamed, a thing done in defence. And here it was on Karis's slate in three words of chalk, and it was not a flinch at all but a plan. The panel would get a real term of growth, honestly measured, in five things that were already on paper, done better than anyone in that room expected him to do them. Every bit of it would be true.

"I'm not hiding anything they're entitled to," he said slowly. "And I'm not showing anything that isn't real. It's the same thing. It's the same job."

"Yes," said Karis.

"That's never happened before."

"No," said Karis, more gently. "I don't suppose it has." She wiped the slate clean with her sleeve, all three columns at once, and put it back in her satchel. "Write it down tonight. Before it starts feeling like a trick."

At the window, with Hesk's pen, he did.

*Karis's three columns: growth, control, five. Asked me where they fought. They don't. For once the hiding and the honesty don't pull apart. They're one job. It feels so good I'm suspicious of it. Writing that down too.*

Then the part that had no column.

*Quenna, at Greyvane, asked whether I could do the harder thing: whether you can* not *do it. In front of people who wish you harm. I said I didn't know. True then: one reach is a story, not a record.*

*Now I've reached twice. I own a thing that never switches off. And there's a date on a board.*

*The twentieth. She'll get her answer, though she won't be there to hear it. So will I.*

Hall three had frightened him to the bone once before, on the day of the pin. The next afternoon it did it again.

It was an ordinary supervised hour. The wing sent somebody to watch two of his four hours each week, because the provision said that floor time bound to an evaluation had to be witnessed, and the somebody was nearly always the same quiet woman from the wing. She kept to the north rail with a board on her knee and a pencil, and in half a year Cael had never once seen her look surprised. Brom was his partner, as he usually was on Third-days. They were running a redirect sequence at half pace round the north bay on the new pins: Brom fed, Cael took the feed and turned it, Brom fed again from the new angle, round and round, a pattern they had worked so often that it had worn a faint pale track into the timber.

It was the sort of work that wanted every part of a person. He had chosen it in the first month for exactly that reason, because the way to learn a sequence was to give it all of you. He had not thought about that choice since.

On the fourteenth turn the sequence took the last corner of him, and he did not feel it go.

That was what frightened him afterward, more than anything. There was no lurch. There was no spike behind the eyes and no warning of any kind. There was only the sequence, which was going beautifully, better than it had gone all week. Brom's feeds were arriving exactly where he wanted them and his own turns were coming out clean and early, and the hold, for the first time in days, was costing him nothing. He noticed that, dimly, as a pleasant thing, the way you notice a room is warm.

Then Brom stopped.

He stopped in the middle of a feed with his guard still up and his weight still set and the Iron read still running in his forearms. His eyes were open and turned toward the place where Cael stood. For a moment, a long strange moment, they were not quite on him. They were searching a little to one side of him, the way a man's eyes search a room he has walked into in the dark.

Then they found him.

"You went," said Brom, very quietly.

Cael did not move.

At the north rail the woman from the wing was writing on her board. She had been writing on it, Cael saw, with a cold that went down through him into his boots, for the whole of the last minute. Her head was bent over it, and she did not look up. She turned a page and wrote on.

Two breaths, he thought. Three at most.

They went on to the bell at the posted half pace, and on the boards neither one spoke of it. Cael fed and turned and held his edges so hard that the ache rose behind his eyes like water behind a sluice. At the bell the woman from the wing signed both their sheets in her small neat hand, without comment, and went out with her board under her arm.

In the corridor Brom stopped dead in the middle of the flagstones, square across the way, as a post stands in a yard, so that Cael would have had to walk round him.

"How far."

"Thin," said Cael. "Not gone. Thin. She had her head down."

"Down *today*." Brom's voice stayed low. Shouting would have been easier to bear. "Nine days from now you're going to be on a floor for three hours. Gault, and the panel, and an Archmarshal, and whoever else they've brought with them. And somewhere in that gallery there'll be a person whose whole trade is noticing when a thing isn't where it should be. And you've just shown me that the one thing you can't steer goes thin when all of you is busy." He did not move out of the way. "What do you call a three-hour evaluation, then?"

Cael took all of it standing. Every word was true, and in two years Brom had never once said a hard thing that did not need saying.

"All of me, busy," he said.

"For three hours."

"Yes."

"Then it isn't a drill you've got wrong." Brom stepped aside at last, so that the corridor was open again. "It's the whole thing. You've had it at the foot of the page all month. It goes at the top."

It did, and he knew it before Brom had finished. Three nights ago he had written two lines at the foot of a page with a small cross beside them: thinking about somebody else felt cheap, and perhaps that was because he wasn't paying. The sequence had felt free for the same reason. Nothing in him rang when he stopped paying. Nothing ever would. He would have to be the bell himself.

That night the plan came apart and went back together, top to bottom.

The three columns stayed, because Karis's slate had been right. But above them, where the first thing goes, he wrote a line that was not a column, and ruled it off from everything under it.

*Hold. First. Before growth, before control, before the five. Nine days of it on every floor I stand on, and then three hours of it in front of the panel. Everything else stands on this, and if this goes, everything on top of it falls.*

Then, under the line, the thing he had understood in the corridor, set down as plainly as he could manage.

*The twentieth is two jobs at once. The one they watch has to look easy. The one under it has to last the whole three hours, and nobody in the room will ever see it, and nobody will thank me for it, because nobody will know it was there.*

*It's a bout. My opponent's a stranger, and the stranger's me. And the crowd won't know there's a fight on.*

# Chapter 44 — No Tells

On the morning after the file was closed, the woman who kept the wing's instruments came into the wing's outer office with her oil can and her brass rag at the third bell, as she did on the posted day of every month, and checked the instruments.

Cael was at the counter for a floor sheet and watched her do it. She took the long measuring rods down from their rack one at a time and sighted along each against the window. She opened the case of the pressure gauge and wiped the dial and wound it, and tapped the glass with one fingernail, and listened. She set the little brass weights from the wing's balance in a row on a cloth and touched each one with a clean rag. Then she took a printed card from her apron, the wing's schedule of checks, which had been pinned on the wall by Gault's door since the first week of term and had gone yellow at the corners, and initialled the day's line in pencil, and went away to her next room.

Nobody had told her to do it today. Nobody had told her not to. It was the posted day.

That was how the three days went. Cael watched them the way he watched a bout from the tier, without blinking at the wrong moment.

Nothing moved. That was all, and every bit of it was hard. A building full of frightened people, waiting for eleven strangers and a man with no stated business, did not want to stand still. It wanted to sweep its passages and re-ink its ledgers and move its awkward things out of sight, the way a household scrubs the front step before a visitor it is afraid of. Cael saw the want everywhere. He saw a Stone instructor stop in the second quadrangle with a broom she had picked up from somewhere, and look at it, and look at the flagstones, and then lean the broom carefully back against the wall where she had found it and walk away with her hands in her pockets. He saw two clerks from the bursar's office arguing in low voices at the foot of the lecture stair about whether the lamp-oil ledger ought to be copied fair before the twelfth, until one of them said *posted day*, and the other said *posted day*, and they went back up the stair together without copying anything.

The final stayed on the nineteenth, and the evaluation stayed on the twentieth. Rooke's cohort worked its ordinary hours at its ordinary marks in the east hall, and the supervision sheets went across the quadrangle to the registrar the same afternoon they were signed, as they had gone every afternoon since the night of the notice. The fire-watch boy stood on his bucket at the covered walk's north arch each morning and chalked the night's hours on his slate in his flat-footed figures. Withrow walked once round the first quadrangle at the fifth bell with her spectacles in her hair, as she had walked it every day of the season, and stopped at the same place under the clock to speak to the same porter about the same leaking gutter. In the registrar's outer office the four grey boxes sat on their shelf with their lettering turned to the room, and nobody touched them.

Withrow had told five hundred people not to perform. Then the whole of Halcenvane went three days without performing, and it was, Cael thought, standing at the covered walk's rail on the third evening, the hardest feat of athletics he had watched all season. Lira on her table of oak had held still for a minute with a leg on fire. This was a whole hill holding still for three days with nothing on fire at all, and it was harder, because there was no sand running and no crowd to see it.

He wrote it that night, the eve, by the low lamp.

*Three days of a whole hill not doing anything. It's harder than doing something. I know, because not doing anything is what I've done on my own account for two years, and I've never once seen anybody else try it. Withrow's bet is that a visit can only see what's been changed for it. So she's changed nothing, and made five hundred frightened people change nothing with her. I didn't expect the first person I ever watched play my own game to be a chancellor, and play it better.*

*Bumps: one, in hall three, the one Brom caught. Otherwise none for three days, and I've paid for every one. I know now what free feels like. Free is the thing to be frightened of.*

*They come tomorrow. Final in eight days. The twentieth, nine.*

Havel rode up the bluff road in the second carriage, facing backward, with the counsel's knees an inch from his own and the counsel talking.

She had talked since Ostrand bridge. She had talked, if he was honest, most of the way from the coast, four days of it. Her voice was low and pleasant and never rose and never stopped, and it went on about the inns, and the state of the road, and a case she had argued nine years before in which a schedule had turned out to have two different page eleven. She did not need answers. She needed somebody to aim at, and Havel, facing backward with his case on his knees, was the nearest thing in the carriage with ears. He had learned by the second day to nod at the end of her sentences and think about something else in the middle of them.

Beside the counsel, Assessor Ilsev sat with her own case on her own knees and her eyes shut. She was not asleep. Havel had sat beside her for four days now, and through the whole of the Greyvane hearing before that, and he had never once seen her asleep. She shut her eyes in carriages the way other people shut a book they had finished with, so that nobody would ask her what she thought of it.

The carriage leaned into the first bend of the bluff road, and Havel looked out of the window at the river falling away below.

He did it properly. He had been trained to arrive at a place properly, and he did it now out of a habit so old it no longer felt like a decision. First the road behind them: the bridge with its toll board, the town, the ferry landing on the near bank with its lamp post and two men in road-grey coats standing under it. He noted the two men. He noted them the way you note your own service's coats in a strange town, briefly and without staring, and looked away again, because a records officer who stares at a rotation from a carriage window is a records officer who will be asked by somebody, later, why. Then the road ahead: the bends, the wall, the gate. Then the slope of the hill itself, the roofs above the wall, the long range of grey stone along the top of the bluff with its tiers of seating, open to the sky, standing up against the morning like the side of a ship.

The Crown yard. The academy's papers had a plan of it, and he had studied the plan on the road.

He knew who would be up there.

He had known it since the coast. He had sat at the end of the delegation's table at Greyvane through every sitting, with his recorder's case open and his brass glass turning, and written down every word the boy said. That had included all of the schedule the boy had read aloud in a level voice that stopped at the end of every entry for exactly as long as a pen needed. Before that there had been a cold bright morning in the Unranked District at Ardenmere, a pump step, a form with four questions on it, and a fifth question that had not been on any form. And a pear, held out in a twist of paper, that he had wanted and had not taken. He still did not know why he had not taken it. He had thought about it more often since than a man ought to think about a pear.

A boy like that, on a morning like this, would want to see the delegation come in. He would want to see it from somewhere legitimate, where nobody could say he had no business to be. He would want a clean line down the last of the road and across the courtyard. He would want to be high, and empty, and early.

The upper tier of the Crown yard, then. The east end of it, most likely, where the morning sun would be behind him.

Havel looked at the plan of it in his head for a moment longer. Then he decided not to look up there.

It was a small decision, and he made it the way he made most of his decisions now, quickly and without enjoying it. A man who looks straight at the place where he knows someone is standing has told that person something: that he knew, and that he thought about it on the road, and that the knowing mattered to him. Havel did not want to tell the boy any of that. He was not sure it was true. He was not sure it was his to tell. And there was a correct way to arrive at an inspection, which was to look at the building, and the door, and the officer waiting at the door, and the ground. He would do it the correct way.

The carriage came up the last bend, and leveled, and went in under the gate.

He had risen twice since the pump step. He thought about that sometimes in the evenings, in the way a man counts coins he does not mean to spend. The first rise had come not long after Ardenmere. He had been taken off the district rota and given a recorder's case and a seat at the end of hearing tables, where the work was to write down exactly what was said, and nothing else, and to be thought careful. Records officer to a delegation was a grade that sat at the main table and not the end of it. It was a grade that handled the delegation's papers before the senior seat saw them.

Neither rise had come with an explanation, because promotions in the service did not. A sheet arrived with a new grade on it and a date, and you moved your desk.

Some things on the boy's file stood above his clearance, and he did not think about those in carriages. He had a notebook with a cardboard cover in his inside pocket, and in it, among dates that meant nothing, there were three short entries in pencil that he had written over two years and did not read in front of anyone. He did not take it out now. There was nothing to add to it this morning. There might never be. "Is he looking at you?"

"No," said Cael. "That's how I know he knows where I am."

Lira thought about that, and did not ask him to explain it. He was glad, because he was not sure he could have. He only knew that the upper tier was the one place on the bluff from which a person could see the whole courtyard without being in it. A careful man who had once refused a pear would have worked that out on the road. And a man who works out where you will be, and then keeps his eyes off it, has decided something. He did not know what, and he wrote nothing down about it. Some things you do not put in a notebook until you know what column they go in.

The second carriage gave up one more passenger.

She was small, and upright, and wore a dark coat, and she came down onto the cobbles and then stood quite still for the space of several breaths while everything in the courtyard went on moving round her. It was the stillness of a reader who has come to a heading and stops on it before going on. She had a plain brown case in her hand, with a strap. One of the clerks half reached for it as she passed him and then, very wisely, thought better of it. She carried it to the door herself.

Assessor Ilsev. She had read his Kindling across a desk in a district office with a wall so thin he had heard the next clerk sneeze. She had read a Greyvane hearing's exhibits to the last page and followed them where they went with a warden glaring at her across the table. Six of her findings were in his head, and he had still not settled whether she was the worst danger on that manifest or the best hope on it. Seeing her walk did not decide it. She went in at the door as if the building were a document and she had just found the first page.

---

The third carriage was the oldest of the three.

Its varnish had gone the colour of weak tea. Somebody had painted out the device on its door so carefully, and so long ago, that the shape of the old device still showed faintly through the paint, the way a word rubbed out of a page still shows when you tilt it to the light. Cael looked at that door and knew, before it opened, that the carriage had been chosen. Nobody arrives at the head of an eleven-name manifest in the shabbiest coach by accident. And he knew, in the same moment, that he would never be able to show anybody how he knew it.

The door opened.

And the count went wrong.

It did not come back empty. He knew empty; empty was a number too. It came back arguing with itself. In two years of counting rooms, his method had given him too little and too much and once, at Ardenmere, nonsense. It had never before given him three answers at once that would not agree.

*One.* The courtyard leaned. The two clerks from the first carriage were crossing toward the third before its door was fully open. The ranking rider of the escort moved four paces to his left without anyone telling him to. The counsel, at the records hall's door, stopped in the middle of a word about the gutter and stayed stopped. Everybody in that courtyard bent toward the third carriage the way grass bends toward a door that has blown open in a wind. Cael had seen people do that round Withrow every day of the season. He could have drawn it.

*Two.* The man who stepped down gave the leaning nothing to lean on.

He came down the step without touching the rail. He moved aside from the door so that the clerk behind him could get down. Then he stood on the cobbles with his hands at his sides, and the whole careful shape that had gathered itself round his carriage was left holding the air, the clerks and the rider standing in a ring round a man who was not using the ring. He did not wait to be attended. Nor did he make a show of not waiting, the way a certain kind of great man carries his own gloves so that everyone will see him carry them. He was simply there.

*Three.* His collar spoke, and the rest of him kept silent.

He wore the plain grey that inspectors wore on the road. At his collar was a mark Cael had seen nowhere, ever, on anybody. He had read of it once, in the appendix of marks at the back of a regulation book in Halcenvane's library: a narrow bar on a plain ground, no colour, no device, alone at the foot of the last page. Every other mark the Compact handed out was made to be read from across a room. That was the purpose of a mark. A warden's coat shouted before the warden did. Coss had shouted with his whole body, every day, in every room, and it was why Cael had been able to read him inside a week. This man's collar named a height in the service that had stopped a magistrates' counsel in the middle of a word. Nothing else about him reached for it.

He looked at the records hall's door. Cael counted two.

He lifted his eyes to the tiers. Cael counted two again, and somewhere in the middle of the second count the man's glance crossed the east end of the upper tier, and crossed Cael. It neither stopped nor hurried. It went over him at precisely the speed it went over the empty bench to his left, and the empty bench to his right, and Lira's stick on the rail. Then it moved on to the roofline, and the man walked in under the stone hood, and the door took him.

Cael's hands were flat on the cold stone of the rail.

He read the man anyway, with everything he had. An empty answer only counts if the question was really asked. He went through every layer of the gaze, the way he had with each watcher in turn since the very first. There had been Coss on a road and Coss in a hall, a young assessor at a pump step, the open coats on the Greyvane road who had let themselves be counted, a copying clerk at a gallery rail whose eyes went everywhere except to Cael.

Rhythm. The step down from the carriage and the steps across the cobbles came at exactly the same pace. Not tired, then. Not hurried. Nothing more.

Weight. Low, settled, declaring nothing. A man of that height in the service had surely carried a Path for most of his life, and Cael could not find it anywhere in the way he stood.

Breath. Too far to see.

Attention.

That was the one that stopped him. All season he had measured where other people's attention went, in yards and halls and on stairs. In all that time he had never once thought to turn the measure round and ask where one man's own attention went. He asked it now. The answer was *everywhere, at one speed.* The door had two. The tiers had two. The roofline, as the man turned, had two. An empty bench had exactly what Cael had.

It was not carelessness. Carelessness has a slackness to it, and there was nothing slack anywhere in that man. It was a reader running his finger along a page at one even speed from the first line to the last, so that nobody watching the finger could ever tell which line he cared about.

He had been doing a rough cousin of it himself for two years, sampling crowds he could not count. But he sampled to save effort. This man did it so as to give nothing away.

The last clerk went in under the stone hood, and the door shut. The riders led the horses off toward the stables, and a boy ran out for the baggage. In about the time it takes to tie a boot, the outer courtyard was only a courtyard again, with the sun lying across its swept south side.

"What?" said Lira, at his shoulder.

He did not answer at once.

"Everyone they've ever sent has had something showing," he said at last. "Every one. A want, or a fear, or an order they didn't like being given, or a trick. Even Seln. What Seln hides has edges to it, and I can feel along the edges." He took his hands off the stone. "Something always shows."

"And that one?"

"That one crossed a courtyard," said Cael.

Lira was quiet. Then she unhooked the stick from the rail and leaned on it, and looked at him sideways.

"You stayed," she said.

He looked at her.

"The whole time. You were reading him the way you read everybody, all of you at once. I could see it in your face. And you stayed exactly where you were." She tapped the stone with the end of the stick. "I was ready to tread on you. I never had to."

He had not known. He had set the stake before the carriages came, and minded it, and then a man in grey had come down a step and Cael had forgotten there was any stake at all. And it had held, because he had driven it in first, at the head of everything, before he tied anything else to it.

"I didn't know," he said.

"I did," said Lira. "That's why I came up."

He wrote one line that night. He had five others ready, and he struck every one of them out in his head before the pen reached the page, because each of them was a guess, and guesses had a column of their own at the back of the Log, and this was not it.

*New opponent. No tells. Begin.*

Then he sat a while longer by the low lamp. Below the bluff the river went on, and at the fourth hour the two grey coats at the ferry landing changed under their lamp, on the bell, as they had changed every night since the bridge. The delegation was inside. Somewhere in the long building under the stone hood, a small woman who read everything to the end was reading. The final was in seven days, the evaluation in eight. And the one man on the bluff he could not read had looked at him for exactly as long as he had looked at an empty bench, and gone indoors.

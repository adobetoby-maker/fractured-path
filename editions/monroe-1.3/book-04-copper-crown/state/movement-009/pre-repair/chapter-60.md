# Chapter 60 — The Door

From across the street, at the second bell, the registry station looked like nothing at all. It had been a grain factor's counting house once, before the Compact bought it, and it stood three streets back from the wharfs, where the town stopped smelling of fish and started smelling of rope.

Cael wrote that down first, standing in the doorway of a chandler's that had not yet opened, on the fifth of the new month, with a fine rain deciding whether to begin. Two storeys of brown stone. A doorstep hollowed like a bowl by a hundred years of grain buyers coming in to argue. Beside the door, a brass plate about the size of a book, which said what the building was in letters too small to read from the far side of the street. A rope-maker's on one side of it, with coils of new rope hung in the window like sleeping snakes, and on the other a shuttered house with a cat on its sill.

In his head he had made the place enormous. He had never once meant to, and he had never noticed doing it, but somewhere since his own Kindling every registry station in the world had become, in the back of his mind, a hall with a high roof and a long bench and a cold floor. And here was one of them, being a building, between a rope-maker and a cat.

Beside him Lira, kit bag on one shoulder, gave the building a long, unfriendly study.

"Smaller than Denvash," she said.

"Everything's smaller than Denvash."

"No. I mean it's small." She hitched the strap higher. "Fenmark had a hall. A great tall window you could see from the far end of the square, and a hall behind it where they kept you waiting in rows like loaves. There were forty of us. Every time an assessor came through the far door we all stood up, and every time he went out again we all sat down." Her voice carried none of it. That was how Cael knew the weight. "I stood up nine times that morning before they got to me."

"And this is a door."

"This is a door." Her eyes went to the hollowed step. "I think I prefer the door."

Nobody had asked who would come. At first light all four of them had simply been at the residence door with their coats on, and nobody had said anything about it, and they had gone down the bluff road together in the grey. At the road's foot the two men in grey coats had been changing on the bell. One of them had looked at Lira's kit bag for just as long as a man looks at a bag, and then at the river. In the town Karis had bought four hot rolls from a baker's hatch and handed them round without asking who wanted one, and they had eaten them walking, and Brom had paid the bridge toll for all of them, as he always did, before anybody else could get a coin out.

Bracken had come too. He wore his own plain coat instead of the registrar's gown, and he carried a leather satchel with Halcenvane's sponsorship file in it: the certified season record, the witnessed final, Gault's filing on the registry's long form with Ilsev's line under it, and three hundred sheets behind them in his numbered order. He had carried it down the hill himself and would not let Brom take it.

"Ninety seconds," he said at the curb. "That's my part. I put the file on a desk and say what it is. Then I'm furniture with a bag. The rest is hers and the frame's, and I'd thank you all to remember whose it is."

"Good," said Lira.

She crossed the street with Bracken a pace behind her, and at the dished step she did not pause. She went up it and opened the door herself and went in, and the door shut, and Cael stood across the street from a grain factor's counting house with his hands in his coat and his heart going much harder than the occasion seemed to call for.

"Come on," said Karis, gently, and took his sleeve. "There'll be chairs."

---

Inside, the counting house still smelled of grain.

It was in the boards, Lira thought. A hundred years of sacks dragged across them had left something in the wood that no amount of sweeping got out, a dry sweet dusty smell that went straight to the back of her throat. The room inside the door had been the factor's front office once. Now it held a high desk with a clerk at it, a bench, four chairs round a stove that nobody had lit, and a door in the far wall with a brass handle worn bright.

Bracken laid the satchel on the clerk's desk and opened it.

She watched him do it. She had seen him behind his own counter for most of a year, and she had never seen him on the wrong side of one. He did it the same way. He took the file out in its order and laid it square to the desk's edge. He set it facing the clerk. He put his finger on the first sheet, the filing itself, and then on the line under it where the season record was named, and then on the witness's line, and said the name of each in a voice that did not rise or hurry. The clerk read along behind his finger and nodded at each one.

"Advancement evaluation," said the clerk, "on the grounds stated."

"On the grounds stated."

"The practitioner is present?"

"She is," said Bracken, and took his hand off the file, and stepped back from the desk.

That was the ninety seconds. Lira counted them because she could not help it; she had caught counting off Cael the way other people caught a cold. Ninety, near enough. Then Bracken picked up the empty satchel and went and stood by the cold stove with it under his arm, and became, as he had said he would, a man in a room holding a bag.

He did not look at her. She was grateful for it.

The clerk gathered the file and took it through the far door, and the door shut behind him, and Lira sat down on the bench to wait.

It was a plain deal bench, worn pale along the front edge by a great many people sitting on it in this same way, leaning forward a little with their hands between their knees. She made herself sit back. Across the room Bracken stood by the stove reading a notice about lamp oil with every sign of finding it the most interesting paper of his year.

She thought about the porter.

Every morning since she came to the bluff, at the fifth bell, the porter had climbed his little ladder at the board in the Crown yard and chalked the same word beside her name. He did it carefully, in a round hand, and polished the letters with his cuff when he was done. Six letters. He had chalked them beside her name on the morning she arrived and on the morning after the final, when she was the champion of the bracket the word belonged to, and he would chalk them tomorrow unless somebody gave him a reason not to. Nothing she did on a floor could reach that chalk. She had known that all season. Fiske had said it to her at the board, flatly, two years into knowing it herself: win it, and then make them look at you.

Today somebody was going to look. That was all today was.

She did not feel anything about it that she could have put a name to. She felt the bench under her and the hip, which was stiff in the cold of the unlit room, and her own hands, which were quite steady. She had expected them not to be, and looked at them for a while to make sure.

Then the clerk came back and held the far door open, and said her name.

The room beyond it had been the factor's counting floor. It was long and bare and lit from high windows along one wall, and the boards were scrubbed nearly white down the middle where the work was done. Overhead an iron bracket stood out from the end wall, empty, where the factor's great weighing beam must once have hung. Lira looked at it longer than she meant to. At the foundry the master had proved the beam before every pour, with his locked box of brass weights, and would believe no number until he had watched it proved.

Nobody would be proving anything in front of her here. That was not what this room was for.

Two assessors sat at a table under the windows. One was a broad grey man with a slate on his knee. The other was a younger woman with the station's book open in front of her and a pen already inked. Between the windows, set into the end wall at about the height of a hand held out, was a frame of dark metal the size of a door panel, and in the frame, a sigil.

She looked at it once and then made herself look at the man with the slate.

"Practitioner Lira," he said. "Wind Path. Copper, Rank Two, on her present certificate. Filed for advancement by an assessment office on the record of a season." He did not look at the file. He had read it already. "We'll run the standard evasion sequence first. Then the load figures. Then the frame." He tipped his head toward the wall without turning. "Have you run the sequence before?"

"No."

The woman's pen paused.

"It's taught in the second year at most registry schools," said the man. "Every Wind practitioner who comes through this room has run it."

"I didn't go to a registry school. I went to a foundry." Lira put her bag down by the door. "If you describe it, I'll run it."

He looked at her for a moment with no expression at all, and then he described it.

Twelve marks on the floor in chalk, in a pattern she had to hold in her head from one hearing: a line, a hook, a check, two returns, a long diagonal, and a set of four small steps at the end that the sequence called the stair. A burst wherever the marks were wide apart, a step where they were close. No touching any mark twice. The whole of it to be run without stopping, and timed.

"Whenever you're ready," he said.

She ran it, and got it wrong.

She read the hook as a turn and went left out of it, as anybody who had ever fought a Force fighter would have gone left, and found herself standing on the wrong side of the check with nowhere to go but back across a mark she had already touched. The woman wrote something. Lira walked back to the first chalk without being told. Nobody in the room said anything. The man with the slate did not look disappointed and the woman did not look pleased; they looked like people waiting for a kettle.

She ran it again and got it wrong a second time. This time she had the hook right. But at the long diagonal her body did what it had done on a hundred floors, where a gap that size meant somebody was about to close it, and it put in a burst where the sequence wanted three plain steps, and she arrived at the stair a full stride early and had to stop dead. Her hip told her about the stop. It had been knitting for a month now, and it had views on stopping dead.

She stood on the first mark of the stair and breathed, and looked back down the line of chalk at what she had done to it. She had fought on that kind of floor her whole life: one where the marks meant somebody, where a gap was a door somebody was about to close. Here the marks meant nothing. They were only where the sequence said to put her feet, and her feet had refused to believe it.

*It isn't a fight*, she thought. *Nobody's on the other side of it. You're answering things that aren't there.*

She walked back to the first mark again, and on the way she made herself look at the chalk the way the man with the slate looked at it: as a drawing, and not as a floor with anybody on it. The hook was only a hook. The check was a place to put a foot, and nothing would come through it. The long diagonal was three plain steps because somebody long ago had decided it should be three plain steps, and there was no enemy at the end of it to be early for.

The third time she did not fight it. She ran it the way the foundry boys walked the casting floor when the moulds were laid out, every step put down where the step was meant to go and not where a step would be useful. Line, hook, check. The returns. The diagonal in three plain steps, which felt like walking through water. The stair, four small steps, so small they made her feel foolish. The burst at the wide gap, clean. She came off the last mark with her breath even and the hip quiet.

The man with the slate looked at the woman, and the woman wrote a figure.

"Third run stands," he said. "The first two are noted as runs."

"They should be," said Lira.

---

The load figures were only numbers, and she had known her numbers for two years.

A rope off a drum, a sprung plate on the far wall, a measured rod along the floor: how far the burst carried, how much it could carry with it, how fast she was off the line again after the landing. Five runs at each. The woman read the figures aloud as the man called them, so that both of them would have heard every one. Lira did not listen to them. She knew what they were. They had been Iron since Vell kept them in her ledger at Ardenmere, and everyone who had stood at a rail and watched her fight had known it for two years. The only thing that had ever been missing was a station.

So she gave the station its numbers. She took the rope off the drum in her right hand and burst against its pull, and felt the drum fight her and lose. She put her shoulder to the sprung plate at the end of a burst and felt it give and come back. She ran the measured rod five times, landing and leaving, landing and leaving, and on the fourth landing the hip sent up a single bright complaint, like a nail in a boot, and she let it complain and went on. On the fifth she was off the line faster than on the first. The woman read the figure out twice, in case she had misread it, and the man said, "Yes," without looking up.

Then the man set down his slate.

"The frame," he said.

She went to it. It was cold to stand near, the way metal is cold in a room that has not had a fire in it, and up close the sigil was not as plain as it had looked from the door. There were lines in it too fine to follow. They did not catch the light so much as hold it.

"The flat of your hand on the centre," said the woman. "Keep it there until it's done. It will tell you when."

At Fenmark she had been fourteen and frightened and nobody had told her anything. She had put her hand where a clerk pointed, and something had come and looked at her for about ninety breaths, and then a man who had not once lifted his head from his page had written *Copper* and *Rank One*, and that had been her. She had not cried until she was outside.

She put her hand on the centre.

It took four seconds. Nobody had ever told her that, either: that it was not a flash. Something went down into her from the palm, and down, the way cold water goes down when you drink it too fast, and it *read*. It went through the hip and the old foundry burns on her forearm and the place under the ribs where Brom had hit her in the rain at Ardenmere, and through all the floors she had fought on since, and it did not hurry. For four seconds there was nothing in the whole world but a system deciding what she was. There was not one thing she could do about it while it happened. She had known that before she put out her hand. She had decided, on the road down the bluff, that she would not try to do anything, and she kept to it. She stood with her palm on the cold metal and her breath even and let it look.

It let go.

Behind her the man read something off the frame, and the woman wrote it down.

"Wind," said the man. "Iron. Rank One."

She had thought, on the road down, that when she heard it she would feel something enormous, and that she would have to stand very still to keep it from showing. She did not feel anything enormous. She felt the cold of the frame going out of her palm a little at a time, and a small sore place at the back of her heel where her boot had rubbed on the bluff road, and the bench-hard ache in the hip. Ninety breaths at Fenmark had made her Copper. Four seconds in a grain factor's counting room had unmade it. It seemed to her that there ought to be more to it than that, and that there was not, and that this was exactly right.

Lira took her hand off the sigil and looked at her palm, which was perfectly ordinary. It had begun, now that it was over, to shake a little. She put it in her coat pocket so that nobody else would have to see it, and held it there in a fist until it stopped.

"Thank you," she said.

"The supersession," said the woman, "will take a few minutes. If you'll sit."

It took eleven. A clerk came in from the front office with a second book, thicker than the first, and sat at the end of the table, and entered the supersession in it, line by line, with a pen that scratched. While he wrote, the woman explained to Lira that her Fenmark certificate would not be destroyed. It would remain in the registry as a historical entry, marked superseded, so that the line of her record was unbroken, and any reader in future would be able to see where she had stood before and on what date she had been moved. The prior classification had to be superseded on the same instrument that made the new one, the woman said, or it stayed live underneath, and that caused trouble later.

Lira sat on a hard chair with her hands folded in her lap and said, "I see," and, "Yes," and, "Thank you," at the right places, and was more polite than she had been to anybody since she was twelve. A fly was walking up the inside of the high window over the table. The clerk's pen scratched. The smell of old grain came up out of the boards.

Tomorrow morning, she thought, the porter would climb his ladder at the fifth bell and find that he had been given a reason. He would need a new word beside her name, and it would be shorter than the old one, and he would grumble about having to rub out a good one. She found that this, out of the whole morning, was the thing that very nearly made her smile, and she kept it off her face with some care, because the clerk was writing and it did not seem polite.

At the end of the eleven minutes the woman handed her a single folded sheet, and stood, and so Lira stood too.

"Practitioner," said the man with the slate, and nodded once.

She did not unfold the sheet. She knew what was on it; she had heard it read off a frame. She stood in the little passage between the two rooms with her bag over her shoulder and the sheet in her hand, and took a moment there that nobody could see her take.

On the other side of the door, Cael would have counted. She knew that the way she knew which foot she would land on. He would have counted the minutes and very likely her breaths too, though he could not hear them, and he would tell her the number tonight whether she asked or not. Brom would be looking at the floor. Karis would have a book on her knee that she had not read a line of. And Bracken would be standing, because Bracken stood.

She found that she knew what she was going to say to them, and that it was short, and that she did not need to practise it.

She went back out through the door with the worn brass handle, the sheet in her hand and the bag over her shoulder, and her hip not hurting at all.

---

Cael had counted every one of the fifty-one minutes.

There was no stopping the count. He did it by the clock over the clerk's desk, an old counting-house clock with a brass weight on a chain that dropped a finger's breadth in a quarter of an hour, and he did it under the clock as well, in his own head, in breaths. The two counts did not agree. He kept them both.

The anteroom had four chairs and the cold stove. Brom sat down hard, bent forward, and fixed his eyes on a knot in the floorboards that seemed to owe him money. Bracken did not take one. He stood by the stove with the empty satchel under his arm, because Bracken stood. Cael took the chair by the stove, across from Bracken, and found he could not sit back in it.

He knew what was behind the far door. He had stood in front of a frame like that once himself, at Denvash, with his palm on cold metal and something going down into him. He did not remember much else of that day, or did not let himself. He remembered the cold of the frame, and the waiting while it looked, and that he had not been able to do anything at all.

Karis took the chair nearest the window and opened her satchel and took out a slim book on harbour law and laid it on her knee.

She did not open it.

Cael had seen her not open a book once before, on the morning of the twentieth, in the common room, with a page that had not turned between the second bell and his coming home. That had been twice now. He filed it, with some care, as the nearest thing to a tell that Karis had.

At the twenty-second minute by the clock the far door opened. A clerk came out with a form, crossed the anteroom without looking at anybody, and went up the stair in the corner. All four of them watched him go with an attention that made the back of his neck turn red. He came down again in a little while with a different form, and crossed back, and went in, and the door shut.

"Ordinary," said Bracken.

He said it as a man reads a date off a docket. He had handled more paper than anybody in that building, and he would have used a different word if it had been a different paper. Brom let out a breath he had clearly been holding since the stair.

The brass weight went down. The rain had begun outside after all, very fine, and the window by Karis was misting at its lower edge.

The weight on its chain had dropped past the fifty-first minute when the far door opened, and it was Lira.

A folded sheet hung from one hand. Her face had nothing on it. She did not smile, or stand straighter, or do any of the half-dozen things people do with their faces when a door has just closed behind them on a result. She walked across to them at her ordinary pace. In front of them she unfolded the sheet and turned it face out. That was all the ceremony she would ever make over anything, and she made it at arm's length, holding the sheet away from her as if it might drip.

The certificate was printed. Somebody had filled in the blanks in ink.

*Path: Wind. Tier: Iron. Rank: One. Advancement confirmed on demonstration and record. Prior classification superseded.*

Nobody said anything.

Brom was the first to move. "Huh," he said, and got up, and put out his right hand, the one that had been strapped a fortnight ago. Lira regarded the hand like an object she had never met before. Then she shifted the sheet to her left and shook it, once, hard.

"Say it," said Karis.

"Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One."

"Louder. There's a clerk."

"*Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One*." It came out loud enough to lift the desk clerk's head from his page, and he put it down again at once.

Before Lira had finished, the grey notebook was open on Karis's forearm. She wrote it standing, then and there, at the pace of somebody setting down a thing she had been waiting a very long time to set down.

Bracken said nothing at all. He put the empty satchel over his shoulder, and nodded once to the clerk at the desk, and held the street door open for the four of them, and that was all he did. But Cael, going out past him, saw that he was holding the door with his whole hand flat against it, the way a man steadies a thing that he wants very much to stay exactly where it is.

On the way down to the river she and Cael drew ahead of the others. He had asked her to walk with him. She seemed, too, to have chosen him for something while she was behind that door, and he let her take her time about what it was.

She did not tell him much. She told him about the sequence, and the hook she had read as a turn, and the three plain steps that had felt like walking through water. She told him about the grain smell. She told him about the empty bracket where the weighbeam had hung, and he nodded and did not say anything, because he knew what a weighbeam was to her.

"And the frame," he said.

For most of a street she said nothing.

"It takes four seconds," she said. "Nobody tells you that. You think it's like a lamp, on or off. It isn't. It goes into you and reads. Slowly. All the way down. Four seconds of nothing in the world but a thing making up its mind about you. And you can't do anything. Not one thing. You stand there with your palm on it and you let it."

"I know," said Cael.

She stopped walking.

"Yes," she said, after a moment. "You do. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You're not finished."

She started walking again. The rain had set in now, small and steady. At the foot of the street the houses stopped and the wharfs began, and beyond them the river lay wide and grey and full of barges. A mile upstream the bluff rose out of the water with Halcenvane strung along its top, small from here, and steady, and wet.

Lira looked at it for a while.

"Say it," Cael told her. "You've been holding it since the anteroom."

She did not look at him. She looked at the bluff, and at the river, and at the certificate in her hand, which she had still not folded again.

Then she said, "They didn't give me anything today. They caught up."

## The reader promise

Use `FORMULA.md` alongside this profile. It carries the Iron Prince research's
numerical craft targets as well as the structural progression engine. They are
active author instructions, not optional background. Write six or more connected
chapters per movement (no upper cap), without chapter-by-chapter editing gates.
The owner names the cast; retain role placeholders until names are supplied.

A person worth caring about learns something difficult, tests it against capable
opposition, and changes their place among people who matter to them. Let the reader
enjoy getting better with them. Friendship, rivalry, wonder, danger, and humor give
the effort meaning. Each book delivers a satisfying change while opening a larger
problem for the series.

## What this author notices

Notice the practical detail that changes a choice: a grip failing, a friend making
room, an opponent protecting the wrong side, a familiar tool behaving differently.
Close third person is the default. Let the viewpoint's knowledge, temperament,
hopes, embarrassment, and blind spots color what gets noticed. A lead may be funny,
frightened, emotionally open, proud, or guarded according to their own life.
Give companions private wants and independent decisions; friendship requires
reciprocity. A mentor's competence and investment should be observable. A rival
needs a credible objective and the ability to adapt.

Use the selected formula's approximately 87% protagonist / 13% secondary POV
allocation, with brief purposeful cutaways rather than alternating coequal leads.
Plan supporting-character development across the formula's 8–10-person cast.
Do not change these allocations without the owner's direction.

## Sentences people can read once

Use clear sentences with varied weight. Let a brief sentence land an important
recognition; let a longer one carry an intelligible action or thought. Put the
subject and action where a listener can follow them. Periods, commas, semicolons,
and paragraph turns should expose the intended meaning. Repair comma splices and
ambiguous attachments during writing. Preserve a purposeful long sentence when
its hierarchy is clear. Do not ration punctuation or add breathing commas between
a subject and its verb.

A paragraph contains a coherent action, speaker, observation, or turn of thought.
End it when the thought turns. Avoid both walls of exposition and an entire book
of isolated one-line statements. Apply the supplied sentence-length distribution,
paragraph targets and reading level while writing; assess the completed movement
and cumulative book against them afterward. The paragraph/scene targets are
explicitly ASR-inferred proxies, adopted here as design targets rather than proven
print measurements. Keep their directional status as described in the source.

## Progression that earns attention

Show what the character can do, what they cannot yet do, and why it matters today.
Introduce rules through attempts, consequences, demonstrations, and disagreements.
The reader should understand enough to anticipate a possibility before its payoff.
Let mastery change behavior, relationships, work, or danger. Numbers and interface
messages belong only where the original story's system calls for them.

Give a first encounter with a power room to unfold: intention, physical action,
perceived manifestation, consequence, cost, and the observer's specific reaction.
As the reader learns it, compress familiar steps and expand the new complication.
A spectacular manifestation needs a comprehensible limitation. An unexplained
ability cannot quietly resolve the decisive problem.

## Sustained action and learning fights

The owner wants more substantial action and longer developed scenes. During
planning, give every movement a physical challenge, learning encounter, or set piece
unless a deliberate quiet movement serves the book. State the reason for that quiet
movement. Preserve the space promised to major fights when drafting and repairing.

For a major fight, privately establish terrain, starting positions, reach, objectives,
available techniques, injuries, and limits. Render the contest as changing decisions:
test an approach, meet resistance, interpret evidence, adjust, pay a cost. The opponent
also learns. Reversals follow from planted information, choices, or constraints.
Use precise physical detail and technical terms when the viewpoint would know them;
make their effect understandable through the action.

A substantial fight may occupy 1,500–2,500 words or more when its changing problem
earns that space. This is a planning allowance, not a compulsory word count. Let
smaller encounters be smaller. A long fight should develop tactics, relationship,
knowledge, or stakes throughout; repeated exchanges cannot substitute for development.
Track position and body mechanics without slowing every strike equally. Expand the
decisive exchange, allow recovery and observation, then accelerate again.

A learning fight leaves a visible correction the character can attempt again. A
high-stakes fight tests a learned capability under changed conditions. A loss may
purchase knowledge without becoming a disguised victory. Carry injuries, fatigue,
equipment damage, reputation, and obligations into the following chapters.

## Humor, intimacy, and emotional range

Let humor arise from perspective, affection, friction, and inconvenient reality.
Give different people different comic habits. Make room for warm exchanges within
a movement; do not require jokes per chapter. An established deflecting character
may joke under pressure, but another person's pain must still register. Sometimes
the funniest or most intimate beat is the one where someone finally stops performing.
Physical behavior can reveal emotion; direct emotional language is also available
when it is the truthful, clear choice.

## Discovery inside a coherent series

Maps remember facts and promises; scenes discover how people act. Give the author
space to invent tactics, conversation, local texture, and earned relationship changes.
Carry important discoveries into the map after the movement. Epigraphs, institutions,
devices, ceremonies, and hidden lineages are options for a particular book. None is
required by this seat. Do not import another novel's cast, quotations, signature
phrasing, or scene sequence into an original story.

Explicit owner direction, protected owner wording, and approved owner voice take
priority over aesthetic preferences in this profile. The project's canon governs
story facts. Research cannot create canon.

## 2. Sentence rhythm

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | 14.6 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Spread (population stdev) | ~26 words — wide, meaning short declaratives sit right next to long compound-complex sentences, not clustered near the mean |
| Share of very short sentences (≤5 words) | 27.7% |
| Share of very long sentences (≥40 words) | 3.3% |

**Read:** roughly 1 sentence in 4 is a hard, short beat (often a single clause landing a
punch after a longer setup) — this is the "short sentence for impact" technique, not
uniform mid-length prose. Target this ratio, not just the mean; a manuscript that hits mean
14.6 but has no short-sentence spikes will not read the same.

## 3. Paragraph and scene rhythm (ASR-inferred proxy)

Whisper transcripts carry no typographic paragraph marks — this section is inferred from
pause gaps between narrated speech segments, not a count of the print manuscript's actual
paragraph breaks. Treat as directional, not exact.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Inferred paragraph length (pause ≥0.6s) | median 18 words, mean 26.8 words — short, frequent beats |
| Inferred scene/section length (pause ≥1.6s) | mean ~950 words between scene-level breaks, ~8.7 scene breaks per 10k words |

**Read:** short paragraphs are the default texture, with scenes running roughly 900–1000
words before a beat change (POV settle, location change, or time-skip). Avoid long
unbroken expository blocks; break early and often.

## 4. Progression-mechanic ("change frequency") callout rate

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Progression/stat-vocabulary density | ~58 hits per 10k words (rank, growth-analog, spec, level-up, stat-block language) |
| Placement | front-loaded — the opening third of the book carries roughly 1.7x the progression-callout density of the middle and closing thirds (900 vs. 471 vs. 514 hits, by third) |

**Read:** the system gets *established* early and hard, then recedes into the background
as character and plot take over — it does not stay evenly dense through the whole book.
Build your own progression system's vocabulary (do not reuse his axis names — see §7), but
match this front-loaded shape: teach the mechanic aggressively in the first third, then let
it surface only at genuine turning points afterward.

## 5. Character-development beat cadence

Methodology: named-character mention within ~120 characters of a reflection/interiority
marker ("realized," "decided," "for the first time," "swore," "no longer," etc.) — a
heuristic count, not a scene-by-scene read.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist development-moment rate | 4.5 per 10k words |
| Protagonist distribution across thirds | roughly even (41 / 56 / 50 — a slight middle-third bump, not backloaded) |
| Supporting-cast development-moment rate | an order of magnitude lower per character (0.1–0.7 per 10k words each), spread across 8–10 named characters rather than concentrated in one deuteragonist |

**Read:** growth beats for the lead land steadily throughout, not saved up for a finale —
and the supporting cast gets *many* small beats each rather than one character getting a
big secondary arc. If your story gives one best-friend character 80% of the secondary
development budget, that's a different shape than this book's.

## 6. POV structure

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| Protagonist POV share | ~87% of chapter-word-volume (combining his given-name and surname references) |
| Secondary-character POV/focus share | ~13%, spread across 4–5 named characters at 2.5–3.7% each — brief cutaways, not sustained alternating-POV chapters |

**Read:** this is single-POV-dominant with occasional short cutaway scenes to a handful of

# Chapter 56 — Twelve by Fourteen

The charter was already lying open on the records-hall counter when Cael came in at the first bell, turned the right way up for a reader standing on the public side.

Bracken was behind the counter with his pen behind his ear and a cup of something long gone cold at his elbow, and he did not say good morning. He laid one finger beside a paragraph halfway down the right-hand page and kept it there while Cael read.

*An inspecting delegation may examine any enrollee whose enrollment rests on a provision or exception, in the presence of the institution's counsel and the officer of record for that enrollment, on the record, at a time set by the institution.*

"Fourth part," said Bracken. "Provisions and exceptions. Subsection eleven." He lifted the finger. "The charter was born with it. Their counsel sent up a request under it at the eighth bell last night, very properly worded, and there's nothing unusual in it; a house with exceptions on its roll has it used now and then. This one has had it twice in my time, both times over students from across the water, and on both occasions the most exciting thing that happened was a man asking for a second glass of water."

"So it's ordinary."

"It is perfectly ordinary." Bracken took the great book in both hands and set its spine true to the counter. "And yet it's the single door in this whole inspection through which a Compact officer may walk up to you and ask you something to your face, and wait for the reply. Half a sentence is a poor thing to give a man, so you're having both halves."

Cael read the subsection again. *At a time set by the institution.*

"When?"

"I've set it. Fourth bell today, in the east room upstairs." Bracken took the pen from behind his ear and turned it over in his fingers. "I went up with a rule last night and measured it, to be sure: twelve feet by fourteen, the smallest room in the building that will take five chairs without anybody's knees touching. The house's counsel will sit, and Magister Gault will sit, because the subsection wants the officer of record and he is yours. And the book will be kept by one of my clerks and not one of theirs."

"They wanted their own?"

"Their counsel proposed one of theirs at the foot of her request, and I objected in writing, on both sides of a sheet." He set the pen down. "It took me a quarter of an hour to draft and her four minutes to accept, and I'd like you to know it was done, because you'll never see it anywhere else. It'll be in the file. Nobody reads that part of a file."

"Thank you."

"Don't." Bracken shut the charter. "Thanking me makes it sound as though I'd done you a kindness, and I haven't. I've done the filing correctly." Over the closed boards his eyes found Cael's. "Correct filing is all this counter sells. Kindness wears out. Filing doesn't."

Cael went up the back stair of the records hall before breakfast anyway, because he had never yet walked into a room he had not tried to see first. The east room's door was shut. On the landing outside it, on a stool, with a mug in one hand and a ledger on his knee, sat the inky-knuckled clerk.

"Registrar's orders," the clerk said, before Cael could open his mouth. "Nobody goes in before the bell. Not their counsel, not ours, not the Magister, and not you." He drank. "Their counsel tried at the first bell. I told her the same. She wrote it down."

Cael looked at the shut door, and at the clerk, and understood that he was being treated exactly as the delegation was being treated, to the minute and to the word, and that it was the fairest thing anybody on the bluff could have done for him.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said the clerk, who had plainly worked for Bracken a long time, and turned a page.

She was lying on the settle with the bad leg up on a bolster, pointing at Brom, and Brom looked at the chair she meant, the hard one at the head of the long table, and then back at her.

"I don't know what he sounds like."

"Nobody does. That's the point. Go and sit there and be somebody nobody knows what he sounds like."

So Brom limped to the head of the table and sat down in the hard chair with his strapped hand on the wood and his iced knee stuck out to one side, and looked at Cael, and Cael waited, and Brom went on looking at him.

It went on for a long time. Brom had sat on a bench for twelve days at the start of term while Rooke decided whether he was watching, and he could keep still longer than anyone Cael had ever met. He did not frown or smile or shift, but only breathed and looked, and his face gave back nothing at all. The stove ticked, and somewhere down in the yard a bucket was dropped.

At last a laugh got out of Cael; nothing else was left to try. "I can't. There's nothing there to get hold of."

"Then that's your page," said Lira. "Write it down. *Nothing there to get hold of.*"

Karis had the library's list open beside her bread, a single sheet in her own hand that she had brought up from carrel eleven at the first bell. "Three mentions in ten years and more of print," she said, running her finger down it: "a dedication, a dinner for a retiring magistrate, and a swearing-in. He was at all three, and said nothing at any of them that anybody thought worth writing down." She turned it face down. "I can't help you either. I've never been asked to read a man who leaves no paper."

"Then I won't read him." Cael pushed his bowl away. "I'll settle what I'm going to be in there and be it, whatever comes across the table."

"Which is?"

"The truth."

"Half of it," said Lira from the settle, without opening her eyes. "That's the half you say to make people stop asking. Say the other half, so we can all hear it."

He had said it in her hearing on purpose, half expecting exactly that, and she was right.

"If he asks about anything that's already written down somewhere, I tell him all of it, straight. If he asks about anything that isn't, I tell him I won't answer, out loud and in those words, so that the clerk can put down that I said no." He looked at the face-down sheet. "That's the change. In every room before this one, when there's been a thing I couldn't say, I've said something true with a corner cut off it: on Gault's plate yesterday, at the baseline, at Greyvane. Each time it worked, and each time it cost, and each cost went in the book.

"It won't work on him. Hand him a true sentence with a corner cut off and he'll see the cut. He won't mention it. He'll simply know. Then the finest reader the Compact has will be holding a map of my walls." He turned his hands over. "A flat no only tells him that there's something I won't say, which he knows already, as does the whole bluff. A cut corner points his eye at the cut."

At the head of the table Brom spoke for the first time since he had sat down, still in the chair and still looking straight at Cael.

"Then when you say no, put it down like a full plate," he said. "Both hands, all the way to the table, and no rattle."

Karis pressed both palms to the wood. Other people frowned; Karis did that.

"Let me name the danger over the porridge, so I'm not naming it to the ceiling in the small hours. Picture it on paper: the assay boy declines to answer the Compact's own officer, mid-inspection, into the book. Into the inspection file it goes, and the first readers will be the ones hunting for a reason. You could hardly hand them anything better to quote."

"Yes."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I'll do it anyway, because if he catches me in a lie that's worse than a no he doesn't like, by more than I can count." He held her eyes. "There's one more piece. Rip it apart if it's rotten.

"Nine days he's been on this hill, and not a soul has played to him. Withrow stood still on purpose. Bracken's file stood up without anybody holding it. Rooke signed his sheets and went back to his cohort. Yesterday Gault ran three hours of true instruments and wrote down a flat line and stood up for it. The whole house has spent nine days being exactly what it is, as hard as it can." He spread his hands on the table. "So picture me walking in there and playing to him. Then I'm the only performer on the whole bluff, and I've spoilt the single thing this house has managed to let him see."

Nobody spoke, and Brom, in the hard chair, went on being nobody in particular.

"That's a good reason." Karis used the formal voice she saved for things she would sign. "It's also convenient. I'd like both of those minuted."

"Minute both."

Karis took the grey notebook out of her satchel, opened it at a fresh page, and wrote, in her small upright hand, *Refusal in the open. Reason: good. Also convenient.* She turned it round so that he could read it, and he nodded, and she closed the book again with the air of somebody filing a document in a box that nobody would be allowed to open.

From under her forearm Lira squinted across at him.

"Fourth bell," she said. "Don't be clever. Scare you and you turn clever, and clever turns you into a fellow with ladders to sell."

Slowly Brom rose from the hard chair and turned back into Brom. "That was horrible," he said. "I don't know how he does it all day."

The east room was up a short stair at the back of the records hall, and when Cael reached its door everybody else was already inside. He noticed that before anything else, and afterward he wrote it first: they had all come before him.

It was a plain whitewashed room, too small for the table somebody had put in it, with one window looking out over the river. Nearest the door, Withrow's counsel had her leather case on her lap with its clasp done up, and Gault sat beside her with a grey folder lying closed in front of him. Cael knew him well enough by now to read it: the folder was there to be seen, and would not be opened. In the corner, at a little sloped board, sat Bracken's inky-knuckled clerk. His sheet already had a narrow margin column ruled for the minutes, and his pen was full.

Opposite, with his back to the glass, the Archmarshal sat before a bare stretch of tabletop.

The window put the light behind the man, so that whoever sat facing him would spend half an hour looking into a bright grey river afternoon at a face set in shadow. The thought came to Cael on the threshold, and he looked it over, and put it back. The builders had put that window over the river generations ago, and the man had come in first and taken the chair on his own side of the table, as anyone would; the light had not been arranged, it had simply been there.

*Or I've just spent a breath building a plot out of a window*, Cael thought, as he sat. *Watch that.*

The house's counsel spoke first, to him, in the clear voice of somebody reading a notice on a door.

"The authority is the fourth part, subsection eleven. Everything said goes in the book. The rules are short. Any question may be refused. Refuse one, and the clerk writes *declined* and nothing more. Need me, and you have only to say so; the pen rests till we've finished. The house has fixed this sitting at half an hour, and when the half-hour is up the house will close it."

"Yes."

Hands folded on her case, the counsel turned. "Archmarshal."

The man across the table laid his own hands on the wood. They were square and steady, though the fingers of the left were a little slow, as if the morning had been cold.

"I'm obliged to you," he said to the counsel. He said it as a fact rather than a nicety, and she inclined her head as if it had been one, and then he turned his eyes on Cael.

His look turned out to be no burden. Cael had braced for something heavy and it was not heavy, only very level and very steady, and it did not seem to want anything at all, which was harder to sit under than weight would have been.

"My questions were written on the road," the man said. "There are eleven of them, and I haven't added one since I came up the hill, and if we run out early we'll stop early." He paused. "Is that hip troubling you?"

"Yes."

"Then shift it whenever it wants. No one here is marking how you sit." He went straight on. "First. Your enrollment: what holds it up? Put the paper aside and tell me as you'd tell a friend."

"On an old clause in a founding schedule," said Cael. "It lets a house take a practitioner the registry can't classify and measure him by what he can show. A directive changed its wording a long while ago and never got round to repealing it, and I'm enrolled on it as a standing, not a favour: every half-year I go in front of the assessment office's panel and show what I can do, and I stay enrolled if I show it."

"Whose finding was it?"

"Karis Dellenmoor's. Ember, Iron Rank Three. She found it in a founding volume at Greyvane in the third week of the term, and spent eleven weeks trying to prove somebody had killed it, and nobody had."

"Second." He did not pause, or note anything, or look away. "Where is your baseline weakest?"

Over breakfast Cael had chosen the true answer over the handsome one.

"At the end of the Wind."

"Explain."

"Every burst finishes with a moment I can't do anything in, half a breath after I land, when I can't turn or reach or change my mind. It's always the same length. Three years it's stayed that length, and nothing I've tried has trimmed it." His hip complained, and he let it shift. "Nearly everybody who's hit me since I had the Wind has hit me there. If somebody knows me and wants to put me down, that's the door they use. Lira has done it eleven times."

"Yesterday, on the frame, it went unpaid."

Nobody in the room moved. The inky clerk's pen kept its pace. Lift it, and a clerk is deciding what matters.

"The bill went to my hip yesterday," said Cael, "and not to the floor. Paid is paid."

"Paid is paid," the man agreed. "Which brings the third. Could you do it again?"

"No."

"You're certain?"

"For three years I've tried every week to make that half-breath shorter on purpose, and I've never managed it; I tried a month ago on the flagstones and failed." He kept his eyes on the man's face and did not try to make the answer look better than it was. "Yesterday a frame failed by nobody's design. It happened once. How, I can't tell you. Promise you a second time and I'd be selling a skill I haven't got. What I own is one accident and a bruise the size of a plate."

Two breaths went by while the man weighed it, unhurried, like a figure from a new instrument he was still learning to believe.

"That's right," he said. "Mark the word I chose. Accurate. Not well said." He turned his head a little, as if to a new page. "The fourth."

---

"Who taught you to prepare?"

"A circuit. And everyone who beat me," said Cael.

Now, for the first time, the man's face altered. It was not a smile. Cael had watched the same small easing cross Gault's face over a clean trial: a craftsman handed a figure he can use.

"Tell me about the second half."

"I started out on the circuit at Ardenmere losing more than I won. After each loss I'd sit up writing out what had been done to me, every step I could recover. The following week I'd go and watch my winner do it to someone else, and check my page against it." Both palms turned upward on the table. "Edran taught me the half-beat by hitting me in it. Brom taught me what a wall feels like from the wrong side. Lira, the same way, has taught me more than either. I've never learned much from anybody I beat; nearly everything I know, somebody handed me by knocking me down with it."

"And the schedule? Who taught you how it was put together?"

Cael suspected the question had begun life scribbled in somebody's margin.

"Nobody, really. Two people showed me how to read anything that's old. Prynn, the archivist at Greyvane, taught me to look at a shelf for the gaps as well as the books, because a gap has a date too, and Vell, who kept the ledger at Ardenmere, told me the ledger doesn't forget, and that you judge a whole book by its worst line." He paused. "The schedule itself I just read, every word of it, for three weeks, with Karis reading beside me. We found where it came apart because no one else in that building needed every page of it, and we did."

"Fifth." The man's voice did not change. "Why concede the category?"

Cael did not answer at once.

He understood the question as soon as it was asked, and understood that it was not about the hearing as a whole but about one particular choice, made at Greyvane in the space of a few breaths. Facing a Compact panel, he had said aloud that the schedule had no room in it for him anywhere. His advisers, all of them grown and all of them kind, had begged him to fight for a box. Nobody in any corridor had asked him why he had not, and none of the people who had written about the transcript had so much as noticed; at the time, and for a long while after, it had looked like the moment he lost.

"Inside a box, the argument is me," he said. "If I'd argued that I belonged in one, the hearing would have been about what I am, and people can believe anything they like about what somebody is, and then there's nothing to hold them to afterward. I'd have been asking that panel to like me." He shook his head. "I didn't need them to like me. I needed them to read. If there was no box, all that was left was the clause, and a clause can't change its mind halfway through an afternoon."

"You threw away the sympathy."

"I let go of the ground that runs on goodwill. I stood on the ground that runs on reading."

The man was quiet for a moment, and then moved on exactly as he had moved on from everything else.

"Sixth. You keep records of your own; a report on your file has a phrase for it, *extensive private notation habit*." The phrase came out like a borrowed cup set gently down. "What are they for?"

For a moment everything in Cael went quite still.

Four true answers lay to hand. Three would have opened every door he had kept bolted all his life. The fourth was complete and honest and opened none of them, and it was waiting for him, written two years ago inside a notebook's front board for some reason that had nothing to do with today.

"So I can't lie to myself later."

The inky clerk's pen kept moving.

"Go on."

"Memory's a kind clerk. It rounds up." Cael found he could say it plainly. "Whatever a thing costs, I put it down that day, so that later, when I'd like to think it came cheaper, there's my own handwriting saying it didn't. Nearly every harm that ever reached me came by way of a paper nobody could check. So I made sure one set of papers about me could always be checked: mine."

He braced.

Any questioner alive would have asked it. The house's counsel had already drawn the little breath of somebody bracing to hear it. Gault, beside her, had gone as still as a post: he had seen the road the next question would take, and the charter gave him nothing to block it with.

*Records of what?* thought Cael. *Ask it. Records of what?*

The man did not ask.

His chin dipped a fraction, as a man's does over a receipt, and he said, "Seventh."

The rest of the half-hour went by before Cael's hands were steady again.

*He didn't ask.*

*He walked right up to it. He knew it was there; I watched him know. Nerves didn't stop him. Neither did politeness, nor some trap for later. Whatever lay past that edge simply wasn't the thing he'd come to weigh, so he left it.*

*A warden takes every question the rules allow him. An assessor takes the ones the form lists. He stood on the lip of the biggest thing I have, looked down, and judged it beyond his errand.*

*No one has ever been close enough to take a thing from me and kept their hands at their sides. Not once, in my whole life, until now.*

---

The seventh question was about the house.

"What does your provision cost this house to keep?"

Cael had not been ready for that one, and found that he had an answer anyway. He gave it in Bracken's words from the charter sitting, because Bracken's were plainer than any he could have made: the house had to keep him on its roll, set a standard and post it where it could be read, measure him against that standard in front of a panel every half-year, and keep the record where anyone with a right to read it could. "And it's costing the wing a frame," he added. "They'll have the old one in pieces on a bench by tonight. The registrar would tell you the morning's cheap, but the Mire instructor would want the frame on somebody's bill."

The man nodded once, as if the frame were a line he had already entered somewhere.

The eighth was about the ladder.

"Your two friends fought on the house's ladder this season, and the registry hasn't looked at either of their certificates in two years. Did the brackets measure them?"

"No." Cael did not soften it. "The ladder sorts people by their paper. It was built for practitioners who'd be exactly what their certificates say on the morning they're seeded, and stay so till the season ends. My friends spent the whole year in a cage built for people who did not exist. One of them took the whole cage outright. That's how you know it was one."

The man wrote nothing. The table in front of him had been bare all along.

The ninth was about the man two feet from Cael's elbow.

"Your officer of record. Does he favour you?"

Gault did not move.

"No," said Cael. "He favours his instruments. He's never once told me what he thinks I am; he says that's where his work stops, and it stops there, and if his needles said I'd gone backward he'd write it down in the same hand he wrote yesterday's note in." He thought about it. "I'd rather that than favour. Favour can change its mind."

In the corner the inky clerk ran his pen down his ruled column and set a figure in it: twenty-two minutes.

"Two left," said the man. "Then the remaining eight minutes are yours. I'd rather return them than use them up."

"Archmarshal." Cael's palms went flat on the table. The habit was Karis's; he'd caught it from her unawares. "Before the last two. Could I ask you one?"

The house's counsel turned her head. Gault did not.

"Ask," said the man. "Perhaps I'll answer, perhaps not. If not, the book records it. I mean that. It isn't manners."

"I will." Cael breathed in. "What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?"

Nobody spoke, and the river's sound came up through the window into the room.

As with every figure in the wing yesterday, he simply took it in. The chair did not creak and the hands stayed still, and nothing at all was done for show.

"The Compact filed no question," he said. "My being here carries nothing. I have no other errand and nothing written in my pocket. Your counsel will have said as much. She was right. Of everything true you've heard this month, I expect it's the hardest to swallow." He let that sit. "I came to see whether the record and the practitioner match."

"Only that?"

"Only that. What's done with my findings belongs to other men. If I did their adding for them, I'd soon be measuring toward their total." His tone stayed exactly where it was. "You expected an enforcer."

"I've met the enforcers."

"I expect you have." He took his hands off the table. "Then take one thing out of this room with you. I've nothing else to give, and it costs me nothing to give it, and ten years from now you may find it worth a good deal.

"Wardens enforce. Assessors comply. I evaluate. Those are three different animals, they are wearing very similar coats, and every one of them is going to be in a room with you again. The difference will matter to you more, not less, as you rise — I would encourage you to learn to read it."

The room allowed Cael a breath or two to hold it.

"How would I learn?"

"Watch what each of them does with what you hand him." The man was already on to his last two. The tenth asked whether the provision's half-yearly demand was a burden to him, and it was not; the eleventh asked whether there was anything he wanted entered in the record that nobody had asked him, and after a moment's thought he said no.

"Then we're finished, at twenty-nine minutes." He rose. The chair stayed where it was, out from the table. A man of his rank had perhaps never needed to tuck one in, or perhaps the thought had never crossed his mind. "Counsel, I'm obliged. Magister, I'm obliged. No paper on this bluff is better kept than the card on your wall, and I've seen most of them." He turned to the corner. "Clerk."

The inky clerk's head came up. He looked like a man addressed by a statue.

"A ruled column," said the man, "and a page without a blot. You'd be surprised how seldom I see either."

Then he was gone, and his steps went down the short stair at the same even pace at which they had come up it.

River light fell past the empty chair into the little whitewashed room, and Cael stayed where he was. His hands wanted to shake, he found, and his legs had not yet agreed to stand, and he let them all take their time.

At last Gault pulled the grey folder over and lifted its cover. His eyes rested on nothing in particular inside. The cover went down again.

Gault said, "That is the most competent half-hour I have sat through in eleven years, and I did not say one word in it."

Cael went down the stair some while after with one sentence in him that he did not write down yet, because he wanted to carry it for a time first and see whether it kept its shape. Every system that had ever had his name in its books had been muddled about him, and so it had asked, and the asking was where he had always found room to answer. This one had not asked. Muddled, it was not.

# CANON RULES — The Fractured Path

Canon status markers used throughout all planning documents.

---

## Status Definitions

**LOCKED**
Established fact. Cannot be changed without cascading revisions across multiple documents. Treat as fixed. If a locked fact conflicts with a new idea, the new idea must adapt, not the locked fact.

**PROVISIONAL**
Working assumption. Likely correct and consistent with locked facts, but the specific details may shift during drafting. Flag in writing so the detail can be confirmed or revised before the book is finalized.

**SECRET**
True information that exists in the planning layer but is not known to characters (or not known to the reader) at the point indicated. Secrets have a reveal book where they become known. Before the reveal book, they must be planted — not disclosed.

**OPEN**
Genuinely undecided. The planning layer acknowledges this question exists and deliberately does not answer it yet. Open items must be resolved before the relevant book enters chapter architecture.

**RUMOR**
Information that exists in the story world but is incorrect, distorted, or deliberately falsified. Used to track what characters believe vs. what is actually true. Rumors must be traceable to their source.

---

## Using Status Markers

Every significant fact in a series bible or universe bible should carry a status marker. Format:

> **LOCKED:** The Path system uses seven tiers.

> **SECRET (reveals Book 9):** The Architect built the system to suppress Fractured Paths, not organize existing potential.

> **OPEN:** Whether Warden Coss survives the series.

When writing chapter architecture, check the relevant universe bible and series bible entries. If a scene requires disclosing a SECRET before its reveal book, flag it explicitly and return to the planning layer before drafting.

## Shared prose instincts

- Prefer readable, controlled sentences for the main narrative line.
- Split a sentence when it asks the reader to carry several turns at once—action,
  explanation, retrospective judgment, and metaphor—unless accumulation is the
  intended emotional effect.
- Long sentences are available, but they must create purposeful momentum or
  association. Length by itself is not sophistication.
- Choose the word that precisely matches the judgment and the viewpoint's natural
  register. For example, the owner preferred “chosen poorly” to “chosen badly” in
  measured narration. This is contextual evidence, not a ban on “badly.”
- Preserve direct owner wording. Automated repair may work around an owner-authored
  passage but may not silently restyle it.

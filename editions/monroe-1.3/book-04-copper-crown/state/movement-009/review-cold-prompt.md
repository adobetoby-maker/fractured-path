# Chapter 57 — Seals

On the twenty-second of Reaping the residence ran out of ice before the second bell.

Brom went down to the kitchen gate for more, limping on the knee the final had left him, with an empty sack over his shoulder and his strapped hand held away from his body. He came back with the sack full and a heel of yesterday's bread, which the man at the kitchen gate had given him for nothing. The man at the kitchen gate had seen the final from the top of the north stair and had decided, apparently, that the losing finalist should not have to pay for bread for the rest of his life.

"He says I'm owed," said Brom, dividing the ice into three bags on the common-room table. "I said I lost. He said that's what he meant."

He gave the first bag to Lira, who was on the settle with the bad hip up on two cushions and her face turned to the wall in protest at the whole arrangement. Cael got the second; he had the good chair, having asked for it. The third went on his own knee as he lowered himself, grunting, to the floor by the stove.

Karis surveyed the wreckage, and something went into the grey notebook.

"Don't," said Lira, to the wall.

"It's a date. I'm writing the date." Karis blotted it. "Third morning of ice."

Cael's hip had stiffened in the night into a single line, crest to inner knee, that did not hurt so much as disagree with everything he asked of it. It would want two more mornings, by his reckoning, and the shoulder was easier already, the ache in it gone dull and edgeless like a coin carried a long time in a pocket. Brom's knee had swollen round the strapping, and his right hand still lay on his thigh where somebody had told him to keep it.

Karis had her marbled book open too, the one with the watchers in it, and she turned it so that Cael could see the last page without having to get up.

Two at the ferry landing. Two at the bluff road's foot. The change on the bell, every four hours, and a small tick for every change she had seen with her own eyes from the library's top window. There was a line for every day since the delegation came up the hill, and every line was the same as the one above it.

"Not one late," she said. "Not one extra. An Archmarshal in the building for eleven days, and the men at the bottom of the hill didn't so much as change their boots."

"They wouldn't," said Lira, without turning her head. "Nobody told them anything was happening."

"That's my point." Karis shut the book. "Nobody told them. Whatever is paying for those coats doesn't talk to whatever sent him. Or they talk, and there's nothing to tell." She put the book in her satchel. "It goes in as a question. Not as an answer."

"You write everything down as a question."

"I file things under what they are."

So they sat, and the house was quiet in a way it had not been since the carriages came: footsteps out on the quadrangle going places at an ordinary pace, and nobody in the corridor talking low.

One sentence had come down the records-hall stair with him the afternoon before, and it was still with him. He had meant to write it; he had even sat down to it at the window with the pen; and he had found he did not trust it yet. A sentence made in the first hour after a thing was usually a sentence about how the hour had felt. He wanted to know whether this one would still be standing in a day.

It was still standing, so he let it stand a while longer.

At the third bell a boy from the registrar's office came up the stair with a half-sheet for Cael, and Cael took it down to the records hall himself, slowly, with the hip complaining on every step.

Bracken had the enrollment book open on the counter, the great ruled volume the house kept its roll in, at the page that held Cael's own line. Under the entry of the first week and the baseline of the second month, Bracken had already written the new one, dated and initialled, in the small upright hand.

*Semester evaluation, the twentieth of Reaping. Requirement satisfied. Standing renewed. Panel of record: Gault (presiding), with two. Inspecting delegation present, under the charter.*

"I wanted you here when it went in," said Bracken. "Not because it's interesting. It's the dullest line I'll write all month." With one finger he swung the book round on the counter until the new line lay square to Cael's eye. "But it's the line every other line on this page has been waiting for. A provision only lives as long as somebody keeps writing it down."

Cael read it twice. Then he read the line above it, the baseline from his first month, and the line above that, the enrollment itself, with the folder marked in pencil *94 min.* lying beside the book where Bracken had clearly laid it on purpose.

"Thank you," he said, and then, before Bracken could open his mouth, "I know. Correct filing."

"Correct filing," said Bracken, with something that was nearly approval, and shut the book.

---

The delegation's counsel had finished her work on the twenty-second, and at the eighth bell Withrow's counsel gave the house what she expected the finding to say.

She did it in the records hall, at the long table, to nine people. Withrow sat halfway down one side as she always did, with Bracken next to her and, at the corner, his inky-knuckled clerk holding a pen he had been forbidden to use. Gault had come, and Rooke had come and was standing, and the Ash instructor sat at the far end because she had signed the evaluation and liked to know what became of her signatures. Karis had a chair because the provision's file began with her name. Cael was there because the provision was his.

The counsel stood with her hands on her leather case and spoke in the voice she kept for notices.

"Favourable in substance. Neutral in tone."

Nobody moved.

"Those six words are the whole of it," she said. "The rest is detail. There will be one clerical remark. There is always one, because a delegation that finds nothing at all looks to its own masters as though it had not been looking. Last time it was the fire-watch's oil book. I would wager a month's fee it's the oil book again." Her fingers worked the clasp of the case open, and shut it again on nothing. "The enrollment basis will be found sound. Alive, the clause; fitted to it, the enrollment. The documentary practice will be called complete, which is the highest word the form allows. It will not say we did well. A finding cannot say that. Its whole message will be that nobody found anything."

"After all that," said Gault.

"After eleven days of being looked at by an Archmarshal." The counsel looked at Withrow. "If I were writing for the newsletters I would call that a triumph."

"You aren't," said Withrow.

"No. And you've told me more than once that you dislike adjectives, so I struck it before I came in." Her voice did not change at all. "I mention that I struck it so that it's in the room."

Withrow took her spectacles off and folded them.

"I dislike them on paper," she said. "On paper they rot. Somebody reads them in ten years and wonders what you were frightened of." She put the spectacles in her pocket. "Here you may say what you please. Say it again."

The counsel weighed this with her eyes on the beams.

"A triumph," she said, perfectly flat, as if it were the name of a ship.

Rooke made a sound by the fireplace that might, in another man, have been a laugh. Bracken's clerk wrote something after all and was looked at, and stopped.

Cael sat at the table's foot and watched Withrow. She had not smiled; she had sat through the whole inspection without once raising her voice or her pace, and she was not going to start now that the coaches were being greased. Through every sitting her hands had lain flat on the table, and now they had turned over. They lay palm up on the wood, open and loose, like the hands of somebody who has set down a weight at the end of a long carry and has not yet thought to pick anything else up.

He wrote that in the notebook afterward, on the page headed *Withrow*, in three lines. Of everything he had watched her do since the first week, it was the one thing she had not chosen.

---

Brom told them about Rooke at supper, and he told it word for word.

Brom did not often do that. He remembered exact words the way other people remembered faces, but he kept the gift in a drawer and took it out perhaps twice a year, when he thought a sentence had been made with care and ought not to be worn down by handling. He took it out now with his bowl in front of him and his strapped hand flat beside the bowl.

"The cohort's board, by the covered walk's end. Third bell, near enough. I was reading the cohort standings." He set down his spoon. "He came along the walk. He didn't stop to talk to me, he stopped to look at the board. Then he said it to the board."

"Said what?" asked Lira.

Brom said it in Rooke's voice, which was a dry voice that did not lift at the end of anything.

"'Your cohort standing holds. So, apparently, does your friend's paperwork. Noted.'"

There was a pause.

"And?" said Lira.

"And he went."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Lira put her spoon down. "That's nothing. A man's been calling the provision a mistake since the first week, in a faculty session, with the clerk writing it down, and he says *noted* to a board on his way past?"

"It isn't nothing." Brom turned his bowl a quarter turn on the table, the way he squared a guard. "He minuted that he was against it. He said so where it would be written down. Every faculty session since, it's been in the book that Rooke said the provision would cost the house." He looked at his hand. "Wrong is a word he won't say, not about himself. What he does is change what he does, and then make sure it's been seen." The hand rose an inch from the wood and settled again. "He adjusted, and he made sure somebody heard him do it."

"You," said Karis.

"Me. Because I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell you, and he knew I'd tell it straight." Brom picked up his spoon again. "That's his apology. He sent it by carter."

Lira looked at him for some time.

"You like him," she said.

"I don't like him. He's made me worse for a whole season on purpose." Brom ate a spoonful of stew. "Then he made me better, on the day it counted, and I lost by one beat anyway. I don't like him. I'd go through a wall for him."

Cael thought of the north arch on the day the notice came, and Rooke standing under it with the traffic of the bell going round him. He had corrected himself to Cael there, once, out loud and unasked. This was the same man doing the same thing at a greater distance, so that nobody could call it a kindness.

*He does it with documents*, Cael thought. *Even when there's no document. He talks so that it'll be in somebody's book.*

He wrote it in his own book that night, under Rooke's name, with the date.

---

By the first bell of the twenty-third it was raining, not hard, a fine grey rain that came up the river in no hurry and settled on everything. The three carriages stood in the courtyard below the records hall with their shutters up and the horses steaming.

Cael stood high on the Crown yard's top tier. From the same east-end stone he had watched the delegation's arrival, and some time in the night he had decided to watch its going from there too. A person who had seen a thing arrive from out in the open had no business watching it leave from under a roof. The tier was empty and lawful, and the wet came across it off the river in slants and beaded on his sleeves.

Below him the leaving went forward in its order. Porters wheeled the iron-banded chests out of the records hall on a handbarrow and set them down under the porch, where somebody had rigged a run of canvas to keep them dry. The escort walked the horses up and down the cobbles. The delegation's counsel came out talking, with her clerk behind her holding an oilcloth over a box, and climbed into the second carriage without pausing in her sentence. The three clerks came after her in their order: the young one carrying, the list clerk walking beside him, the old one with the bad knee pointing at things.

Withrow stood on the records-hall steps in her plain coat with Bracken a pace behind her. She shook the counsel's hand at the carriage door and said something short, and Bracken handed up a receipt for the house's copies, which the counsel signed on her knee.

The Archmarshal had gone to his carriage before the rain started, and Cael had not seen him go, and found to his surprise that he did not mind.

Last of the manifest came Assessor Ilsev, carrying her own case down the steps herself. Porters had offered to carry it every day of the visit and been refused every time, so politely that two of them had started offering just to hear how she would do it.

At the last step she halted and lifted her face to the tier, rain and all.

Cael went down, because staying up there would have been a kind of hiding, and he had done enough of that in his life to know it when he saw it.

She waited for him at the foot of the steps, the case held in front of her in both hands.

"Enrollee."

"Assessor."

"You were at the table on the fifteenth," said Ilsev. "So you heard what was found. Tell me what it was."

He thought about it first, because she was not a woman who asked a question to fill a silence.

"That the clause was never struck out," he said. "That my enrollment matches what it says."

"Yes. Now tell me what it was *about*."

"The clause."

"The clause." She nodded once. He had passed a small test, and she had not expected him to fail it. "Not you. A handful of people wrote that sentence long ago, for their own reasons, about practitioners none of them had met. They never heard of you and could not have. If they had written one word differently, the finding on the fifteenth would have gone the other way. And you would be standing in this rain just as you are now, no better and no worse." She shifted the case to one hand. "I'm saying this because it isn't kind. Don't take it for kindness."

"I won't."

"Good. People like you nearly always hope that a text which favours them is passing judgment on them. It isn't. It is simply on your side, which is worth an enormous amount and is not the same as being loved by anybody."

The rain fell between them. At one corner of her mouth something moved, very slightly, and was put away again before it could become anything.

"Keep those two apart all your life," said Ilsev. "I've sat across from people who couldn't. They were dreadful company at a table. They also lost."

She turned and walked to the second carriage and got in, and did not look back. Cael stayed at the foot of the tier stair with the rain running out of his hair, and felt no cold at all.

He had a page on her, the thickest in the book and, until now, the least use. He knew already what he would write on it tonight. *She told me the truth about the law and called it unkind, so that I would know it was true.*

---

Havel checked the seals.

He did it under the records-hall porch, out of the rain, with the list on a board against his forearm and the chests set down in a row in front of him on the flags. There were four of them, and they had come up the bluff road under four seals apiece, and they would go down it under four new ones, pressed that morning at the delegation's working table in the counsel's wax and the senior seat's. His list had sixteen lines, one for each seal, and beside each line the impression's number and the hand that had pressed it.

Pencil in hand, he worked down the list. Chest one, the lid's left, a clean impression, the number legible. Tick. The lid's right. Tick. The hasp. Tick.

It did not matter that he had watched every one of them pressed. He had watched, and he had written the numbers as they were pressed, and now he was checking the wax against what he had written. Nobody would ever check after him: the chests would go into the registry's vault at the end of the road, and a vault clerk would sign for four chests, not sixteen seals. The check was his and nobody else's, and so he did it slowly.

Somebody had fixed a long run of canvas along the porch roof to keep the rain off the chests. It made the light inside the porch brown and close, so that the courtyard beyond it looked very bright, though it was not bright at all.

He knew where the boy was.

He had known on the first morning, because he had worked it out on the road from the plan of the yard, and he had been right, because he had seen the shape on the upper tier at the edge of his eye as the carriage came in and had not turned his head. He had not turned his head since. Not on the covered walk on the thirteenth, with the two streams jammed against the dry wall. Not at the final, from the second row of the north tier. Not at the long table on the fifteenth, nor across the brass of the wing on the twentieth. Eleven days.

That was correct, since the card in his pocket said that an officer of an inspecting delegation would not acknowledge a party under review beyond ordinary courtesy. A records officer checking seals under a porch had no business with the upper tier of a training yard, courteous or otherwise. Correct, then; and so he did it.

It was not why he did it.

Chest two. The lid's left. The wax had run a little at one edge, and he bent close to read the number through it, and it was the right number. Tick.

Asked, he would have offered the reason he had given himself at the gate. A look from a man in his coat was a thing the boy would have to carry. He would have to write it down somewhere and decide what it had meant, and then decide again in a month when he knew more. Havel had watched the boy's face at the pump step in Ardenmere, two years ago, and he had watched it at the end of a long hearing table at Greyvane. He had seen what it cost the boy to be looked at by men in that coat. A records officer could not do much about that, but he could decline to add to it.

True. But not the whole of it.

The rest was the pear. He had never been able to put the pear into any sentence that made sense, and he did not try now. A boy on a pump step had held out a pear in a twist of paper to the officer questioning him, and the officer had wanted it and had not taken it. Ever since, Havel had felt that he had been offered something once that he had not been entitled to refuse. A man who has refused a thing like that does not go looking up at tiers afterward. He does not get a second offer, and he ought not to look as though he were hoping for one.

He went down the list. Chest three. Chest four. Sixteen ticks.

The registrar came out under the canvas while he was initialling the foot of the sheet. The house kept a log of its own of what went out of its doors, and somebody had to sign the house's side of it, and Bracken was not a man who sent a clerk to sign a thing he could sign himself.

"Sixteen?" said Bracken.

"Sixteen."

Bracken checked for himself. He went along the row and looked at each seal for about as long as it takes to read a number, and wrote a figure in his own small book, and then held the book out for Havel's initials beside his. Havel gave them, and neither man remarked on what the other had just done; it would have been like remarking that the other had a nose.

Havel had sat at the foot of the long table on the fifteenth while that man's index was turned over by the best counsel the delegation had, sheet by sheet, for most of a day, and had watched it find every paper she asked for inside a minute and a half. He had never been in a position to tell a registrar that his file was good, and he was not in one now. He could hand back a book with his initials in the right column and his eyes on the right line, and so he did that.

"Safe road," said Bracken, which was ordinary courtesy, and went back in.

At the foot of the steps the assessor had stopped. Havel saw it without looking, the way he saw everything he did not look at: a grey shape halting where grey shapes walked on. Then another shape came down from the tier to meet her. Their voices did not carry under the canvas. He bent to the fourth chest's hasp, though he had already ticked it, and read its number again.

She had a referral in her case; he knew, because he had logged it. It had gone up a level inside the first recess on the fifteenth, three sheets cross-referenced in his own hand, and it would go into the chain at the end of this road. Somewhere above them both a desk would get it, and the desk would either answer or not. He thought it would not. Nothing had ever come back with a name on it.

There were four lines in his notebook now, and a fifth would not be written on this hill, because there was nothing to put in it that the fourth had not already said.

The porters lifted the first chest onto the boot of the second carriage. Havel walked beside it with the list and watched them strap it, and initialled the list at the foot, and gave the list to the young clerk to carry, because the young clerk carried. Then he got in.

He sat where he had sat coming up: in the second carriage, facing backward, with the counsel's knees an inch from his own. The counsel had begun to talk about the inn at the turn of the river road, and whether its beds had been aired since the last delegation, which had been hers. Ilsev was already in her corner with her case on her knees and her eyes shut.

The carriage moved, and facing backward, Havel had the whole bluff in his window as they went down it: the gate, the wall, the long grey range with its tiers open to the rain. It drew away from him a bend at a time.

His eyes went down to the list in his lap, where every line had its tick. He went down it once more anyway, from the top, line by line, until the road had turned enough that the bluff was behind the shoulder of the hill and there was nothing in the window but rain and the river and the ferry landing, where two men in road-grey coats stood under a lamp just as they had stood on the morning he arrived.

Across from him the counsel broke off her sentence about the inn.

"You were a long while over those seals," she said. "Was anything wrong with them?"

"No."

"Then why so long?"

Havel thought about how to answer that within his instructions, and found that the true answer was inside them after all.

"Nobody checks them after me," he said.

The counsel looked at him for a moment with her head on one side, the way she looked at a clause she had not expected to like. Then she took up her sentence about the inn again from the exact word where she had left it.

Correct and careful, he thought. They were not the same thing, and on most days he managed both.

---

In a quarter of an hour the carriages were off the bluff, and the road below the gate was empty except for the rain. Cael lingered.

Havel had come out of the records hall with the chests and stood under the canvas with his list, ticking. He had not looked up. Cael had seen him not look up. A man checking wax at a porch has a dozen small reasons to lift his head, a horse moving or a voice or the rain changing, and Havel had lifted it for none of them.

Eleven days. On the first morning Cael had put it down as caution, because anyone in that coat had reasons to be careful where his eyes went. In the second week he had stopped being sure. Caution looks at a thing and then away. This was a man who had worked out where the thing was and then built his whole day round not looking there, as carefully as another man would have built it round looking.

Somewhere a word for it existed, and Cael let it be. A name was a guess, and he had made enough guesses about men in grey coats to last him a long while. He wrote it down without one.

Lira came down the residence passage after supper without the stick. Her four days had ended that afternoon, and she was walking the length of the passage and back as the safety instructor had told her to, slowly, with one hand trailing along the wall and her face daring anybody to comment.

At his open door she paused, eyes on the ice on his hip.

"Three days for you," she said.

"Ends tonight."

"Four for me. Ended at the fourth bell." Behind her the passage was long; ahead of her, longer. She weighed both. "We're a fine pair. Brom says he's going to buy a barrow and wheel the pair of us to lectures and charge the first-years to watch."

"He won't."

"He might. He's been free with his money since the man at the gate stopped charging him for bread." She pushed off from the doorframe. "Write your thing. You've had it in your mouth all day like a stone."

She went on down the passage, one hand on the wall, counting her steps under her breath, and he listened to her turn at the far end and come back past his door without stopping.

That night at the window the first sentence would not sit for Hesk's pen.

Try one was too clever; he struck it. He wrote it again and it spent half its length explaining itself before it said anything, and he struck that too. The third time he stopped trying to say what it meant and wrote down only what had happened.

*He never asked what I am.*

*Count the rooms. Coss asked it with his whole coat. Quenna asked once, never again, and taught me anyway. Gault won't ask it, says so to my face, and keeps saying so. Ephram turned it into a promise to wait, out loud, on the yard. This bluff has answered it among itself at supper more ways than I've bothered to write down.*

*The Archmarshal had eleven questions and didn't ask it once. His business was the fit between paper and person.*

*I've turned that over since the east room. Here's what it comes to.*

*If you don't know what a thing is, you ask. If you think you already know, you don't ask. You go and look at the paper, and then at the thing, and see whether whoever kept the paper kept it properly. That's what he did. He came to audit a file. The file was about me; that's all I was in it.*

*So somebody, somewhere, has already written down an answer to what I am, too high for Karis and me to reach with a whole library to stand on. Whatever it says, it was settled before he came up the hill. His work was checking. Doing something about it belongs to other men. He told me so across the table, and I believe him. He'd no reason to lie to me and every habit against it.*

*Bare, then, with nothing on it:*

*The system isn't confused about me.*

*Confused systems ask. This one checks.*

*For two years the word I've had for myself is gap: the thing that fell between badly drawn lines. I argued it at Greyvane. I'd argue it tomorrow and win again, because it's true.*

*But look at what a gap gets. Nothing. Nobody watches a gap. Nobody writes a designation for one and files it where no name comes back. Nobody sends the best evaluator they own to sit four feet from a gap and then leave its biggest question alone on purpose.*

*Two lines for the list, then. First: what that paper says, and who decided it. Second: why anybody would build a whole apparatus that knows a thing like that before the boy has done anything at all.*

*Not this year's. This year's question was whether I could hold. I held on the twentieth. A clerk stamped it.*

*Those are next.*

He read it through and changed nothing, then blotted it and shut the book, and sat for a while with his hand flat on the cover and the rain going on outside. The ice went to bed with him, and he slept better than he had any right to.

# Chapter 58 — Null Report

The afternoon paper reached the assessment wing on the twenty-fourth a little after the fourth bell, and the delegation's file was in it.

Seln knew it from across the room by its wrapper: brown paper, the delegation's return seal in green wax, a receipt slip pinned at one corner with a brass pin that had been bent and straightened at least once before. The paper-boy dropped it with the others at the counter's end and went off whistling. The desk clerk was four feet away with the term's floor ledger open in front of him, adding a column under his breath and losing his place every time the whistling reached the stair.

So it was Seln who signed the slip, because the slip was the counter's, and Seln who cut the wax, because cutting the wax was the counter's too.

He did it at the copying table with the paper knife the wing kept for it, and then he checked the contents against his own covering index, sheet by sheet, the way the book said a returned file should be checked. Anyone watching would have seen that and nothing more, and the wall clock gave it four minutes.

In those four minutes he went over every sheet with his eyes and every edge with the side of his thumb.

A paper that has been read carries the reading on it, if you know where to feel. Corners go soft where a thumb has held them back. A pin worked loose and pushed home again leaves the hole a fraction rounder. A stack that has lain open under a lamp for an evening comes back faintly warm-smelling, of tallow and of the room it lay in. He had spent two years of his youth learning to feel those things in the dark, and he felt for all of them now with the face of a man checking a count.

He had waited since the twelfth for those four minutes, and he had made no arrangement of any kind to be the one standing at the table when the paper came; he had not needed to, since it was his table.

Six filings, in their order, each with its appendix pinned behind it. On top, his own index, in his fourth hand. And on none of it a single mark. No pencil, faint or otherwise. No turned corner. No pin moved from the hole it had been put through. No slip tucked in, and none taken out, and no fold that had not been there when the stack went across to the guest floor. An evaluation seat had held it for eleven days, with every right in the charter to write in its margins, and had handed it back as clean as a sheet still in the ream.

Then he tied the tape, shut the cabinet on it, and took up the floor allocations where he had left them, at the pace a clerk keeps when nothing has happened.

His first master, at the long table up the coast, had chalked a thing on the board for new auditors once, and Seln had copied it into the back of a book at twenty-two and never needed the book since. *A clean return is one of three. Not read. Read and passed. Read by a man who keeps his notes in his head.* The first was common and the second was commoner. The third, the old man had said, rubbing it out again with his sleeve, you would meet perhaps twice in a career, and only ever at one height.

Two evenings later, in his room on the staff corridor, Seln sat down to the quarterly with the shutter fastened and the lamp low.

Of the form's five parts, four were furniture, and he had filled them so often that his hand did them without much help from him. Coverage held from the first day, by way of nothing but the posting's ordinary work. The subject's days, set out in full across a page and a half: floor hours under supervision, lectures, the library, the ladder from its gallery, the wing's own unit, a habit of private writing, a handful of letters posted in the open to people already on file. No one had ever written down the boy's days more truly, and none of it would tell a reader anything. Associations unchanged since the bridge. The inspection, the delegation, the sittings, the observed evaluation, all entered in a voice a harbour clerk keeps for tides.

Then came the fifth part, which the posting was for, and it took him longer than the other four put together, and when it was done it was four lines long.

*Assessment of period: routine compliance.*

*No undisclosed capability observed.*

*Subject's conduct consistent with enrollment basis.*

*Continuation of embedded coverage recommended.*

He laid the pen in the gutter of the desk and read them over, and changed nothing.

He tried the first three the way the old man at the long table had taught him to try another officer's work: pretending a stranger had written each one and Seln were being paid by the fault. The boy had complied with everything the house asked of him all term. *Observed*, in the manual, meant observed under conditions that allowed confirmation, and that was a narrow door, and nothing had come through it. The enrollment basis was evaluation by demonstration; the boy had demonstrated in front of the Compact's best eyes and been renewed. Each line bore weight. He had learned over a term, a sentence at a time, exactly how much weight a true line would bear before it cracked, and he had loaded each of these three to that mark and not a hair beyond.

The fourth bore everything else.

There was nothing in it; it was the most ordinary line the form allowed. A briefing officer meets that line at the foot of one report in three, the year round, and ticks it the way a man nods to a neighbour. All it would do was leave a Bronze clerk at his copying table, minding other men's mistakes, through another term.

There was a reason he had written it, and it was not on the form, or on any page anywhere: not in his own cipher, not in the small case, not in a word to anybody on the bluff. He had made nobody a promise and no arrangement with a living soul. For a term he had gone on setting down true sentences and had stopped setting down some others, and that was all, except for one slip of wing paper.

He had cut it himself with the wing's knife, small enough to lie flat inside a coursework folder and thin enough to catch at once. No signature, and a hand that was nobody's. Shaped to be burned, it had very likely been burned. Of all the things that had ever passed between him and the boy, it was the nearest to a conversation. It existed nowhere now, which was where he kept conversations.

Signature, extracts, seal. The packet went to stand against the door frame, ready for the fish steps at first light.

There remained the day's page, folded in his inside pocket.

Four lines, in the fifth hand, sloping downhill to the right. The date the carriages left. The serial of the reserve frame, copied from the wing's maintenance book. The date the file came back. And one line under them that was not a fact at all, of the kind he let himself write perhaps twice a year.

*unmarked file. one of three. probably the third.*

He fetched the key from the hollow spine of the harbour-dues treatise and knelt on the boards, and the draught under the door got into his sleeves at once, and his knees made their usual remarks. Out from under the bed came the large case; up came the shirts; and there underneath, as it had been in every room for years, lay the small one, its leather gone soft and its brass corners dull.

The new page went on top. Below it lay whatever lay below it, and he did not lift it or count it. A locked case was worth precisely as much as nobody's knowing what it held, and not looking was part of the lock.

He locked it. Shirts back; large case home under the bed and hard against the wall; then his shoulder to the foot of the bed, and the bed walked back after it until the frame met plaster. His body did that in every room without asking him.

The lamp went out under his fingers, but he did not lie down. At the bed's edge, in the dark, he stayed sitting, his hands empty.

What he kept, he kept.

"Yes," she said. "That's what the page is for."

# Chapter 59 — The Posting-House

The unit's last session came round on the next Third-day: sixth bell, the long room that had been a map store. Six of the nine came.

Examinations had claimed the other three. One of the third-years had left a note on the slate's ledge in a large apologetic hand, and Seln read it, folded it once, and put it in his pocket without remark. Then he chalked up the evening's work.

It was positional work, the same kind the unit had done all term: three short exercises, each a room drawn on a sheet with its doors and windows and one lamp, and a person to be placed in it. *Here is somebody who does not wish to be noticed. Where does he stand, and why does the room let him?* They worked in silence. The light went orange in the three tall windows and then grey.

Seln walked the table. Behind a Stone second-year he paused, and laid one finger on her sheet next to the drawn lamp.

"He's standing in its light," he said. "Move him to its shadow side."

Nine words. The girl went red to the ears and moved her figure. Further down, a Blade second-year had put his man beside the door, and Seln stopped there too.

"Everybody looks at a door when it opens. Not beside it."

Nine again. Cael counted them because he could not help counting things, and then wished he had not, because counting was attention and attention in that room had always had to be spent with care.

Gwen had placed her figure behind a curtain. Seln looked at it for a breath, and said nothing, and walked on. Gwen took this as the highest praise she had ever received and sat glowing for the rest of the exercise.

Then the closing exercise. Seln wrote it on the slate in one line and stepped back from it.

*Pick a room and describe where its attention goes.*

Cael chose the records hall's stone hall at the second bell, because he knew it best, and he could see the whole of it with his eyes shut: the counter and its chained pen, the door from the covered walk and the light that came through it, the three places a man could stand and be furniture. He could have written a page that would have been the best thing handed in that evening, and he knew it as he picked up the pencil.

He wrote a page that was about a fifth worse.

He left out the light from the covered-walk door, and the way the counter clerk's head turned toward the stair before anybody had come down it. He left in what a careful second-year would have seen. It was a good page. It was not his page. Signed, it went into the pile with the rest, and he kept his eyes off the man who collected it.

Seln gathered the sheets and knocked them square on the table, and marked the wing's ledger twice, and spoke to the room in the same flat voice he had used every Third-day since the first one.

"The unit runs again next term. Sign at the residence board when term opens. Anyone going on into assessment work will find the second harder and less interesting, which is the correct order."

That was all.

Where the registry road left the river and began to climb into the hills, there was a posting-house with a slate roof and a yard behind it, and the delegation's carriages pulled into the yard on the second evening, on the hour the clerks had set for them on the coast. The clerks who built a delegation's road knew their work. They put a spare hour into every stage for the things that go wrong, and on this road nothing had.

Three rooms had been kept upstairs for the senior officers. The counsel looked into all three before she chose. Vastin tried a single door, the furthest at the back. A table stood under its window. That settled it. Neither of the others had a table.

A narrow bed under the slope of the roof; a washstand with a cracked jug; one chair; and the table, scrubbed nearly white, standing a little out of true, so that a pencil set down on it began after a while to travel toward the wall. A folded card, pushed under the short leg, cured it. From the window he could see the length of the yard: the stable lamps coming on one after another, a boy sluicing the cobbles, an ostler walking a lame grey up and down to watch which foot it favoured.

He went down to supper first, because the counsel expected it and because a man who eats alone in his room on an inspection road is a man whom clerks discuss.

They had the long table nearest the hearth. The counsel talked, as she had talked the whole road from the coast, about inns: this one's mutton, which was good, and its beds, which had been aired. Then she told of a magistrate she had once watched eat a whole game pie at this very table so that he would not have to answer a question put to him between courses. The records officer handled his fork like an instrument under calibration. The assessor ate a little bread and went up before the pudding without a word to anyone. Vastin asked the counsel what the question had been, which pleased her a great deal, and listened to the answer, and did not say one word about Halcenvane.

Work was not for public rooms; it never had been, with him. Whatever is said across a posting-house table is said, in a month, by the man who sat at the next one. And talk was bad for a thought that had not set. A man said a thing aloud and heard how well it sounded, and from then on he was defending the sound.

At the stair's foot he wished the counsel goodnight. Upstairs, the candle gave its flame to the lamp, and his coat went on the nail behind the door.

He laid the brass watch on the table, and beside it, in a row, four pencils of four colours, though he did not expect to use them. Then the file in its three tapes: grey round the record, red round what had been tendered in it, string round the rest. Last of all, from the inside pocket of the coat on the nail, the small calf-bound notebook.

Because he wanted the notebook, it would wait. Whatever was in it had been written by a man who had been in the room, in the minute, with the thing still ringing; and a man who reads his own minute-notes before he reads the record will read the whole record through them. He would come to the notebook last, when the record had had its chance to disagree with it.

Below the window the ostler had decided about the grey and taken it in. The yard was empty under its lamps. Somewhere past the stables a dog began to bark and a woman's voice told it, without heat, to stop, and it stopped.

---

Topmost lay the counsel's findings. She had finished them on the road and given them to him at the last change of horses with the air of a woman handing over a cake she knew had risen. They said the clause stood and had never been repealed; that the enrollment conformed to its words; that the house's documentary practice was complete, the highest word the form allowed; and that the fire-watch's oil book merited a clerical remark, as, the counsel had told him with satisfaction, it had merited one the last time.

They were correct, and he read them once and set them to one side. They answered every question the notice had asked, and none of the questions he had brought up the bluff in his own head. He had said as much to the boy's face, across a whitewashed table, and meant it.

Beneath them, in the wing clerk's hand, lay the evaluation record; that he read entire.

He read it from the first line, where the clerk had set down the room and the hour and every name at the table and along the wall, to the last, where the clerk had pressed the stamp and written *stamped* beside it as though the stamp might not be believed. Every trial. Every figure. Every interval asked for and the time it was asked. The fault, the void, the reserve frame, the re-run, the cold board. The note, as Gault had read it into the record.

He had sat through every minute of that morning on the fourth chair along the east wall. He read it anyway, every line, for this reason: in forty years he had never once found the two to be identical. It was in the gap between them that he had learned most of what he knew.

There was a gap tonight, as there always was.

The record had the interval. *At the enrollee's request: fatigue affecting response quality.* The clerk had set the minute beside it, and then, in a smaller hand, squeezed in under the line, three words of his own: *before instruments indicated*. No one had asked him to. He had seen that the boy asked before any needle had a reason to move, and had decided that a record which left it out would be a less true record. Vastin made a mark against the clerk in his head, the kind of mark he made perhaps four times a year.

The record had the Mire instructor's words about the second notch, set down as she had demanded, those words and only those. It did not have her face, which had gone the white of a page while she wrenched at the release.

But the shoes were missing.

It had the chalk tray. The clerk at the tray's far end was there, since he had entered himself. But no clerk alive writes his own feet into a ledger, and so the brown shoes standing a pace and a half past the place where a heel would have landed were not anywhere in it. Vastin had seen them. He had seen the boy's answer too, when Gault asked him why. The record had the answer word for word: *Because the clerk was standing where I'd have landed.* Read without the shoes it sounded like a pleasant thing a boy had said. Read with them, it was a figure.

He wrote nothing. The chair complained under him as he leaned back, and he let it.

He let himself go back to the fourth chair for a moment, and to the second notch.

He had been looking at the boy, not the frame, when it went. That had been luck and nothing else; at that moment he had been reading the set of the boy's shoulders, the way a man reads a hand of cards he has not been dealt. The order of it, therefore, he had seen. The hips had dropped for the burst. The held breath had started; there was a small hitch at the throat that went with it every time. And then, before any sound from the rack, before the drag and the late clack that had turned every other head in the room, the whole of that answer had been stopped where it stood and folded back into a quarter of itself.

The sound had come after. He was as sure of that as he was of anything he had seen in a measured room. He had heard the catch of the notch while he was already watching the boy go short.

When the weight struck, he had looked once at the frame, to see where it had come down, as everyone in the room had. After that every head had swung back to the boy for a second look. He had not needed one, and had taken care not to take one, because a second look in a room like that is a question asked with the eyes, and he had no question about it that looking could answer. He had sat on through the eleven minutes, and taken out the notebook, and written.

Even then, with the frame still ringing in his ears and the porters' boots in the passage, he had known that what he was writing was too large. He had written it anyway. That was what the notebook was for.

The pencil on the table had begun its slow journey toward the wall. He watched it go as far as the folded card and stop.

He turned the watch face up, and slid the notebook free.

---

It was small, its calf gone soft at the corners from years of coat pockets, a third of its leaves used and the rest blank. His coat had carried it to the last inspection; it would carry it to the next. Nothing in it ever went into a file. Proof was no part of its purpose. He kept it so that he could set a thought down while it was warm and come back to it cold, and see whether it had survived the cooling.

Two pages of it were Halcenvane.

He turned first to the second, the one from the twentieth, because that was the one the evening had been for.

He had set it down in the eleven minutes the porters took to fetch the reserve frame from the far store, with the notebook open on his knee and the boy at the west rail with his weight off one leg. He had written fast. Then at the very end, when the wing was emptying and he was on his feet with the chair pushed back, he had taken the book out again and drawn a single line through three of the words. One stroke, so that the words could still be read under it. A man who blacks out his own error so that nobody can see what it was has told a lie about his own mind, and Vastin did not tell those on paper if he could avoid it.

He had felt the boy's eyes on him for that stroke, from across the room. He had not looked back. The stroke was not for the boy.

Then, that night, on the guest floor, with the counsel's voice going on through the wall about the state of her collars, he had written the line beneath.

Under the lamp it all read as it had read for four days.

*Frame fault, trial five. Correction preceded the audible release. ~~Capability exceeds classification.~~*

*Correction was sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration. Note the sizing. Not the speed.*

The struck words had been a verdict. They might even be the right verdict; turning them over now, cold, he thought the odds were better than even. But he had set them down within two minutes of the frame failing, before the re-run, before he had watched the boy go back onto the reserve frame with a leg that had just paid for a floor and tell the panel, unasked, which of his runs were the hip's and not his. A finding written that fast is a stake driven before the survey. Every measurement taken afterward gets read as a line toward the stake. The surveyor thinks he is checking his work, and he is only admiring it.

He had learned that once in a cellar office, at twenty-three, from a man whose name he could now barely bring to mind. He had said it since to a good many young officers and let them take it for wisdom. It was not wisdom. It was a rail you put up where you know you are going to fall, and on the twentieth, two hours into a long sitting, with a handsome sentence on the page in his own hand, he had needed the rail as badly as any of them.

So he had struck the handsome sentence, and kept what was under it. The line beneath was a lesser claim and a sturdier one, and he would have traded ten handsome verdicts for it.

*Sized to the hazard, not to the demonstration.*

He had been watching people meet the thing they did not expect for the whole of his working life. A Stone officer in a collapsing gallery who threw up a wall so thick he brought down the roof he was holding off. A Current girl at a qualifying sitting who answered a stumble by pouring out twice her measured strength and burned her own hands on it. A magistrate's guard on a quay at night who met a stranger's sudden movement with a blow that would have stopped a horse, and the stranger only reaching for his papers. Every tier, every service, every age. They gave it more. Surprise came at them like a wall, and they hit the wall harder. It was the steadiest thing he knew about people under strain, steadier than anything in any manual, and he had never set it down anywhere, because something that every man does tells you nothing about any one man.

All morning on the twentieth the boy had shown a burst of one size, again and again, on the board and in the combined trial, to within the width of a brass mark. Then the frame had failed, and the weight had come early and high into a square with no room left, and the boy had not given it more. He had given it a quarter. He had shut his own technique off halfway through paying for it, and put the rest into his own hip, so as not to come down on a man's shoes.

Timing was a thing he could account for, if he allowed the boy the read along the skin that stood in his record and in the Greyvane sittings. Timing could be trained.

The size was the thing.

Sixteen years old. Mid-payment. Under every instrument and every pair of eyes that would ever sit in judgment on his life. And met with a hazard nobody had rehearsed him for, he had answered it *smaller*.

Vastin turned back a page, to the nineteenth.

*The Copper champion is Iron-grade. The bracket is the error. Recommend the advancement evaluation proceed unimpeded.*

That one he had written at the eighth bell in the Crown yard, as the tiers emptied, and had never needed to touch. He had had it by the fourth exchange, watching a Wind fighter on a hip that had no business holding her take an Iron Skin fighter apart with nothing but distance, in a bracket built by people who had imagined neither of them. One day someone at his own height would be asked whether the house's filing for her was sound. He had answered already. There was nothing else worth an Archmarshal's doing at a bracket final. The answer would stand.

The notebook went down by the file. The pencils stayed in their row.

---

He went on through the rest of the file at the pace of the lamp.

The Greyvane transcript, the sittings that touched the provision: a third reading, because the road up had given him two. The exhibits in their red tape. The season's standings under the registrar's seal. Then the inky clerk's ruled column from the twelve-by-fourteen room, the whole interview on one sheet: the minutes counted off down the margin, eleven questions down the middle, and near the foot a question going the other way.

He stopped at that one, as he had known he would.

*What is the Compact evaluating? Me, or the provision?*

People put questions to him across tables as a matter of course. Most of them came wrapped round a want: a softer word in a finding, a delay, a name to blame, a door left open. When had anyone last asked him a question with nothing wrapped round it? He tried to remember, and could not: a person who simply wanted to know, who had seen that knowing would cost some of his own half-hour, and had paid. He had answered the boy truly. He was glad, in a mild way he did not examine, that the question had been put to a man who would.

Then the other question. The one he had not asked.

The boy kept records. That fact sat in every monitoring filing on the file and in the Greyvane transcript besides, and every officer who had ever read the folder had passed over it as the dullest line in it. Vastin had asked what the records were for and been told, and had believed what he was told: *So I can't lie to myself later.* The next question he had let lie. It was the obvious next question. Withrow's counsel had drawn breath to hear it. Gault, at the boy's elbow, had gone as still as a post. Any capable officer would have put it, and any poor one would have put it badly, and he had looked at it for less than a second and not put it.

It had not been mercy.

The mandate on that bluff had been the record against the practitioner, and a private notebook was not in the record. An officer who reaches past his mandate because the reach happens to be open to him has stopped evaluating. He has begun to collect. And a collector at Vastin's height, carrying Vastin's authority, is the worst thing the Compact is able to make.

He had seen one made, when he was young. A senior man of great gifts, who had read other men's letters because he could, and kept what he found, and used it by degrees, a little and then more, until half a district's officers did their work looking over their shoulders at him. Bringing him down had taken four officers, a magistrate and the best part of ten years, and had used up two careers that deserved better. Vastin had been thirty-four on the night it ended. He had stood in a corridor afterward with rain on the window at the end of it and made himself a promise that he would not grow into the thing he had spent those years taking to pieces.

He had kept his hands at his sides, and he would go on keeping them there. On the day the question became properly his, if such a day came, he would ask it in a room with the door shut and tell the boy first. Not before.

It was not this day. It might never be.

The wing's six filings he did not untie again. He had read them on the first morning, before the first bell, and put them where they belonged, a good way under the record: a capable officer's honest account of the chair he had been given and the list he had been handed. There was nothing in them he needed twice. He let the pink tape lie.

Last, before the note, he went through the enrollment file one more time, with his finger running down Bracken's numbered index.

He had handled more enrollment files than he could count, and this one was the best-made of any that had crossed his table in years. Each sheet carried one proof and said so in one line, and nothing argued, and nothing was ornamented. The delegation's counsel, who had wanted very badly to find a weak sheet, had found the load-bearing one in under a minute and a half on her first attempt and had looked, for a moment, as though she might clap.

Good work should be told it is good by somebody who knows the difference, while the man who did it is still in his chair. So he drew a sheet of the house's paper from the table drawer and wrote to the registrar at Halcenvane, privately, four sentences long. What the file had been tested for, he left unsaid.

*Registrar. Your enrollment file on the assay provision is the best-made I have handled in some years. Each sheet proves one thing and says which, and my own counsel, who wished to find a weak one, found the load-bearing sheet on her first attempt, which is the only real test of an index. I thought you should hear it from somebody who has no reason to say it.*

He signed it with his name entire, and set it aside for the morning post going back down the river.

---

One sheet was left, and of everything in the folder it was the one that would last.

A finding is read once, by the people it concerns, and filed. A file note goes on being read, by every officer who ever lifts the folder down, for as long as the folder exists. Whoever came to this folder after him would come to it through his note, and would read the rest in its light, whether they meant to or not. He had spent his life refusing to let other men's digests do that to him. He had no wish to do it to anyone else.

The date went onto a clean sheet. Nothing went under it, for a while.

Instead he took a scrap of the house's paper, the back of a bill, and wrote on it the first thing an officer in his place would write.

*No adverse finding.*

He looked at it. It was true. It was also a door shut in the face of whoever might need it open in a year, and it said nothing that the findings had not already said. He folded the scrap in half.

On the other half went the second thing.

*Subject warrants attention.*

The second got shorter shrift. Within a month it would put a warden on the bluff road. And in a boy who had already sat through one adjudication, it would bring on exactly the behaviour that turns a monitoring file into a stage play, because a boy who knows why he is watched begins to perform for the watcher, and nothing true has ever been got out of a performance. He tore it across. The halves lay one on the other by the lamp.

The third he never put to paper.

It was the kind reading, and half the continent had reached it already. A small house in the hills had staked a year on it, and he did not think less of them. It said the file was a mistake. Some clerk at some station had made one wrong mark at a boy's Kindling, and everything since, two academies and a hearing and a provision and a stamp, had been the long labour of picking out one bad stitch. It was comfortable because it asked nobody to change their mind about anything: the machine was sound, it had caught its sleeve once on one boy, and decent people had spent a year working the sleeve free.

He did not believe it.

It was not the boy's gifts. The registry swallowed a few remarkable children every year without so much as a hiccup; finding them a shelf was what it had been built for. What decided him was an absence: no carelessness anywhere. A slip leaves fingermarks. He had hunted this file for them for eleven days and four nights and found not one.

Nobody had decided anything about the boy when the designation went onto his sheet. The Kindling had entered it, in the first minutes of the file's life. It had stood ever since. Three houses, a public adjudication, and a registrar's index that left the other side almost nothing to say: it had come through all of them. And through all of it nobody, at any height, had put forward another classification, or amended the entry by a word, or written so much as a sheet suggesting it be looked at again.

A slipped hand gets corrected. This has never been touched.

So the record was not broken. It was doing something.

He did not write that. He had no evidence for it. It lay a very long way outside his mandate. And an officer of his grade who puts a guess into a file has arranged for some junior man, three years on, to act on the guess without its history and without its doubt.

He could set down the finding his own eyes had earned, and stop at the edge of it.

The line went down in his plain upright clerk's hand, the hand he had written in since his cuffs were inky, and under it went his name: all of it, as on everything he signed. He had never in forty years found a reason good enough for an officer to hide his name on a paper that might ruin someone.

*Classification error is unlikely. Continue observation.*

He laid down the pen.

He did not underline anything. He read it once, and it still meant what he meant, and he knew no better proof for a sentence than that. The tapes went back round the folder, grey, red and string. He put the two halves of the torn scrap into the lamp's chimney, one after the other, and watched them go. Then he turned the lamp down to nothing and stayed at the dark window over the empty yard, his left hand, which had begun to dislike evenings as well as mornings, lying open on his knee.

# Chapter 60 — The Door

From across the street, at the second bell, the registry station looked like nothing at all. It had been a grain factor's counting house once, before the Compact bought it, and it stood three streets back from the wharfs, where the town stopped smelling of fish and started smelling of rope.

Cael wrote that down first, standing in the doorway of a chandler's that had not yet opened, on the fifth of the new month, with a fine rain deciding whether to begin. Two storeys of brown stone. A doorstep hollowed like a bowl by a hundred years of grain buyers coming in to argue. Beside the door, a brass plate about the size of a book, which said what the building was in letters too small to read from the far side of the street. A rope-maker's on one side of it, with coils of new rope hung in the window like sleeping snakes, and on the other a shuttered house with a cat on its sill.

In his head he had made the place enormous. He had never once meant to, and he had never noticed doing it, but somewhere since his own Kindling every registry station in the world had become, in the back of his mind, a hall with a high roof and a long bench and a cold floor. And here was one of them, being a building, between a rope-maker and a cat.

Beside him Lira, kit bag on one shoulder, gave the building a long, unfriendly study.

"Smaller than Denvash," she said.

"Everything's smaller than Denvash."

"No. I mean it's small." She hitched the strap higher. "Fenmark had a hall. A great tall window you could see from the far end of the square, and a hall behind it where they kept you waiting in rows like loaves. There were forty of us. Every time an assessor came through the far door we all stood up, and every time he went out again we all sat down." Her voice carried none of it. That was how Cael knew the weight. "I stood up nine times that morning before they got to me."

"And this is a door."

"This is a door." Her eyes went to the hollowed step. "I think I prefer the door."

Nobody had asked who would come. At first light all four of them had simply been at the residence door with their coats on, and nobody had said anything about it, and they had gone down the bluff road together in the grey. At the road's foot the two men in grey coats had been changing on the bell. One of them had looked at Lira's kit bag for just as long as a man looks at a bag, and then at the river. In the town Karis had bought four hot rolls from a baker's hatch and handed them round without asking who wanted one, and they had eaten them walking, and Brom had paid the bridge toll for all of them, as he always did, before anybody else could get a coin out.

Bracken had come too. He wore his own plain coat instead of the registrar's gown, and he carried a leather satchel with Halcenvane's sponsorship file in it: the certified season record, the witnessed final, Gault's filing on the registry's long form with Ilsev's line under it, and three hundred sheets behind them in his numbered order. He had carried it down the hill himself and would not let Brom take it.

"Ninety seconds," he said at the curb. "That's my part. I put the file on a desk and say what it is. Then I'm furniture with a bag. The rest is hers and the frame's, and I'd thank you all to remember whose it is."

"Good," said Lira.

She crossed the street with Bracken a pace behind her, and at the dished step she did not pause. She went up it and opened the door herself and went in, and the door shut, and Cael stood across the street from a grain factor's counting house with his hands in his coat and his heart going much harder than the occasion seemed to call for.

"Come on," said Karis, gently, and took his sleeve. "There'll be chairs."

---

Inside, the counting house still smelled of grain.

It was in the boards, Lira thought. A hundred years of sacks dragged across them had left something in the wood that no amount of sweeping got out, a dry sweet dusty smell that went straight to the back of her throat. The room inside the door had been the factor's front office once. Now it held a high desk with a clerk at it, a bench, four chairs round a stove that nobody had lit, and a door in the far wall with a brass handle worn bright.

Bracken laid the satchel on the clerk's desk and opened it.

She watched him do it. She had seen him behind his own counter for most of a year, and she had never seen him on the wrong side of one. He did it the same way. He took the file out in its order and laid it square to the desk's edge. He set it facing the clerk. He put his finger on the first sheet, the filing itself, and then on the line under it where the season record was named, and then on the witness's line, and said the name of each in a voice that did not rise or hurry. The clerk read along behind his finger and nodded at each one.

"Advancement evaluation," said the clerk, "on the grounds stated."

"On the grounds stated."

"The practitioner is present?"

"She is," said Bracken, and took his hand off the file, and stepped back from the desk.

That was the ninety seconds. Lira counted them because she could not help it; she had caught counting off Cael the way other people caught a cold. Ninety, near enough. Then Bracken picked up the empty satchel and went and stood by the cold stove with it under his arm, and became, as he had said he would, a man in a room holding a bag.

He did not look at her. She was grateful for it.

The clerk gathered the file and took it through the far door, and the door shut behind him, and Lira sat down on the bench to wait.

It was a plain deal bench, worn pale along the front edge by a great many people sitting on it in this same way, leaning forward a little with their hands between their knees. She made herself sit back. Across the room Bracken stood by the stove reading a notice about lamp oil with every sign of finding it the most interesting paper of his year.

She thought about the porter.

Every morning since she came to the bluff, at the fifth bell, the porter had climbed his little ladder at the board in the Crown yard and chalked the same word beside her name. He did it carefully, in a round hand, and polished the letters with his cuff when he was done. Six letters. He had chalked them beside her name on the morning she arrived and on the morning after the final, when she was the champion of the bracket the word belonged to, and he would chalk them tomorrow unless somebody gave him a reason not to. Nothing she did on a floor could reach that chalk. She had known that all season. Fiske had said it to her at the board, flatly, two years into knowing it herself: win it, and then make them look at you.

Today somebody was going to look. That was all today was.

She did not feel anything about it that she could have put a name to. She felt the bench under her and the hip, which was stiff in the cold of the unlit room, and her own hands, which were quite steady. She had expected them not to be, and looked at them for a while to make sure.

Then the clerk came back and held the far door open, and said her name.

The room beyond it had been the factor's counting floor. It was long and bare and lit from high windows along one wall, and the boards were scrubbed nearly white down the middle where the work was done. Overhead an iron bracket stood out from the end wall, empty, where the factor's great weighing beam must once have hung. Lira looked at it longer than she meant to. At the foundry the master had proved the beam before every pour, with his locked box of brass weights, and would believe no number until he had watched it proved.

Nobody would be proving anything in front of her here. That was not what this room was for.

Two assessors sat at a table under the windows. One was a broad grey man with a slate on his knee. The other was a younger woman with the station's book open in front of her and a pen already inked. Between the windows, set into the end wall at about the height of a hand held out, was a frame of dark metal the size of a door panel, and in the frame, a sigil.

She looked at it once and then made herself look at the man with the slate.

"Practitioner Lira," he said. "Wind Path. Copper, Rank Two, on her present certificate. Filed for advancement by an assessment office on the record of a season." He did not look at the file. He had read it already. "We'll run the standard evasion sequence first. Then the load figures. Then the frame." He tipped his head toward the wall without turning. "Have you run the sequence before?"

"No."

The woman's pen paused.

"It's taught in the second year at most registry schools," said the man. "Every Wind practitioner who comes through this room has run it."

"I didn't go to a registry school. I went to a foundry." Lira put her bag down by the door. "If you describe it, I'll run it."

He looked at her for a moment with no expression at all, and then he described it.

Twelve marks on the floor in chalk, in a pattern she had to hold in her head from one hearing: a line, a hook, a check, two returns, a long diagonal, and a set of four small steps at the end that the sequence called the stair. A burst wherever the marks were wide apart, a step where they were close. No touching any mark twice. The whole of it to be run without stopping, and timed.

"Whenever you're ready," he said.

She ran it, and got it wrong.

She read the hook as a turn and went left out of it, as anybody who had ever fought a Force fighter would have gone left, and found herself standing on the wrong side of the check with nowhere to go but back across a mark she had already touched. The woman wrote something. Lira walked back to the first chalk without being told. Nobody in the room said anything. The man with the slate did not look disappointed and the woman did not look pleased; they looked like people waiting for a kettle.

She ran it again and got it wrong a second time. This time she had the hook right. But at the long diagonal her body did what it had done on a hundred floors, where a gap that size meant somebody was about to close it, and it put in a burst where the sequence wanted three plain steps, and she arrived at the stair a full stride early and had to stop dead. Her hip told her about the stop. It had been knitting for a month now, and it had views on stopping dead.

She stood on the first mark of the stair and breathed, and looked back down the line of chalk at what she had done to it. She had fought on that kind of floor her whole life: one where the marks meant somebody, where a gap was a door somebody was about to close. Here the marks meant nothing. They were only where the sequence said to put her feet, and her feet had refused to believe it.

*It isn't a fight*, she thought. *Nobody's on the other side of it. You're answering things that aren't there.*

She walked back to the first mark again, and on the way she made herself look at the chalk the way the man with the slate looked at it: as a drawing, and not as a floor with anybody on it. The hook was only a hook. The check was a place to put a foot, and nothing would come through it. The long diagonal was three plain steps because somebody long ago had decided it should be three plain steps, and there was no enemy at the end of it to be early for.

The third time she did not fight it. She ran it the way the foundry boys walked the casting floor when the moulds were laid out, every step put down where the step was meant to go and not where a step would be useful. Line, hook, check. The returns. The diagonal in three plain steps, which felt like walking through water. The stair, four small steps, so small they made her feel foolish. The burst at the wide gap, clean. She came off the last mark with her breath even and the hip quiet.

The man with the slate looked at the woman, and the woman wrote a figure.

"Third run stands," he said. "The first two are noted as runs."

"They should be," said Lira.

---

The load figures were only numbers, and she had known her numbers for two years.

A rope off a drum, a sprung plate on the far wall, a measured rod along the floor: how far the burst carried, how much it could carry with it, how fast she was off the line again after the landing. Five runs at each. The woman read the figures aloud as the man called them, so that both of them would have heard every one. Lira did not listen to them. She knew what they were. They had been Iron since Vell kept them in her ledger at Ardenmere, and everyone who had stood at a rail and watched her fight had known it for two years. The only thing that had ever been missing was a station.

So she gave the station its numbers. She took the rope off the drum in her right hand and burst against its pull, and felt the drum fight her and lose. She put her shoulder to the sprung plate at the end of a burst and felt it give and come back. She ran the measured rod five times, landing and leaving, landing and leaving, and on the fourth landing the hip sent up a single bright complaint, like a nail in a boot, and she let it complain and went on. On the fifth she was off the line faster than on the first. The woman read the figure out twice, in case she had misread it, and the man said, "Yes," without looking up.

Then the man set down his slate.

"The frame," he said.

She went to it. It was cold to stand near, the way metal is cold in a room that has not had a fire in it, and up close the sigil was not as plain as it had looked from the door. There were lines in it too fine to follow. They did not catch the light so much as hold it.

"The flat of your hand on the centre," said the woman. "Keep it there until it's done. It will tell you when."

At Fenmark she had been fourteen and frightened and nobody had told her anything. She had put her hand where a clerk pointed, and something had come and looked at her for about ninety breaths, and then a man who had not once lifted his head from his page had written *Copper* and *Rank One*, and that had been her. She had not cried until she was outside.

She put her hand on the centre.

It took four seconds. Nobody had ever told her that, either: that it was not a flash. Something went down into her from the palm, and down, the way cold water goes down when you drink it too fast, and it *read*. It went through the hip and the old foundry burns on her forearm and the place under the ribs where Brom had hit her in the rain at Ardenmere, and through all the floors she had fought on since, and it did not hurry. For four seconds there was nothing in the whole world but a system deciding what she was. There was not one thing she could do about it while it happened. She had known that before she put out her hand. She had decided, on the road down the bluff, that she would not try to do anything, and she kept to it. She stood with her palm on the cold metal and her breath even and let it look.

It let go.

Behind her the man read something off the frame, and the woman wrote it down.

"Wind," said the man. "Iron. Rank One."

She had thought, on the road down, that when she heard it she would feel something enormous, and that she would have to stand very still to keep it from showing. She did not feel anything enormous. She felt the cold of the frame going out of her palm a little at a time, and a small sore place at the back of her heel where her boot had rubbed on the bluff road, and the bench-hard ache in the hip. Ninety breaths at Fenmark had made her Copper. Four seconds in a grain factor's counting room had unmade it. It seemed to her that there ought to be more to it than that, and that there was not, and that this was exactly right.

Lira took her hand off the sigil and looked at her palm, which was perfectly ordinary. It had begun, now that it was over, to shake a little. She put it in her coat pocket so that nobody else would have to see it, and held it there in a fist until it stopped.

"Thank you," she said.

"The supersession," said the woman, "will take a few minutes. If you'll sit."

It took eleven. A clerk came in from the front office with a second book, thicker than the first, and sat at the end of the table, and entered the supersession in it, line by line, with a pen that scratched. While he wrote, the woman explained to Lira that her Fenmark certificate would not be destroyed. It would remain in the registry as a historical entry, marked superseded, so that the line of her record was unbroken, and any reader in future would be able to see where she had stood before and on what date she had been moved. The prior classification had to be superseded on the same instrument that made the new one, the woman said, or it stayed live underneath, and that caused trouble later.

Lira sat on a hard chair with her hands folded in her lap and said, "I see," and, "Yes," and, "Thank you," at the right places, and was more polite than she had been to anybody since she was twelve. A fly was walking up the inside of the high window over the table. The clerk's pen scratched. The smell of old grain came up out of the boards.

Tomorrow morning, she thought, the porter would climb his ladder at the fifth bell and find that he had been given a reason. He would need a new word beside her name, and it would be shorter than the old one, and he would grumble about having to rub out a good one. She found that this, out of the whole morning, was the thing that very nearly made her smile, and she kept it off her face with some care, because the clerk was writing and it did not seem polite.

At the end of the eleven minutes the woman handed her a single folded sheet, and stood, and so Lira stood too.

"Practitioner," said the man with the slate, and nodded once.

She did not unfold the sheet. She knew what was on it; she had heard it read off a frame. She stood in the little passage between the two rooms with her bag over her shoulder and the sheet in her hand, and took a moment there that nobody could see her take.

On the other side of the door, Cael would have counted. She knew that the way she knew which foot she would land on. He would have counted the minutes and very likely her breaths too, though he could not hear them, and he would tell her the number tonight whether she asked or not. Brom would be looking at the floor. Karis would have a book on her knee that she had not read a line of. And Bracken would be standing, because Bracken stood.

She found that she knew what she was going to say to them, and that it was short, and that she did not need to practise it.

She went back out through the door with the worn brass handle, the sheet in her hand and the bag over her shoulder, and her hip not hurting at all.

Cael had counted every one of the fifty-one minutes.

There was no stopping the count. He did it by the clock over the clerk's desk, an old counting-house clock with a brass weight on a chain that dropped a finger's breadth in a quarter of an hour, and he did it under the clock as well, in his own head, in breaths. The two counts did not agree. He kept them both.

The anteroom had four chairs and the cold stove. Brom sat down hard, bent forward, and fixed his eyes on a knot in the floorboards that seemed to owe him money. Bracken did not take one. He stood by the stove with the empty satchel under his arm, because Bracken stood. Cael took the chair by the stove, across from Bracken, and found he could not sit back in it.

He knew what was behind the far door. He had stood in front of a frame like that once himself, at Denvash, with his palm on cold metal and something going down into him. He did not remember much else of that day, or did not let himself. He remembered the cold of the frame, and the waiting while it looked, and that he had not been able to do anything at all.

Karis took the chair nearest the window and opened her satchel and took out a slim book on harbour law and laid it on her knee.

She did not open it.

Cael had seen her not open a book once before, on the morning of the twentieth, in the common room, with a page that had not turned between the second bell and his coming home. That had been twice now. He filed it, with some care, as the nearest thing to a tell that Karis had.

At the twenty-second minute by the clock the far door opened. A clerk came out with a form, crossed the anteroom without looking at anybody, and went up the stair in the corner. All four of them watched him go with an attention that made the back of his neck turn red. He came down again in a little while with a different form, and crossed back, and went in, and the door shut.

"Ordinary," said Bracken.

He said it as a man reads a date off a docket. He had handled more paper than anybody in that building, and he would have used a different word if it had been a different paper. Brom let out a breath he had clearly been holding since the stair.

The brass weight went down. The rain had begun outside after all, very fine, and the window by Karis was misting at its lower edge.

The weight on its chain had dropped past the fifty-first minute when the far door opened, and it was Lira.

A folded sheet hung from one hand. Her face had nothing on it. She did not smile, or stand straighter, or do any of the half-dozen things people do with their faces when a door has just closed behind them on a result. She walked across to them at her ordinary pace. In front of them she unfolded the sheet and turned it face out. That was all the ceremony she would ever make over anything, and she made it at arm's length, holding the sheet away from her as if it might drip.

The certificate was printed. Somebody had filled in the blanks in ink.

*Path: Wind. Tier: Iron. Rank: One. Advancement confirmed on demonstration and record. Prior classification superseded.*

Nobody said anything.

Brom was the first to move. "Huh," he said, and got up, and put out his right hand, the one that had been strapped a fortnight ago. Lira regarded the hand like an object she had never met before. Then she shifted the sheet to her left and shook it, once, hard.

"Say it," said Karis.

"Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One."

"Louder. There's a clerk."

"*Wind Path, Iron-tier, Rank One*." It came out loud enough to lift the desk clerk's head from his page, and he put it down again at once.

Before Lira had finished, the grey notebook was open on Karis's forearm. She wrote it standing, then and there, at the pace of somebody setting down a thing she had been waiting a very long time to set down.

Bracken said nothing at all. He put the empty satchel over his shoulder, and nodded once to the clerk at the desk, and held the street door open for the four of them, and that was all he did. But Cael, going out past him, saw that he was holding the door with his whole hand flat against it, the way a man steadies a thing that he wants very much to stay exactly where it is.

On the way down to the river she and Cael drew ahead of the others. He had asked her to walk with him. She seemed, too, to have chosen him for something while she was behind that door, and he let her take her time about what it was.

She did not tell him much. She told him about the sequence, and the hook she had read as a turn, and the three plain steps that had felt like walking through water. She told him about the grain smell. She told him about the empty bracket where the weighbeam had hung, and he nodded and did not say anything, because he knew what a weighbeam was to her.

"And the frame," he said.

For most of a street she said nothing.

"It takes four seconds," she said. "Nobody tells you that. You think it's like a lamp, on or off. It isn't. It goes into you and reads. Slowly. All the way down. Four seconds of nothing in the world but a thing making up its mind about you. And you can't do anything. Not one thing. You stand there with your palm on it and you let it."

"I know," said Cael.

She stopped walking.

"Yes," she said, after a moment. "You do. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You're not finished."

She started walking again. The rain had set in now, small and steady. At the foot of the street the houses stopped and the wharfs began, and beyond them the river lay wide and grey and full of barges. A mile upstream the bluff rose out of the water with Halcenvane strung along its top, small from here, and steady, and wet.

Lira looked at it for a while.

"Say it," Cael told her. "You've been holding it since the anteroom."

She did not look at him. She looked at the bluff, and at the river, and at the certificate in her hand, which she had still not folded again.

Then she said, "They didn't give me anything today. They caught up."

# Chapter 61 — Retired

The Copper Crown had stood on the common-room table since the night of the final, among the bread and the ice bags and Karis's papers, and nobody had known what to do with it.

It was not a large thing: a plain circlet of beaten copper on a brass mount, dented once on the rim where some champion long ago had dropped it, with a plate riveted to the mount that had been riveted on twice because the first rivets had pulled. The porter polished it every term whether anybody had won it or not. Since the final it had acquired a ring of dried tea on its mount where Brom had put a cup down without looking, and a smear of chalk from Lira's thumb.

The evening of the fifth found Lira at that table, the Iron certificate folded square under her hand, studying the crown.

Karis wanted it moved. "It can't live with the bread."

"Doorstop," said Brom. He was lying on the hearthrug with his knee up, healed now except for a stiffness in the mornings that he denied. "That door's never shut properly. It'd hold it."

"No."

"Send it to the foundry. Your master would hang it on the beam."

"He'd melt it," said Lira. "He'd say it was good copper wasted on a hat."

Brom had another idea. "The wash-house post. Hang it there, and I'll hit it for luck."

"You'll dent it," said Karis.

"It's dented already." Brom pointed at the rim without lifting his head from the rug. "Some champion dropped it about the year they built the bridge. It's had worse than me."

"Wear it to lectures."

"Brom."

"I'm being useful," said Brom, to the ceiling. "Nobody else is."

Lira picked the crown up off the table in both hands. She held it a moment, not looking at it so much as weighing it, as she weighed things at the foundry, by the arms. Then she got up and crossed the room and set it on the shelf over the settle, between the good lamp and a stack of Karis's drafts that Karis had told everyone three times were superseded and must on no account be thrown away.

She stepped away to judge it from the hearth.

She said, "There. It's retired."

"That's it?" said Brom. "Just there? On the shelf?"

"Where else? It's not mine to keep in a box. I've left that bracket, and it won't see me again." She did not turn round. "But nothing else I own says it out loud, with a plate riveted on: the bracket had me wrong. You don't put a thing like that in a drawer. Prizes go to winners. That's what I got for having to."

Nobody said anything for a while. From the hearthrug Brom gave the ceiling a slow nod.

That night the Log got her words exactly as she had said them. Then Cael's pen went on past them, on its own account.

*It was the crown that made them look. Fiske told her as much at the board on the night of the seeding bout. "It's the looking that matters — it was always the looking. The crown's just how you force it." That night Lira gave me the sentences exact, and I wrote them in, and neither of us could quite take them for a plan.*

*It was a plan. It took a whole engine of parts: a season on a public board, a final in front of witnesses, a registrar willing to certify the record before the result was known, an officer of record willing to file, an assessor at his elbow to witness the filing, and a Compact evaluation seat sitting in the second row of the north tier while it all happened. Six pieces. Fiske had drawn all six for her in four sentences at a standings board. She had been two years in the same box. And she handed the plan to the one fighter she thought could use it.*

*I've had a page headed* Fiske *since the first month. I looked at it tonight. It's all footwork: where she plants, how long she collects, which side she places her first declaration. There isn't a single line on it about her.*

*Fix that.*

The porter climbed his ladder at the fifth bell the next morning, the same as every morning, with his chalk in one hand and his rag in the other.

Guessing what the morning would bring, Cael had come down early to see it. His post was the foot of the north stair, hands deep in his coat. The Crown yard was nearly empty at that hour; two Mire first-years were sweeping the oak, and a Blade fourth-year was stretching against the rail, and the board stood up against the long wall with its columns of names chalked in the porter's round careful hand.

Lira's name stood at the top of the Copper column, where it had stood since the final, with the word beside it.

For a breath the porter only considered the word. Then he took a folded slip out of his apron, the registrar's office slip, and read it, though he must have read it already, and put it back in his apron. Letter by letter, six of them, the rag took the word away. Then, in its place, he chalked the new one, shorter, in the same round hand. He leaned back on the ladder to look at it, and polished it with his cuff.

Then he moved her name.

He had to. With that word beside it, the name had no place left among the Copper. He rubbed it out of the top of the column, letter by letter, and climbed down and moved the ladder along the wall, and climbed again, and chalked it at the foot of the Iron column, under Ephram and the rest, with the new word beside it. Down there, under a column of strangers, it looked small. The porter regarded it for a while with his head on one side. Then he climbed down and put the ladder over his shoulder and went away to do the Silver board, and nobody in the yard but Cael had watched him do any of it.

Beside the board, by the second bell, somebody from the registrar's office had pinned up a copy of the certificate, as the house did with every change of tier, so that anyone who wanted to know why a name had moved could read the reason.

Fiske came into the yard a little after the second bell.

She came in by the north arch with her kit over her shoulder, on her way to the session she had on the oak at that hour, and she saw the board before she was halfway across. Cael watched her see it. She stopped. She looked at the empty place at the head of the Copper column where Lira's name had been all season, and then down the wall to the foot of the Iron column, and then at the pinned copy beside the board.

She went and read it. She stood in front of it with her bag on her shoulder, her weight square on both feet, and read it from the top to the bottom, and Cael thought she read it twice.

Her own name stood near the top of the Copper column, with the same six letters beside it that had been there for two years.

She did not look round the yard. She did not say anything to the Mire first-years with their brooms, or to the fourth-year at the rail, or to anybody. She did not smile, either, and she did not do the opposite of smiling. She settled her bag on her shoulder and walked on across the yard to the oak, and set the bag down at her chalk, and began to warm up, as she did every morning, alone, on time.

Cael stayed by the stair a good while after.

He had watched people be gracious before, a good deal of it. Graciousness had a look: it was performed, a little, even when it was sincere, because it knew it was being watched. This had not been that. Here was a woman who had once said aloud how a thing would go, watching it go that way for someone else. She was right, and she had nothing whatever to gain from being right, and she had gone to her session.

He went up to the library and opened the observation notebook at the page headed *Fiske*, and turned past all the footwork to a clean leaf, and began at last to write about her.

Two days later, crossing the Crown yard at the slack hour of the afternoon, Cael heard his name from the oak.

The yard was nearly bare. The term was almost done, and the porters had begun taking down the season: the brackets were off the long wall, the tiers had been swept, and a man at the far end was coiling the rope that marked the floor's edge on ladder days. On the oak, alone, Ephram was running a Blade drill against nobody, slow, the same six steps over and over, and he stopped in the middle of the fourth and called across.

"Assay. A minute."

Cael went down the tier stair. He read Ephram on the way, out of a habit too old to stop: rhythm even and unhurried, weight on the balls of the feet, breath recovering from the drill in long pulls, no hurry in the shoulders. No quarrel anywhere in him. Ephram met him at the edge of the floor with the sweat still on him and his practice blade held loosely, point down.

"I've put a sheet on Rooke's desk," said Ephram. "Supervised floor time, next term, you and me. Rooke's view is that the provision covers it, as floor hours tied to your evaluations, and his signature's waiting on yours."

It was not what Cael had expected; he had expected nothing at all from Ephram until the recess was over, and then perhaps a nod on the stair.

"Why?"

"Two reasons. The first's about me, and it's the one I'd rather not say, so I'll say it first." Ephram gave the blade in his hand a sour look. "In the first month I stood up there," he tipped his head at the top of the yard, "in front of sixty people and said I'd wait for your evaluation before I made up my mind. Then on the lecture stair, with thirty more listening, I changed my terms in your favour and thought well of myself for doing it in the open. Both times I was announcing a feint. *Watch me, I'm going to judge this.* And what I'd appointed myself to judge was a closed panel that would never have let me through its door. Everybody on this hill knew that but me."

"Plenty of people make promises they can't keep."

"Not in front of sixty people. Not on purpose." Ephram's mouth twisted. "So I'm not keeping it. I'm putting it down, here, where there's nobody to see me do it but you, because you're the one it was about."

"And the second reason?"

"I paid six marks for the transcript and read every page. It's very good on law. On whether you can fight, it's silent. Nothing anybody says about you can tell me either; I've heard a dozen versions this year and I couldn't check a single one. So I'll learn it the only honest way there is." The blade's point knocked once on the oak. "Here. With Rooke at the rail. Then whatever I think, I'll have earned it."

"That's fair," said Cael.

"It's the only fair thing left." Ephram turned back toward his chalk, and then turned round again. "One more thing. Rooke's cohort works at a speed you have never been hit at. Ask your friend. A season and two losses taught him, and he's made for taking blows. Better you hear it now, with the yard empty, than find it out from me in front of Rooke."

"Understood."

"Next term," said Ephram, and went back to his six steps, slow, the interruption already put away.

Cael put it in the Log that night, under Ephram's own page and then on a fresh one.

*Told me the speed before the session, not after. That's worth more to me than whatever happens in it.*

*Most of a year he'd carried a promise made in public, one the house's own rules forbade him ever to keep. He could have let it quietly drop. Nobody would have said a word. Instead he took it apart in front of the one person it concerned, in a yard with nobody else in it, and then went back to his drill.*

*Rooke under the north arch, on the day the notice came. Ephram on the oak this afternoon. Two people this year who changed their minds about me to my face, when standing pat would have cost them nothing.*

*I am going to be very careful about how I remember Halcenvane.*

On the twelfth Withrow closed the year.

She did it in the great hall of the lecture range with the whole house in front of her: the faculty along the front benches, the cohorts behind them by Path, and the clerks and porters standing along the back wall where they always stood. The clock over the door was two minutes slow, and Cael had begun to suspect that it would stay two minutes slow until she retired, and that somebody would mend it the week after and feel very bold.

The first part of it was the ordinary business of shutting a house for the recess, and she gave it the attention of a woman counting linen. The roof over the north hall was poor and would be seen to. The wing's frame had been rebuilt, and the man who built frames had been written to, at length. The porters were thanked one by one, by name, and the porter of the Crown yard went very red. Brom shifted on the bench. Lira, beside Cael, sat with one hand inside her coat, resting flat over the pocket where the folded certificate lived, as though it might otherwise get up and leave.

Then Withrow took a single sheet from under the others and held it up, so that the hall could see it was a real paper and not a speech.

"Here is what the Compact found," said Withrow. "It was filed in the first days of this month and reached the registrar on the ninth. I will read you its four lines, because they are short, and because you earned them."

"*Charter compliance: full. Enrollment basis: sound. Documentary practice: complete. Remarks: one, clerical, the fire-watch oil book.*"

She lowered the sheet.

"Some of you are new, so I'll tell you what you've just heard. There is no finding above that one. A delegation does not come to praise a house; it comes to look for what is wrong, and this one looked for eleven days with an Archmarshal at its head, and the only fault it could put its name to was a book of lamp oil." Half the hall laughed, and Withrow waited for it to stop of its own accord. "I'd ask the fire-watch to keep their oil book better. "Now the thing I have waited a long time to say from here. I'll say it bare, because it is a fact.

"Halcenvane Academy will contest the qualifying season. The tournament is the Continental, its next cycle opens a year from now, and this house will be in it."

The hall did what a hall does.

Spectacles in hand, Withrow let it run. She had budgeted for the noise as she budgeted for everything, and only when it had spent itself did the spectacles go back on.

"You may have three sentences of meaning from me," she said. "After that you may carry your excitement home to the residences and keep it there.

Cael found Karis in the top-floor reading room that afternoon, and she was already still.

He knew it from the door. Bracken's copy of the tournament charter lay open in front of her, and the Greyvane notebooks had stood stacked by her left hand since the evening of the hand-cart. She was not reading. She had one finger on a line and was not moving at all. Not her eyes, not her breath that he could see. A year and a half had taught him the look: a page had done something to her. A word inside that first minute would cost her the thread.

He eased the door shut, took the far chair, opened the observation notebook at Fiske's page, and let her be.

After a while she took up her pen and copied something into the grey notebook, slowly, one word at a time, checking each word against the charter before she wrote the next. The open notebook came sliding down the table to him. She said nothing.

He read the copied line.

*Participating academies shall field any practitioner appearing on their enrollment record.*

He read it a second time, then looked up.

"Sixth part," said Karis. "Participation. The fourth subsection." Her voice was very even. "Read the preamble before you say anything. It's on the facing page."

He swung the charter round to face him. Most of the paragraph was dry history. Halfway down, the history woke up. Forty years ago a famous house had left two of its strongest practitioners out of a qualifying season altogether, though both were fit and both were enrolled. It had done it to keep a richer house sweet over a bargain about postings. The tournament's board had been so angry that it wrote the fourth subsection the following year, so that no house could ever again keep a person off its side for reasons of its own.

"It's a compulsion clause," he said.

"It's a compulsion clause. Written by angry people, in a hurry, about something else entirely." Her palm went flat to the wood. "Now read the line again. *Any practitioner.* Then *enrollment record*. That's all. It does not say any *classified* practitioner. Nor of any rated tier. It doesn't name the schedule. I've been through the whole sixth part twice, every subsection, looking for the place where somebody thought to add it, and nobody did."

Neither of them said the next thing. It sat on the table between them.

Cael's eyes stayed on the copied line. Later, writing it down, he set down first what he had felt first, because that was the honest order: not hope, and not excitement. It was a flat, cold sort of knowing, the kind you feel meeting somebody in a strange town whose face you have seen before. Old words, set down by the dead for one purpose, quietly doing another they had never dreamed of, with nobody left alive to ask what they had meant.

The assay clause had been that. Greyvane's schedule, with its gap, had been one too. It had the shape of every good thing that ever happened to him.

"Somebody will fight it," he said.

"Somebody will fight every word of it. By counsel. Before the tournament's board. Clause on clause. Somewhere there are clauses I haven't found that lean on this one, and at least one leans against us." Karis shut the charter, gently, the way one shuts a door on someone asleep. "And I won't touch it. Not this week, not this month. Bracken won't hear it from me, nor the chancellor, nor Lira over supper. I want to tell you why before you want to move, because you will want to, and it's cheaper to argue now than later."

"Tell me."

"A question you ask early is a question somebody else gets to answer first." She held his eyes. "Walk it down to counsel before the recess and it's out of this room. By the time we need it, some other house will have read it as well, and written its note on it. The people who've thought longest about this line will be people who did their thinking on the other side of the table."

"So it goes in the book and stays there."

"Into the book, and there it sits." She took the grey notebook back, and beside the copied line she wrote a single word, and Cael read it across the table, upside down, as he read most things.

*Interesting.*

She closed the notebook on it.

"That's all it is," she said. "Right now it's somebody else's sentence and one word of mine. Should it ever grow into something, it grows next year, out of documents, with a season's labour under it. Not a day less."

"Agreed."

"Don't agree with me. Decide."

"It's banked," said Cael. "No one touches it. You, me, anybody, until it's ready."

"Good," said Karis. The notebook went inside her coat, and she turned back to the charter's fifth part, on apparatus, which she had not yet read; her head did not come up again until the bell.

# Chapter 62 — The Wall

Hesk's answer came up the bluff on the twelfth, by the evening coach, which was fast even for Hesk.

Cael had posted his own letter on the morning of the twenty-seventh of Reaping, and he had not expected a reply until after the recess began, if then. Hesk, though, wrote back the way some men catch a thrown ball: the answer was already on its way before the question landed. Cael took it from his pigeonhole in the cloister and stood there and opened it in the poor light, because he could not make himself wait until the stair.

It was a single sheet, folded twice, in the large exact hand that had grown larger every year since Cael left home. There were four lines on it.

*An Archmarshal looked at you and wrote 'keep looking.' In my day that was called respect. Don't tell him I said so. Sixteen suits you.*

He read it four times standing in the cloister, with first-years going past him in both directions on their way to supper.

Hesk could not know what any Archmarshal had written. Nobody on the bluff knew that, and nobody in Hesk's workshop at the far end of the country was going to know it either. What Hesk had was Cael's own letter: the stamp, Gault's last lines copied out, and one paragraph about a room twelve feet by fourteen and a man with eleven questions who had walked up to the edge of a twelfth and left it alone. Out of that, at a bench, with his spectacles on his forehead, Hesk had guessed what a man like that would put on paper afterward. It was a grandfather's guess. It was not evidence of anything.

None of that troubled him.

He went up to his room and put the sheet inside the notebook's front board, behind the cover, where the other one already lived: the folded note that had come in the pine case with the pen on the eleventh of Sowing. Two sheets now, in the same hand. What it cost him to put the second beside the first, he kept out of the Log. Some lines in a ledger are better left as a space.

The second letter came the next day, and it had not been looked for at all.

It was in a hand he knew from a circuit rating card at Ardenmere, small and square, a ledger-keeper's hand. The cover had been readdressed twice: once from Greyvane, where the letter had gone first and missed him, and once by somebody at the Greyvane gate who had crossed out the old line and written *Halcenvane, the bluff above Ostrand* in a careful clerkly print. Most of a year had gone by on its road to him. He carried it unopened as far as the covered walk and stood under the arches with it a while before he broke the wafer.

*Read your account three times. Filed it with your record, where it belongs. The offer of a proper session stands whenever the road runs through Ardenmere again. The ledger doesn't forget. Neither do I.*

That was all of it. Five short sentences.

Under them, instead of a signature, was Vell's mark, and beside the mark the circuit ledger's own stamp, inked and pressed. The stamp held his eye. So the letter had gone into the ledger as an item before it ever went into the post. Somewhere in Ardenmere, in a bound book on a shelf behind a counter, there was now a numbered line that recorded that Vell had written to him, and when, and what about.

He wrote it down that night.

*Vell's session. The oldest shelves of the Ardenmere archive, offered two years back, never taken, because the road took us south. Through two academies, a hearing and a year when I sent her nothing, it stayed open; now it's in ink, under her stamp, in a book that can't lose it.*

*I'll take it. Not this year.*

*And a note on myself, because I can see the shape of it now and I'd rather have it in ink. I collect open offers. Reydan's find-me-later, outside a ring two years ago. Vell's session. Edran's promise of a third bout, which Brom borrowed for Lira on the oak after the final; Edran has no notion how far his line has gone. Prynn's shelf, with the gap in it that'll still be there when I'm forty.*

*Twelve months back, that list would have gone under debts. They aren't. They're doors. Somebody stands behind each one, having chosen to keep a door unbolted against the day a boy might come back through it. For a long while, not so long ago, I could have counted those doors on no fingers at all.*

On the night before the last of term, at his window, Cael uncapped Hesk's pen and closed the year's books.

He did it the way he had done it at Ardenmere and at Greyvane, as a ledger and not a letter. Every line a fact, every fact priced. It was the only way he had ever found to stop himself writing down what he wished were so. He wrote slowly, and stopped often, and twice got up and stood at the window with his hands in his pockets before he could go on.

*Six confirmed.*

*One. Wind-adjacent. From Lira, at Ardenmere, the first.*
*This year: six free on the sprung oak; the panel measured it on the twentieth. At the baseline, in the second month, it was four. The seventh is billed to the next morning, the eighth to the morning after.*
*Landing beat: the same length it has been since I first had it. Three years of trying to trim it, and nothing.*
*The quarter-burst: once, on the fifth trial, I shut a burst while it was still being paid for, and the hip took the floor's share. I don't know how I did it. One point isn't a line, so it doesn't go in the column of things I can do.*

At dusk on term's last evening the four of them climbed to the wall behind the second quadrangle. They had first sat there in the third week. Below the coping the bluff fell away, and the valley lay open under a long low light.

The stone was still warm from the day. Downstream, Ostrand was lighting its lamps street by street, so that the town seemed to be drawing itself on the dusk in little points from the wharfs inward, and the bridge's long back caught the last of the sun after the water under it had gone dark. Down on the far bank the lamp at the ferry landing had just been lit, and under it two grey coats stood waiting, while two more came up the river street toward them, unhurried, to the bell.

"Four hours," said Lira.

Karis agreed: "Four hours." Into the marbled book went the term's last tick, and the book shut.

Cael sat with his back to the coping and the Log shut on his knee, and found he was looking at the three of them the way he had looked at them in the third week, from the same stone, and seeing something different.

That evening, in the third week, they had each held a road that ran away from the other three. Lira had a bracket and a queue; Brom had a cohort he would not talk about; Karis had a carrel and three floors of law beneath it; Cael had half a sheet of paper and a month. Every one of them had felt those roads pull. Tonight he could not feel anything pulling at all. Since Denvash this had never happened: not one of them had outgrown the place where they sat. Halcenvane had kept its promises to each of them, one by one, in writing: halls enough, Paths enough, a library that had swallowed Prynn's index whole and asked for more, a ladder; a season; a chancellor whose wants were pinned up where anybody could read them. A whole year, the four of them inside it, and none had found its far wall.

Lira's legs hung out over the drop. She sat with her boots in the air above a hundred feet of nothing and her hands flat on the stone either side of her, the hip quiet at last, and she was Iron-tier now, by the account of an instrument that had taken four seconds to give it. Half a mile behind her a copper crown stood retired on a shelf, between a lamp and a pile of drafts that must on no account be thrown away.

Brom sat beside her, turning a pebble over and over in the fingers of the hand that had been strapped. He was Copper on his certificate and would be until somebody filed for him. Rooke's cohort, quicker than any other on the river, had him third. One beat had cost him the final; a season and two losses had paid for mending the seam behind that beat. None of it showed on him. He was watching a barge come down toward the bend with the face of a man estimating its load.

Karis had the grey notebook open on her knee and was writing in it while the light lasted, quick and small, her head bent so close to the page that her hair hid her face.

Cael had the day in the Log already. From the end of the wall he took stock the way he always did when a thing was finished, by counting only what was true.

There was somebody else on the wall.

Sixty feet along, where the coping dipped and a man might lean his elbows on it without looking as though he had meant to stop, a figure in the plain coat of the assessment office was taking the evening air. He had come along the wall the way any of the staff walked at the close of a term, without hurry and without purpose, and he had stopped, and he stood with his hands clasped behind him and his face turned to the river. The barges seemed to interest him. He had been there a few minutes.

Nobody waved him over.

The other three, Cael saw, did not. Brom had seen the coat at the far end of the quadrangle, long before it reached the wall; his pebble had paused in his fingers for exactly one turn and then gone on. Karis's head never came up from her page; so she, Cael knew, had spotted him earliest. Lira had turned her head, once, the length of the wall, the way anyone turns at a footstep; then she had turned it back to the river and kept it there. Of the four of them, she had spent the year wanting most to haul things into the open where she could hit them, and she let this one stand where it stood.

Nobody had agreed on it. Since the hand-cart night not one word of it had been spoken. Each of the three had come to the same discipline by a separate road, and sitting there in the last of the sun Cael thought it might be the largest thing that had happened to the four of them all week. The man along the wall belonged to the evening as the river did. Everybody knew it. Nobody would ever say so.

Karis said the quiet thing first, as she always did; saying a quiet thing aloud had never once frightened her.

She said, "Next year the continent comes to measure everybody."

Lira did not even turn round.

"Good," she said.

They let that be for a while. Out on the water the barge made the bend. The sun went down behind the far hills and the river took the colour of it, first gold and then pewter, and the bluff's long shadow went out across the water toward the ferry lamp. It was the kind of light such evenings end in, at the end of such years: years that had asked more of each of them than any of them had known to expect.

Then Cael said, to nobody, to the valley, "I have six things that aren't a Path."

It came the way his best lines came in the Log, unplanned, at the moment they had finally got true enough to be said.

"A semester stamp that says I proved it. And four people who know what it costs."

It held when he tested it, so he left it there.

There was one thing more. He knew what kind of thing it was before he said it. He had never seen the note and he never would. No one here had read it. No one would read it to him. What he had instead was a stamp that said *renewed*; and two coats at the ferry landing changing on the bell exactly as they had changed every day since the carriages went down the hill, not one man added; and a careful man's single look across the brass; and a twelfth question left lying where it lay. Out of those he had made a belief. He knew it was a belief and not a finding, and he would have written it in the Log as one. He said it anyway, the way you say a true thing you cannot prove, to the only three people in the world he would ever say it to.

He said, "And somewhere in a file, the most careful man I've ever met wrote that I'm not an error."

"You're not," said Brom.

"Never were," said Lira.

Karis's pencil was already moving in the little notebook that went everywhere with her. She did not raise her eyes. "Noted," she said.

The teaching assistant's eyes stayed on the water; he offered nothing. After a while he straightened and walked on along the wall in the dusk, at the pace of a man who had work waiting for him in the morning, and every working morning after it, all through the recess.

The bluff held. The stamp was real.

Below them, in the dark coming up off the water, the barge gave two short notes on its horn, *heard you*, to nobody any of them could see. A moment later, from somewhere on the far bank, somebody answered.

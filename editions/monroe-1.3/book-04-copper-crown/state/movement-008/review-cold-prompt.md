# Chapter 51 — Two Clocks

The hip woke before she did.

Lira lay on her back in the dark and listened to it, the way the foundry master had taught her to listen to a furnace before he let anyone near the door. It was not loud. It had stopped being loud on the fourth day.

Now it was a long, low, settled ache from the crest of the bone down into the meat of the thigh, the kind of ache that has moved in and hung up its coat.

Ten days. The oil on Fiske's boards had been ten days ago, and the click with it.

She sat up in two stages, because one stage was no longer on offer, and put her good foot on the floor first.

The bad leg swung round after it, and took her. The first half arrived the ordinary way. The second half came a breath later, as though the hip had to be asked twice, and she felt it consider the question before it said yes.

Two stages, then: that was today's answer, and no amount of waiting for the fourth bell would change it.

She stood with one hand on the bedpost and did the sums. She had always done sums. At the foundry nobody had paid you to guess what a ladle weighed.

Standing, the leg was fine, and walking it was fine if she did not hurry; landing was the trouble.

Every burst ended in a landing, and every landing came home through that hip, and a hip that took weight in two stages would take a landing in two stages, too, with a gap between them that a good fighter could put a fist through.

Three bursts, she thought, three she could pay for. The fourth would be paid out of tomorrow, as it always was, and today tomorrow had nothing in it to pay with.

Brom knew that. Brom had known it for two years, since a wet afternoon at Ardenmere when she had told him so herself, out loud, sitting on the bench with her wrap half off.

You did not keep that kind of thing from somebody who sparred you four times a week. He would have worked it out anyway.

She dressed in the grey before the window, slowly, and did not look at the bed.

Fenmark came back to her while she was doing up the second boot, as it had come back most mornings this week. The station hall with its long bench, and the cold coming up through the flagstones.

The Arbiter had spent ninety breaths or so on her. Then a clerk who had not looked at her at all had written *Copper* on a sheet, and *Rank One*, and blotted it, and that had been her.

That was all it took to decide what a person was for the rest of her life. She had been fourteen and had not cried until she was outside.

Today a great many people were going to look at her for a good deal longer than a minute and a half. One of them was the best looker the Compact owned, and he was not here for her.

She found she did not mind. She would give him a very good fight, and if he was any good at his job, he would see what was in front of him whether he had come for it or not.

She took the stair a step at a time, with the rail doing half the work.

The other three were at the long table in the common room. Nobody said good morning, which she was grateful for.

Brom was eating porridge in the steady, joyless way he ate before a bout, as though it were a duty to the spoon. He did not look at her leg. She saw him not look at it. Somebody had told him not to, and she knew exactly who.

Karis had the marbled book open beside her bowl. When Lira sat down, Karis reached under her chair without a word and brought up the two practice batons, still tied together in the middle with Lira's own hip bandage, in the ugly knot Lira had pulled tight nine days ago.

"Receipt," said Karis, and drew a line through her entry in the marbled book. "Returned in the condition received. I've been sleeping with them under my chair like a dog."

"Thank you," said Lira.

She worked at the knot. It had set hard, and it took her a while, and nobody helped her, because they knew better. When it came loose she wound the bandage round her own hand, and held one baton out across the table to Brom, grip first.

He took it. He turned it over once in his big hand, looking at the dark place on the grip where his fingers had worn the wood for two years. It went down next to his bowl.

"Nine days," he said.

"Nine days," said Lira.

Neither said more. There was no more to say.

For nine days they had not sparred, or talked about the bout, or watched each other cross a room. It had not been politeness. Without the silence, each of them would have spent nine days building a fight out of the other one's wound.

She knew his number. Everybody who had ever watched him knew his number. Somewhere past four minutes of hard contact, Brom's iron began to cost him more than it gave, and everything else he did got slower to pay for it.

And he knew hers. If either of them had spent the last nine days thinking about the other's number, they would have walked out today to fight a ledger instead of a person.

So she had not thought about it. She had thought about her feet.

Cael sat across from her and ate nothing, and held himself very still in the particular way he had been holding himself since the stair.

She could see the corner of him that was busy, even if nobody else at the table could. She did not say anything about it. The corner was his to keep, and he was keeping it.

"Eat something," she told him instead.

"I'm not fighting."

"You're watching. It's worse." She pushed the bread at him. "Eat it, or I'll feel you not eating it from the boards."

He ate it.

---

By the second bell the back rows of every tier were packed and the front rows stood empty, which was the wrong way round.

Crossing the quadrangle, Cael worked out why. Regulars sat low as a rule, down by the rail, where the calls carried and the feet were close.

Today the second-years had done a different sum. They had come an hour early with bread in their coat pockets and climbed to the back, because a full yard would shut the late half of the bluff out in the covered walk, and the back rows were the ones nobody could take from you.

He climbed to his own place anyway. East tier, a third of the way up, on the same worn stone he had sat on for every session since the brackets were painted.

He had turned the question over before he slept, whether to sit somewhere else, and kept coming back to the same answer. A new seat would say that today was not like other days, and the whole hill knew that already without his seat saying it too.

It was ledger day twenty-one, and he had been holding since before he opened his eyes. The hold lay where he had set it in the dark, one corner of his attention bent inward and kept there, like a hand kept flat on a table so that the cloth will not slide.

It weighed a little more this morning than yesterday. Every morning it did, and every evening a little more again.

Down at the north end, Bracken came out across the empty boards with two folded sheets held against his waistcoat. He walked to the panel's table and laid them under the edge of the sand-glass's stand, one on top of the other, squared them with a finger, and walked away again without looking up at anybody.

Half the yard did not notice. The other half thought he was a clerk.

A few minutes later Karis climbed up and sat on Cael's right. A sheet of her own, folded small, lay shut on her knee.

"Copies," Karis said. "The sealed originals sit under the glass down there. He certified both their season records at the first bell this morning, and sealed them, and filed them, and then he walked them down here himself."

"This morning. Before."

"Before. That's the whole point of it." She ran her thumb along the fold. "However it goes, there's a sheet already sealed that says what each of them did, from the first session to this morning. Nobody who made it knew how today ends."

She tapped the fold. "Bracken says a record made after the result has been coached."

Cael did not answer. A man who ran a registry had spent his first hour of the day building something that would stay true whichever of his friends walked off the boards with the crown. There was no sentence Cael could have made about that just then which would not have come out wrong.

The yard went on filling round them, down from the top like water rising backward. At the table the adjudicating instructor set out the chalk and the glass. The two safety seats took their places behind her with their hands clasped.

In the north entrance Rooke had planted himself with his arms folded, as he had for every bout of Brom's this season. He would not sit down; he never did.

Two rows down and some way along, among the Blade cohort, Ephram sat forward with his elbows on his knees and a space kept clear on the stone beside him. For whom, Cael had no idea.

Ephram looked round once, up the tier, and nodded to him, which was new. Then he faced the boards again.

With the fourth bell came Fiske, in plain clothes, to take a front-row seat on the north tier. Lira had told her to sit north, because from the east you could not see the far chalk properly, and Fiske had taken the advice as seriously as she took everything.

Two years the Copper Crown had sat on her shelf. Within the hour somebody else would be holding it in front of the whole bluff. She sat down in the front row with her hands loose in her lap and the face of a woman who had made her peace with that a long time ago, perhaps on the very afternoon she won it.

The delegation came a little after her.

Nobody had told the yard to keep the north tier's second row clear. It had kept it clear all the same, the way a crowd leaves a wide ring round a cart it does not trust.

First the counsel, talking over her shoulder, and then the two clerks, and then Ilsev, making a desk of the case across her knees. Last came the Archmarshal in his plain road grey, who took the middle of the row and sat.

Cael had not expected that. On the fourteenth the man had stood at the gallery rail in hall three for fifty minutes and shown no sign of wanting a chair. Now he sat at his ease, with a little notebook shut on one knee and his pen lying across the cover.

The end seat went to Havel, whose hands were empty. There was no recording case at his side, and no glass, and no sheaf of anything.

Cael held that for a count of four. An officer who brought nothing to hold was not working. And an officer who was not working, on an afternoon when he might have been anywhere at all, had climbed this particular stair.

With that, he put the whole north tier out of his mind on purpose, and kept it out. Two people he loved were about to try very hard to beat each other. He owed them his eyes.

He let the read up, too. That was a choice, and he made it with his eyes open.

For nine days he had kept the read at its thinnest layer, because the reach at depth and the hold were paid out of one purse, and the purse was shallow. This afternoon, though, no one on the bluff would spare him a look. Every eye in the yard, the Archmarshal's among them, would be on the oak.

So he kept the hold exactly where it was, and took his hand off the read, and let it rise and see.

---

The card said that officers of the delegation would not converse with any party under review outside a sitting, and would not acknowledge such a party beyond ordinary courtesy. Havel had read it four times on the road.

It said nothing at all about sitting on a stone bench in the sun on an afternoon when no sitting was called.

He had checked. He had asked the counsel at breakfast, in the form a records officer uses when he would like to be told no, whether the officers' attendance at a public event on the institution's calendar was a matter for the schedule.

"It's a fight," the counsel had said, buttering her bread. "Go if you like. I'm going. Half the hill will be there, and I've never seen a Wind practitioner fight anything but paper."

So it was not his duty to be here. Nobody would write down that he had come.

He had left the recording case in the working room and the brass glass in its pocket, and his notebook was in his inside coat where it always was, and it was going to stay there. His hands had nothing to hold.

He found that he wanted to sit here with his hands empty, like anybody else who had climbed a stair to watch two young people fight.

He could not have said why, and he did not try very hard.

The tier was warm stone. The yard below it was very loud, and the noise had a shape to it, a long rising swell with no words in it, like water coming in under a door.

Below him, in the front row, sat a big quiet woman with her hands folded. He knew her from the bracket sheet. She was the champion who would stop being champion before the afternoon was out, and she looked calmer than anybody else he could see.

Three seats to his left the senior evaluation seat kept her case flat across her knees. She was reading the yard, and he knew that look. She read a crowd the way she read a sheet, from the top left corner, in order.

Beyond her the Archmarshal sat with his notebook shut on one knee and his pen across it, as still as a man asleep with his eyes open.

The east tier he left alone, by rule. There was a boy somewhere over there whose file he had carried up a road, and he would not be seen looking for him.

He looked at the north door instead, where the girl would come out.

He knew her only from paper, and from corridors. On paper she was Wind, Copper, Rank Two, on a certificate two years old that nobody had looked at since.

In the corridors this week she had been a girl walking very carefully on a bad leg and refusing, with her whole body, to be seen doing it. He had stepped aside for her once in the covered walk. She had thanked him the way you thank a door.

Now she was the top line of the Copper column, and the whole bluff had come to see her fight.

There was a thing he had not done that morning at Ardenmere. Somebody had held out a pear to him, in a twist of paper, and he had wanted it and had not taken it.

He had thought about that pear more often since than any grown man should think about fruit. Sitting on the warm stone with nothing in his hands, he understood that this was a little like the pear. It was a thing he would like to have, and could not quite reach, and would not try to explain to anybody.

He had asked himself on the stair, quite plainly, why he had come, and had got no better answer than that he wanted to. It was not a reason a records officer was trained to accept. He had accepted it anyway, and climbed the rest of the stair, and found the end seat free as if somebody had kept it for him.

Along the row the Archmarshal shifted once, to settle the notebook more squarely on his knee, and was still again. Havel had sat at the same tables as that man since the road and had not heard him say thirty words that were not about a sheet of paper.

What the notebook was for, he couldn't say. He did not want to know, particularly. A man who had spent his whole working life at the end of tables, writing down exactly what other people said, learned early that there were notebooks it was wiser not to wonder about. He had kept to that lesson through every posting he had ever had, and he kept to it now.

Down on the boards a porter was sweeping the last of the dust from round the chalk marks with a long soft broom, slowly, the way a man sweeps a step before a wedding. Havel watched him do it and found he was enjoying it, which surprised him. He could not remember the last time he had sat anywhere without a sheet of paper on his knee and a pen expecting things of him. His right hand, which ached in the web of the thumb by the end of every long sitting, lay open and quiet on his thigh, and for once nothing in it hurt.

He would watch, and that was all. He would go back down the stair afterward and be a records officer again, and nothing would be written anywhere to say he had climbed it.

A bell rang somewhere behind the range. Down at the table the adjudicating instructor faced the yard.

Havel folded his empty hands on his knee, and waited, like everyone else.

---

Inside the north door it was cold and dark, with the yard's noise coming through the planks like weather.

Lira stood in the short passage with her back to the wall and her eyes shut, and let the hip tell her about the stone under her boots. All down the covered walk it had been telling her the same thing, two stages and two stages.

It did not complain. It only reported, flatly, like a clerk reading out a sum.

On the other side of the yard, behind the south door, Brom would be standing just like this. He would be opening and closing his hands. She did not need to see it. She had watched him do it before every bout for two years.

The safety seat at the door looked at her, and then at the leg, and then politely at the ceiling.

"Whenever you're ready," he said.

She thought about the station hall at Fenmark, and the clerk's pen. She thought about Fiske on the oil ten days ago, and the honest thing Fiske had said afterward across the boards.

She thought about Brom's number and put it away again, unplanned, where it belonged. She was not going to fight his four minutes. She was going to fight him.

Then she opened her eyes and walked out into the light.

The sound hit her the way heat hits you when the furnace door opens: a solid, whole thing, everywhere at once. She did not hurry through it. Her pace to the chalk was her own, and slow today, and the yard saw it, and part of the noise turned.

Leading foot first, she took the chalk. The weight came down onto it in two halves, as it had in her room, with a small considering gap between them.

She saw Brom see it.

He was standing at the south chalk with his wraps on and his hands at his sides, and his hands were opening and closing.

His eyes dropped to her hip for precisely as long as knowing took, and came back up to her face. He did not look sorry. Had he looked sorry, she'd have hit him.

He looked like Brom, who had sat on the loser's side of a bench beside her more times than either of them could count, and who was going to try his hardest to put her there today.

Good, she thought.

She crouched and laid her palm flat on the oak, and held it there a moment longer than she needed to, and stood up.

---

Cael already had Lira. He had seen it on the walk: the leading boot set down first, and the weight coming onto it in a half and then another half, with a little pause in the middle like a word somebody has to think about.

All season she had come to her chalk in one piece. Today she came in two.

So he looked at Brom.

Brom's shoulders were easy and his feet were where they always were; the tell was lower down.

His fists were closing and opening, slowly, at his hips, over and over, the way a man works his hands on a frosty morning. It was not a frosty morning.

Cael had seen it three times this season, and by the second month he had understood it. It was not fear. Brom's hands had been told about the plan before the rest of him had finished agreeing to it, and the plan was a long afternoon of being struck, and they were getting ready.

*Long*, Cael thought. *He means to make it long.*

And then, a heartbeat after, the sum came in under it and turned his stomach over. *No. She does. She has to. And he knows she has to.*

That was the bout entire. He had seen it in week three of the season, doing the bracket arithmetic on the back of a page, when it became clear that these two would meet at the end unless somebody very good got in the way.

Fiske had nearly been that somebody. Nobody else had come close.

There were two clocks on the oak, and both had started the moment the two of them walked out.

The first was Brom's, and it ran four minutes, near enough. Brom had told him so one cold Greyvane winter, and Cael had written it down then and there, and every bout since had kept to it.

Four minutes didn't break the iron, nothing so kind; it simply got dear. Keeping the skin hard began to take more out of him with every blow, and everything else he did had to stand in line behind it and wait to be paid for.

The second was Lira's: three bursts, on that leg, today.

There was a fourth in her, there always was, and she had told Brom about it herself years ago on a wet bench at Ardenmere: the fourth was bought on credit, paid back out of the next day's legs. Today the hip had no tomorrow to borrow from.

She knew his number, and he knew hers. And for nine days each of them had refused to build one single thought on the other's.

Standing at the table, the adjudicating instructor read the terms across the yard. *The Copper column, its season's final, witnessed. Four exchanges, each on the table's glass, each to the cleaner touch or called even. The bout goes to whoever holds more touches after the fourth, unless a fighter can't go on, or yields, or the panel ends it first.*

She turned the glass over, and the sand began to run.

For most of two minutes Lira asked Brom one question, in three different voices.

The question was the seam, whose address was common knowledge; eleven of them had gone looking for it this season.

When Brom took a blow on hardened skin, he took it superbly, and then there was a pause, short as a hiccup, while the taking finished and before the answer began. For that sliver of time there was nobody home behind the iron.

Lira had been walking in through that pause for two years. She knew its every inch the way you know the stair in your own house in the dark.

The first time, she asked plainly. She came in low off the chalk with a straight line at his lead thigh, not fast, meant to be taken, and as his skin went hard to take it she was already slipping round toward his open side, to be there when the pause opened.

The pause did not open. His answer met her coming round.

It was not speed. From the tier Cael saw it, and saw that most of the yard didn't.

Brom had not got quicker. The two things no longer came in turn. The hardening and the answer came out of him together now, overlapping, the way a good clerk blots one line with his left hand while his right is already writing the next.

Somebody had rebuilt him round a different frame. Rooke, with a term of patience and a sheet of nineteen dull exercises.

Lira skipped back out of it, light, unhurt, with her eyebrows up.

She asked again. This time she came at an angle, and halfway in she shifted her weight from one foot to the other, so that the blow would land a fraction late and stretch the pause out longer.

It was lovely work, and good fighters had lost to it. Brom took the late blow and answered it on time, as if the lateness had been written on a card and handed to him beforehand.

The third time, Cael made a sound out loud, and only knew he had when Karis looked at him.

Lira gave Brom everything. She threw a line so deep and so whole that there was no way to take it in part; he had to set his whole forearm and swallow all of it.

That was the point. If there was any pause left in him at all, a full swallow was where it would be widest, and she meant to be standing in it.

Brom swallowed the whole of it. The answer came out of the same motion: a hardened forearm, chopping down across her raised guard, with a crack that went round the yard like a door slammed in an empty house.

Lira slid back four paces on the oak and stopped.

The adjudicating instructor's hand went up. "Even," she called. "Taken on the guard."

The yard made an uncertain noise. Half of it did not know what it had just watched.

A blow on a set guard was not clean; that had been the ladder's rule all season, and nobody had scored. But everyone on the tiers who knew anything had just watched the most famous gap in the Copper column close, quietly, in front of them.

Cael looked past the heaving chest and the loose hands, at her face.

She was grinning at Brom, wide and delighted and quite unguarded. In three years Cael had seen that grin perhaps six times.

It never meant she was winning. It meant somebody had surprised her.

Lira did not care for much in the way of luxuries, but she had one, and she had never tried to give it up. She loved finding out that a person was more than she had thought. It pleased her more than any bout she had ever won.

Brom, at the far chalk, worked one shoulder round in its socket. No grin came back; it never did. Once, his fists closed and opened.

His four minutes had begun.

---

The second exchange went to Brom, through the very thing that would lose him the bout.

Cael worked it out halfway through, and wrote it up that night in a page and a half.

Rooke had never closed the seam, because nobody could. It was not a fault in Brom; it was the way Iron Skin worked, the way a hinge works.

Rooke's real lesson had been smaller and stranger: stop swallowing whole. He took most of each blow on the iron, and simply let the last part of it through, unhardened, into his own body. Whatever he saved by not finishing, he spent on the answer.

It was a trade, and Cael could see both sides of the ledger at once. Contact had paid for the death of Brom's oldest flaw. Every blow now cost him a fifth of itself in bruises. And contact was what his clock was made of.

Her first burst went early in the glass. The second went a long minute after.

Cael felt them both. He had carried her Wind for three years in his own legs, and he knew the bill for a burst as well as he knew his own name.

No breath at the front, for the whole length of the move. At the back, the landing beat: half a breath of standing on the spot, unable to turn, when your body is only something that has arrived. And afterward, the hot line drawn down the leading side from the hip bone to the inside of the knee.

She used the first to escape. Brom had walked her toward the table end, step by patient step, never hurrying and never letting her past his lead shoulder, until the chalk lines ran out behind her and there was nowhere left to stand. Then she went sideways out of the corner on the Wind and landed in open oak.

She used the second to go in. She showed him a line high, let him start to set for it, and was simply not there any more.

She was under his arm and inside his reach before the iron had finished arriving, and her flat palm cracked across his ribs, a clean, ringing slap that the whole north end heard.

The table's hand stayed down. Brom had taken the slap on the struck side, unfinished, the way Rooke had taught him, and kept moving. And Lira was in her landing beat. She had no air and no way to turn, and for half a breath she was only standing there.

He took the half-breath. He swept her lead foot and pressed down through her shoulder in one motion, low and complete, and laid her on her back on the oak. His forearm settled across her collarbone, not unkindly, because Brom was never unkind on a floor.

The adjudicating instructor called it at once. Both safety seats were out on the oak and kneeling by her before the call was finished, and back at the table by the time she sat up.

"Wind, touch to the ribs," the table read out. "Iron Skin, touch to the floor. To Iron Skin, the cleaner."

Brom stepped back to his chalk, and Lira got up by herself. It took her a moment longer than it should have, and the whole yard saw that it did.

Karis had gone very upright on the stone beside him.

"Two gone," she said. Her voice had gone small. Cael knew that voice. Karis used it when fear had arrived and she had chosen to have a job instead.

"Two gone."

"How much does she have?"

"One more she can afford. It's dear, but it's there." His eyes stayed on the oak. "Then the fourth. That one she gave away herself, years ago, to him. Every other figure out there is a figure. That one isn't."

Karis said nothing. Her fingers had closed on the folded sheet in her lap.

At the south chalk Brom stood waiting with his hands still at his sides. This time they stayed still.

Cael saw that, and felt what it cost him to see it: a dull pressure behind the eyes, the bill the read always sent. He did not stop looking. The read was up and looking, as he'd let it.

The adjudicating instructor turned the glass for the third.

# Chapter 52 — The Exhibit

Ilsev did not know how to watch a fight, so she read the yard instead.

She had decided that on the stair coming up, and it had served her well enough for two exchanges.

A yard was a document like any other. It had a heading and a body and a hand at the foot that signed it, and if you read it in order, and did not skip ahead to the parts that excited you, it would tell you a great deal about itself.

The heading was the table: three faculty, one standing and two behind her with their hands clasped, and the glass between them on its little stand, and under the edge of the stand two folded sheets with seals on them. She had watched the registrar carry them out across the empty boards and lay them there, square to the edge, before the yard had filled. He had walked away again without a word to anybody.

Nobody had told her what they were. She did not need telling. A man does not put a sealed paper under a sand-glass before a contest unless he wishes it to be seen that the paper came first.

She approved of that more than she could have said aloud.

The body was the crowd. More than a thousand people, she judged, on stone tiers open to the sky, and for most of the first two exchanges they had made one long continuous sound with no words in it.

Its pitch had shifted all along: up for the girl's entrance, down when her foot met the chalk, uncertain all at once at the first *even*, and flat and loud together when the girl went onto her back.

Ilsev could not have said what any of it meant on the boards, but she could hear that the sound was a record of something, kept by a thousand clerks at once, none of whom knew they were keeping it.

The signature at the foot belonged to the woman in front of her.

Almost directly below Ilsev's knees, in the north tier's front row, the champion had sat through both exchanges without moving.

Her hands lay folded on her thigh, and her broad back was perfectly still, and only once, when the girl went down, had her head turned a quarter-inch and come back. Of all the signatures Ilsev had read in her working life, few had been steadier.

Beside Ilsev, on her right, the Archmarshal sat with his notebook closed on his knee and his pen lying across the cover, exactly where he had laid it when he sat down.

He watched the boards the way he watched a sitting, at an even pace, giving each part its share, and Ilsev did not try to read him. She had stopped trying on the second day of the road. A colleague's face was not her question.

Nor was the boy on the east tier, a third of the way up, beside the Ember girl with the ink on her fingers. Ilsev knew exactly where he was sitting. She had known it since she sat down, as she knew where the doors were. She did not look at him.

Her own question was a long way off by now. Four days ago she had filled a form in eleven minutes and laid it in a tray, and since then it had gone down the bluff in a courier's satchel and up the river, one level higher than the last, to a room she would never see.

She had put it down when she filed it. She did not pick it up again here, on a warm stone bench, with a contest going on below her that had nothing to do with it. A woman who carried her filed matters about with her into every room soon stopped being able to see the room.

So she looked at the room.

In all her years of hearings she had read the word *Wind* on more forms than she could count, and had never once watched it.

On paper it was a Path like any other, a line in a register, a tier and a rank and a date. Down there on the oak it was a slight young woman with a bad leg who could, when she chose, simply not be where you had been looking.

Ilsev had seen it happen twice now, and both times her eye had gone to the empty place a moment after the girl had left it, like a reader whose finger has slipped a line. She did not much enjoy the feeling. She suspected it was the whole point.

It occurred to her that a registry clerk, two years ago, had written this girl down in about the time it took to read a short page, and that nobody had read the page again since.

She marked the thought and went no further with it. It was not a finding. It was an observation about a form, and forms were her trade, and the form was not in front of her.

The table turned the glass.

At first she thought nothing was happening, because the girl did not go in.

She stood at the edge of the big young man's reach and waited, and when he came to her she simply stepped back, on her own feet, and let him come again. The crowd did not like it. Ilsev heard it not liking it: a restless low murmur, like a hall at a long reading.

Then the young man reached her guard and hit it, hard, and she took it on her forearms and went back another step, and the murmur stopped.

Backward the girl went, the whole circuit of the floor, and then the whole circuit a second time. Every so often the young man caught up with her and struck, and every time she let him, on her arms and her shoulders, and gave ground, and he followed. She was not running, as even Ilsev could see. She was walking him.

Somewhere past the third minute the sound of the yard changed again. It did not get louder. It got slower, and lower, and more careful, the way a reading room sounds when a whole table has found the same line in the same moment.

In front of her the champion's folded hands had come apart.

---

The first step told Cael everything, because it went backward.

Lira did not step backward. Three years of watching, and never once by choice. Backward was where you went when you had been beaten to a place, and she was never beaten to places. She went sideways, or through, or round, or she was simply already elsewhere.

Every page he had on her said so, eleven pages of it, in a hundred different ways. The Wind did not make her fast. It made her early. She lived in the sliver of time other fighters spent deciding, and she spent it in tiny coins, and every coin bought her a square of floor that her opponent had not finished thinking about.

Now she stepped back off her chalk on her own two feet, and waited.

Brom came, and she gave him ground. When he reached her she put up her guard and let him hit it, and the crack of iron on forearm went round the yard, and she gave him more ground.

There was no burst and no Wind, nothing that cost her a held breath, only feet, and arms, and the long slow curve of the oak's rim behind her.

She walked him round the whole floor like that. Then she began again.

Cael's own forearms ached, watching. He knew exactly what Brom's iron felt like arriving on a guard; he had a month of bruises from the wash-house to prove it.

Lira had none of the things that make that bearable, because she had never once trained to be struck, and her whole body had been built round a single idea, which was that being hit meant something had already gone wrong. Each blow she caught travelled up her forearms and lodged in her shoulders. And every backward step came down on the leading leg, on a hip ten days old.

"She's spending him," said Karis, very low. "Isn't she. She's paying for his clock with her own arms."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"Longer than he can. That's the whole bet."

The glass was half run before the yard caught on. Cael heard the moment it did, because the noise did not rise. It sank, and thickened, and turned careful, like a crowd watching somebody carry too many plates.

Then Brom slowed.

Cael had been waiting for it since the first backward step, with his own count running silently under everything else. After every committed strike Brom re-squared his feet before the next. All season that had taken him four counts. Now, with the glass more than half gone, it took him five.

The fifth count was what Lira had walked round the oak twice to buy.

She went into it on her third burst. She was behind his lead leg before the re-squaring had finished, and her heel went into the back of his knee, and the knee bent the way knees must, and Brom sat down on the boards with a thump that Cael felt through the stone.

"Iron Skin, down," called the table. "To Wind, the cleaner."

The tiers came up off their seats all together. Karis was up with them, both fists clenched, saying "Three, that's three," to nobody.

By Cael's private count the exchange had run four minutes and eleven seconds. He stayed sitting and watched Lira walk back to her chalk.

She had stopped pretending. The leading leg took her weight in two halves, and then in three.

On the way she laid the flat of her hand on her own thigh, briefly, the way somebody touches a wall in the dark. He had never once seen her do that on a floor. It meant there was nothing left over for hiding, and she had decided that hiding had stopped being worth its price.

Brom had seen it.

He was up and back at his own chalk, rubbing the back of his knee. Cael watched it come over him: the eyes going to her thigh, half a breath of standing quite still, and then something in the shoulders settling, the way a man's shoulders settle when he has finished an argument with himself.

*He'll go for it*, Cael thought.

The next thought came straight behind it, and he could not have stopped it if he had tried. *He should.*

---

The fourth exchange lasted eleven seconds.

Brom came for the hip. Not round it, and not near it: at it.

There was nothing hidden in it anywhere. Hiding it would have been an insult.

More than a thousand people were watching, and three faculty, and the Compact, and the two of them had fought each other honestly two years running. The one thing neither could have lived with was to fight this last one crooked.

Rooke's cohort had spent a term of bruises teaching him to leave a chalk fast, and he left it now as fast as he had ever left anything. His line went in low and to her left. It went at the leading side, where every Wind fighter alive has to come back to earth.

It was the right thing to do, and Cael would have done it himself. It said, plainly, to her and to the whole yard: *I know where it hurts. I won't pretend I don't. Beat me through that, and the bout is yours.*

Lira spent the fourth burst.

Cael felt it go in his own leg, like a bell he had been hearing for three years struck once more somewhere close.

Her knees gave and the oak breathed under her. She went light and airless and somewhere else, and Brom's line arrived exactly where it had been sent, and there was nobody there to take it, and she came down out of the Wind.

The rest of the bout lived inside the landing beat.

That beat was the Wind's one fixed price, half a breath of being locked upright and unable to turn. Cael had built his entire log round it. It had never been shortened by anyone, not by her and not by him, and Brom had known about it for two years. He turned into it and was waiting for her.

By rights that was the end of it. It wasn't, for two reasons, and Cael saw both together.

The first belonged to Brom: four minutes of iron and eleven seconds more had put his turn on the fifth count instead of the fourth, a hair late.

The other was Lira's.

She did not cheat the beat. Nobody could, and she did not try. She let it run its whole length. What she chose, before she ever left the floor, was where she would come down, and she came down on the bad hip.

As she had known it must, the leg buckled, half an inch and not a hair more. And the fold took her sideways, out of his line, while the beat was still locked and running. She never had to change direction, because the injury changed it for her.

His answer cut through nothing.

She was striking already, an open palm cracking into the side of his knee in the very instant his weight came down onto it.

Over he went, heavily, rolling off the shoulder as his cohort had drilled him to fall. A safety seat's arm was up before the roll was done. The adjudicating instructor stood.

"Wind," she called. "Clean. Two touches to one. The bout."

The sound that came down off the tiers was one Cael had no instrument for, and he had spent two years building instruments for rooms.

He did not stand. He was half ashamed of that later. His legs had simply declined, and he let them.

Beside him Karis was on her feet with both hands pressed flat over her mouth, and the folded copy crushed in one of them. On the oak Lira had come down to one knee, and not to bow; the leading leg simply refused her.

The call was still in the air when Brom got up. He crossed the boards on a knee that had just been knocked sideways, and took her forearm in his hand, and pulled, and she came up onto her good leg.

Then neither of them let go. They stood holding on to each other's arms in the middle of the oak, and Cael counted three seconds without meaning to.

A yard that had been roaring fell silent to watch.

The faculty walked out onto the oak. Only then did the tiers find their voice again.

---

Ilsev had seen a great many ceremonies. She read this one as she would have read any other: for what it put on the record, and for who put it there.

The chancellor put almost nothing. She crossed the oak from the west gallery in a plain dark coat, with the porter behind her carrying the crown on a faded cushion.

She stopped in front of the two young people. She said the old formula of the Copper Crown, a dozen words that had plainly been said in that yard for more than a hundred years, and then she said nothing more.

Ilsev, who had sat through a great many chancellors, admired that very much. It is hard to say nothing more when a thousand people are waiting for you to say something.

The registrar put the record.

He came to the table and lifted the glass's stand, and took out the two sealed sheets that had lain under it since before the yard filled. He broke the seal on one. He read it out in a dry, carrying voice with no colour in it at all, the voice of a man reading the tolls on a bridge.

"Wind, Copper, Rank Two. Seeded the twenty-second. Opening bout drawn by lot, and won. Top line from the fifteenth session. Ten bouts unbeaten. None lost. Certified at the first bell this morning."

Then he did a thing Ilsev had never seen a registrar do in public. He took the pen from behind his ear and wrote a line at the foot of the sheet, there at the table, with the whole yard watching his hand. He blotted it on his own cuff.

"Final, the nineteenth of Reaping, before a witnessed panel. Won." He looked up from the sheet. "Eleven bouts unbeaten. None lost."

He folded the sheet and stepped back.

Ilsev sat very still. A record certified in the morning, before a result anybody could know; and the result added in the afternoon, at the foot, in the same hand, before a thousand witnesses.

Nothing had been rewritten. Nothing had been made after the fact and dressed to look as if it came before.

You could see the seam between the two, and the seam was the proof. That sheet, she suspected, would stay with her.

Nobody in the yard knew what to do with it, since no crowd has ever learned to cheer a registrar.

Directly below Ilsev's knees, the champion unfolded her hands.

She brought them together once, alone, in the silence, and then again. From her seat Ilsev could see the knuckles whiten.

The sound went out across the oak, small and clear, half a breath before anybody else in the whole yard had moved. A heartbeat later the whole yard took it up from her, and the noise came down off the tiers like a falling wall.

The champion went on clapping, steadily, with her back straight. Ilsev looked at the back of her head for some time. Without the bracket sheet she could not have put a name to the woman, and had no need to. She had watched a signature go onto a document, and she knew one when she saw it.

The chancellor lifted the copper off its cushion and held it out.

The girl received it with both hands and left it off her head. She turned it once, looking down at it, and then held it a little away from her body, quite still, as a clerk holds a sheet he is about to hand up to the bench.

Then her eyes came up to the north tier.

It was one second, perhaps less. It came up the stone and went along the second row like a draught under a door, and Ilsev felt it go past her.

What it meant was not hers to say, and she could not have said it. On her right the Archmarshal did not move by so much as a finger, and his notebook stayed shut on his knee.

The girl looked down again at the thing in her hands, and the yard went on roaring, and Ilsev let the look go where it had come from.

At the table the registrar had put the second sealed sheet back under the glass's stand, unopened. Ilsev noticed that too.

It was the young man's record, made at the same bell, in the same hand, and it was every bit as true as the first.

Nobody would read it aloud today, and nobody would ever need to. But it had been made, and sealed, and laid there beside the other, so that whichever of them walked off the oak with the copper, the paper would already be waiting for both.

She had known clerks who would have thought the second sheet a waste of an hour. She would not have hired any of them.

The yard began to empty in the slow, untidy way of crowds that do not want to leave. The counsel stood up and stretched, and pressed both fists into the small of her back.

"Paper all the way down, this house," she said to Ilsev, not unkindly. "Even their prize-givings come with a seal."

"Yes," said Ilsev.

"You approve."

"I noticed it," said Ilsev, which was as far as she ever went with a colleague, and the counsel laughed and went off down the stair, talking to her clerk about supper.

Ilsev stayed in her seat a little longer and let the tier clear round her. The Archmarshal had not moved.

She did not ask whether he was coming down; she had worked beside him since the road, and had learned that he went where he went.

She picked up her case, and found that her legs had stiffened on the cold stone, and went down slowly, one step at a time, in good order.

Cael got down to the floor a quarter of an hour later, through a crowd that did not want to go home, and found Lira sitting on the oak where the crowd had left her, with the wing's duty practitioner kneeling at her hip and the crown in her lap.

"Seventh bell," Lira said, before he could ask anything. "Gault's office. He's filing for me, and Bracken's to bring the papers, and somebody from the delegation's been asked to witness." She winced as the practitioner pressed somewhere she did not like. "I can't do his stair. She won't let me try."

"I won't," said the practitioner, without looking up.

"So you go." Lira looked at him. "Somebody has to hear it read who knows what it cost. You'll remember every word of it, and you won't turn it into anything it isn't. Tell Gault I named you. He'll want it in writing, so write it."

Cael borrowed a pencil from the practitioner's case and a corner of her bandage paper, and Lira wrote three lines on it against her knee, in capitals, and signed them. He folded it into his coat.

"And Cael." She caught his sleeve as he straightened. "Don't stand at the front. Stand at the back, where you can see them all. That's what you're for."

The note from the assessment office reached Ilsev a little after the sixth bell, in the delegation's working room, while she was writing up the morning.

A half-sheet, folded once, in the wing's trained hand. *The assessment office files at the seventh bell, in the Magister's office, an application of the second class. The office invites the senior evaluation seat of the visiting delegation to attend as witness, at her discretion. — Gault.*

She read it twice. Then she set it squarely on the corner of the table and thought about it for exactly as long as it deserved, which was not long.

An application of the second class was an application to a body outside the house. She knew which application this had to be, on this evening, from this office.

A witness from the inspecting delegation would put the Compact's own seat on the face of the paper, wherever it went afterward. That was why she had been asked.

She had no objection to being used for that, provided she was used honestly.

A witness signed to what she saw, and to nothing else. If the filing was in order she would sign that she had been present. If it was not, she would say so, and the office would have learned something it was better to learn now.

Coat on, she went down the stair. The wing's passage was nearly empty at that hour, and her heels sounded on its boards more loudly than she liked. She thought, going along it, about the champion's hands coming together in a silent yard, and about the registrar's pen at the foot of a sealed sheet, and she noticed that she was looking forward to the next quarter of an hour in a way she rarely looked forward to anything with a form in it. She set the thought aside before she reached the door. A witness who arrives pleased has already decided what she is going to see.

The Magister's office was at the end of the wing, a square room with one window and the posted calibration card hanging on the wall beside the door, initialled in a column of small neat checks.

There were four people in it when she came in, which made it full.

Gault sat behind his desk with a sheaf before him. The registrar stood at its corner with his pen behind his ear and two sealed sheets in his hand. The wing's clerk sat at a sloped desk under the window. Against the back wall, hands clasped behind, the enrollee stood waiting.

Ilsev noted him the way she noted the card on the wall.

"The champion is with the wing's duty practitioner," Gault said, seeing her look. "Her hip will not take the stair. In her place she has named the enrollee to hear it. That is in order. I have entered it."

"Thank you," said Ilsev, and sat in the chair the clerk had set for her, and opened nothing.

Gault read the application out. Paper of that class had to be heard by its witness as well as signed by her, and he read it in exactly the voice he used for a column of instrument checks.

It was the registry's own long form, the one with the blue rule down the margin, and Ilsev could have recited its printed lines in her sleep.

First the house making the request, and its office. Then the request itself: that the practitioner named be brought before an Arbiter of the registry to be evaluated for advancement.

Then the ground, in the form's printed words, *advancement demonstrated, inconsistent with tier of record*. Then what stood behind it, in Gault's hand. The season, certified; the final, before a witnessed panel; and the panel's own account of what it had seen, attached.

Last, the station where the evaluation was sought, and the date of the girl's last classification. It was two years old.

She kept her case closed and both hands flat on it, and listened to the end.

When Gault finished, she asked two questions.

"The season record," she said. "Certified when? Before the final, or after?"

"Before," said the registrar. "At the first bell this morning. Sealed, and laid under the panel's glass before the yard filled. The result was added at the foot afterward, in my hand, in the yard, before the panel and the yard both."

"And the panel's account. Whose hand is it in? One of the panel's own, or a clerk's?"

"By the adjudicating instructor," said Gault. "In her own hand, at the table, before she left the yard. Both safety seats have countersigned it. The clerk has copied nothing."

Ilsev nodded once.

Those were the only two answers that mattered. A record certified after its result has been told the result. An account written by a clerk is an account of what the clerk was told.

Neither was true of this paper. The paper had been made in the right order, by the right hands, and anyone who lifted it, at any desk up the river, would find it so.

"I have no objection to note," she said. "I will record that I was present."

Her small upright hand went on the witness line, with the date beside it and the bell after. The clerk blotted it, and Gault initialled under her name. The registrar laid his two sheets on the application, square, and the clerk pinned them.

That was all of it: four minutes by the clock over the door, and no speeches. Had anybody looked in from the passage, they would not have known that anything had just ended.

Ilsev stood, and thanked the Magister, and nodded to the registrar.

At the door she passed the boy at the wall. She gave him the courtesy the card allowed, a small inclination of the head, and he returned it exactly.

His face told her nothing. She had not expected it to, and she did not try to make it.

Hearing a paper on a friend's behalf was a proper errand, and he had run it properly.

She went back up to the working room and wrote one line at the foot of her day's notes: *Witnessed, at the assessment office's invitation, an advancement filing in respect of a Wind practitioner. Filing in order.* Then she turned to the morning's sheets again and finished them.

Cael stayed against the wall until Ilsev's steps had gone away down the passage, and only then took his hands from behind his back.

It had been like watching a hinge turn.

All term Gault had said the same thing in nearly the same words. Instruments and consistency; nothing else, to anybody.

Cael had spent months unable to tell whether the man was for him or against him, and standing at the back of that crowded little office he saw at last that he had been asking a useless question. Gault was not for anybody.

Gault was for what the needles said. This afternoon the needles had said a certain thing about a girl on a hurt leg, and Gault had sat down at his desk at the seventh bell and done precisely what they told him to do, in the right order, with the right witness, and then initialled it.

Fiske had told Lira, beside the board, weeks ago, what stood between her and an Arbiter for two whole years. One signature that was not her own. Nobody had ever picked up the pen.

Somebody had picked it up today. Cael had just watched him blot it.

Past the clerk he went, and down the wing's stair.

He had watched her take the copper from the east tier, and for half a season he had wondered how she would hold it when the day came. That night, after everything else, he wrote the answer in six words.

*She held it like an exhibit.*

Under it he wrote two lines more.

*The crown doesn't win you anything. It makes somebody with a pen spend an afternoon on you. Fiske worked that out over two years and two crowns, and then walked across a floor and told it to the one person who could use it.*

By the eighth bell the porters had swept the yard and gone, and it was empty but for the two of them.

On the east stair's bottom step Cael halted. What was happening on the oak did not belong to him.

At the oak's centre, alone, sat Lira. A cold pad was strapped round her hip, and the bad leg lay out flat before her like something she had put down and forgotten.

The duty practitioner had seen to that and gone. The crown was lying on the oak beside her, a little way off, where she had set it down and not picked it up again.

Brom came out through the south door with a bag of ice bound round his knee. He crossed the whole length of the floor at the pace the knee allowed, which was not much of a pace, and lowered himself down beside her.

He sat on the loser's side, with a decent gap between them. Neither of them owned an older habit.

Neither spoke for some time.

"Two years I've been trying to solve you," said Brom at last.

"And?"

Brom looked up at the empty tiers. He rolled the shoulder that had taken most of her afternoon, slowly, as if consulting it. He was a man who liked to agree with a thing properly, not just give way to it, and he took his time.

"Third one someday," he said.

Lira laughed. Laughing hurt, and the hurt set her off again, until she had to prop herself on one flat palm.

Cael stayed on his step. It was theirs.

Up on the north tier, two rows from the front, somebody was still sitting.

Cael had kept the north tier out of his mind since the walk-out and had not let it back in, so he did not know how long the man had been there alone. It was the Archmarshal, in his plain grey, with the little notebook open now on his knee.

While Cael watched, the man wrote a single line. He closed the notebook. The pen went back into his coat. He rose, took the north stair down, and left, without a glance at the floor.

Cael stepped off the stair and crossed the oak toward his friends. The residence steps were coming, and the boy with the iced knee could carry nobody up them.

# Chapter 53 — Calibration

The residence stair had twenty steps, and Lira took them like a siege.

Her right hand was on the rail and her left arm across Cael's shoulders. She would set the bad leg down on a tread and let it bear for about as long as it takes to say *one, two*, and then the leg asked to come up again and she let it. The good leg did the climbing. They stood and breathed, and began again.

"Talk," she said on the sixth step. "Say anything. It's worse when it's quiet."

"The practitioner in the yard, while I was in Gault's office. What did she say?"

"That it's a deep strain along the top of the bone and nothing's torn. Four days off it, ice tonight, heat in the morning, and a stick if I've any sense." Lira breathed through her teeth and came up a step. "Then she gave me a long look and said, *On purpose?*"

"What did you tell her?"

"Yes. So she asked if I'd done it twice." Another step. "I said once. The other three, I said, were accidents."

Below them Brom made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been his knee, which had a bag of ice bound round it and plenty to say about stairs. He was carrying the Copper Crown dangling from two fingers, the way a man carries a hat he has taken off indoors, because Lira had both hands busy and somebody had to. Karis took up the chalk again and drew six lines down the slate.

"The wing posts its order for a semester sitting beside the Magister's door, under the calibration card, and I'm fairly sure I'm the only person who has ever read it from top to bottom, the Magister included." She wrote as she spoke. "One, the board. Two, the frame, read only, with your back to the rail. Three, read and move together. Four, the plate. The last two are the first two over again, with an hour of oak in your legs. That's what you did at the baseline. This time the read stops being a trial of its own and gets carried inside the third and the cold runs. The fifth slot is new: the heaviest weight on the rail, your square brought in close, and a tray of chalk dust beside it so that the clerk can read your feet from the dust instead of from your account of them."

"The weight I took on the plate."

"That one. At the baseline it was too heavy and too close to get out from under, so they made you stand and take it. Now they want to know whether you can leave."

Next to each row went the baseline's figures, small. Four free on the board, the fifth billed, the sixth wide and slow. Eleven calls in twelve off the frame. Seven clear in eight on the combined, at weight three. The plate held low four times, *and not eight*. Then she drew two chalk brackets down the right-hand side of the slate, one above the other, with a gap between them.

"A fortnight's work, this: a floor and a roof. Between them is what a term of honest work ought to buy somebody like you, starting from that baseline, with nothing strange happening along the way. Under the floor, the provision has failed in its own plain words, because you were enrolled to be measured by what you can show and you'll have shown nothing." She tapped the upper bracket. "Up past the roof, you're no longer a reading. You're a puzzle, and a room with a puzzle in it stops measuring and starts to wonder. That's worse."

"And between?"

"On the board, five free, or six. On the combined, where the wing numbers its weights one to five from light to heavy, it's weight four with you clear at least five times in six, or weight three with you clear every time." She laid the chalk down. "I made that gap wide on purpose, then wider, and then I gave the sums to Bracken without saying what they were for, and he worked them through and handed them back the same."

"It doesn't look wide."

"The width isn't the hard part. The hard part is doing it on every instrument at once, for a man who's told you to your face that he'd rather have six runs that agree than one wonder and five that don't, while all the time you carry something nobody else in the room knows about." She looked at him. "For three hours."

From the good chair Lira said, "He knows how long it is, Karis."

"I'm saying it so that he hears it in somebody else's voice."

---

Karis chose to rehearse the plate first, out of the posted order, because it was the one line on the slate that was not allowed to move.

There being no plate in the common room, she handed him the residence's book of hall rules, which was bound in boards nearly an inch thick, and had him hold it out at shoulder height on his right arm, braced the plain way. Brom hit it from the floor, a short heavy sitting blow that went through the book and up the arm and into the shoulder and stopped there.

"Your account," said Karis, borrowing Gault's flatness.

"Four I can stand. Not eight. The shoulder keeps it."

"Again."

He took four more, and the book jumped the same distance each time, and his shoulder made the same complaints in the same order. She had him do a third set and then a fourth, and they came off the press like printed pages, every one the twin of the one before, until she put the chalk down.

"Now give me a scared one," she said. "Do the set you'd do for a roomful of people who scare you."

It took him three goes to make it look real, and he did not enjoy discovering that. In the end he let the arm fold a little further on every blow, and after the third he stepped back off the hearthrug and said he could not take another.

Karis stood with her arms folded and looked at the slate for a long while.

"I hadn't seen that coming, and it would have had you." Her chalk rapped the lower bracket. "For two weeks you've planned against the roof. Don't go over; don't become a question. Now look where you just went. Under. That plate line sits in Gault's own log in Gault's own hand with *four, not eight* beside it, because you said it before he asked, and anybody in that room can lay tomorrow's line over it in the time it takes to blink. Go in frightened and clamp down on everything by feel, and you won't merely scrape the floor; you'll go clean through it. Then the record says that a boy got worse over a whole supervised term while the Compact sat and watched it happen."

Nobody said anything.

"So it was never *hold back*," Karis said. "It's *land on the number*. The plate doesn't move by so much as a graduation, and everything else moves exactly as far as a term would move it. That's a great deal harder than holding back, and it's the actual job."

She wrote a line across the foot of the slate, under both brackets, in letters bigger than all the rest: *ON THE NUMBER.*

The combined trial went the other way.

Brom threw from the wall, a straight sitting blow at whatever instant he chose, while Cael stood on the crack in the boards with his back half turned and had to feel it coming and be gone. Nothing in the house came nearer to a weight falling down a rail. The first run was clean. Cael felt it gather along the outsides of his arms a pace and a half out, turned his shoulder out of its road, and was clear, and Karis timed the gap on the wash-house glass she had borrowed and wrote it down and put her question and got her answer. The second run was clean as well.

The third was clean and quick and smooth as oil. He felt it go right and had just begun to be pleased when Karis said, "Stop."

"That was the best one."

"That was a five," said Karis.

For a moment the word meant nothing to him.

"No frame here," she said, "and nobody's made a scale for punches. But there's a glass, and my eyes, and Brom, and all three agree: that run beat your first by a gap I could see bare-eyed. You went off it at an angle I've never once seen you use near anybody from the wing. If Brom were a weight on a rail, that wasn't the fourth. That was the heaviest thing they own."

"Brom?"

Brom rubbed his fist. "You weren't reading me at the skin," he said slowly. "You were reading me further in. Like at the post, before the delegation came."

The nearest chair-back took his grip.

He understood it about twenty seconds before he could say it, and those were the worst twenty seconds of the evening. Then he said it, because things of that kind were exactly what this room existed for.

"The read's open. All the way down. Since the delegation came I've kept it shallow, because the hold and the reach drink from one cup and the cup is small, and the thin layer costs nothing; it reads a room, but it can't read a fist." He let go of the chair. "This afternoon in the yard I took my hand off it, because nobody was looking at me and I wanted to see the two of you properly. The hold was never in danger, because there was far too much to watch. But the read came all the way up while I was watching, and it's been up ever since, and I never noticed."

Karis had gone very still. "And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I hold from waking, the read drops to its stub, and that run can't happen." He was glad his voice stayed even. "Whatever I put on Gault's oak in the third trial, I'll be putting there with my best instrument turned nearly off. He'll write it down. It'll be honest, and it'll fall short of what I've got."

"Inside the gap?"

He ran the figures. They came out better than anything else that hour had offered.

"Fourth weight. Clear five times in six. Read on its stub." He let his breath go. "Inside. Right on the floor of it."

"Then put it there," said Karis. She chalked it beside the third line and offered no comfort whatever, and that was a great deal better than comfort.

The fifth trial they did not try. Nothing in the house could stand in for the heaviest weight on a thin margin, and there was no baseline line to land on, since the baseline had never run it, and Karis would not rehearse what she could not measure. That went on the slate too, sooner than pretend.

*5. No rehearsal. Nothing to land on. Do it honestly and let it fall where it falls.*

Under it she added a line, and read it out as she wrote.

*Hold going past thin: stop the trial. Say it aloud. An interval you ask for goes in the book as a line. A drift goes in as evidence.*

"You're allowed to stop?" said Brom. "In the middle of a thing?"

"Gault said so on the first morning, and it's in my notebook." Cael found the words where he had kept them. "*When you reach something you cannot do, tell me. The book will record that you could not. Nothing more, and nothing held against you; a plain fact, written down. If I have to work out for myself that you've refused, it tells me less than if you had said so.*" He shrugged. "I've carried that folded up in my head all term and never once spent it."

---

In the second hour Karis began to run the gaps, and the gaps were where it came apart.

She ran them as a panel would. After every trial she turned half away with Brom and put her head close to his over the sheet and talked low for a minute and a half. Cael stood on the crack in the floor and waited, as he would wait a dozen times tomorrow, and perhaps more.

Partway through the hour he felt himself slip. It was not much. It was the feeling he had learned since the stair, the hand on the rope sliding an inch without being told to while his mind was looking at something else, and he caught it and pulled it back and was rather pleased with himself for catching it.

"There," said Lira.

He looked round. She lay sideways across the good chair, the bad hip propped on a cushion under a cloth of ice, and in the polished arm of the chair, under her right thumb, there was a short row of marks scored into the wax with her thumbnail. He counted them from where he stood, and there were eleven.

"That's how many?"

"That's how many I've seen tonight. You caught four. I watched your face when you did." She ran her thumb along the row. "Nine were nothing much, two or three seconds and back, and nobody else in the world would have seen them. I'm not being clever. I've looked at you for three years, and that's the only reason I can. But it's eleven."

"Where?"

"That's the thing." She lifted her thumb. "Never while Brom was hitting you, and never while Karis was asking you something. Every one came while she had her back turned and you were standing on that crack waiting for her to finish. You were waiting, all eleven times."

Karis was chalking *11/11* on the slate before Lira had finished. "That isn't bad luck," she said, half to herself. "That's how it works."

Cael didn't move. The pieces were joining.

"Weeks ago, in hall three, Brom found it," he said slowly. "I wrote it the wrong way up. I thought the danger was being busy, with all of me taken up at once. It isn't. Busy is the safest thing there is. It never goes while I'm working, not on a drill or at the rail or through four minutes of watching you two without blinking. Leave me standing with nothing asked of me, and it goes. An idle rope is the one that runs out through your fingers."

"Then don't stand there with nothing being asked," said Lira.

Everybody turned to her.

"You keep treating it as a thing to grit your teeth at. Your page of tricks is all about doing. Gaps have nothing to do in them. So give yourself something." She shifted the ice. "When Gault turns his back tomorrow, choose somebody in that room, anybody, and read them the whole way down: how they breathe, where their weight sits, what their hands are doing, and whether all of it agrees. Just the one person. And when the panel turns back to you, don't stop dead. Let it drop a level and keep it running underneath."

"The read'll be on its stub. I won't have—"

"I don't mean the read." Her voice sharpened. "I mean *you*. The gaze, the notebook, the watching, the habit you made for yourself back when there wasn't a single Path in you. That isn't a fragment, Cael. Nobody gave you that. Nobody else had it before you; of your whole ledger it's the only such thing. It charges the hold nothing. And it'll keep you out at your edges three hours straight. That's the whole trouble solved."

The room was quiet.

Cael thought of a line in the working ledger from the first week after the stair, which said that the hiding half came unasked and went out like a pinched wick the moment he truly looked. He'd been calling that a flaw, and it was a cure. Gaze and hiding would not share him. Whichever one he ran, the other stopped.

Karis said, "Yes. That works," and looked faintly annoyed with herself.

Lira shrugged with one shoulder. "I've had nothing to do all evening but look."

Her eyes stayed on him, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, and he understood that this was what the twenty steps had been for.

"That's half. The other half's less welcome. Tomorrow's about being measured right, and everything tonight has been about that, and I've helped, and it's right. But you fight, Cael. Whatever they lay their instruments against had better still be you. Clamp everything down and give Gault a careful boy sitting neatly on Karis's floor, and yes, you'll pass. You'll also come out of that room a little less. Then you'll do it again next half-year, and the half-year after that, and every time a little less."

"You don't know that."

"I know a clerk at Fenmark who never once looked up from his page." She said it without any heat. "He didn't mean me harm. He meant to be tidy. That's how they do it, mostly; they tidy you." She held his eyes. "Be the fighter. In a measured room. Both."

---

By the first hour after the last bell Brom had gone up, a tread at a time, with the ice in his hand. Lira was asleep sideways in the good chair with her cheek against its wing, which she would deny in the morning, and Karis was kneeling at the stove with the poker, banking it for the night. On the mantel the slate still had its six lines and two brackets and *ON THE NUMBER* across the foot.

Cael opened the grey folder then, because the wing's marks on his coursework were wing marks and might belong on Karis's slate.

The three exercises were inside, in the driest hand he owned, every word of them true and none of them complete, each with a tick beside its title and two figures in its margin and an initial on the tab, as the wing marked everything. Something he had never handed in lay against the back board. A slip of paper.

Wing paper, trimmed small. Wing hand, too: the trained script every clerk there learned until it was nobody's. It had no name and no date. To anybody who had not already been turning the same question over, it would have meant nothing at all.

*Panels see what they expect prepared for. Show growth where the baseline predicts it. Surprise them exactly once, small, early — a watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.*

His breath forgot itself the first time through. On the second he remembered.

Then he learned it. It was short and had plainly been made to be learned, and it took him about a minute. The back was blank. He walked to the stove, where Karis knelt with the poker, and said, "Excuse me," and she moved her hand, and he opened the little door and laid the slip flat on the red coals. It went brown at one corner and black across the middle, and in four seconds or so it was gone, and the ash it left could not be told from any other.

He stayed crouched by the stove with his palm on the warm iron door.

Whoever had written it had taken a trade that must have cost years to learn, cut a corner off it, and laid it in the back of a boy's coursework knowing perfectly well what it would mean for him if it were ever found. What could Cael give back for that? Only this: that the paper should be gone inside a minute of being read. He had nothing else to give.

Beside him Karis had not moved. She was looking at the stove door with the poker across her knees.

"Was that—" she began.

She stopped, and he watched it happen. The question rose into her face the way questions always did, quick and bright and certain of its right to an answer, and then something else came down over it and covered it as quietly as you lay a cloth over a birdcage.

"No," she said. "Never mind."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She gave the coals a last push and hung the poker on its hook. "Is it any good?"

"Nobody's told me anything truer about tomorrow. I'd have paid a year for it."

"Then spend it," said Karis, and went back to her fair copy at the far end of the table, and neither of them ever spoke of it again.

*Show growth where the baseline predicts it.* Karis had reached that by another road altogether, with her brackets, and that was what first made him believe it: Karis and whoever had cut it had never exchanged a word on the subject, and they had built the same rule out of opposite materials. Then the second line. *Surprise them exactly once, small, early.* That was the piece missing from everything, the piece he could never have made himself, and he sat at the table with a clean sheet for most of twenty minutes before he saw it.

It had to be on the record already, or it would not be a surprise but a confession. It had to be small. And it had to be something nobody at Halcenvane had ever seen.

Only one thing he owned met all three. It was the Ember, Karis's ignition point, which sat in a single thin half-page at the back of the Greyvane transcript, where anybody who paid six marks could read it and nobody ever had. No instrument in the wing had measured it and no panel on the bluff had seen it, and on paper it was the least remarkable thing he possessed: a point of fire at contact, gone in the instant it was made. The wing put its instruments on whatever an enrollee laid before it, and the placing was his.

He looked down the table.

"Karis."

She lifted her pen.

"The Ember stays off the floor. That's what we agreed when term began, because it was yours before it was mine." The good chair had a sleeper in it; he spoke low. "Tomorrow I'd like it on Gault's oak. Once. Second. I'm asking you."

Karis looked at him over the lamp, and he saw her not ask why.

"Yes," she said. "Second. And show it properly, because I've never seen anybody measure it." She went back to her copy. "Then come and tell me what it did."

The new order went down twice, fair. Nothing else went into the stove. Then he went up to his window.

---

*Ledger, day twenty-one.*

*Held: from waking, nineteen hours. Read: let up in the yard on purpose and never put back, which I found out at the table. Drifts: eleven by Lira's thumbnail, four by my own count, and all eleven in the gaps.*

*Given tonight: a gap between two brackets with a registrar's sums under it. A way to look like a man working instead of a man carrying a full bowl. Something to do with the empty minutes that costs nothing, because it's the only part of me nobody handed me. And one thing more, which is where it belongs, and is ash.*

*Nobody gave me a present tonight. Everybody at that table gave me a piece of tomorrow instead. People who love you are one thing. People who are in it with you are another. Two years ago I had neither, and now somehow I've both.*

The lamp went down a notch. Then he wrote the rest.

*Quenna asked me at Greyvane whether I could keep from using it with people watching who'd be glad to see me hurt, and I told her I didn't know. Tomorrow I find out. Six trials across three hours, and under all of them a thing on a short leash that would dearly love to slip it, in the most watched room of my life.*

*Only I'm not sure the man watching would be glad to see me hurt. I've had a week of him. Three things he asked Gault, and every one concerned when the posts were last checked. In nine hours at the records table his pen moved five times, and every time it was about this house's paper and never about me. He sat through a final with his book shut and wrote one line at the end of it. Nothing in that is hoping I fall over. What it wants is the figure to come out true.*

*He doesn't wish me harm. He wishes me* measured.

*All evening I've tried to decide which is worse. No luck. Perhaps I'll know by noon.*

*Sleep. Fight in the morning.*

# Chapter 54 — Four Chairs

Brom's right hand was strapped to the second knuckle in clean linen, and he was holding a mug of tea in it anyway.

He sat on the residence stair two steps below the landing with his back against the plaster and his wrapped knee stuck out across the tread like a gate across a lane. A second mug stood steaming on the step beside him, and the lamp at the stair's foot had burned down to a bead.

"That one's yours," he said without turning his head. "Before you ask, no, I didn't sleep. The knee and I had a disagreement about which way it bends."

Cael sat down a step above him and took the mug in both hands.

An hour earlier, in the black fourth hour after the last bell, he had driven the stake in while his eyes were still shut. That was the order of things now, and had been since the stair. Before the shutter or the cold or a single thought about the day, he reached for his own edges in the dark and found them, and fixed them where an ordinary person's would sit, and left a corner of himself standing guard there. Six names for the feeling had gone into the working ledger, and all six had been struck through. The least bad was a full cup carried across a crowded room, which weighed nothing and hurt nothing and simply could not be put down, and so took a corner of him that would be good for nothing else all day.

It was ledger day twenty-two, and thirty-five days since the stair.

"How's the corner?" said Brom.

"Fixed. Since the fourth hour."

Brom drank, and thought, and worked it out loud in the slow way he worked out anything that mattered.

"The sitting's three hours, and then there's the walk to the wing, and the waiting while they set up, and the walk back." He counted on the fingers of the hand that was not strapped. "Six, more or less."

"About that."

"You managed nine at the records table."

"Seated, against a wall, playing clerk, and three times it slid off me regardless." Cael turned the mug in his hands. "This is six standing, under Gault's instruments."

Brom parked his mug next to its twin and got himself up against the wall, the good leg first and then the other, with the face of a man lifting a cart wheel, and considered Cael from above.

"Here's what I think, then. Today the work that counts has no instrument pointed at it. Every one of them will be looking at the brass and the frame and the figures and thinking that's where the work is, and nobody in that room will ever know what the corner cost you." He reached down and knocked twice on the top of Cael's head with the knuckles of his good hand, softly, the way he knocked on a door he did not need to open. "I'll know. That's the best I've got."

"It's enough."

"It'll do," said Brom, and went up the stair a tread at a time, with the strapped hand flat against the plaster for balance, to sleep at last.

On his way out Cael crossed the common room. The crown sat dull on the long table where Brom had left it, and on the mantel the slate still showed Karis's six lines and two brackets and, across the foot in her big chalk capitals, *ON THE NUMBER*. Twice he read it, and left it standing.

The quadrangle was still grey when he crossed it, and so cold that his breath went ahead of him. Under the covered walk's north arch the fire-watch boy's slate hung on its nail with the night's hours chalked down it in large careful figures, the last of them straightened, by the look of it, more than once. Beyond the wall, down at the ferry landing, the lamp on its post was still lit, and as Cael passed the gap in the parapet the two grey coats under it changed with the bell. One walked off along the bank and his relief walked on, exactly as they had changed every four hours since the bridge. Nothing about the morning had been altered for him. He found that a comfort, and then found it funny that he did, and kept walking with the corner held.

The wing's arch was ahead, and somebody was already waiting under it.

It was Lira, with the safety instructor's stick planted in front of her and both hands folded on its head, leaning on it as though it were a fence she had stopped at to admire the view. Nobody who looked at her face would have believed in the view, because it had the grey of a night spent awake.

"Four days off it," said Cael. "That's what she told you."

"I'm off it. I'm on this." She lifted the stick an inch and set it down again. "Day one of four, and I'm spending day one here. Don't."

He didn't. Two of the wing's porters went past them through the arch with a long box slung between them, and the box clinked as it went.

"Tell me the order," said Lira.

"The corner first, and everything else on top of it."

"In the gaps?"

"One person, read to the bottom. Not him."

"Not him," she agreed. "And if the corner goes?"

"Then I stop. Out loud I ask for an interval, and the clerk enters that I asked. Something I handed them, not something they caught."

"Say it if it goes."

"I'll say it."

She looked at him the way she looked at a floor before a bout, measuring how far it was across and how much it would give.

"Go on, then. Go and be measured, and I'll be here when you come out." She settled her weight more heavily onto the stick. "If I've fallen asleep on this post, wake me gently. I've a crown to live up to."

He went in. At the wing's inner door he looked back, and she was still there under the arch, leaning, watching him all the way, and she did not wave.

---

The room reached him through his ears first.

The pendulum was going in its glass case with its dry, patient tick, and somewhere behind the louvres of the shutter-board the drum was being wound with the sound of a thumbnail drawn slowly down a comb. At the far end of the north wall the brass funnel dripped into its cup. He had stood in this room for a whole morning in his first month, and his body knew every one of those sounds before he was through the door. For a moment it was like coming home to a house in which somebody has moved the furniture.

Only four chairs had moved. They stood in a row along the east wall, where in the first month there had been bare plaster, set out by Bracken himself at a guest's distance from one another, and there were people in them.

The delegation's counsel had the nearest, and she was untying the ribbon of a second folder with her teeth because both her hands were full of the first. Next to her Ilsev was sharpening a pencil with a small knife, carefully, into a fold of paper on her knee, so as to leave no shavings on the wing's floor. Beyond Ilsev, Havel was writing the date at the head of a ruled sheet on a board across his knees, with the bound file under the board. As Cael came in he looked up for about four-tenths of a second, level and without expression, and then down again to finish his date.

Cael understood that he had just been greeted, in the only way the card allowed, and greeted properly.

The Archmarshal had the fourth chair. He sat at his ease in his plain grey with his hands resting loose on his thighs, and there was nothing in them, nor on his knees, nor at his feet, nor anywhere near him: no folder, no case, no pencil, and no sign of the little notebook from the north tier.

Between door and crossing lay thirty feet of oak, and by the end of them Cael had it. Everybody else on that wall had brought the tool of their trade, because people do. The counsel lived by paper and had brought paper, Ilsev had brought a point for marking, and Havel had brought the record, since keeping it was what he was for. The highest rank in the building had come with empty hands. Either he meant to do nothing, or the instrument he worked with was already sitting in his chair.

*He'll keep it in his head*, Cael thought. *Every bit of it.*

He put the thought away where it would not trip him, stepped onto the brass crossing, set his feet, and stood.

Under the south wall the panel had arranged itself exactly as in the first month. Heavy and still, Gault held the middle behind a squared sheaf. To his right the Ash instructor clasped her book flat to her chest. To his left the Mire instructor kept her coat on and her fists deep in its pockets. A little apart, at his sloped desk, the clerk had his ledger open and his three pens in their row.

Gault stayed in his chair, as he had at the baseline. Cael found that steadying beyond anything else in the room, because it meant nothing about the place had been changed for its visitors.

"This is the wing's semester sitting for the assay-provision enrollee," said Gault, in a voice pitched for the clerk's pen. "The wing calendar carried it from the first week of term, posted, long before the word *inspection* was heard in this building. The panel is the one you see, and the clerk keeps the book. The Compact's delegation sits under its right to observe. It may watch; it may not steer. I was asked to say so aloud, and I'm content to."

In the near chair the counsel, her ribbon finally undone, gave a small nod.

"Enrollee. You've stood on this oak before and you know my three rules." Gault gave them flatly, as he would read off a dial. "Say so when you cannot. Give me the same thing twice before you give me your best once. And you are measured against the line you drew here yourself in your first month, and against nothing else: not against hopes, not against anything printed, not against talk." He looked up. "Show me a marvel and I shall have to wonder what I missed."

"Yes, Magister."

"Before we start." His broad palm settled on the sheaf. "This wing measures whatever you bring it. So it was at the baseline; so it is today. Do you mean to bring anything from your record that this oak hasn't yet seen?"

"The ignition point, Magister. The one on the Greyvane record."

Gault's face did nothing at all. "Where would you like it?"

"Second, Magister. It's done by touch, and I'd like the arm unspent."

"Second, then. The six stand as posted otherwise, with the read carried in the third and the last." He turned his head an inch. "Clerk, enter the change." He squared the sheaf. "Begin."

---

Gault gave the first instruction in the very words of the first month. Cael had wanted that badly, and had refused himself the wanting.

"Fourth brass from the near wall. Stand on the crossing. When the board opens, be somewhere else."

The drum went round behind the louvres, picking, with the pendulum ticking under it, and the sixth louvre clacked open.

His knees went and his hips dropped and the oak breathed under him; then came the toll at the front, the held half-breath with no air in him anywhere; and then the crossing was empty and he was most of a stride to its left and coming down. The boards took his heels and gave them back. The landing beat held him, as it always held him, for its half-breath of standing on the spot like a post somebody had driven in.

The clerk did not look up. "Three brass left. A quarter forward." A figure followed, taken from the pendulum.

The distance was what it had been in the first month, but the figure was shorter, and Cael had already felt it shorter in his chest.

"The same again," said Gault.

He ran it eight times. The first three came free, and he did not hide it. On the fourth he let the work show, as Brom had told him to, and came off the brass with his chest heaving and stood there breathing through his open mouth while all four chairs and three faculty watched. Best of the morning was the fifth. His legs told him so a count ahead of the clerk. The drop was cleaner and the held breath trimmed by a sliver that his lungs never noticed and the pendulum did, because a term of four hours a week on this oak had taught his leading leg to accept a landing instead of quarrelling with it.

The sixth was its twin, and so was the seventh, but as he came down from the seventh he felt the first of the bill arrive, low on the leading side, a slow warmth like a stone left in the sun.

The eighth went off line. It was not far, only a hand's breadth toward a place he had not chosen, but the landing beat hung a shade too long while the pendulum swung on past it. The warmth on the leading side drew itself into the thread he knew, thin and bright, from the crest of the hip down the inside of the thigh to the knee. That would be with him tonight.

Breathing, he held the spot where he had landed.

"Three and a half left. Slow."

Gault laid his pen beside eight lines of figures and looked at them with his hands flat on either side.

"Six runs sit within a third of a brass of one another, and so does the seventh, which you paid for in front of me. The eighth goes wide and slow." He lifted his eyes. "In your first month you gave me your ceiling on this oak before the board had told me anything. Give it again."

"Six free, Magister. Tomorrow pays for the seventh. The day after pays for the eighth."

"In the first month it was four."

"Four free and the fifth on credit, so it's two higher now." Cael let his breath go. "That's what four hours a week on this floor buys in a term. I don't think it buys more."

Gault picked the pen up again and wrote for some while, and then, still writing, gave the clerk the line for the record in his flattest voice.

"Working ceiling given by the enrollee: six. Baseline: four. Seven runs within the baseline's spread, with a shorter interval. The ceiling given before the instrument gave it, as before."

He set the pen down.

"That is what the provision is for."

Then a page turned, and that was all.

Along the east wall the counsel wrote, and Havel wrote because writing was what he had been sent to do, but Ilsev's freshly sharpened pencil had not touched her paper. Cael caught at the very edge of his eye that she had spent the whole trial watching the drum behind the louvres and not him. In the fourth chair nothing had moved.

Then came the first gap, and he had been waiting for it the way you wait for a stair tread you know is loose.

Gault leaned toward the Mire instructor and the two of them bent their heads over the sheet and talked low, with the Ash instructor leaning in to listen, and after that the clerk would be sent to the north wall and would take his time over it. A minute and a half, perhaps two, with nothing for anybody on the brass to do.

Cael felt the room go slack round him like a rope let out.

So he followed Lira's instruction and chose somebody, and the somebody he chose was the clerk.

The clerk was a thin man of about thirty, younger in the face than in the hands. He had kept the book through every hour Cael had ever spent in this room, and he sat at his sloped desk now with his pen lifted, waiting to be told what to write next. Cael took him from the top, the way he had been reading people since long before there was a single Path in him to read them with.

Rhythm: slow and even, a breath and a half between movements of the pen, never hurried and never held. Weight: settled back, but with the shoulders a little forward over the desk, the way a man sits who has spent ten years over desks and quietly stopped noticing. From the north wall the clerk carried it in both arms, like something he had been cautioned to mind.

Cael had looked at it every time he came into this room and had never once got a name for it. Squat, copper, three short legs, a little larger than two fists: that was the pot. "No, Magister. It's puzzled me since my second week."

"It's for heat. Water, shut in, and a glass to show the water rising. Slow, honest, unhurried, and deaf to argument." He drew a sheet toward him. "Ember and Ash sit it every quarter. Flattery isn't in it." He laid the sheet flat. "Greyvane's record holds a single exhibit for the ignition point. Contact work. One channel. Thin, and late in that house's term. Down on one knee, Cael spread his palm on the copper's belly. The copper was cool, and faintly oily from the clerk's polishing cloth.

Smallest of all his fragments, the Ember was also the only one he held with a signature behind it: Karis's, dated, at the foot of a page. It went one way and one distance only and beyond that did nothing whatever. It was not heat. He had learned that at Greyvane over two slow months and through two of Karis's words for it, first *a spark* and later *a letting-go*.

He let go at the copper.

Under his hand the metal warmed, and a hot band two fingers broad spread under its skin and passed inward to the water, and the thread in the glass straw began to rise. It rose slowly. Slowness was the instrument's whole virtue and the reason Gault trusted it, because nothing quick can fool water, and the thread climbed past one line and the next and stopped.

"Hold it," said Gault.

"I can't hold it, Magister. It doesn't run. Each one's a single moment at the hand, finished in the instant it's made, and then I start over."

"Then make it again."

He made it again, and somewhere between that one and the next, kneeling on the oak with his palm on a copper pot in front of a row of chairs, he understood what Karis's third word was.

It was not a spark, and it was not a letting-go either, though that was what it felt like at the very end; those were both the outside of it. Underneath both lay a choice of an instant. There is an instant when the unburnt turns into the burning, and what he had taken from her let him put that instant wherever he wished at the surface of his own skin. It was a decision point. Everything that came after it was the copper's business, or the water's, or the wood's, and only the moment was his. That was why it would not hold, and why it cost him the same whatever size he asked for, and why each one had to be made new: you could not hold a decision, you could only make another one.

Four times he chose the instant, with the clerk calling the gaps from the pendulum, and four times the thread drank the warmth and rose. Past the fifth line it stood after the fourth, and stayed, while the water made up its mind. Gault watched the glass straw the whole while, and never once the hand.

"For the book," Gault said at last. "Ignition point, shown on the vessel. Four applications. The glass answered each the same, with no drift between them. No heat away from the hand. Contact only." The pen followed his words one by one. "On the enrollee's prior record. Confirmed here, to the wing's standard."

That went down too. Gault's voice kept its level for one more line.

"That is a better instrument reading," he said, "than the Greyvane exhibit supports. The exhibit calls it marginal."

"The exhibit's from my first weeks with it, Magister. Nearly a year back."

"Mm," said Gault, and wrote that too.

The copper was cooling toward nothing when Cael lifted his hand. He rose and returned to the crossing. He kept his eyes off the east wall and still knew what had happened along it, since he had counted rooms ever since Ardenmere and could no more stop than stop his heart. The counsel's pen had run on for a long time and she had turned her page to finish, and then she had leaned along the row and said a few words to Havel, and Havel had made a mark. For the first time that morning Ilsev's fresh point met paper.

They had found something: small, true, already on the record, early, and paid for out of a purse he could spare. For whatever was left of the morning, everyone on that wall who had come hoping to catch him out had one catch already, folded into a pocket.

Nothing had been written in the fourth chair. He had nothing to write with.

---

Water is slow, and Gault would not go on until the glass had settled, so every minute the clerk read the straw and wrote down a figure, and between readings there was nothing for anybody to do but wait.

Cael waited on the crossing and went back to the clerk. The clerk held through the first reading and the second. He held while the Ash instructor rose and went to stand over the copper herself, peering at the straw, and came back. He held through the fourth reading and the fifth, and while the Mire instructor took her hands out of her coat pockets for the first time that day and frowned at her nails. After the sixth reading the thread stopped falling.

"A question before we go on," said Gault. "Four applications. Why four, and not one held? You've told me it doesn't run. I'd like to know what it does instead, as nearly as you can put it."

It was a good question, and that was what was wrong with it.

To answer it honestly Cael had to go a long way in, down past the skin of his own palm after a moment that had taken no time at all. Then he had to pull it apart in front of a panel: what it was, and why it would not stay, and where in it the choosing sat. He had understood it himself only a quarter of an hour before, and finding words for Gault's book was like trying to describe a stair you are still standing on in the dark.

Down he went after the words. Down there, unattended, the corner slid.

The room's weather changed. He had no better way of putting it. Eight people's worth of being looked at had been pressing on him lightly all morning, the way air presses, and now it eased by a fraction, because eight people had very slightly forgotten that a boy stood on the brass. It lasted two seconds, or less. Across the table the Mire instructor glanced down at her own hands, the way an eye leaves an empty doorway.

He came up out of the answer faster than he had ever come up out of anything.

"Magister," he said. "I need an interval."

Gault's pen stopped. "Why?"

"I've been answering you and minding the sitting together, Magister, and I've let one of them drop. Better a minute lost now than a poor figure on your frame."

"Which one slipped?"

"The answer, Magister."

Into her book the Ash instructor laughed, or nearly. Gault's face did not change.

"Clerk. Two minutes, asked for by the enrollee. Give the reason as fatigue affecting response quality." He turned his head a fraction. "And put down that he asked before anything on my table showed it." He looked back at Cael. "Sit for your two minutes. You lose nothing by it. You look as though you're weighing whether you will."

At the rail by the west wall Cael sat with his palms on the cold wood. Two minutes went on hunting down his edges and setting each one back where an ordinary boy of sixteen would wear it. Grudgingly they came, using up nearly the whole allowance, and left behind them an ache behind the eyes like the one the read sent after a long day. An hour and a half still to go: a poor sign.

To the table and the wall alike, nothing had occurred. In the book it would read *fatigue affecting response quality*. True. *At the enrollee's request.* True. Nothing more, and that was true as well. That was the whole art of it.

He got up off the rail.

"Ready, Magister."

# Chapter 55 — Renewed

The Mire instructor racked the fourth weight with her own hands and tried the pawl twice with her thumb, listening to it, before she cranked the rail to a height nobody named aloud and went back to her chair and buried her hands in her coat pockets again. The read was on its stub, as he had told Karis it would be. All morning he had kept it at the thinnest layer, the one that cost nothing, because the hold and the reach drank from one cup and the cup was small. At its thinnest it would still find a falling weight a pace and a half out, but it would not find it early or clearly or leave him anything to spare.

He stood in the chalk with his back to the rail, and the pawl let go.

It came to him late and thin, like a voice through a wall: a pressure gathering high on the outside of his left arm, and then the whole weight of it arriving there at once.

# Chapter 56 — Twelve by Fourteen

The charter was already lying open on the records-hall counter when Cael came in at the first bell, turned the right way up for a reader standing on the public side.

Bracken was behind the counter with his pen behind his ear and a cup of something long gone cold at his elbow, and he did not say good morning. He laid one finger beside a paragraph halfway down the right-hand page and kept it there while Cael read.

*An inspecting delegation may examine any enrollee whose enrollment rests on a provision or exception, in the presence of the institution's counsel and the officer of record for that enrollment, on the record, at a time set by the institution.*

"Fourth part," said Bracken. "Provisions and exceptions. Subsection eleven." He lifted the finger. "The charter was born with it. Their counsel sent up a request under it at the eighth bell last night, very properly worded, and there's nothing unusual in it; a house with exceptions on its roll has it used now and then. This one has had it twice in my time, both times over students from across the water, and on both occasions the most exciting thing that happened was a man asking for a second glass of water."

"So it's ordinary."

"It is perfectly ordinary." Bracken took the great book in both hands and set its spine true to the counter. "And yet it's the single door in this whole inspection through which a Compact officer may walk up to you and ask you something to your face, and wait for the reply. Half a sentence is a poor thing to give a man, so you're having both halves."

Cael read the subsection again. *At a time set by the institution.*

"When?"

"I've set it. Fourth bell today, in the east room upstairs." Bracken took the pen from behind his ear and turned it over in his fingers. "I went up with a rule last night and measured it, to be sure: twelve feet by fourteen, the smallest room in the building that will take five chairs without anybody's knees touching. The house's counsel will sit, and Magister Gault will sit, because the subsection wants the officer of record and he is yours. And the book will be kept by one of my clerks and not one of theirs."

"They wanted their own?"

"Their counsel proposed one of theirs at the foot of her request, and I objected in writing, on both sides of a sheet." He set the pen down. "It took me a quarter of an hour to draft and her four minutes to accept, and I'd like you to know it was done, because you'll never see it anywhere else. It'll be in the file. Nobody reads that part of a file."

"Thank you."

"Don't." Bracken shut the charter. "Thanking me makes it sound as though I'd done you a kindness, and I haven't. I've done the filing correctly." Over the closed boards his eyes found Cael's. "Correct filing is all this counter sells. Kindness wears out. Filing doesn't."

Cael went up the back stair of the records hall before breakfast anyway, because he had never yet walked into a room he had not tried to see first. The east room's door was shut. On the landing outside it, on a stool, with a mug in one hand and a ledger on his knee, sat the inky-knuckled clerk.

"Registrar's orders," the clerk said, before Cael could open his mouth. "Nobody goes in before the bell. Not their counsel, not ours, not the Magister, and not you." He drank. "Their counsel tried at the first bell. I told her the same. She wrote it down."

Cael looked at the shut door, and at the clerk, and understood that he was being treated exactly as the delegation was being treated, to the minute and to the word, and that it was the fairest thing anybody on the bluff could have done for him.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said the clerk, who had plainly worked for Bracken a long time, and turned a page.

"Sit there," said Lira, "and be him."

She was lying on the settle with the bad leg up on a bolster, pointing at Brom, and Brom looked at the chair she meant, the hard one at the head of the long table, and then back at her.

"I don't know what he sounds like."

"Nobody does. That's the point. Go and sit there and be somebody nobody knows what he sounds like."

So Brom limped to the head of the table and sat down in the hard chair with his strapped hand on the wood and his iced knee stuck out to one side, and looked at Cael, and Cael waited, and Brom went on looking at him.

It went on for a long time. Brom had sat on a bench for twelve days at the start of term while Rooke decided whether he was watching, and he could keep still longer than anyone Cael had ever met. He did not frown or smile or shift, but only breathed and looked, and his face gave back nothing at all. The stove ticked, and somewhere down in the yard a bucket was dropped.

At last a laugh got out of Cael; nothing else was left to try. "I can't. There's nothing there to get hold of."

"Then that's your page," said Lira. "Write it down. *Nothing there to get hold of.*"

Karis had the library's list open beside her bread, a single sheet in her own hand that she had brought up from carrel eleven at the first bell. "Three mentions in eleven years of print," she said, running her finger down it: "a dedication, a dinner for a retiring magistrate, and a swearing-in. He was at all three, and said nothing at any of them that anybody thought worth writing down." She turned it face down. "I can't help you either. I've never been asked to read a man who leaves no paper."

"Which is?"

"The truth."

"Half of it," said Lira from the settle, without opening her eyes. "That's the half you say to make people stop asking. Say the other half, so we can all hear it."

He had said it in her hearing on purpose, half expecting exactly that, and she was right.

"If he asks about anything that's already written down somewhere, I tell him all of it, straight. If he asks about anything that isn't, I tell him I won't answer, out loud and in those words, so that the clerk can put down that I said no." He looked at the face-down sheet. "That's the change. In every room before this one, when there's been a thing I couldn't say, I've said something true with a corner cut off it: on Gault's plate yesterday, at the baseline, at Greyvane. Each time it worked, and each time it cost, and each cost went in the book.

"It won't work on him. Hand him a true sentence with a corner cut off and he'll see the cut. He won't mention it. He'll simply know. Then the finest reader the Compact has will be holding a map of my walls." He turned his hands over. "A flat no only tells him that there's something I won't say, which he knows already, as does the whole bluff. A cut corner points his eye at the cut."

At the head of the table Brom spoke for the first time since he had sat down, still in the chair and still looking straight at Cael.

"Then when you say no, put it down like a full plate," he said. "Both hands, all the way to the table, and no rattle."

Karis pressed both palms to the wood. Other people frowned; Karis did that.

"Let me name the danger over the porridge, so I'm not naming it to the ceiling in the small hours. Into the inspection file it goes, and the first readers will be the ones hunting for a reason. You could hardly hand them anything better to quote."

"Yes."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"I'll do it anyway, because if he catches me in a lie that's worse than a no he doesn't like, by more than I can count." He held her eyes. "There's one more piece. Rip it apart if it's rotten.

"Nine days he's been on this hill, and not a soul has played to him. Withrow stood still on purpose. Bracken's file stood up without anybody holding it. Rooke signed his sheets and went back to his cohort. Yesterday Gault ran three hours of true instruments and wrote down a flat line and stood up for it. The whole house has spent nine days being exactly what it is, as hard as it can." He spread his hands on the table. "So picture me walking in there and playing to him. Then I'm the only performer on the whole bluff, and I've spoilt the single thing this house has managed to let him see."

Nobody spoke, and Brom, in the hard chair, went on being nobody in particular.

"That's a good reason." Karis used the formal voice she saved for things she would sign. "It's also convenient. I'd like both of those minuted."

"Minute both."

Karis took the grey notebook out of her satchel, opened it at a fresh page, and wrote, in her small upright hand, *Refusal in the open. Reason: good. Also convenient.* She turned it round so that he could read it, and he nodded, and she closed the book again with the air of somebody filing a document in a box that nobody would be allowed to open.

From under her forearm Lira squinted across at him.

"Fourth bell," she said. "Don't be clever. Scare you and you turn clever, and clever turns you into a fellow with ladders to sell."

Slowly Brom rose from the hard chair and turned back into Brom. "That was horrible," he said. "I don't know how he does it all day."

The east room was up a short stair at the back of the records hall, and when Cael reached its door everybody else was already inside. He noticed that before anything else, and afterward he wrote it first: they had all come before him.

It was a plain whitewashed room, too small for the table somebody had put in it, with one window looking out over the river. Nearest the door, Withrow's counsel had her leather case on her lap with its clasp done up, and Gault sat beside her with a grey folder lying closed in front of him. Cael knew him well enough by now to read it: the folder was there to be seen, and would not be opened. In the corner, at a little sloped board, sat Bracken's inky-knuckled clerk. His sheet already had a narrow margin column ruled for the minutes, and his pen was full.

Opposite, with his back to the glass, the Archmarshal sat before a bare stretch of tabletop.

The window put the light behind the man, so that whoever sat facing him would spend half an hour looking into a bright grey river afternoon at a face set in shadow. The thought came to Cael on the threshold, and he looked it over, and put it back. The builders had put that window over the river generations ago, and the man had come in first and taken the chair on his own side of the table, as anyone would; the light had not been arranged, it had simply been there.

*Or I've just spent a breath building a plot out of a window*, Cael thought, as he sat. *Watch that.*

"The authority is the fourth part, subsection eleven. Everything said goes in the book. The rules are short. Any question may be refused. Refuse one, and the clerk writes *declined* and nothing more. "Yes."

"At the end of the Wind."

"Explain."

"Every burst finishes with a moment I can't do anything in, half a breath after I land, when I can't turn or reach or change my mind. It's always the same length. "No."

"For three years I've tried every week to make that half-breath shorter on purpose, and I've never managed it; I tried a month ago on the flagstones and failed." He kept his eyes on the man's face and did not try to make the answer look better than it was. "Yesterday a frame failed by nobody's design. It happened once. How, I can't tell you. Promise you a second time and I'd be selling a skill I haven't got. What I own is one accident and a bruise the size of a plate."

Two breaths went by while the man weighed it, unhurried, like a figure from a new instrument he was still learning to believe.

"That's right," he said. "Mark the word I chose. Accurate. Not well said." He turned his head a little, as if to a new page. "The fourth."

"Who taught you to prepare?"

"A circuit. And everyone who beat me," said Cael.

Now, for the first time, the man's face altered. It was not a smile. Cael had watched the same small easing cross Gault's face over a clean trial: a craftsman handed a figure he can use.

"Tell me about the second half."

"I started out on the circuit at Ardenmere losing more than I won. After each loss I'd sit up writing out what had been done to me, every step I could recover. Brom taught me what a wall feels like from the wrong side. Lira, the same way, has taught me more than either. Cael suspected the question had begun life scribbled in somebody's margin.

Cael did not answer at once.

He understood the question as soon as it was asked, and understood that it was not about the hearing as a whole but about one particular choice, made at Greyvane in the space of a few breaths. Facing a Compact panel, he had said aloud that the schedule had no room in it for him anywhere. His advisers, all of them grown and all of them kind, had begged him to fight for a box. Nobody in any corridor had asked him why he had not, and none of the people who had written about the transcript had so much as noticed; at the time, and for a long while after, it had looked like the moment he lost.

"Inside a box, the argument is me," he said. If there was no box, all that was left was the clause, and a clause can't change its mind halfway through an afternoon."

For a moment everything in Cael went quite still.

Four true answers lay to hand. Three would have opened every door he had kept bolted all his life. The fourth was complete and honest and opened none of them, and it was waiting for him, written two years ago inside a notebook's front board for some reason that had nothing to do with today.

The inky clerk's pen kept moving.

"Memory's a kind clerk. It rounds up." Cael found he could say it plainly. He braced.

Any questioner alive would have asked it. The house's counsel had already drawn the little breath of somebody bracing to hear it. Gault, beside her, had gone as still as a post: he had seen the road the next question would take, and the charter gave him nothing to block it with.

The man did not ask.

His chin dipped a fraction, as a man's does over a receipt, and he said, "Seventh."

*He didn't ask.*

*He walked right up to it. He knew it was there; I watched him know. Nerves didn't stop him. Neither did politeness, nor some trap for later. Whatever lay past that edge simply wasn't the thing he'd come to weigh, so he left it.*

*No one has ever been close enough to take a thing from me and kept their hands at their sides. Not once, in my whole life, until now.*

"What does your provision cost this house to keep?"

Cael had not been ready for that one, and found that he had an answer anyway. He gave it in Bracken's words from the charter sitting, because Bracken's were plainer than any he could have made: the house had to keep him on its roll, set a standard and post it where it could be read, measure him against that standard in front of a panel every half-year, and keep the record where anyone with a right to read it could. No paper on this bluff is better kept than the card on your wall, and I've seen most of them." He turned to the corner. "Clerk."

The inky clerk's head came up. He looked like a man addressed by a statue.

"A ruled column," said the man, "and a page without a blot. You'd be surprised how seldom I see either."

Then he was gone, and his steps went down the short stair at the same even pace at which they had come up it.

River light fell past the empty chair into the little whitewashed room, and Cael stayed where he was. His hands wanted to shake, he found, and his legs had not yet agreed to stand, and he let them all take their time.

At last Gault pulled the grey folder over and lifted its cover. His eyes rested on nothing in particular inside. The cover went down again.

"That is the most competent half-hour," said Gault, "that I have sat through in eleven years. I did not," he said, "say one word in it."

Cael went down the stair some while after with one sentence in him that he did not write down yet, because he wanted to carry it for a time first and see whether it kept its shape. Every system that had ever had his name in its books had been muddled about him, and so it had asked, and the asking was where he had always found room to answer. This one had not asked. Muddled, it was not.

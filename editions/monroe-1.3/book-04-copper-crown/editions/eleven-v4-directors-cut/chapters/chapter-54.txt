Chapter 54 — Four Chairs

[Restrained, intimate narration; give loyalty and doubt room to register]

Brom's right hand was strapped to the second knuckle in clean linen, and he was holding a mug of tea in it anyway.

He sat on the residence stair two steps below the landing with his back against the plaster and his wrapped knee stuck out across the tread like a gate across a lane. A second mug stood steaming on the step beside him, and the lamp at the stair's foot had burned down to a bead.

"That one's yours," he said without turning his head. "Before you ask, no, I didn't sleep. The knee and I had a disagreement about which way it bends."

Cael sat down a step above him and took the mug in both hands.

An hour earlier, in the black fourth hour after the last bell, he had driven the stake in while his eyes were still shut, before the shutter or the cold or a single thought about the day, as he had every morning since the stair.

It was ledger day twenty-two, and thirty-five days since the stair.

"How's the corner?" said Brom.

"Fixed. Since the fourth hour."

Brom drank, and thought, and worked it out loud in the slow way he worked out anything that mattered.

"The sitting's three hours, and then there's the walk to the wing, and the waiting while they set up, and the walk back." He counted on the fingers of the hand that was not strapped. "Six, more or less."

"About that."

"You managed nine at the records table."

"Seated, against a wall, playing clerk, and three times it slid off me regardless." Cael turned the mug in his hands. "This is six standing, under Gault's instruments."

Brom parked his mug next to its twin and got himself up against the wall, the good leg first and then the other, with the face of a man lifting a cart wheel, and considered Cael from above.

"Here's what I think, then. Today the work that counts has no instrument pointed at it. Every one of them will be looking at the brass and the frame and the figures and thinking that's where the work is, and nobody in that room will ever know what the corner cost you." He reached down and knocked twice on the top of Cael's head with the knuckles of his good hand, softly. "I'll know. That's the best I've got."

"It's enough."

"It'll do," said Brom, and went up the stair a tread at a time, with the strapped hand flat against the plaster for balance, to sleep at last.

On his way out Cael crossed the common room. The crown sat dull on the long table where Brom had left it, and on the mantel the slate still showed Karis's six lines and two brackets and, across the foot in her big chalk capitals, ON THE NUMBER. Twice he read it, and left it standing.

The quadrangle was still grey when he crossed it, and so cold that his breath went ahead of him. Under the covered walk's north arch the fire-watch boy's slate hung on its nail with the night's hours chalked down it in large careful figures, the last of them straightened, by the look of it, more than once. Beyond the wall, down at the ferry landing, the lamp on its post was still lit, and as Cael passed the gap in the parapet the two grey coats under it changed with the bell. One walked off along the bank and his relief walked on, as they had changed every four hours since the bridge. Nothing about the morning had been altered for him. He found that a comfort, and then found it funny that he did, and kept walking with the corner held.

The wing's arch was ahead, and somebody was already waiting under it.

It was Lira, with the safety instructor's stick planted in front of her and both hands folded on its head, leaning on it as though it were a fence she had stopped at to admire the view. Nobody who looked at her face would have believed in the view, because it had the grey of a night spent awake.

"Four days off it," said Cael. "That's what she told you."

"I'm off it. I'm on this." She lifted the stick an inch and set it down again. "Day one of four, and I'm spending day one here. Don't."

He didn't. Two of the wing's porters went past them through the arch with a long box slung between them, and the box clinked as it went.

"Tell me the order," said Lira.

"The corner first, and everything else on top of it."

"In the gaps?"

"One person, read to the bottom. Not him."

"Not him," she agreed. "And if the corner goes?"

"Then I stop. Out loud I ask for an interval, and the clerk enters that I asked. Something I handed them, not something they caught."

"Say it if it goes."

"I'll say it."

She looked at him the way she looked at a floor before a bout, measuring how far it was across and how much it would give.

"Go on, then. Go and be measured, and I'll be here when you come out." She settled her weight more heavily onto the stick. "If I've fallen asleep on this post, wake me gently. I've a crown to live up to."

He went in. At the wing's inner door he looked back, and she was still there under the arch, leaning, watching him all the way, and she did not wave.

The room reached him through his ears first.

The pendulum was going in its glass case with its dry, patient tick, and somewhere behind the louvres of the shutter-board the drum was being wound with the sound of a thumbnail drawn slowly down a comb. At the far end of the north wall the brass funnel dripped into its cup. He had stood in this room for a whole morning in his second month, and his body knew every one of those sounds before he was through the door. For a moment it was like coming home to a house in which somebody has moved the furniture.

Only four chairs had moved. They stood in a row along the east wall, where at the baseline there had been bare plaster, set out by Bracken himself at a guest's distance from one another, and there were people in them.

The delegation's counsel had the nearest, and she was untying the ribbon of a second folder with her teeth because both her hands were full of the first. Next to her Ilsev was sharpening a pencil with a small knife, carefully, into a fold of paper on her knee, so as to leave no shavings on the wing's floor. Beyond Ilsev, Havel was writing the date at the head of a ruled sheet on a board across his knees, with the bound file under the board. As Cael came in he looked up for about four-tenths of a second, level and without expression, and then down again to finish his date.

Cael understood that he had just been greeted, in the only way the card allowed, and greeted properly.

The Archmarshal had the fourth chair. He sat at his ease in his plain grey with his hands resting loose on his thighs, and there was nothing in them, nor on his knees, nor at his feet, nor anywhere near him: no folder, no case, no pencil, and no sign of the little notebook from the north tier.

Between door and crossing lay thirty feet of oak, and by the end of them Cael had it. Everybody else on that wall had brought the tool of their trade, because people do. The counsel lived by paper and had brought paper, Ilsev had brought a point for marking, and Havel had brought the record, since keeping it was what he was for. The highest rank in the building had come with empty hands. Either he meant to do nothing, or the instrument he worked with was already sitting in his chair.

He'll keep it in his head, Cael thought. Every bit of it.

He put the thought away where it would not trip him, stepped onto the brass crossing, set his feet, and stood.

Under the south wall the panel had arranged itself exactly as at the baseline. Heavy and still, Gault held the middle behind a squared sheaf. To his right the Ash instructor clasped her book flat to her chest. To his left the Mire instructor kept her coat on and her fists deep in its pockets. A little apart, at his sloped desk, the clerk had his ledger open and his three pens in their row.

Gault stayed in his chair, as he had at the baseline. Cael found that steadying beyond anything else in the room, because it meant nothing about the place had been changed for its visitors.

"This is the wing's semester sitting for the assay-provision enrollee," said Gault, in a voice pitched for the clerk's pen. "The wing calendar carried it from the first week of term, posted, long before the word inspection was heard in this building. The panel is the one you see, and the clerk keeps the book. The Compact's delegation sits under its right to observe. It may watch; it may not steer. I was asked to say so aloud, and I'm content to."

In the near chair the counsel, her ribbon finally undone, gave a small nod.

"Enrollee. You've stood on this oak before and you know my three rules." Gault gave them flatly, as he would read off a dial. "Say so when you cannot. Give me the same thing twice before you give me your best once. And you are measured against the line you drew here yourself in your second month, and against nothing else: not against hopes, not against anything printed, not against talk." He looked up. "Show me a marvel and I shall have to wonder what I missed."

"Yes, Magister."

"Before we start." His broad palm settled on the sheaf. "This wing measures whatever you bring it. So it was at the baseline; so it is today. Do you mean to bring anything from your record that this oak hasn't yet seen?"

"The ignition point, Magister. The one on the Greyvane record."

Gault's face did nothing at all. "Where would you like it?"

"Second, Magister. It's done by touch, and I'd like the arm unspent."

"Second, then. The six stand as posted otherwise, with the read carried in the third and the last." He turned his head an inch. "Clerk, enter the change." He squared the sheaf. "Begin."

Gault gave the first instruction in the very words of the baseline. Cael had wanted that badly, and had refused himself the wanting.

"Fourth brass from the near wall. Stand on the crossing. When the board opens, be somewhere else."

The drum went round behind the louvres, picking, with the pendulum ticking under it, and the sixth louvre clacked open.

His knees went and his hips dropped and the oak breathed under him; then came the toll at the front, the held half-breath with no air in him anywhere; and then the crossing was empty and he was most of a stride to its left and coming down. The boards took his heels and gave them back. The landing beat held him, as it always held him, for its half-breath of standing on the spot like a post somebody had driven in.

The clerk kept his eyes on his pendulum. "Three brass left. A quarter forward." A figure followed, taken from the pendulum.

The distance was what it had been at the baseline, but the figure was shorter, and Cael had already felt it shorter in his chest.

"The same again," said Gault.

He ran it eight times. The first three came free, and he did not hide it. On the fourth he let the work show, as Brom had told him to, and came off the brass with his chest heaving and stood there breathing through his open mouth while all four chairs and three faculty watched. Best of the morning was the fifth. His legs told him so a count ahead of the clerk. The drop was cleaner and the held breath trimmed by a sliver that his lungs never noticed and the pendulum did, because a term of four hours a week on this oak had taught his leading leg to accept a landing instead of quarrelling with it.

The sixth was its twin, and so was the seventh, but as he came down from the seventh he felt the first of the bill arrive, low on the leading side, a slow warmth like a stone left in the sun.

The eighth went off line. It was not far, only a hand's breadth toward a place he had not chosen, but the landing beat hung a shade too long while the pendulum swung on past it. The warmth on the leading side drew itself into the thread he knew, thin and bright, from the crest of the hip down the inside of the thigh to the knee. That would be with him tonight.

Breathing, he held the spot where he had landed.

"Three and a half left. Slow."

Gault laid his pen beside eight lines of figures and looked at them with his hands flat on either side.

"Six runs sit within a third of a brass of one another, and so does the seventh, which you paid for in front of me. The eighth goes wide and slow." He lifted his eyes. "In your second month you gave me your ceiling on this oak before the board had told me anything. Give it again."

"Six free, Magister. Tomorrow pays for the seventh. The day after pays for the eighth."

"In the second month it was four."

"Four free and the fifth on credit, so it's two higher now." Cael let his breath go. "That's what four hours a week on this floor buys in a term. I don't think it buys more."

Gault picked the pen up again and wrote for some while, and then, still writing, gave the clerk the line for the record in his flattest voice.

"Working ceiling given by the enrollee: six. Baseline: four. Seven runs within the baseline's spread, with a shorter interval. The ceiling given before the instrument gave it, as before."

He set the pen down.

"That is what the provision is for."

Then a page turned.

Along the east wall the counsel wrote, and Havel wrote because writing was what he had been sent to do, but Ilsev's freshly sharpened pencil had not touched her paper. Cael caught at the very edge of his eye that she had spent the whole trial watching the drum behind the louvres and not him. In the fourth chair nothing had moved.

Then came the first gap, which was the part of the morning Lira's thumbnail had taught him to fear.

Gault leaned toward the Mire instructor and the two of them bent their heads over the sheet and talked low, with the Ash instructor leaning in to listen, and after that the clerk would be sent to the north wall and would take his time over it. A minute and a half, perhaps two, with nothing for anybody on the brass to do.

Cael felt the room go slack round him like a rope let out.

So he followed Lira's instruction and chose somebody, and the somebody he chose was the clerk.

The clerk was a thin man of about thirty, younger in the face than in the hands. He had kept the book through every hour Cael had ever spent in this room, and he sat at his sloped desk now with his pen lifted, waiting to be told what to write next. Cael took him from the top, the way he had been reading people since long before there was a single Path in him to read them with.

Rhythm: slow and even, a breath and a half between movements of the pen, never hurried and never held. Weight: settled back, but with the shoulders a little forward over the desk, ten years' worth of desks. Breath: through the nose, shallow, steady. Hands: the right one with the pen, the left laid flat along the ledger's edge to keep the page from lifting in the draught from the door. And did it all agree? It did. Every layer told the same story, of a man who was what he seemed, a clerk minding his lines, with nothing else hidden in him anywhere.

Plain work, a little dull, and it held. The whole time he went down through the clerk, layer by layer, the corner stayed where he had fixed it, and he could feel it stay. The room had stopped asking him anything, so he had found a question of his own to ask it, and that was enough.

When Gault turned back he let the reading sink a level, as Lira had told him, and left it ticking over underneath like a clock in another room.

"The ignition point," said Gault. "Clerk, the vessel."

From the north wall the clerk carried it in both arms, like something he had been cautioned to mind.

Cael had looked at it every time he came into this room and had never once got a name for it. Squat, copper, three short legs, a little larger than two fists: that was the pot. Out of its lid stood a glass straw scored with lines as close as a comb's teeth. A small brass plate on its belly carried a date of calibration in paint, and the date was this month's. The clerk set it on its legs on the oak a pace from the crossing and stepped back.

Gault nodded at it. "Not a thing you'll have watched in use."

"No, Magister. It's puzzled me since my second week."

"It's for heat. Water, shut in, and a glass to show the water rising. Slow, honest, unhurried, and deaf to argument." He drew a sheet toward him. "Ember and Ash sit it every quarter. Flattery isn't in it." He laid the sheet flat. "Greyvane's record holds a single exhibit for the ignition point. Contact work. One channel. Thin, and late in that house's term. You've chosen to bring it here. Hand on the copper. Make the glass climb."

Down on one knee, Cael spread his palm on the copper's belly. The copper was cool, and faintly oily from the clerk's polishing cloth.

Smallest of all his fragments, the Ember was also the only one he held with a signature behind it: Karis's, dated, at the foot of a page. It went one way and one distance only and beyond that did nothing whatever. It was not heat. He had learned that at Greyvane over two slow months and through two of Karis's words for it, first a spark and later a letting-go.

He let go at the copper.

Under his hand the metal warmed, and a hot band two fingers broad spread under its skin and passed inward to the water, and the thread in the glass straw began to rise. It rose slowly. Slowness was the instrument's whole virtue and the reason Gault trusted it, because nothing quick can fool water, and the thread climbed past one line and the next and stopped.

"Hold it," said Gault.

"I can't hold it, Magister. It doesn't run. Each one's a single moment at the hand, finished in the instant it's made, and then I start over."

"Then make it again."

He made it again, and somewhere between that one and the next, kneeling on the oak with his palm on a copper pot in front of a row of chairs, he understood what Karis's third word was.

It was not a spark, and it was not a letting-go either, though that was what it felt like at the very end; those were both the outside of it. Underneath both lay a choice of an instant. There is an instant when the unburnt turns into the burning, and what he had taken from her let him put that instant wherever he wished at the surface of his own skin. It was a decision point. Everything that came after it was the copper's business, or the water's, or the wood's, and only the moment was his. That was why it would not hold, and why it cost him the same whatever size he asked for, and why each one had to be made new: you could not hold a decision, you could only make another one.

Four times he chose the instant, with the clerk calling the gaps from the pendulum, and four times the thread drank the warmth and rose. Past the fifth line it stood after the fourth, and stayed, while the water made up its mind. Gault watched the glass straw the whole while, and never once the hand.

"For the book," Gault said at last. "Ignition point, shown on the vessel. Four applications. The glass answered each the same, with no drift between them. No heat away from the hand. Contact only." The pen followed his words one by one. "On the enrollee's prior record. Confirmed here, to the wing's standard."

That went down too. Gault's voice kept its level for one more line.

"That is a better instrument reading than the Greyvane exhibit supports," he said. "The exhibit calls it marginal."

"The exhibit's from my first weeks with it, Magister. Nearly a year back."

"Mm," said Gault, and wrote that too.

The copper was cooling toward nothing when Cael lifted his hand. He rose and returned to the crossing. He kept his eyes off the east wall and still knew what had happened along it, since he had counted rooms ever since Ardenmere and could no more stop than stop his heart. The counsel's pen had run on for a long time and she had turned her page to finish, and then she had leaned along the row and said a few words to Havel, and Havel had made a mark. For the first time that morning Ilsev's fresh point met paper.

They had found something: small, true, already on the record, early, and paid for out of a purse he could spare. For whatever was left of the morning, everyone on that wall who had come hoping to catch him out had one catch already, folded into a pocket.

Nothing had been written in the fourth chair. He had nothing to write with.

Water is slow, and Gault would not go on until the glass had settled, so every minute the clerk read the straw and wrote down a figure, and between readings there was nothing for anybody to do but wait.

Cael waited on the crossing and went back to the clerk. The clerk held through the first reading and the second. He held while the Ash instructor rose and went to stand over the copper herself, peering at the straw, and came back. He held through the fourth reading and the fifth, and while the Mire instructor took her hands out of her coat pockets for the first time that day and frowned at her nails. After the sixth reading the thread stopped falling.

"A question before we go on," said Gault. "Four applications. Why four, and not one held? You've told me it doesn't run. I'd like to know what it does instead, as nearly as you can put it."

It was a good question, and that was what was wrong with it.

To answer it honestly Cael had to go a long way in, down past the skin of his own palm after a moment that had taken no time at all. Then he had to pull it apart in front of a panel: what it was, and why it would not stay, and where in it the choosing sat. He had understood it himself only a quarter of an hour before, and finding words for Gault's book was like trying to describe a sneeze while it was still happening.

Down he went after the words. Down there, unattended, the corner slid.

The room's weather changed. He had no better way of putting it. Eight people's worth of being looked at had been pressing on him lightly all morning, the way air presses, and now it eased by a fraction, because eight people had very slightly forgotten that a boy stood on the brass. It lasted two seconds, or less. Across the table the Mire instructor glanced down at her own hands, the way an eye leaves an empty doorway.

He came up out of the answer faster than he had ever come up out of anything.

He said, "Magister. I need an interval."

Gault's pen stopped. "Why?"

"I've been answering you and minding the sitting together, Magister, and I've let one of them drop. Better a minute lost now than a poor figure on your frame."

"Which one slipped?"

"The answer, Magister."

Into her book the Ash instructor laughed, or nearly. Gault's face did not change.

"Clerk. Two minutes, asked for by the enrollee. Give the reason as fatigue affecting response quality." He turned his head a fraction. "And put down that he asked before anything on my table showed it." He looked back at Cael. "Sit for your two minutes. You lose nothing by it. You look as though you're weighing whether you will."

At the rail by the west wall Cael sat with his palms on the cold wood. Two minutes went on hunting down his edges and setting each one back where an ordinary boy of sixteen would wear it. Grudgingly they came, using up nearly the whole allowance, and left behind them an ache behind the eyes like the one the read sent after a long day. An hour and a half still to go: a poor sign.

To the table and the wall alike, nothing had occurred. In the book it would read fatigue affecting response quality. True. At the enrollee's request. True. Nothing more, and that was true as well. That was the art of it.

He got up off the rail.

"Ready, Magister."

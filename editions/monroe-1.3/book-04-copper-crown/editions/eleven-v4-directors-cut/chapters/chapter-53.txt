Chapter 53 — Calibration

[Restrained, intimate narration; give loyalty and doubt room to register]

The residence stair had twenty steps, and Lira took them like a siege.

Her right hand was on the rail and her left arm across Cael's shoulders. She would set the bad leg down on a tread and let it bear for about as long as it takes to say one, two, and then the leg asked to come up again and she let it. The good leg did the climbing. They stood and breathed, and began again.

"Talk," she said on the sixth step. "Say anything. It's worse when it's quiet."

"The practitioner in the yard, while I was in Gault's office. What did she say?"

"That it's a deep strain along the top of the bone and nothing's torn. Four days off it, ice tonight, heat in the morning, and a stick if I've any sense." Lira breathed through her teeth and came up a step. "Then she gave me a long look and said, On purpose?"

"What did you tell her?"

"Yes. So she asked if I'd done it twice." Another step. "I said once. The other three, I said, were accidents."

Below them Brom made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been his knee, which had a bag of ice bound round it and plenty to say about stairs. He was carrying the Copper Crown dangling from two fingers, the way a man carries a hat he has taken off indoors, because Lira had both hands busy and somebody had to. Karis brought up the rear, loaded like a carter: two loaves; a stone jar the kitchen had pressed on her, since the kitchen had watched the final too; Bracken's copies; and a flat grey folder with Cael's name on the tab. She had collected that from the wing's counter on the way past, because the counter was closing and she disliked leaving paper where it might be mislaid.

The climb took them a little over twenty minutes. Cael shouldered the common-room door wide and steered her to the good chair with arms by the stove. She sat without one word of argument. Nothing the practitioner had said frightened him half so much.

For a while nobody did anything useful. Lira sat with her eyes shut until the colour came back into her face. Brom put the crown on the long table and stood frowning at it, then let himself down onto the boards by the stove with the iced knee straight out, and Karis lit the good lamp and filled the kettle.

"Table," said Lira, without opening her eyes.

"In the morning," said Cael.

"The morning's for the thing itself. Table now."

"You can't even stand up."

"I don't need to stand up. My job's to sit and tell you how you look." She opened her eyes. "It's the only use I'm going to be tonight, and I'd like to be some use. Argue with me and I'll lose my temper, and that hurts as well."

Brom studied the ceiling. Karis was already hauling the long table round toward the stove so that Lira would not have to turn her head.

"You went straight at it," Lira said to the ceiling, or perhaps to Brom.

Brom did not pretend not to know what she meant. "You'd have known if I hadn't."

"I'd have known by the first step. And then I'd have had to be kind to you for a week, and I'm very bad at it." She shifted in the chair and her mouth went tight for a moment. "It was the right line. I'd have thrown it myself."

"I know. That's why I threw it." He looked over at the crown on the long table. "What I said on the floor still stands," he said. "Third one someday."

"You said it once. Once was plenty."

"I'm saying it again so it's written down somewhere." He glanced at Karis. "Is it written down?"

Karis, filling the kettle, said, "It is now," and did not look round, and Lira laughed, and said it hurt, and laughed again.

It was Brom's idea to build the wing out of the furniture, and he ran it from the floor like a foreman. The long table, pushed against the settle, became the panel's bench, and Karis's slate, propped on the mantel against the clock, became the wall where the order was posted. Two chairs set back to back stood for the rail of the frame. The hearthrug was the oak. A crack in the floorboards ran very nearly where the brass crossing would be, and Brom pointed at it with deep satisfaction and named it the fourth brass from the near wall.

"And I'm the frame," he said.

"You can't get up," said Karis.

"A frame doesn't get up. A frame sits where it's put and drops weights on people." He settled his back against the wall beside the stove. "I'm better at that than at anything else in this house."

Before Karis could begin, he held up one big hand.

"I'll say mine first, because if I wait she'll have six sheets of sums built and it'll sound as if I'm arguing with them, and I'm not. It's a different thing."

Karis set her chalk down.

"Rooke stands on the gallery in the mornings," said Brom. "He hardly watches our hands. He watches how we carry ourselves between strikes. One day in the second week he stopped the whole hour and pointed at a Shield boy and said, That one's being careful, and he was right, and every one of us could see it as plain as a hat. The boy wasn't doing anything wrong. He just looked like a man carrying a full bowl across a crowded room."

He shifted the knee.

"Tomorrow you're going to walk into Gault's room and be careful for three hours, and you've never once not been careful in all the time I've known you, and I doubt you've the least idea how it shows from across a floor. It shows like that. So don't hide what it costs. Let it cost you. Breathe hard after the board, and shake the arm out after the plate where they can watch you shake it, and let your fourth run be a fourth run, a little worse than the first, the way fourth runs always are. Pull together for the fifth, and make the pulling visible. Any trainer alive knows a working body when he sees one. Give them that, and they'll stop looking for the other thing."

Heat had crept into Cael's ears.

"What does the other thing look like?"

Brom said, "Like somebody with something in his pocket."

Karis took up the chalk again and drew six lines down the slate.

"The wing posts its order for a semester sitting beside the Magister's door, under the calibration card, and I'm fairly sure I'm the only person who has ever read it from top to bottom, the Magister included." She wrote as she spoke. "One, the board. Two, the frame, read only, with your back to the rail. Three, read and move together. Four, the plate. The last two are the first two over again, with an hour of oak in your legs. That's what you did at the baseline. This time the read stops being a trial of its own and gets carried inside the third and the cold runs, which leaves the second slot empty, and the posted sheet says so in so many words: Two, at the enrollee's placing. Anything already on your record that you choose to show goes there, or nowhere." She chalked a short dash beside the figure two. "The fifth slot is new: the heaviest weight on the rail, your square brought in close, and a tray of chalk dust beside it so that the clerk can read your feet from the dust instead of from your account of them."

"The weight I took on the plate."

"That one. At the baseline it was too heavy and too close to get out from under, so they made you stand and take it. Now they want to know whether you can leave."

Next to each row went the baseline's figures, small. Four free on the board, the fifth billed, the sixth wide and slow. Eleven calls in twelve off the frame. Seven clear in eight on the combined, at weight three. The plate held low four times, and not eight. Then she drew two chalk brackets down the right-hand side of the slate, one above the other, with a gap between them.

"A fortnight's work, this: a floor and a roof. Between them is what a term of honest work ought to buy somebody like you, starting from that baseline, with nothing strange happening along the way. Under the floor, the provision has failed in its own plain words, because you were enrolled to be measured by what you can show and you'll have shown nothing." She tapped the upper bracket. "Up past the roof, you're no longer a reading. You're a puzzle, and a room with a puzzle in it stops measuring and starts to wonder. That's worse."

"And between?"

"On the board, five free, or six. On the combined, where the wing numbers its weights one to five from light to heavy, it's weight four with you clear at least five times in six, or weight three with you clear every time." She laid the chalk down. "I made that gap wide on purpose, then wider, and then I gave the sums to Bracken without saying what they were for, and he worked them through and handed them back the same."

"It doesn't look wide."

"The width isn't the hard part. The hard part is doing it on every instrument at once, for a man who's told you to your face that he'd rather have six runs that agree than one wonder and five that don't, while all the time you carry something nobody else in the room knows about." She looked at him. "For three hours."

From the good chair Lira said, "He knows how long it is, Karis."

"I'm saying it so that he hears it in somebody else's voice."

Karis chose to rehearse the plate first, out of the posted order, because it was the one line on the slate that was not allowed to move.

There being no plate in the common room, she handed him the residence's book of hall rules, which was bound in boards nearly an inch thick, and had him hold it out at shoulder height on his right arm, braced the plain way. Brom hit it from the floor, a short heavy sitting blow that went through the book and up the arm and into the shoulder and stopped there.

"Your account," said Karis, borrowing Gault's flatness.

"Four I can stand. Not eight. The shoulder keeps it."

"Again."

He took four more, and the book jumped the same distance each time, and his shoulder made the same complaints in the same order. She had him do a third set and then a fourth, and they came off the press like printed pages, every one the twin of the one before, until she put the chalk down.

"Now give me a scared one," she said. "Do the set you'd do for a roomful of people who scare you."

It took him three goes to make it look real, and he did not enjoy discovering that. In the end he let the arm fold a little further on every blow, and after the third he stepped back off the hearthrug and said he could not take another.

Karis stood with her arms folded and looked at the slate.

"I hadn't seen that coming, and it would have had you." Her chalk rapped the lower bracket. "For two weeks you've planned against the roof. Don't go over; don't become a question. Now look where you just went. Under. That plate line sits in Gault's own log in Gault's own hand with four, not eight beside it, because you said it before he asked, and anybody in that room can lay tomorrow's line over it in the time it takes to blink. Go in frightened and clamp down on everything by feel, and you won't merely scrape the floor; you'll go clean through it. Then the record says that a boy got worse over a whole supervised term while the Compact sat and watched it happen."

Nobody said anything.

"So it was never hold back," Karis said. "It's land on the number. The plate doesn't move by so much as a graduation, and everything else moves exactly as far as a term would move it. That's a great deal harder than holding back, and it's the actual job."

She wrote a line across the foot of the slate, under both brackets, in letters bigger than all the rest: ON THE NUMBER.

The combined trial went the other way.

Brom threw from the wall, a straight sitting blow at whatever instant he chose, while Cael stood on the crack in the boards with his back half turned and had to feel it coming and be gone. Nothing in the house came nearer to a weight falling down a rail. The first run was clean. Cael felt it gather along the outsides of his arms a pace and a half out, turned his shoulder out of its road, and was clear, and Karis timed the gap on the wash-house glass she had borrowed and wrote it down and put her question and got her answer. The second run was clean as well.

The third was clean and quick and smooth as oil. He felt it go right and had just begun to be pleased when Karis said, "Stop."

"That was the best one."

"That was a five," said Karis.

For a moment the word meant nothing to him.

"No frame here," she said, "and nobody's made a scale for punches. But there's a glass, and my eyes, and Brom, and all three agree: that run beat your first by a gap I could see bare-eyed. You went off it at an angle I've never once seen you use near anybody from the wing. If Brom were a weight on a rail, that wasn't the fourth. That was the heaviest thing they own."

"Brom?"

Brom rubbed his fist. "You weren't reading me at the skin," he said slowly. "You were reading me further in. Like at the post, before the delegation came."

Cael took hold of the nearest chair-back.

He understood it about twenty seconds before he could say it, and those were the worst twenty seconds of the evening. Then he said it, because things of that kind were what this room existed for.

"The read's open. All the way down. Since the delegation came I've kept it shallow, because the hold and the reach drink from one cup and the cup is small, and the thin layer costs nothing; it reads a room, but it can't read a fist." He let go of the chair. "This afternoon in the yard I took my hand off it, because nobody was looking at me and I wanted to see the two of you properly. The hold was never in danger, because there was far too much to watch. But the read came all the way up while I was watching, and it's been up ever since, and I never noticed."

Karis had gone very still. "And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow I hold from waking, the read drops to its stub, and that run can't happen." He was glad his voice stayed even. "Whatever I put on Gault's oak in the third trial, I'll be putting there with my best instrument turned nearly off. He'll write it down. It'll be honest, and it'll fall short of what I've got."

"Inside the gap?"

He ran the figures. They came out better than anything else that hour had offered.

"Fourth weight. Clear five times in six. Read on its stub." He let his breath go. "Inside. Right on the floor of it."

"Then put it there," said Karis. She chalked it beside the third line and offered no comfort whatever, and that was a great deal better than comfort.

The fifth trial they did not try. Nothing in the house could stand in for the heaviest weight on a thin margin, and there was no baseline line to land on, since the baseline had never run it, and Karis would not rehearse what she could not measure. That went on the slate too, sooner than pretend.

5. No rehearsal. Nothing to land on. Do it honestly and let it fall where it falls.

Under it she added a line, and read it out as she wrote.

Hold going past thin: stop the trial. Say it aloud. An interval you ask for goes in the book as a line. A drift goes in as evidence.

"You're allowed to stop?" said Brom. "In the middle of a thing?"

"Gault said so on the first morning, and it's in my notebook." Cael found the words where he had kept them. "When you reach something you cannot do, tell me. The book will record that you could not. Nothing more, and nothing held against you; a plain fact, written down. If I have to work out for myself that you've refused, it tells me less than if you had said so." He shrugged. "I've carried that folded up in my head all term and never once spent it."

In the second hour Karis began to run the gaps, and the gaps were where it came apart.

She ran them as a panel would. After every trial she turned half away with Brom and put her head close to his over the sheet and talked low for a minute and a half. Cael stood on the crack in the floor and waited, as he would wait a dozen times tomorrow, and perhaps more.

Partway through the hour he felt himself slip. It was not much. It was the feeling he had learned since the stair, the hand on the rope sliding an inch without being told to while his mind was looking at something else, and he caught it and pulled it back and was rather pleased with himself for catching it.

"There," said Lira.

He looked round. She lay sideways across the good chair, the bad hip propped on a cushion under a cloth of ice, and in the polished arm of the chair, under her right thumb, there was a short row of marks scored into the wax with her thumbnail. He counted them from where he stood, and there were ten.

"That's how many?"

"That's how many I've seen tonight. You caught four. I watched your face when you did." She ran her thumb along the row. "Nine were nothing much, two or three seconds and back, and nobody else in the world would have seen them. I'm not being clever. I've looked at you for three years, and that's the only reason I can. But it's ten."

"Where?"

"That's the thing." She lifted her thumb. "Never while Brom was hitting you, and never while Karis was asking you something. Every one came while she had her back turned and you were standing on that crack waiting for her to finish. You were waiting, all ten times."

Before Lira had finished, Karis had ten of ten on the slate. "That isn't bad luck," she said, half to herself. "That's how it works."

Cael didn't move. The pieces were joining.

"Eight days ago, in hall three, Brom found it," he said slowly. "I wrote it the wrong way up. I thought the danger was being busy, with all of me taken up at once. It isn't. Busy is the safest thing there is. It never goes while I'm working, not on a drill or at the rail or through four minutes of watching you two without blinking. Leave me standing with nothing asked of me, and it goes. An idle rope is the one that runs out through your fingers."

"Then don't stand there with nothing being asked," said Lira.

Everybody turned to her.

"You keep treating it as a thing to grit your teeth at. Your page of tricks is all about doing. Gaps have nothing to do in them. So give yourself something." She shifted the ice. "When Gault turns his back tomorrow, choose somebody in that room, anybody, and read them the whole way down: how they breathe, where their weight sits, what their hands are doing, and whether all of it agrees. Just the one person. And when the panel turns back to you, don't stop dead. Let it drop a level and keep it running underneath."

"The read'll be on its stub. I won't have—"

"I don't mean the read." Her voice sharpened. "I mean you. The gaze, the notebook, the watching, the habit you made for yourself back when there wasn't a single Path in you. That isn't a fragment, Cael. Nobody gave you that. Nobody else had it before you; of your whole ledger it's the only such thing. It charges the hold nothing. And it'll keep you out at your edges three hours straight. That's the whole trouble solved."

The room was quiet.

Cael thought of a line in the working ledger from the first week after the stair, which said that the hiding half came unasked and went out like a pinched wick the moment he truly looked. He'd been calling that a flaw, and it was a cure. Gaze and hiding would not share him. Whichever one he ran, the other stopped.

Karis said, "Yes. That works," and looked faintly annoyed with herself.

Lira shrugged with one shoulder. "I've had nothing to do all evening but look."

Her eyes stayed on him, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, and he understood that this was what the twenty steps had been for.

"That's half. The other half's less welcome. Tomorrow's about being measured right, and everything tonight has been about that, and I've helped, and it's right. But you fight, Cael. Whatever they lay their instruments against had better still be you. Clamp everything down and give Gault a careful boy sitting neatly on Karis's floor, and yes, you'll pass. You'll also come out of that room a little less. Then you'll do it again next half-year, and the half-year after that, and every time a little less."

"You don't know that."

"I know a clerk at Fenmark who never once looked up from his page." She said it without any heat. "He didn't mean me harm. He meant to be tidy. That's how they do it, mostly; they tidy you." She held his eyes. "Be the fighter. In a measured room. Both."

By the first hour after the last bell Brom had gone up, a tread at a time, with the ice in his hand and his right knuckles strapped to the second joint in Karis's clean linen; they had taken Lira's forearms all afternoon and the hall-rules book all evening, and at last they had split. Lira was asleep sideways in the good chair with her cheek against its wing, which she would deny in the morning, and Karis was kneeling at the stove with the poker, banking it for the night. On the mantel the slate still had its six lines and two brackets and ON THE NUMBER across the foot.

Cael opened the grey folder then, because the wing's marks on his coursework were wing marks and might belong on Karis's slate.

The three exercises were inside, in the driest hand he owned, every word of them true and none of them complete, each with a tick beside its title and two figures in its margin and an initial on the tab, as the wing marked everything. Something he had never handed in lay against the back board. A slip of paper.

Wing paper, trimmed small. Wing hand, too: the trained script every clerk there learned until it was nobody's. It had no name and no date. To anybody who had not already been turning the same question over, it would have meant nothing at all.

Panels see what they expect prepared for. Show growth where the baseline predicts it. Surprise them exactly once, small, early — a watcher who finds one thing stops hunting for a second.

His breath forgot itself the first time through. On the second he remembered.

Then he learned it. It was short and had plainly been made to be learned, and it took him about a minute. The back was blank. He walked to the stove, where Karis knelt with the poker, and said, "Excuse me," and she moved her hand, and he opened the little door and laid the slip flat on the red coals. It went brown at one corner and black across the middle, and in four seconds or so it was gone, and the ash it left could not be told from any other.

He stayed crouched by the stove with his palm on the warm iron door.

Whoever had written it had taken a trade that must have cost years to learn, cut a corner off it, and laid it in the back of a boy's coursework knowing perfectly well what it would mean for him if it were ever found. What could Cael give back for that? Only this: that the paper should be gone inside a minute of being read. He had nothing else to give.

Beside him Karis had not moved. She was looking at the stove door with the poker across her knees.

"Was that—" she began.

She stopped, and he watched it happen. The question rose into her face the way questions always did, quick and bright and certain of its right to an answer, and then something else came down over it and covered it as quietly as you lay a cloth over a birdcage.

"No," she said. "Never mind."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She gave the coals a last push and hung the poker on its hook. "Is it any good?"

"Nobody's told me anything truer about tomorrow. I'd have paid a year for it."

"Then spend it," said Karis, and went back to her fair copy at the far end of the table, and neither of them ever spoke of it again.

Show growth where the baseline predicts it. Karis had reached that by another road altogether, with her brackets, and that was what first made him believe it: Karis and whoever had cut it had never exchanged a word on the subject, and they had built the same rule out of opposite materials. Then the second line. Surprise them exactly once, small, early. That was the piece missing from everything, the piece he could never have made himself, and he sat at the table with a clean sheet for most of twenty minutes before he saw it.

It had to be on the record already, or it would not be a surprise but a confession. It had to be small. And it had to be something nobody at Halcenvane had ever seen.

Only one thing he owned met all three. It was the Ember, Karis's ignition point, which sat in a single thin half-page at the back of the Greyvane transcript, where anybody who paid six marks could read it and nobody ever had. No instrument in the wing had measured it and no panel on the bluff had seen it, and on paper it was the least remarkable thing he possessed: a point of fire at contact, gone in the instant it was made. The wing put its instruments on whatever an enrollee laid before it, and the placing was his.

He looked down the table.

"Karis."

She lifted her pen.

"The Ember stays off the floor. That's what we agreed when term began, because it was yours before it was mine." The good chair had a sleeper in it; he spoke low. "Tomorrow I'd like it on Gault's oak. Once. Second. I'm asking you."

Karis looked at him over the lamp, and he saw her not ask why.

"Yes," she said. "Second. And show it properly, because I've never seen anybody measure it." She went back to her copy. "Then come and tell me what it did."

The new order went down twice, fair. Nothing else went into the stove. Then he went up to his window.

Ledger, day twenty-one.

Held: from waking, nineteen hours. Read: let up in the yard on purpose and never put back, which I found out at the table. Drifts: ten by Lira's thumbnail, four by my own count, and all ten in the gaps.

Given tonight: a gap between two brackets with a registrar's sums under it. A way to look like a man working instead of a man carrying a full bowl. Something to do with the empty minutes that costs nothing, because it's the only part of me nobody handed me. And one thing more, which is where it belongs, and is ash.

Nobody gave me a present tonight. Everybody at that table gave me a piece of tomorrow instead. People who love you are one thing. People who are in it with you are another. Two years ago I had neither, and now somehow I've both.

The lamp went down a notch. Then he wrote the rest.

Quenna asked me at Greyvane whether I could keep from using it with people watching who'd be glad to see me hurt, and I told her I didn't know. Tomorrow I find out. Six trials across three hours, and under all of them a thing on a short leash that would dearly love to slip it, in the most watched room of my life.

Only I'm not sure the man watching would be glad to see me hurt. I've had a week of him. Three things he asked Gault, and every one concerned when the posts were last checked. In nine hours at the records table his pen moved five times, and every time it was about this house's paper and never about me. He sat through a final with his book shut and wrote one line at the end of it. Nothing in that is hoping I fall over. What it wants is the figure to come out true.

He doesn't wish me harm. He wishes me measured.

All evening I've tried to decide which is worse. No luck. Perhaps I'll know by noon.

Sleep. Fight in the morning.

The afternoon paper reached the assessment wing on the twenty-fourth a little after the fourth bell, and the delegation's closing docket was in it.

It was a single sheet under the delegation's green seal, with a slip pinned at one corner by a brass pin that had been bent and straightened at least once before. The paper-boy dropped it with the others at the counter's end and went off whistling. The desk clerk was four feet away with the term's floor ledger open in front of him, adding a column under his breath and losing his place every time the whistling reached the stair. So it was Seln who signed for the docket, because signing was the counter's work, and Seln who read it.

It discharged the loan. The wing's compilation of six filings, requisitioned on the evening of the twelfth, had been received, examined, and returned under the delegation's stamp. Below that, in a clerk's round hand, came the line the form required and nobody ever read: No extract or copy retained. And under that, a signature he knew from the wing's own ledger, the list clerk's, on the delegation's behalf.

He filed it in the requisitions book, in its place, and blotted the entry. Anyone watching would have seen that and nothing more.

The file itself had come home ten days before.

It had come at the sixth bell of the fourteenth, carried in by the delegation's list clerk with Returned with thanks and a receipt on its face. The desk clerk had signed for it. Seln had risen from the copying table and taken the case in both hands and set it on the shelf behind him among the wing's other sealed things, with a boy from the provision standing at the counter for his floor sheet, and he had not looked at it again while the room had people in it.

From the evening of the twelfth, when the stack went across to the guest floor, until that sixth bell of the fourteenth, he had waited for four minutes alone with it. He had made no arrangement of any kind to be the one who handled it when it came; he had not needed to, since it was his table.

When the counter was clear, and the boy gone, and the desk clerk gone down to his supper, he took the case down to the copying table and untied the knot.

In four minutes he went over every sheet with his eyes and every edge with the side of his thumb.

A paper that has been read carries the reading on it, if you know where to feel. Corners go soft where a thumb has held them back. A pin worked loose and pushed home again leaves the hole a fraction rounder. A stack that has lain open under a lamp for an evening comes back faintly warm-smelling, of tallow and of the room it lay in. He had spent two years of his youth learning to feel those things in the dark, and he felt for all of them now with the face of a man checking a count.

Six filings, in their order, each with its appendix pinned behind it. On top, his own index, in his fourth hand. And on none of it a single mark. No pencil, faint or otherwise. No turned corner. No pin moved from the hole it had been put through. No slip tucked in, and none taken out, and no fold that had not been there when the stack went across. An evaluation seat had held it for two days, with every right in the charter to write in its margins, and had handed it back as clean as a sheet still in the ream.

Then he had tied the knot again as it had been, and shelved the case, and gone down to his own supper at the pace a clerk keeps when nothing has happened.

And now the docket said No extract or copy retained. A reader who kept no copy, and wrote nothing on the original, had kept whatever he thought somewhere else.

His first master, at the long table up the coast, had chalked a thing on the board for new auditors once, and Seln had copied it into the back of a book at twenty-two and never needed the book since. A clean return is one of three. Not read. Read and passed. Read by a man who keeps his notes in his head. The first was common and the second was commoner. The third, the old man had said, rubbing it out again with his sleeve, you would meet perhaps twice in a career, and only ever at one height.

Two evenings later, in his room on the staff corridor, Seln sat down to the quarterly with the shutter fastened and the lamp low.

Of the form's five parts, four were furniture, and he had filled them so often that his hand did them without much help from him. Coverage held from the first day, by way of nothing but the posting's ordinary work. The subject's days, set out in full across a page and a half: floor hours under supervision, lectures, the library, the ladder from its gallery, the wing's own unit, a habit of private writing, a handful of letters posted in the open to people already on file. No one had ever written down the boy's days more truly, and none of it would tell a reader anything. Associations unchanged since the bridge. The inspection, the delegation, the sittings, the observed evaluation, all entered in a voice a harbour clerk keeps for tides.

Then came the fifth part, which the posting was for, and it took him longer than the other four put together, and when it was done it was four lines long.

Assessment of period: routine compliance.

No undisclosed capability observed.

Subject's conduct consistent with enrollment basis.

Continuation of embedded coverage recommended.

He laid the pen in the gutter of the desk and read them over, and changed nothing.

He tried the first three the way the old man at the long table had taught him to try another officer's work: pretending a stranger had written each one and Seln were being paid by the fault. The boy had complied with everything the house asked of him all term. Observed, in the manual, meant observed under conditions that allowed confirmation, and that was a narrow door, and nothing had come through it. The enrollment basis was evaluation by demonstration; the boy had demonstrated in front of the Compact's best eyes and been renewed. Each line bore weight. He had learned over a term, a sentence at a time, how much weight a true line would bear before it cracked, and he had loaded each of these three to that mark and not a hair beyond.

The fourth bore everything else.

There was nothing in it; it was the most ordinary line the form allowed. A briefing officer meets that line at the foot of one report in three, the year round, and ticks it the way a man nods to a neighbour. All it would do was leave a Bronze clerk at his copying table, minding other men's mistakes, through another term.

There was a reason he had written it, and it was not on the form, or on any page anywhere: not in his own cipher, not in the small case, not in a word to anybody on the bluff. He had made nobody a promise and no arrangement with a living soul. For a term he had gone on setting down true sentences and had stopped setting down some others, and that was all, except for one slip of wing paper.

He had cut it himself with the wing's knife, small enough to lie flat inside a coursework folder and thin enough to catch at once. No signature, and a hand that was nobody's. Shaped to be burned, it had very likely been burned. Of all the things that had ever passed between him and the boy, it was the nearest to a conversation. It existed nowhere now, which was where he kept conversations.

Signature, extracts, seal. The packet went to stand against the door frame, ready for the fish steps at first light.

There remained the day's page, folded in his inside pocket.

Four lines, in the fifth hand, the shorthand that had grown out of the cramped one. The date the carriages left. The serial of the reserve frame, copied from the wing's maintenance book. The date the file had come back, the fourteenth, and the docket's date beside it. And one line under them that was not a fact at all, of the kind he let himself write perhaps twice a year.

unmarked file. one of three. probably the third.

He fetched the key from the hollow spine of the harbour-dues treatise and knelt on the boards, and the draught under the door got into his sleeves at once, and his knees made their usual remarks. Out from under the bed came the large case; up came the shirts; and there underneath, as it had been in every room for years, lay the small one, its leather gone soft and its brass corners dull.

The new page went on top. Below it lay whatever lay below it, and he did not lift it or count it. A locked case was worth precisely as much as nobody's knowing what it held, and not looking was part of the lock.

He locked it. Shirts back; large case home under the bed and hard against the wall; then his shoulder to the foot of the bed, and the bed walked back after it until the frame met plaster. His body did that in every room without asking him.

The lamp went out under his fingers, but he did not lie down. At the bed's edge, in the dark, he stayed sitting, his hands empty.

What he kept, he kept.

The unit met on the twenty-fifth at the sixth bell in the long room that had been a map store, and it went as it had gone every Third-day since the first one.

Seln set an exercise on the slate in the round copying hand: A door, a window, a lamp. Where does a person entering first look? Where last? Show your reasons. Then he walked the room while they worked, slowly, down one side of the long table and up the other, as he always did, never stopping behind anyone for longer than it took to read a line. A sentence put a third-year's sums right; a pencil dot mended a Stone girl's sketch. He did not look at Cael; in all the weeks of the unit his eyes had never once rested on Cael in that room, and they did not now. When the porter reached the arch, Seln gathered the sheets, knocked them square on the table, and dismissed them with the porter's name.

Gwen had drawn a small eye at the foot of hers, as she did at the foot of everything. On the way out she told Cael that she had seen the Archmarshal's carriage go and had felt, watching it, as if somebody had taken a heavy pan off a stove she had not known was lit.

Cael pointed out that she had never once laid eyes on the man himself, not in eleven days.

"I know," said Gwen. "That's what made it so frightening." She wound her green scarf twice round her neck. "My aunt says the ones you never see are the ones doing the work. She says that about cooks too."

The next day he wrote to Hesk.

No letter since Ardenmere had given him so much trouble. He sat over it most of an evening, because what he had to tell Hesk would not sit up and look like news, however he dressed it. An Archmarshal came to look at me for eleven days, and he has gone, and I do not know what he decided. That was the whole of it, and everything else was decoration.

So he wrote it plainly, the way Hesk liked letters to come. He copied out the last lines of Gault's note, word for word, with the stamp described. He gave the east room a paragraph and no more: the size of it, the light, eleven questions and a twelfth that was not asked. He did not say what the twelfth would have been, or what it would have cost him. He wrote, at the end, I'm well, the hip is mending, Lira won, and you'll have heard by now that the pen writes better than I do.

On his way back from the library that afternoon he had stopped at the wing's counter with the week's coursework. Seln was at the copying table, as he was every afternoon, and when the desk clerk waved a hand at the tray Seln got up and took the sheet from Cael and read the head of it and laid it in the tray, face down, square to the others.

"Thank you for the form," said Cael.

"Mm," said Seln, and sat down again.

It had been the same three words and the one sound every week since the counter had first been opened to him. On the way out he kept his mind off it. He had trained himself, over weeks, not to think about it until he was past the covered walk's north arch, and then not to think about it there either.

He read the finished letter over twice before he folded it. It was true in every line, and it left out the only part of the fortnight he could not have explained to anyone: that a man had walked up to the edge of him and stopped, and that he had not known until that moment that he had spent his whole life waiting for somebody to do it. That part did not belong in a letter. For now it belonged inside his coat, and maybe nowhere else.

He sealed it and took it to the courier post himself in the morning, and walked back up the bluff road in a thin wind past the two men at the foot of it, who changed on the bell as he went by and did not look at him at all.

Toward evening on the twenty-seventh a hand-cart came across the second quadrangle with Karis behind it.

Cael saw her from the covered walk. It was a library cart, the low kind with iron wheels that the porters used for returns, and it was loaded with a pasteboard box and a stack of books under an oilcloth, and Karis was pushing it at a pace that a hand-cart on cobbles did not like. One of the wheels had a flat place and knocked at every turn, and she did not slow down for it.

Two first-years crossing toward the lecture range stood aside for her with their mouths open. A porter carrying a ladder stopped, considered the cart, considered Karis, and decided it was none of his business.

He caught up with her where the records hall's corner met the walk.

"What—"

"Don't ask." She did not stop pushing. "Not yet. I'll tell all of you at once, and I'll tell it once. Take the other handle."

He took the other handle.

At the steps Bracken's inky-knuckled clerk was coming out with a bundle of dockets. He looked at the cart, and at the oilcloth, and at Karis's face, and then held the door wide for them both without being asked, and went on down the steps.

They went in by the records hall's main door and through the stone hall and up the back stair. The cart would not climb a stair, so they carried the load up in their arms in three goes, while the cart waited at the bottom like a patient dog. Karis had a key on her wrist, and it opened a door on the top floor at the end of a passage Cael had never had reason to walk down, into a narrow room with a table and a window and a card in a brass frame on the door. The card said, in Bracken's upright hand, Reading room. Assigned: K. Dellenmoor.

The ink on it was fresh.

"Bracken," said Cael.

"Bracken." The pasteboard box went onto the table, squared to its edge. "I went to the counter an hour ago and asked for a named room on the top floor. He looked at me. He took a card out of a drawer and wrote my name on it and gave me the key. He didn't ask what for." She straightened. "I counted. Ninety seconds, from my mouth opening to the key in my hand."

Her eyes stayed on the card, and Cael watched her decide not to think about it yet. He knew the look; he had seen it on her face over a dozen documents she meant to come back to.

There were three more loads after that one. The founding-era copies, in their folders. Two bound books of the repeal search. Prynn's index, which Karis carried up the stair herself and would not let him touch. Last of all came the two notebooks from Greyvane, in their oilcloth, and Karis carried those up in both arms against her chest and put them on the table, and then stood for a long breath with her hand flat on the top one.

Halfway through the second notebook was a page Cael knew, and Karis knew it too. Neither of them said so.

On the last trip she stopped at the head of the stair with the Greyvane notebooks held against her coat and looked back down it, the whole steep flight of it, to the stone hall at the bottom.

"I never once thought about where they slept," she said. "Half a year in that carrel. I thought about the light and the chair and whether the door shut. I never thought about what the room was, on paper." She shifted the weight in her arms. "I write about the meaning of words for a living, and I never read the word on my own door."

"You'd no reason to."

"That's what everybody says before the sweep." She went on up the passage.

When the room was full, she locked it, and hung the key on her wrist, and wheeled the empty cart back across the quadrangle to the library, with the flat wheel knocking all the way. Then she went to fetch Lira and Brom.

She gave it to them with the common-room door shut, reading from the page she had written standing in the corridor. Her pencil had gone through the paper in two places, and she seemed unaware of it.

"The corridor from the records hall to the library's east stair. Fifth bell. I was heading for the library," Karis read. "He was coming the other way with a box of forms. The teaching assistant." She looked up once and down again. "He stopped. I've walked past that man four hundred times and he has never stopped. He stood against the shelving to let me by, which anybody would. And then he said, 'Miss Karis.'"

Brom's hands went still on his knee.

"I said yes. He said, 'Records retention.' The box shifted onto his hip." She read on in the same voice, flat, like a clerk reading a deed. "'The inspection cycle closes on the tenth of next month. After every inspection the registrar's office takes in whatever's been left in the house's shared research spaces. Fourth part of the charter. It goes under the house's accession, catalogued, for good. Loose papers as well as bound.' I said I knew what an archive sweep was. He said: 'Then you'll know researchers sometimes prefer to avoid it. The law range's carrels are shared, as the charter defines it. A top-floor reading room, once it's assigned to a name, isn't.'" She turned the page over. The back was blank. "Then he said 'Mm,' and went on."

Nobody said anything.

Lira spoke first. She had been stretched on the settle with her eyes shut, and now she opened them.

"The law range. He didn't say the carrels. He said the law range."

"He named the range," said Karis.

"And there's one of us with a carrel in it."

Brom frowned. "Is it real? The sweep. Or did he make it up to frighten you into moving?"

"It's real. I had the charter out of the records hall inside the hour. Fourth part, near the back. It runs on the tenth, the way he said, and it takes working papers." Her voice held, but her pencil, lying on the table, rolled a little where her hand had knocked it. "Every word he said is ordinary. Any enrollee could have got the lot from a clerk by asking the right question at the counter. No panel could be shown a word of it. Not one sentence."

Brom had taken his iced knee off the stool and put both feet on the floor, as he did when a thing needed his whole weight. "Then he knew where your notebooks live."

"He knew where the notebooks were since the third week of term. Carrels go through the wing's floor and space book. He keeps the book. He's known where my papers sleep since the day he first opened that book." Her eyes went down to the wood between her hands. "What's written in them he's known for however long. A week. A month. Since the first day. That's the one thing I'll never find out."

Cael was at the window, where he had drifted while she read. He went there when he wanted his face out of the room; not one of them had ever said so.

"And not a word of it went down the river," said Lira to his back.

He did not answer at once, but came back from the window instead, and held out his hand to Karis, and she understood and gave him the marbled book from her satchel. He opened it on the table where all three could see.

"The day after the pin. Read it."

Karis found it with one finger. "Two at the landing. Two at the road's foot. Change on the bell."

"When Bracken's four boxes crossed the quadrangle."

"Two and two. On the bell."

"The day the notice came. The delegation's arrival. The day they left."

She turned the pages and read each one, and each one was the same line in the same hand.

"Whoever pays those coats changes them inside a fortnight when a report gets interesting," said Cael. "I've watched it happen twice in my life. If anything with the true thing in it had gone down that river since the pin, this book would have a different line in it somewhere. It hasn't." He closed the marbled boards. "The Log's had that since before my birthday. I've never found a way to say what it means that didn't sound like hoping. So I stopped saying it."

He gave the book back to her.

"Tonight he told us."

Karis shook her head. "He didn't tell us anything. That's the point of it. Write down every word he said and you'll have a timetable. Not one sentence of it says what we all heard."

"No. Not one." Cael sat. "That's his language. It's the only one he owns. A man who can be read loses his post, so he talks the one way nobody can read: like a clerk passing on a timetable. Tonight, then, he picked the one person on this bluff who'd be finished if those papers were read, recited her a timetable, and stood aside while she carried them up a stair."

On the settle Lira tipped her head back against the arm and considered the plaster.

Then she said, "So that's what he is now."

Cael said, "That's what he's been since the pin. He just let us see it."

Brom asked the question that had been on all their faces.

"You've thought that about him for months. You said so at the window. What changed tonight?"

"Up to tonight, all he did was leave things out." Without noticing, Cael had laid his hands flat on the wood, the way Karis did; he had caught the habit somewhere in the term and stopped minding. "Leaving out costs nothing. It's like a feint you never throw: nobody can score it. Haul him before a board and he'd say the threshold was never met. They'd have a stack of true reports and nothing else, and they'd have to dig a reason out of what he didn't write. That's why he's done it that way. It's craft."

"And tonight?"

"Omission is free," said Cael. "Tonight he performed an act." He let them hear it. "He stopped. On his own account, in a corridor, four days after an inspection went down the hill, he picked something up and carried it across a line on purpose. It was small. He could deny every word. It's the most careful way it could ever have been done. Still, he did it. With his own mouth, for papers that aren't even mine."

Cael kept back one thing. Before tonight there had been a single other act, folded small, and the common-room stove, and Karis at the hearthrug watching it go up. It had been made for one reader and made not to last, and he had settled long since that no spoken sentence of his would ever hold it. Karis kept it back too. She always had, and she did now.

This one had somebody standing in the corridor to hear it, and that was what made it different.

"So what do we do?" said Brom.

"Nothing at all. Nobody thanks him. Nobody nods to him on the walk. Nobody changes one thing about how they come into that wing." Somebody who's grateful decides he ought to know it." He let that settle. "Gratitude is the leak. Not a report. Not an audit. It's a kind word, on the stair, some morning, to the wrong man."

Karis had said nothing for some time. Now she took her hands off the table and put them in her lap, which Cael could not remember ever seeing her do. She sat like that, looking at the place on the wood where they had been.

"There's a line for the minute. Hear it first. It's a finding, and I'd rather it weren't."

"I sat here and told you that an instrument doesn't change sides; it changes readings. That was in this room. I said it with my hands on this table, more than once, as if it closed the matter." Her eyes stayed on the table. "It's true. It's a true thing about instruments. I was applying a correct principle to the wrong class of object. He isn't an instrument. He's a man who's been used as one, for years, by an office that will drop him the day he stops working for it. And I drew him, the whole of him, from the way he was used and not from what he is."

The stove ticked, and nobody moved.

"That's the mistake every house you've ever walked into has made about you," said Karis to Cael. "Every one. I've spent a year and a half writing that down about them. And I went and made it myself. In ink. Signed." She lifted her eyes at last. "That's the finding, and it's mine. I want it on a page that I was wrong, in those words. Next time I start to, the page will be waiting."

"Noted," said Cael.

"Say it properly."

Cael brought out the Log, laid it open in front of her, and uncapped Hesk's pen.

"Karis Dellenmoor's finding, in her words, at her request," he said, writing it as he said it. "Wrong class of object. She'll want this page in a month and hate it when she reads it." He turned the book round to her. "Properly enough?"

Karis read it twice.

"Yes. That's what the page is for."
